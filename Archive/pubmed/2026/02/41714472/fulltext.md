# FOXK1 induced upregulation of KIF20A promotes hepatocellular carcinoma progression via Wnt/β-Catenin/EMT signaling


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-026-06131-2.


## Introduction
  Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common type of primary liver cancer, accounting for 75% to 85% of all primary liver cancer cases [1]. According to GLOBOCAN 2020 data, HCC ranks as the sixth most commonly diagnosed cancer worldwide and the third leading cause of cancer-related death, with approximately 900,000 new cases and over 830,000 deaths each year [2, 3]. HCC exhibits a significantly higher incidence in males than in females, with a male-to-female ratio of approximately 2–3:1. High-incidence areas are primarily found in Asia and sub-Saharan Africa, particularly in China, which accounts for nearly half of the global HCC cases due to the high prevalence of hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection [4, 5]. While the typical age of onset is over 50 years, younger patients are also commonly affected in HBV-endemic regions. Major risk factors for HCC include chronic HBV or hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection, liver cirrhosis, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), obesity, type 2 diabetes, long-term alcohol consumption, and exposure to aflatoxins [6, 7]. In recent years, HCC incidence has declined in certain regions owing to widespread HBV vaccination and advancements in antiviral therapies. However, in some developed countries, the incidence continues to rise, mainly due to the growing prevalence of metabolic syndrome-related conditions [8, 9]. Although radical hepatectomy offers curative potential for some patients, the high rate of postoperative recurrence and metastasis remains a major obstacle to long-term survival. Recently, targeted therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have shown promising efficacy in a variety of cancers, including HCC. Nevertheless, a substantial proportion of patients still experience poor clinical outcomes. Therefore, it is imperative to identify novel molecular targets to enhance the therapeutic effectiveness in HCC.

  Kinesin family members (KIFs) are ATP-dependent molecular motor proteins that move along microtubule tracks, playing critical roles in intracellular cargo transport, organelle positioning, regulation of microtubule dynamics, and cell cycle progression [10, 11]. With over 40 members and numerous subfamilies, including KIF1–KIF14, the human KIF family is extremely large and diverse. Motor domains, neck regions, and tail domains make up their usual structures; the former two are in charge of microtubule binding and force generation, and the latter two are in charge of cargo recognition [12, 13]. Evidence suggests that cancer cell motility, invasion, resistance to chemotherapy, and aberrant expression or functional dysregulation of KIF family members are intimately linked to carcinogenesis and cancer progression [14, 15]. As an example, Eg5 inhibitors target KIF11 (Eg5), a gene that is essential for spindle formation and chromosome segregation; overexpression of this gene promotes fast tumor cell division [16, 17]. Several malignancies, including breast, lung, and kidney tumors, are associated with a poor prognosis when KIF14 expression is high. KIF14 plays an essential role in controlling the cell cycle’s G2/M phase [18]. Overexpression of KIF2A has been associated with tumor metastasis in gastrointestinal and neurological malignancies; members of the KIF2 family, such as KIF2A, have microtubule depolymerase activity, which affects cell motility and mitosis by controlling microtubule dynamics [19–22]. Members of the KIF3 and KIF5 families also modulate the metabolism and shape of tumor cells via their roles in organelle transport and signal transduction. In addition, several KIF proteins have the ability to influence the tumor microenvironment through controlling the intracellular membrane system, which enhances the release of immune modulatory molecules and makes immune evasion easier. By modifying the cytoskeleton and signaling pathways, KIF proteins also affect the properties of cancer stem cells and the tolerance to chemotherapy. These mechanisms are leading to a growing consensus that members of the KIF family could play a pivotal role in cancer diagnostics, biomarkers, and precision targeted treatments. But how KIFs manifest themselves and what they do in HCC were major mysteries.

  Transcription factors and the Wnt signaling pathway play a complex and critical role in tumorigenesis, especially in HCC, where their interactions significantly influence disease progression [23, 24]. The canonical Wnt pathway involves β-catenin-dependent signaling: following Wnt ligand binding to its receptor Frizzled and co-receptor LRP5/6, the β-catenin destruction complex is blocked, resulting to β-catenin buildup in the cytoplasm and its eventual translocation into the nucleus [25, 26]. In the nucleus, β-catenin binds to T-cell factor/lymphoid enhancer factor (TCF/LEF) family transcription factors to stimulate transcription of target genes such as c-Myc and Cyclin D1, which promote cell proliferation and block apoptosis, causing tumor initiation and development [27, 28]. In HCC, mutations or abnormal activation of β-catenin are widespread, resulting in constitutive Wnt pathway activation that supports tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis. Moreover, many transcription factors not only act as downstream effectors of Wnt signaling but also regulate the pathway through feedback mechanisms [29, 30]. In liver cancer, for instance, FOXM1 promotes Wnt ligand expression, which in turn increases Wnt system activity, speeding up cell cycle progression and invasive characteristics [31, 32]. Wnt/β-catenin signaling controls SOX9, a liver cancer stem cell marker. SOX9 maintains the ability of cancer stem cells to self-renew and differentiate, which contributes to tumor recurrence and chemoresistance. Furthermore, Snail and Slug are transcription factors that work in tandem with the Wnt pathway to control EMT, which in turn increases the migratory and metastasis potential of HCC cells. Intricate positive and negative feedback loops are formed by these transcription factors and Wnt signaling molecules. These molecules generate multilayered regulatory networks that impact the tumor microenvironment and immune evasion in addition to modulating tumor cell activity. The interaction nodes between transcription factors and the Wnt pathway have recently gained attention as a potential target for precision therapy and new drug development in liver cancer due to the critical role they play in HCC development. But the possible mechanism involving transcription factors and the Wnt signaling pathway was still not well understood.

  This study aimed not only to identify key KIF family members as diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) but also to elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which they drive tumor progression. Through the integration of multiple machine learning algorithms and multi-cohort transcriptomic data, we systematically screened and validated nine KIFs with high diagnostic potential, with KIF20A emerging as the most clinically relevant candidate. Beyond expression-level associations, our study provides the first evidence that KIF20A promotes HCC progression via activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT). Moreover, we identify FOXK1 as a novel transcriptional activator of KIF20A, establishing the FOXK1–KIF20A–β-catenin axis as a previously unrecognized regulatory mechanism in HCC. These findings bridge the gap between biomarker discovery and mechanistic understanding, offering new insights into therapeutic targeting of aggressive HCC.


## Materials and methods

### Cell culture and transfection
  Human hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cell lines (Huh-7, SUN398, SNU-449, HepG2, MHCC-97 L) and normal hepatic epithelial cells (THLE-2) were obtained from the Shanghai Institute of Biological Sciences (Shanghai, China). Cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO₂. For gene knockdown, lentiviral vectors expressing shRNAs targeting FOXK1 or KIF20A (or a non-targeting control) were constructed and packaged in HEK293T cells using standard protocols. HCC cells were infected with lentiviruses in the presence of 8 µg/mL polybrene, followed by puromycin selection (2 µg/mL) to establish stable knockdown lines. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed by RT-qPCR and Western blot. For gene overexpression, plasmids encoding FOXK1 or KIF20A (pcDNA3.1 backbone) and corresponding empty vectors were transfected into HCC cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection for downstream assays.


### Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)
  Total RNA was isolated from cultured cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA purity and concentration were assessed with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, and samples with an A260/A280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0 were selected for subsequent analysis. Reverse transcription was performed using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara, Japan) with 1 µg of total RNA in a 20 µL reaction system. Quantitative PCR was carried out using SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, USA) on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad). Each reaction was run in triplicate in a 10 µL volume containing cDNA template, SYBR Green mix, and gene-specific primers. The amplification protocol consisted of initial denaturation followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C and annealing/extension at 60 °C. Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH serving as the internal reference gene. All primer sequences used were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) and are as follows: FOXK1 forward: 5′-TCCAGGAGCCGCACTTCTA-3′, reverse: 5′-CTCCGGGATGTGGATCTTCA-3′; KIF20A forward: 5′-TGCTGTCCGATGACGATGTC-3′, reverse: 5′-AGGTTCTTGCGTACCACAGAC-3′; GAPDH forward: 5′-GGAGCGAGATCCCTCCAAAAT-3′, reverse: 5′-GGCTGTTGTCATACTTCTCATGG-3′.


### Cell viability assay
  Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay (Dojindo Laboratories, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 4 × 10³ cells per well in 100 µL complete medium and allowed to adhere overnight. At 24, 48, and 72 h post-treatment or transfection, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated at 37 ℃ for 2 h in the dark. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Each group included at least three technical replicates, and all experiments were independently repeated three times. Background absorbance from blank wells (medium + CCK-8, without cells) was subtracted. Cell viability was expressed relative to the control group, which was normalized to 100%.


### Colony formation assay
  For colony formation analysis, 500 cells per well were seeded into six-well plates in complete medium after trypsinization. Cells were cultured for 10–14 days under standard conditions (37 ℃, 5% CO₂), with medium gently refreshed every 3–4 days. When visible colonies (≥ 50 cells/colony) formed, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature, followed by staining with 0.1% crystal violet (Solarbio, China) for 10 min. Excess dye was removed by rinsing with distilled water, and plates were air-dried. Colonies were imaged and counted manually under a light microscope (Olympus, Japan).


### Cell invasion and migration assays
  Cell migration and invasion were assessed using Transwell chambers with 8-µm pore polycarbonate membranes (Corning, USA). For invasion assays, the upper surface of the membrane was precoated with 60 µL of Matrigel (BD Biosciences, USA) diluted 1:8 in serum-free DMEM and incubated at 37 ℃ for 2 h to allow gel solidification. Migration assays were performed without Matrigel. Transfected or treated cells were trypsinized, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in serum-free DMEM. A total of 1× 10⁵ cells in 200 µL of serum-free DMEM containing 5% FBS were added to the upper chamber, while 600 µL of DMEM supplemented with 20% FBS was added to the lower chamber as a chemoattractant. After incubation for 24 h at 37 ℃ with 5% CO₂, non-migrated or non-invaded cells on the upper surface were removed with a cotton swab. Cells that had traversed to the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Solarbio, China) for 10 min, rinsed with distilled water, and air-dried. Migrated or invaded cells were counted in five randomly selected fields per insert under a light microscope (Olympus, Japan).


### Western blot
  Total protein was extracted from cells using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Roche, Switzerland). Lysates were incubated on ice for 30 min and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 ℃ to collect the supernatant. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit (Beyotime, China). Equal amounts of protein (30–50 µg) were mixed with 5× SDS loading buffer, boiled at 100 ℃ for 5 min, and separated by SDS-PAGE (10–12% gels). Proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA) using a wet transfer system at 100 V for 90 min. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with the following primary antibodies: FOXK1, KIF20A, CDK4, CDK6, Cyclin D1, β-catenin, E-cadherin, N-cadherin, Slug, Snail, Twist1, and β-actin (loading control). After three washes in TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000; Proteintech, China) for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were detected using ECL reagents (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and visualized with a chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon 5200, China). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, USA). Each experiment was independently repeated three times.


### Xenograft model
  After receiving approval from Huizhou Hospital’s Laboratory Animal Welfare Ethics Committee, all research involving animals were carried out in compliance with institutional norms. From Shanghai JieSiJie Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), we procured 24 female BALB/c nude mice of specified pathogen-free (SPF) grade. The mice ranged in age from four to six weeks and weighed between eighteen and twenty-two grams. The mice were kept in a controlled environment with a 12-hour light/dark cycle, access to standard chow and water, and a temperature range of 22 ± 2 °C. The humidity level was 50–60%. During the logarithmic growth phase, SUN398 cells that were stably transduced with sh-KIF20A-1, sh-KIF20A-2, or sh-NC were collected. After that, they were washed twice with PBS and resuspended at a concentration of 1 × 10⁷ cells/mL to create the subcutaneous xenograft tumor model. A 100 µL PBS solution containing 1 × 10⁶ cells was subcutaneously injected into the right flank of every mouse. Volume of the tumor was determined by measuring its length and width squared and then dividing the result by two, and this was done every four days using calipers. The mice were carefully observed for any indications of discomfort or unusual behavior during the entire trial. Following the 28-day period, the mice were put to sleep using isoflurane, and the tumors were meticulously removed, photographed, and weighed. In order to conduct histological investigation, tumor tissues were either fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde or immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. This was done to facilitate the extraction of proteins and RNA. The experiment was conducted twice separately to guarantee the results were consistent and reliable, and each group of mice comprised of eight (n = 8).


### Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP)
  The EZ-ChIPTM kit (Millipore, marketed by Kaihong Biotech, Qingdao, Shandong, China) was used to conduct ChIP tests in accordance with the instructions provided by the manufacturer. To summarize, 6 cm culture dishes were used to sow SUN398 and Huh-7 cells, and they were allowed to develop to a confluence of about 80–90%. The cells were cultured at 27 °C for 15 min while gently rocking them to cross-link them. A final concentration of 1% formaldehyde was added directly to the growth medium. After 5 min of adding 125 mM glycine, the cross-linking process was stopped. The next step was to harvest the cells by scraping them after two washes with cold PBS that contained protease inhibitors. Agarose gel electrophoresis confirmed the presence of DNA fragments ranging from 200 to 400 bp, which were produced by lysing cell pellets using the SDS lysis solution supplied in the kit and then shearing chromatin by sonication on ice with a Bioruptor sonicator (Diagenode, Belgium). The chromatin solution was given an overnight incubation at 4 °C with 5 µg of anti-FOXK1 antibody (Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) after being pre-cleared with protein G agarose beads. As a negative control, normal rabbit IgG (Abcam) was utilized. Protein G agarose beads were used to capture immunocomplexes, which were then washed sequentially with buffers containing low salt, high salt, LiCl, and TE. After inverting the DNA-protein cross-links with an overnight heating at 65 °C, the samples were processed with RNase A to remove RNA and proteinase K to remove proteins. As a last step, DNA was spun-filtered to remove contaminants before being analyzed quantitatively by real-time PCR with gene-specific primers to determine the extent to which FOXK1 bound to distinct promoter regions.


### Luciferase activity detection
  We cloned particular promoter regions encoding the probable FOXK1 binding sites of the KIF20A gene into the pGL3-basic luciferase reporter vector (Promega, USA) in order to study the transcriptional regulatory mechanisms underpinning KIF20A expression. The promoter segments were built with both the wild-type (WT) and mutant (MUT) variants in mind. The mutant variant was created via site-directed mutagenesis at predicted FOXK1 binding motifs, which were found in the JASPAR database. Two new structures, pGL3-KIF20A-WT and pGL3-KIF20A-MUT, were born from this process. A density of 4,000 cells per well of SUN398 and Huh-7 cells was grown overnight to guarantee firm adhesion for the luciferase activity experiment. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, cells were co-transfected with 100 ng of WT or MUT luciferase reporter plasmids and 10 ng of pRL-TK Renilla luciferase plasmid (internal control, Promega) using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA). Following 48 h of incubation, the cells were broken down using the passive lysis buffer that came with the kit. The luciferase activity was then quantified using a microplate luminometer that belonged to Kejia, located in Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China, and the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System, manufactured by Promega in the USA. As a measure of transfection effectiveness, the firefly luciferase activity was standardized to that of the Renilla luciferase. In each of the three separate studies, the transfections were carried out three times. The functional impact of FOXK1 binding on KIF20A promoter activity was assessed by comparing the WT and MUT groups and expressing the data as relative luciferase activity (Firefly/Renilla).


### Data collection
  In order to analyze 424 patients diagnosed with HCC, we used the TCGA-LIHC cohort of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) to retrieve RNA sequencing data in Fragments Per Kilobase per Million mapped reads (FPKM) format, as well as associated clinicopathological and survival information. Standard normalizing procedures were used to convert the raw FPKM expression data to Transcripts Per Million (TPM) values. The expression matrix was then log₂(TPM + 1) converted for further analysis, which improved the comparability of the samples. We took mRNA expression profiles and clinical annotations from various Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets for the purpose of independent validation and cross-platform comparison. These datasets include GSE121248, which contains 70 HCC tissues and 37 normal liver tissues, GSE14520, which contains 242 HCC tissues and 239 normal samples, GSE39791, which contains 71 HCC tissues and 71 matched normal tissues, GSE54236, which contains 79 HCC tissues and 80 normal tissues, GSE112790, which contains 179 HCC tissues and 15 adjacent normal tissues, GSE113996, which contains 20 HCC tissues and 20 normal tissues, and GSE144269, which contains 70 HCC tissues and 70 normal tissues. The GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) was accessed to obtain all datasets, which were subsequently analyzed using R software (version 4.2.2). The “limma” and “affy” packages were used to normalize and correct the background of the microarray data, and the “sva” package was used to adjust batch effects as needed. From the GSE125449 and GSE138709 datasets, we extracted raw gene-barcode matrices for five HCC patients to use in their single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) research. Filtering out cells of poor quality, normalizing, dimensionality reducing, grouping, and annotating cell types according to canonical marker genes were all steps in the preprocessing of scRNA-seq data using the Seurat program (version 4.3.0). Reproducible and standardized bioinformatic processes were followed in all analyses.


### Single-cell RNA-seq data processing
  Raw count matrices (matrix.mtx, features.tsv, barcodes.tsv) were downloaded from the GEO database and imported into Python as an AnnData object using the Scanpy package (v1.9.1). Cells expressing fewer than 200 genes or genes expressed in fewer than 3 cells were excluded. To reduce technical and biological artifacts, cells with > 2,500 detected genes or > 5% mitochondrial gene content were also removed, as these likely represent doublets or dying cells. Quality control metrics, including gene counts, UMI counts, and mitochondrial content, were visualized using violin and scatter plots. Filtered data were normalized to 10,000 counts per cell using scanpy.pp.normalize_total, followed by log-transformation with scanpy.pp.log1p. Highly variable genes (HVGs) were identified via the “Seurat v3” method (implemented in scanpy.pp.highly_variable_genes) and used for downstream analysis. Data were scaled, and dimensionality reduction was performed using principal component analysis (PCA). When applicable, batch effects were corrected using Harmony or BBKNN (scanpy.external.pp.bbknn). The normalized and batch-corrected expression matrix was exported into R (v4.3.0) and further analyzed using the Seurat and MAESTRO pipelines. Clustering was performed using a shared nearest neighbor graph, and visualized via t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP). Cluster-specific marker genes were identified using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test via Seurat’s FindAllMarkers function. Additional analyses, including pseudotime trajectory inference and functional enrichment, were conducted using default or optimized parameters according to best practices in single-cell RNA-seq workflows.


### Differential expression analysis
  In the GSE121248 dataset, which contains gene expression profiles of 70 HCC tissues and 37 normal liver tissues, we initially extracted the expression matrix of 45 Kinesin Family Members (KIFs). Using the platform-specific annotation file (GPL570), the microarray data’s probe IDs were mapped to gene symbols. When numerous probes corresponded to the same gene, the average expression value was computed. In order to normalize the expression data before statistical analysis, it was log₂transformed. Under the assumption of equal variances, we utilized the unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test implemented in R (version 4.2.2) to identify KIFs that were differentially expressed between normal tissues and HCC. We used the Benjamini-Hochberg test to control the false discovery rate (FDR) and modify the p-values for multiple testing. KIF genes were deemed statistically significant if their adjusted p-value was less than 0.05. To illustrate the expression patterns and significance levels of differentially expressed KIFs, the findings were displayed using heatmaps and volcano plots, respectively, created with the “pheatmap” and “ggplot2” R tools.


### Identification of optimal diagnostic gene biomarkers for HCC
  First, we used the glmnet R package (version 4.1) and the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression approach to find reliable diagnostic biomarkers for HCC. We utilized the GSE121248 dataset to find differentially expressed KIFs (DE-KIFs) between normal liver tissues and HCC as input features. To find the best penalty parameter (λ) for minimizing classification error, ten-fold cross-validation was employed, and genes with non-zero coefficients were chosen as the most informative. At the same time, a model called SVM-RFE was set up using the R packages caret and e1071. This model used recursive elimination to rank features according to how important they were to the classifier. Using the lowest average misclassification rate during 10-fold cross-validation, the best selection of genes was chosen. In order to arrive at a final collection of gene biomarkers that represent the most stable and consistent diagnostic features, candidate biomarkers were intersected from both the LASSO and SVM-RFE models. The diagnostic performance of each gene and the combined signature was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. The pROC program in R was used to measure accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, area under the curve (AUC), and accuracy itself. In addition, the GSE121248 dataset was used to develop a logistic regression model that included the seven overlapping marker genes. The model was trained using the glm function with the family parameter set to binomial in order to predict if a sample was HCC or normal. We used the model’s predicted probabilities to create ROC curves, and we measured the model’s classification performance using AUC and other pertinent metrics.


### Pan-Cancer analysis of KIFs
  Pan-cancer RNA-seq data (log₂(TPM + 1)) and corresponding clinical annotations from 33 TCGA cancer types were downloaded from the UCSC Xena platform. Expression data for 45 Kinesin Family genes (KIFs) were extracted along with relevant metadata (age, sex, tumor stage, treatment, OS, and DSS). Differential expression between tumor and normal tissues was assessed using Wilcoxon tests, and p-values were adjusted with the Benjamini–Hochberg method (FDR < 0.05). Cox regression models were used to evaluate the prognostic value of each KIF gene, with OS and DSS as endpoints, adjusted for key clinical factors. Results were visualized as heatmaps, boxplots, and forest plots using R (v4.2.2).


### Prognostic analysis of KIFs
  In order to determine the Kinesin Family Member (KIF) genes’ predictive importance in patients with HCC, we first used the median expression value to divide the TCGA-LIHC cohort into “high-expression” and “low-expression” categories for each KIF gene. To show how the two groups differed in OS, Kaplan-Meier survival curves were made using the “survival” and “survminer” R programs. We used the log-rank test to statistically evaluate survival differences, and we regarded a p-value less than 0.05 to be significant. Furthermore, the relative risk of death associated with increased KIF expression was quantified using hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using Cox proportional hazards models. The use of Schoenfeld residuals allowed for the verification of the proportional hazards assumption. Receiving operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted using the “timeROC” and “pROC” R packages to further evaluate the predictive accuracy of KIFs in HCC prediction. Area under the ROC curve (AUC) values closer to 1.0 indicate stronger discriminative power, which was used to quantify the model’s performance in predicting six-year OS. In addition, we adjusted for clinical variables such as age, gender, tumor stage (TNM), histological grade, and treatment status in both univariate and multivariate Cox regression models to determine the independent predictive significance of each KIF gene. To be considered independent prognostic biomarkers, genes have to maintain their statistical significance in the multivariate model. The analyses were carried out using R version 4.2.2. For the purpose of clarity, survival plots were labeled with the number at risk and p-values.


### Statistical analysis
  All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 19.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), and R software version 4.2.2. When comparing two groups, we utilized independent-samples t-tests. When comparing several groups, we used one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test, as long as the conditions of normality and homogeneity of variances were met. Unless otherwise stated, the data is shown as the mean plus or minus the standard deviation (SD). For data distribution normality, we utilized the Shapiro-Wilk test, and for variance homogeneity, we used Levene’s test. Appropriate non-parametric tests, such the Kruskal-Wallis test or the Mann-Whitney U test, were employed for variables that did not follow a normal distribution or had unequal variances. Depending on the distribution of the data, Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients were used for correlation studies. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significance levels were annotated as follows: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, and ∗∗∗p < 0.001. All graphs and statistical annotations were generated using GraphPad Prism or R for consistency and visual clarity.


## Results

### Nine DE-KIFs were identified as diagnostic genes for HCC
  Out of the 45 KIFs that were analyzed, 27 genes were discovered to have differential expression in the GSE121248 dataset, with 17 genes showing an upregulation and 10 genes showing a downregulation (|log₂FC| > 1, adjusted p < 0.05). Using the “pheatmap” R package, we created a clustering heatmap to show how these differentially expressed KIFs (DE-KIFs) were expressed in all 107 samples (70 HCC and 37 normal tissues). This heatmap clearly showed that the tumor and normal groups had different expression profiles (Fig. 1A). Coordinated expression patterns among certain gene subsets were revealed by pairwise correlation analysis among 27 typical DE-KIFs using the Pearson correlation coefficient, which allowed for a deeper exploration of the functional linkages (Fig. 1B). A pair of powerful machine learning methods, LASSO and SVM-RFE, were used to evaluate the diagnostic value of these DE-KIFs. The LASSO logistic regression model, which was refined using 10-fold cross-validation and was implemented via the “glmnet” package, used the minimal lambda value to pick 17 feature genes linked with HCC (Fig. 1C). Parallel to this, the “e1071” and “caret” R packages were used to implement the SVM-RFE method, which progressively removed characteristics that were not very meaningful. The result was an ideal subset of eleven candidate genes, as shown in Fig. 1D. There were nine diagnostic marker genes that were shown to overlap when the LASSO and SVM-RFE results were combined: KIF3A, KIF3B, KIF5B, KIF11, KIF13A, KIF13B, KIF20A, KIFC3, and KLC2 (Fig. 1E). Figure 1F showed the results of a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve with an AUC of 0.992, indicating that the logistic regression model built using the “glm” function in R, which was based on these genes, performed exceptionally well in distinguishing HCC from normal tissues. Figure 1G showed the results of plotting ROC curves for six separate marker genes to evaluate their diagnostic capability. All six genes had strong discriminatory capacity, with an AUC > 0.7. Figure 1H showed a bar plot that helps to visualize the expression levels of nine representative genes in the GSE121248 dataset. These genes were shown to demonstrate substantial differential expression between normal samples and HCC. By applying the Boruta feature selection algorithm to the TCGA-LIHC dataset, 27 important KIF genes were shown to be strongly linked with HCC, which helped to confirm and refine these findings (Fig. 1I). Figure 1J showed that six important diagnostic genes—KIF3A, KIF5B, KIF11, KIF13B, KIF20A, and KLC2—were identified when Boruta, LASSO, and SVM-RFE results intersected. Figure 1K showed that six genes were further validated in the TCGA cohort. Out of them, five showed significantly changed expression between HCC and normal liver tissues: KIF3A, KIF5B, KIF11, KIF20A, and KLC2. Lastly, the diagnostic capability of these genes was confirmed by ROC curve analysis in the TCGA dataset. All of the identified genes showed high AUC values (Supplementary Figure S1), indicating that they could be reliable biomarkers for HCC identification.

### The prognostic value of diagnostic KIFs in HCC
  Using survival data from the TCGA-LIHC dataset, we further assessed the predictive efficacy of the four critical diagnostic genes (KIF3A, KIF11, KIF20A, and KLC2). Overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free interval (PFI) in HCC patients were found to be substantially correlated with elevated expression levels of these genes, specifically KIF3A, KIF11, KIF20A, and KLC2 (log-rank p < 0.05), as shown in Fig. 2A and C. We focused on the 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates and performed time-dependent ROC (Time-ROC) analysis using the “timeROC” package in R to further evaluate their prognostic performance. With area under the curve (AUC) values greater than 0.7, KIF20A proved to be the most reliable prognostic gene out of the six that had been previously found (Fig. 2D). Four external datasets—GSE54236, GSE116174, ICGC-LIRI-JP, and GSE14520—were investigated to validate these findings in independent cohorts with respect to KIF20A expression and survival outcomes. Its repeatability and clinical relevance were confirmed by the fact that elevated KIF20A expression was substantially linked to poor prognosis in each cohort (Fig. 2E and F). In addition, KIF20A expression was found to be an independent predictive factor for OS (Fig. 2G), DSS (Fig. 2H), and PFI (Fig. 2I) in the TCGA cohort. This conclusion was reached after multivariate Cox regression analysis that included clinical factors such as age, sex, tumor stage, and grade. We conducted a meta-analysis utilizing hazard ratios from several cohorts to evaluate the overall effect size and consistency across studies. The results showed that the pooled HR was greater than 1, confirming KIF20A as an important adverse prognostic biomarker in HCC (Fig. 2J). Furthermore, Fig. 2K showed that the independent prognostic effect of KIF20A in predicting OS among HCC patients was further supported by Cox regression analysis in GSE116174 and GSE14520. The results of these validations across multiple cohorts provide strong evidence that KIF20A is a reliable and useful predictive gene for hepatocellular cancer.

  Next, we used TCGA clinical annotation data to investigate if there was a correlation between the expression levels of four diagnostic genes—KIF3A, KIF11, KIF20A, and KLC2—and clinical progression markers in patients with HCC. The possible involvement of KIF20A and KIF11 in enhancing the aggressiveness and malignant transformation of HCC was shown by the substantial correlation between greater expression of these genes and advanced clinical stage (Stage III-IV) and higher pathological grade (Grade 3–4) (Fig. 3A and B, Wilcoxon test, p < 0.05). ### KIF20A promotes the proliferation HCC cells in both in intro and in vivo
  Using the GSE125449 and GSE138709 datasets, we performed a single-cell transcriptome study to gain a better understanding of KIF20A’s function and expression selectivity in HCC. By clustering and annotating cells into separate cell populations using the Seurat program, we found that KIF20A was mostly expressed in malignant epithelial cells, indicating that it was upregulated specifically in tumors at the single-cell level (Fig. 4A and B). In order to confirm these results, we looked at large amounts of transcriptomic data from various separate GEO cohorts, such as GSE14520, GSE39791, GSE54236, GSE112790, GSE113996, and GSE144269. In every dataset, we consistently found that HCC tissues had significantly higher levels of KIF20A expression than normal liver tissues (Fig. 4C, all p < 0.01). Then, we looked at KIF20A expression in a group of HCC cell lines (HepG2, SNU-449, SUN398, Huh-7, and MHCC-97 L). qRT-PCR and Western blot analysis proved that KIF20A was significantly overexpressed in SUN398 and Huh-7 cells, in particular, when it came to mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 4D). Downstream functional studies were conducted on these two lines. Figure 4E showed the results of a study that used lentiviral delivery of sh-KIF20A constructs to perform shRNA-mediated knockdown of KIF20A in SUN398 and Huh-7 cells. The goal of the study was to evaluate the biological role of KIF20A in HCC cell growth. Silencing KIF20A dramatically reduced cell viability and colony-forming capacity, suggesting its crucial function in tumor cell proliferation, according to functional assays such as Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) and clonogenic survival assays (Fig. 4F and G, p < 0.01). Figure 4H showed that additional examination of TCGA data showed that KIF20A expression was positively correlated with important cell cycle regulators such as CDK4, CDK6, and a proliferation gene signature. This suggests that KIF20A plays a role in the progression of the cell cycle. Western blotting experimentally confirmed this by showing that CDK4, CDK6, and Cyclin D1 expression was lowered upon KIF20A knockdown (Fig. 4I). Figure 4J showed the results of gain-of-function experiments in which SUN398 and Huh-7 cells were transfected with the pcDNA3.1 + KIF20A plasmid and KIF20A expression was successfully raised. Figure 4K and L showed that overexpressing KIF20A confirmed its oncogenic function by dramatically increasing cell proliferation and clonogenic capacity in both cell lines. Figure 4M showed that KIF20A overexpression increases the protein levels of CDK4, CDK6, and Cyclin D1, as determined by Western blotting. Last but not least, in vivo xenograft studies were performed with nude mice that had SUN398 cells subcutaneously implanted with either a control or a stable KIF20A knockdown. Figure 4N and O showed that compared to the control group, tumor growth was dramatically inhibited by silencing KIF20A, with smaller tumor volumes and lower final weights, as observed every 4 days (p < 0.01). Taken together, these results showed that KIF20A is a powerful oncogene in HCC, regulating the CDK4/CDK6/Cyclin D1 axis to promote cell proliferation.

### KIF20A distinctly promotes the metastasis of HCC cells via Wnt/β-catenin/EMT signaling
  We then moved on to examine how KIF20A contributes to the metastasis potential of HCC cells. The effects of KIF20A overexpression and knockdown on cell migration and invasion were assessed using functional assays. Silencing KIF20A greatly hindered the migratory and invasive capabilities of SUN398 and Huh-7 cells in Transwell tests, regardless of whether Matrigel coating was used or not. This was demonstrated by the decreased number of cells passing through the membrane (Fig. 5A, p < 0.01). Moreover, we found that overexpression of KIF20A distinctly promoted the migration and invasion of HCC cells (Fig. 5B). Our investigation into whether KIF20A affects the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway and the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) was further prompted by the known involvement of these pathways in the progression and metastasis of HCC. Correlation analysis based on the TCGA-LIHC dataset revealed that KIF20A expression was significantly and positively associated with several Wnt/β‐catenin/EMT-related genes, including CTNNB1 (R = 0.412, P < 0.001), TWIST1 (R = 0.278, P < 0.001), and CDH2 (R = 0.312, P < 0.001). In contrast, no significant correlation was observed between KIF20A and SNAI1, SNAI2, or TWIST2. Interestingly, CDH1, an epithelial marker, also showed a weak but significant positive correlation with KIF20A (R = 0.170, P < 0.001), which may reflect tumor sample heterogeneity and partial EMT states within HCC. When EMT marker genes were combined into a composite score, KIF20A expression remained significantly correlated with overall EMT activation (R = 0.180, P = 5.17 × 10⁻⁴), suggesting that high KIF20A expression is associated with a mesenchymal-like transcriptional profile in HCC(Fig. 5C). Using a TCF/LEF1-responsive promoter design, we conducted luciferase reporter assays to functionally evaluate Wnt pathway activity. There were direct regulatory effects on the canonical Wnt pathway, as shown in Fig. 5D and E, when KIF20A overexpression considerably increased transcriptional activity and KIF20A knockdown considerably decreased TCF/LEF1-driven luciferase activity. We utilized Western blotting to investigate the expression of important EMT indicators and Wnt-related proteins, such as N-cadherin, E-cadherin, Slug, Snail, Twist1, Twist2, and β-catenin, in order to delve deeper into the molecular mechanism. Western blot analysis revealed that silencing KIF20A led to a clear mesenchymal-to-epithelial phenotypic shift in both SUN398 and Huh-7 cells (Fig. 5F). Knockdown of KIF20A resulted in a marked decrease in the expression of mesenchymal markers, including N-cadherin, Slug, Snail, Twist1, Twist2, as well as the Wnt effector β-catenin, while the epithelial marker E-cadherin was strongly upregulated. Conversely, ectopic overexpression of KIF20A produced the opposite effect (Fig. 5G), characterized by elevated β-catenin and mesenchymal marker expression together with a reduction in E-cadherin levels. These findings indicate that KIF20A promotes EMT progression in HCC cells, at least in part through activation of β-catenin–associated signaling. To further evaluate the role of KIF20A in HCC metastasis in vivo, a tail-vein injection lung colonization model was established using SUN398 cells with stable KIF20A knockdown. As shown in Fig. 5H, mice in the sh-NC group developed numerous visible metastatic nodules on the lung surface, whereas both sh-KIF20A-1 and sh-KIF20A-2 groups exhibited a markedly reduced metastatic burden. Consistently, H&E staining (Fig. 5I) confirmed extensive tumor colonization in the lungs of control mice, while only limited and smaller metastatic foci were observed in the KIF20A-silenced groups. Quantification of metastatic nodules (Fig. 5J) demonstrated a significant decrease in the number of lung metastases upon KIF20A knockdown (p < 0.01), indicating that suppression of KIF20A markedly impairs the metastatic capability of HCC cells in vivo.

### Rescue experiments confirm KIF20A regulates HCC progression via the Wnt/β-Catenin pathway
  Lastly, we conducted rescue experiments with pharmacological modulators to provide further evidence that the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is functionally involved in mediating the oncogenic effects caused by KIF20A. The KIF20A knockdown cells were treated with SKL2001, a small-molecule agonist that stabilizes β-catenin and activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. Western blot analysis showed that transfection with sh-KIF20A-1 markedly reduced KIF20A protein levels in both SUN398 and Huh-7 cells compared with the sh-NC control, confirming efficient knockdown. Treatment with SKL2001 did not interfere with the depletion efficiency of KIF20A(Fig. 6A). Figure 6B, p < 0.01, showed that SKL2001 restored SUN398 and Huh-7 cell growth to levels similar to control cells by reversing the inhibitory effect of KIF20A knockdown on Colony formation assay. In addition, Western blot examination showed that SKL2001 restored the levels of proteins involved in cell cycle (CDK4, CDK6, Cyclin D1) and Wnt signaling effectors (β-catenin, TCF7, LEF1) that had been downregulated after KIF20A silencing (Fig. 6C). Furthermore, the results of the Transwell migration and invasion experiments showed that the invasive and migratory properties of KIF20A-silenced Huh-7 and SUN398 cells were restored after SKL2001 treatment. This provides more evidence that the activation of Wnt/β-catenin compensates for the degradation of KIF20A (Fig. 6D). Further support for this came from Western blotting, which showed that N-cadherin, Slug, Snail, Twist1, and Twist2 were all upregulated after KIF20A knockdown (Fig. 6E), and that SKL2001 treatment restored their downregulated levels. We used LGK974, an inhibitor of porcupine Wnt ligand release, to test the converse hypothesis that KIF20A overexpression could be mitigated by blocking Wnt signaling. Western blot analysis confirmed successful overexpression of KIF20A in both SUN398 and Huh-7 cells compared with the pcDNA3.1 control. Treatment with the Wnt/β-catenin inhibitor LKG974 did not alter the expression level of exogenously expressed KIF20A, indicating that LKG974 acts downstream of KIF20A and does not affect its protein stability(Fig. 6F). Figure 6G showed that KIF20A-mediated growth promotion is dependent on Wnt signaling, and inhibiting LGK974 considerably reduced the higher proliferation caused by KIF20A overexpression in Colony formation assay. The effect of LGK974 on the elevation of CDK4, CDK6 and Cyclin D1 caused by KIF20A overexpression was validated by Western blotting (Fig. 6H). Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 6I from Transwell assays and Fig. 6J from Western blot analysis, LGK974 successfully restored E-cadherin expression and suppressed EMT-associated proteins upregulated by KIF20A. These findings suggest that LGK974 reverses the enhanced migration and invasion capacity in cells overexpressing KIF20A. The results of these gain- and loss-of-function rescue experiments strongly suggest that KIF20A activates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway and its downstream EMT machinery, which in turn promotes HCC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. Additionally, they suggest that therapeutically reversing KIF20A-driven oncogenic phenotypes can be achieved by modulating this pathway.

### FOXK1-Mediated upregulation of KIF20A expression in HCC
  In order to understand how KIF20A is controlled during transcription, we started by obtaining the 2,000 bp sequence of the human KIF20A gene’s upstream promoter from PubMed NCBI GenBank and the UCSC Genome Browser. We analyzed this sequence in light of the fact that KIF20A is transcribed in the antisense orientation. We aggregated predictions from four separate databases—HRTFDB, FIMO (JASPAR), TFLink, and TRRUST—to find possible transcription factors (TFs) that could bind and control the KIF20A promoter. After creating a Venn diagram to find candidates that overlapped, four common transcription factors (TFs) were identified: FOXK1, CTCF, SP2, and FOXA2 (Fig. 7A). Figure 7B and C showed that FOXK1, CTCF, and SP2 had high positive correlations with KIF20A expression, as shown by further expression correlation analysis using the TCGA-LIHC dataset (Spearman r > 0.6, p < 0.001). Supplementary Figure S3A showed the results of the Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, which imply that FOXK1 may have functional importance since it is the sole gene linked with a significantly lower overall survival rate in HCC patients. The four TFs shown strong diagnostic value (AUC > 0.85) for differentiating HCC, according to differential expression analysis utilizing TCGA data, which was demonstrated in Figure Supplementary Figure S3B and S3C. Tumor tissues compared to normal liver tissues showed considerable upregulation of all four TFs. The positive correlation between FOXK1 and KIF20A expression across independent cohorts was further confirmed by consistent findings in the ICGC-LIRI-JP dataset and various external GEO datasets (GSE114236, GSE104580, GSE109211, GSE112790, GSE113996, GSE116174, GSE14426) (Supplementary Figure S3D). Supplementary Figure S3E showed that KIF20A maintained strong prognostic accuracy for long-term survival prediction when tested using time-dependent ROC analysis. Several cancer types, such as ovarian cancer (OV), liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), and adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC), were discovered to have an association between FOXK1 expression and tumor progression and clinical outcomes in a pan-cancer context (Supplementary Figure S3F). Figure 7D and E showed the results of our query of the JASPAR database, which revealed a high-scoring FOXK1 binding motif (CTTGGAAACAACCA) on the sense strand of the KIF20A promoter, which allowed us to investigate direct transcriptional regulation. A high binding peak for FOXK1 at the KIF20A promoter was discovered by ChIP-seq analysis utilizing the Cistrome Data Browser (dataset GSM956690), lending credence to this hypothesis (Fig. 7F). Physical binding between FOXK1 and the KIF20A promoter region was confirmed by AlphaFold3-based protein-DNA docking using the human FOXK1 amino acid sequence from UniProt. This validates the direct contact physically. The docking model brought attention to certain contact sites between DNA nucleotides and amino acid residues. These locations are shown in Fig. 7G with the help of red circles and labeled insets that illustrate resolution at the residue level. We constructed site-directed mutations to disrupt the binding site and validate their function based on the FOXK1 motif that was predicted by JASPAR (Fig. 7H). In addition, The results of the Western blotting assay corroborated the higher expression of FOXK1 in HCC cell lines (SUN398, HepG2, MHCC-97 L, Huh-7, and Huh-7)(Fig. 7I). Moreover, we analyzed GEO datasets and found that FOXK1 expression was distinctly increased in HCC specimens(Fig. 7J). Then, ChIP assays using an anti-FOXK1 antibody demonstrated that FOXK1 directly binds to the promoter region of the target gene in both SUN398 and Huh-7 cells. As shown in 7 K, FOXK1 enrichment at the target promoter was markedly higher in FOXK1-overexpressing cells compared with the control group, whereas FOXK1 silencing significantly reduced its occupancy. IgG served as a negative control and showed minimal background enrichment, while Input DNA was used for normalization. Quantitative analysis of ChIP-qPCR results is presented as folds of input, confirming that FOXK1 binding to the promoter is positively regulated by FOXK1 expression levels. In addition, the functional significance of the predicted FOXK1 binding site was confirmed by dual-luciferase reporter assays utilizing wild-type and mutant promoter constructs, which showed a significantly decreased luciferase activity in the mutant group (Fig. 7L, p < 0.01). Finally, we used RT-qPCR and Western blotting to confirm transcriptional regulation. Figure 7M and N showed that a significant reduction in KIF20A expression at both the mRNA and protein levels was observed when FOXK1 was knocked down. Our findings suggested that FOXK1 acted as a direct upstream activator of KIF20A transcription in HCC.

### Rescue assays reveal FOXK1–KIF20A–Wnt/β-Catenin axis in HCC progression
  In order to gain a better understanding of how FOXK1 and KIF20A work together, we conducted a number of rescue studies to check if KIF20A is involved in the oncogenic effects of FOXK1 in HCC cells. Western blot analysis showed that silencing FOXK1 significantly reduced KIF20A protein expression in both SUN398 and Huh-7 cells, whereas ectopic expression of KIF20A restored KIF20A levels despite FOXK1 knockdown(Fig. 8A). Figure 8B, p < 0.01, showed that clonogenic survival experiments restored colony-forming ability to near-control levels in both SUN398 and Huh-7 cells by overexpressing KIF20A, thus reversing the proliferation deficits caused by FOXK1 knockdown. In order to explore the wider scope of transcriptional regulation, we used TCGA-LIHC data to conduct a correlation analysis. The results showed that FOXK1 expression was positively correlated with CDK4 and CDK6, two important regulators of proliferation (Spearman r > 0.5, p < 0.001; Supplementary Figure S4). This suggests that FOXK1 might control cell cycle progression through downstream targets like KIF20A. After FOXK1 was silenced, the protein expression of CDK4, CDK6, and Cyclin D1 was decreased; however, KIF20A overexpression restored this expression, as seen in Western blot analysis (Fig. 8C). Regarding the metastatic potential, it was shown in Transwell migration and invasion assays that overexpressing KIF20A significantly restored the migratory and invasive capabilities that had been impaired by FOXK1 knockdown. This confirms that KIF20A promotes the invasiveness of HCC cells by acting downstream of FOXK1 (Fig. 8D). The fact that KIF20A was found to have a strong positive connection with many Wnt/β-catenin signaling components, such as CTNNB1, TCF7, and LEF1 (Fig. 8E), suggests that this route may facilitate the signaling between FOXK1 and KIF20A. In line with previous findings, Western blot analysis demonstrated that overexpressing KIF20A restored the expression of EMT markers including β-catenin, N-cadherin, Snail, Slug, and Twist1, as well as reverted the upregulation of E-cadherin caused by FOXK1 knockdown (Fig. 8F). This provides further evidence that KIF20A plays a crucial role in EMT mediated by FOXK1. On the other hand, we conducted loss-of-function rescue tests to determine if KIF20A is critical for FOXK1’s tumor-promoting activities. Western blot analysis showed that ectopic expression of FOXK1 markedly increased KIF20A protein levels in both SUN398 and Huh-7 cells, whereas silencing KIF20A using sh-KIF20A-1 did not affect FOXK1 expression. As expected, FOXK1 protein levels were efficiently elevated following FOXK1 overexpression, and KIF20A protein levels were significantly reduced upon sh-KIF20A-1 transfection(Fig. 8G). In all cell lines, functional experiments using clonogenic assays demonstrated that KIF20A knockdown greatly eliminated the proliferative benefit of FOXK1 overexpression (Fig. 8H, p < 0.01). Despite the upregulation of proliferation-associated genes (CDK4, CDK6, Cyclin D1) due to FOXK1 overexpression, Western blotting examination demonstrated that KIF20A silencing decreased their expression (Fig. 8I). Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 8J, KIF20A plays a crucial role in FOXK1-driven metastatic behavior, as KIF20A knockdown dramatically reversed FOXK1-induced migration and invasion in Transwell experiments. Lastly, Western blot analysis provided additional confirmation that knocking down KIF20A reversed the activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and EMT program, which was triggered by FOXK1. This was observed in Fig. 8K, where mesenchymal markers were downregulated and epithelial marker E-cadherin was upregulated. The results of these investigations show that FOXK1 enhances the progression of HCC by activating KIF20A through transcriptional activation. KIF20A then increases Wnt/β-catenin signaling and EMT, which drive the proliferation, migration, and invasion of tumor cells.

## Discussion
  HCC is one of the most common and deadly malignancies worldwide [33]. Current clinical treatments for HCC include surgical resection, local ablation techniques such as radiofrequency ablation and microwave ablation, liver transplantation, transarterial chemoembolization (TACE), and recently developed systemic therapies like molecular targeted drugs (e.g., sorafenib, lenvatinib) and immune checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies) [34, 35]. The overall prognosis is still poor; despite these treatments improving patient survival to a certain degree, the 5-year survival rate is usually around 20%. This is mainly because of the problems with early diagnosis, multifocal tumors, and high rates of recurrence after surgery [36, 37]. The alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) is the serum biomarker that is most frequently utilized for clinical diagnosis and prognosis. Although AFP is moderately sensitive, it is not very specific because increased levels can be seen in benign liver illnesses like hepatitis. Evidence suggests that the AFP-L3 isoform and PIVKA-II, commonly known as DCP, enhance diagnostic accuracy, particularly in the early diagnosis of HCC [38, 39]. However, these biomarkers still face significant limitations, including poor sensitivity in detecting small-sized tumors or AFP-negative HCC cases, as well as relatively high false-negative rates. Moreover, there remains a lack of clinically applicable molecular markers that can reliably capture tumor biology, predict patient prognosis, and guide treatment response. Future research should therefore focus on integrating multi-omics approaches—including gene expression profiling, circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), tumor-associated microRNAs, and proteomic signatures—to identify more sensitive and specific diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers for HCC [40]. Furthermore, by delving further into the molecular mechanisms that drive HCC development, namely changes in signaling pathways and the tumor microenvironment, we can improve patient survival and quality of life through early accurate diagnosis and the creation of tailored treatment plans.

  KIFs are a group of motor proteins that play crucial roles in intracellular transport, cell division, and maintenance of cellular architecture [10, 41]. New research indicates that HCC is one of many tumors where KIF dysregulation contributes to carcinogenesis, progression, and metastasis. Through the regulation of important signaling pathways like Wnt/β-catenin and EMT, the abnormal expression of some KIFs in HCC has been associated with increased cancer cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and resistance to apoptosis [42–44]. There has been encouraging progress in the identification of KIFs as possible diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets in liver cancer, thanks to their crucial functions in cancer cell biology and tumor growth [45–48]. This study addresses a gap in the literature by comprehensively screening and validating numerous kinesin family member (KIF) genes as diagnostic and prognostic indicators for HCC. At first, the GSE121248 dataset was used to identify 27 KIF genes with differential expression. Out of those, nine were cross-validated using two machine learning algorithms, LASSO and SVM-RFE, and the results demonstrated a very good diagnostic accuracy (AUC = 0.992). The TCGA database was used to confirm these genes, which showed that they are reliable and stable. Evidently, these KIF genes are involved in the formation of HCC and may be useful for clinical diagnostics. In addition, assessments of survival linked four genes, one of which was KIF20A, to patient outcomes including overall survival, disease-specific survival, and progression-free survival. Furthermore, KIF20A demonstrated its significance as a prognostic biomarker for HCC by demonstrating independent prognostic prediction ability, which was validated across many independent cohorts. Further evidence that KIF20A and KIF11 have a role in tumor malignancy progression is the strong correlation between their expression levels and the clinical stage and grade of HCC. In addition, pan-cancer research showed that these KIF genes were related with patient prognosis and showed substantial differential expression across a variety of tumor types, suggesting that they play important roles in tumor biology and have therapeutic promise. In conclusion, this study not only adds to our knowledge of the role of KIF genes in HCC diagnosis and prognosis, but it also lays the groundwork for future research into the underlying mechanisms and the creation of targeted therapies.

  KIF20A is a molecular motor protein belonging to the kinesin family, primarily involved in spindle formation and cell cycle regulation during mitosis [49]. Recent years have shown that KIF20A is related with tumor invasion, metastasis, and proliferation, and that it is abnormally overexpressed in many tumor types. It could be a therapeutic target for cancer treatment because its overexpression is associated with increased malignancy and worse clinical outcome [50–52]. As an example, CRPC progression was accelerated by activating the androgen receptor signaling pathway, which was caused by KIF20A, which was discovered to be abnormally overexpressed in this type of prostate cancer. Autocrine factors secreted by KIF20A activated AR and promoted the growth of castration-resistant cells. The development of CRPC was effectively inhibited by inhibiting KIF20A function, either by silencing the genes or by treating with the particular inhibitor paprotrain. For the first time, this research demonstrated that KIF20A drives CRPC and showed that it can be a therapeutic target [53]. Khongkow et al. found that FOXM1 controls KIF20A expression directly; both factors are important in the paclitaxel response of breast cancer cells. By facilitating aberrant mitotic spindle formation and mitotic catastrophe, cells exposed to paclitaxel-induced senescence were FOXM1 or KIF20A depleted. Additionally, the FOXM1-KIF20A axis was identified as a fundamental mechanism underpinning paclitaxel resistance and a potential target for enhancing chemotherapy outcomes; high levels of both proteins were associated with poor patient survival and paclitaxel resistance [54]. In their study, Chen et al. found that KIF20A accelerates the evolution of HCC by stabilizing c-Myc and inhibiting its degradation via FBXW7. This, in turn, leads to increased glycolysis and tumor growth. Most importantly, by decreasing c-Myc-driven mismatch repair (MMR) expression, KIF20A inhibition enhanced the efficacy of PD-1 immune checkpoint treatment. Researchers demonstrated that targeting the KIF20A-c-Myc axis in conjunction with anti-PD-1 treatment greatly improves immunotherapy results in HCC using liver-specific KIF20A deletion animals. Based on these results, KIF20A may be a biomarker or therapeutic target for immune checkpoint inhibitors [52]. But reports of KIF20A expression and function in HCC were few. In this study, we systematically characterized the oncogenic role of KIF20A in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and delineated the molecular mechanisms underlying its contribution to tumor progression and metastasis. Through integrated analysis of single-cell and bulk transcriptomic datasets, combined with functional assays, we demonstrated that KIF20A is highly expressed in malignant HCC cells and promotes tumor cell proliferation by upregulating key cell cycle regulators, including CDK4, CDK6, and Cyclin D1. Beyond its proliferative role, KIF20A was found to enhance HCC cell migration and invasion by activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and inducing epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), as evidenced by the upregulation of β-catenin and its downstream EMT transcription factors (Slug, Snail, Twist1/2). Pharmacological rescue experiments further confirmed the functional dependence of KIF20A-driven phenotypes on Wnt/β-catenin signaling. Importantly, in vivo experiments using a tail vein injection lung colonization model revealed that silencing KIF20A significantly suppressed HCC metastatic potential, as indicated by a marked reduction in both the number and size of metastatic lung nodules. These in vivo findings provide strong support for the critical role of KIF20A in promoting distant metastasis in addition to primary tumorigenesis. Taken together, our results reveal a dual oncogenic role of KIF20A in driving both tumor growth and metastasis through the Wnt/β-catenin/EMT axis. These findings not only deepen our mechanistic understanding of HCC progression but also highlight KIF20A as a promising therapeutic target and prognostic biomarker with potential utility in predicting treatment response and metastatic risk.

  TFs are key proteins that regulate gene expression by recognizing and binding to specific DNA sequences, thereby controlling the transcriptional activity of target genes. Tumor start and progression are largely regulated by transcription factors [23, 55]. Their ability to regulate gene expression has implications for tumor cell development and metastasis, as it can affect cell proliferation, apoptosis, differentiation, and migration [56, 57]. The abnormal activation of many transcription factors, including β-catenin, c-Myc, HIF-1α, and NF-κB, is believed to drive tumor growth, particularly in HCC [58, 59]. In order to aid in the growth, invasion, and metastasis of liver cancer, these transcription factors regulate genes relevant to tumors, which speeds up cell cycle progression, improves anti-apoptotic signaling, and triggers EMT. One of the several transcription factors in the FOX family, FOXK1 (Forkhead Box K1) controls cell proliferation, metabolism, and differentiation, among other things. The involvement of FOXK1 in tumor formation and progression has recently been demonstrated in research. Overexpression of the oncogene FOXK1 is common in a variety of malignancies, including HCC. Through controlling genes associated with cell cycle, EMT, and metabolic reprogramming, it enhances tumor cell motility, invasion, and proliferation [60–63]. Particularly in liver cancer, elevated FOXK1 expression is linked to a worse prognosis and more aggressive tumor behavior [64–66]. This is partially because it activates signaling pathways like Wnt/β-catenin and PI3K/AKT, which are responsible for tumor development and metastasis. As a result, FOXK1 has the makings of a biomarker and therapeutic target for HCC. In this study, we identified FOXK1 as a transcriptional activator of KIF20A, directly binding to its promoter to elevate KIF20A expression and promote proliferation, migration, and invasion of HCC cells. This FOXK1–KIF20A interaction was consistently validated through multi-cohort bioinformatics analysis and confirmed using clinical tissue samples. High FOXK1 expression was associated with worse patient outcomes, suggesting its potential as a prognostic biomarker. Functionally, FOXK1-driven KIF20A upregulation was shown to activate the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and induce epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), thereby facilitating tumor progression. Loss-of-function and rescue experiments further demonstrated that KIF20A is required for FOXK1-mediated oncogenic effects, establishing the FOXK1–KIF20A–Wnt/β-catenin axis as a critical driver of HCC pathobiology. These findings not only deepen our understanding of HCC molecular mechanisms but also highlight this regulatory axis as a promising therapeutic target.

  This study has several limitations. First, functional validation of the FOXK1–KIF20A axis was mainly performed in vitro using hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines, without in vivo confirmation or validation in clinical specimens, which may limit the translational relevance of the findings. Second, although our results indicate that FOXK1 promotes Wnt/β-catenin activation via KIF20A, the downstream molecular events, potential pathway crosstalk, and involvement of other regulatory factors remain to be fully elucidated. Third, while multiple public databases and multi-cohort clinical data were utilized for correlation and prognostic analysis, the absence of validation in large-scale, multicenter prospective cohorts may affect the generalizability of our conclusions, especially given patient heterogeneity within public datasets.


## Conclusion
  In this study, we identified nine differentially expressed kinesin family (DE-KIF) genes as potential diagnostic biomarkers for HCC through integrated machine learning analyses, including LASSO and SVM-RFE. Among these, six genes—KIF3A, KIF5B, KIF11, KIF13B, KIF20A, and KLC2—demonstrated robust diagnostic value in both GSE121248 and TCGA datasets. Prognostic analyses further highlighted KIF20A as an independent predictor of overall survival, disease-specific survival, and progression-free interval in HCC patients, with consistent validation across multiple cohorts. Pan-cancer analysis revealed that these diagnostic KIFs were differentially expressed and associated with clinical outcomes in various tumor types. Functional assays showed that KIF20A significantly promotes HCC cell proliferation and metastasis both in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, KIF20A enhances tumor progression by activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway and inducing EMT, effects that were reversed by pathway-specific activators and inhibitors in rescue experiments. Additionally, we identified FOXK1 as an upstream transcription factor that positively regulates KIF20A expression, correlating with poor prognosis in HCC. Collectively, these findings underscore the critical role of KIF20A and its regulatory network in HCC development, suggesting that KIF20A may serve as a promising biomarker and therapeutic target in hepatocellular carcinoma.
