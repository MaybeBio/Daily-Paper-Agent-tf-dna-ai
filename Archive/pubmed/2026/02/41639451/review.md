## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text including abstract, main text, methods, and supplementary information descriptions.
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific validity, technical soundness, novelty, and significance of the claims as presented in the provided material. No assessment of editorial fit or publication policy.
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors present PARM, a convolutional neural network trained on MPRA data from human promoters, which predicts promoter activity from DNA sequence alone. They claim PARM is lightweight, cell-type-specific, and can identify functional transcription factor binding sites, design synthetic promoters, and reveal regulatory grammar including positional preferences and motif–motif interactions.
- **Visible evidence base** Main text figures 1–5 (described), Extended Data figures 1–10 (described), Supplementary figures 1–4 (described), Supplementary tables 1, Supplementary data 1–3, methods section.
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Actual figure images, numerical values for all reported correlations, full hyperparameter search details, code availability, detailed statistical test outputs, and complete datasets are not provided in the submitted text. These are essential for full verification.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript presents a valuable and timely contribution to the field of regulatory genomics. The core idea of combining a focused MPRA library with a lightweight CNN is practical and addresses a real need for scalable, cell-type-specific promoter activity models. The validation across multiple cell lines and conditions is commendable. However, several technical aspects require clarification and additional analysis to fully support the claims, particularly regarding the interpretation of in silico mutagenesis, the generalizability of the model, and the statistical robustness of the reported findings.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in regulatory genomics, computational biology, and gene expression regulation would be interested. The work offers a cost-effective method for generating predictive models of promoter activity, which is relevant for understanding gene regulation, interpreting non-coding variants, and designing synthetic promoters for biotechnology applications. The findings on regulatory grammar, such as positional preferences of transcription factors, are of broad interest to molecular biologists.
- **Major strengths**
  - The experimental design is thoughtful, particularly the development of a promoter-focused MPRA library that reduces cost and cell requirements.
  - The model is computationally efficient, making it accessible to a wide range of laboratories.
  - The validation strategy is multi-faceted, including cross-cell-line predictions, comparison with existing models, and experimental confirmation of predicted regulatory elements.
  - The discovery of cell-type-specific and condition-specific regulatory patterns, along with the positional grammar of transcription factor motifs, provides novel biological insights.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **R1-M1** (Severity: Major, Blocking: Yes, Axis: Technical soundness) The claim that PARM identifies "functional" transcription factor binding sites is not fully supported. The in silico mutagenesis approach identifies positions that affect predicted activity, but this is a computational prediction. The experimental validation is limited to a small number of examples. The authors need to provide a more systematic experimental validation of the predicted regulatory sites, or temper the language to "predicted functional" sites.
  - **R1-M2** (Severity: Major, Blocking: Yes, Axis: Technical soundness) The comparison with Enformer and Borgoi is incomplete. The authors state that PARM performs similarly but is more conservative. However, the metrics used for comparison are not fully described, and the analysis is limited to a small set of promoters. A more comprehensive benchmark on a larger, diverse set of promoters with clear statistical testing is needed to support the claim of comparable performance.
  - **R1-M3** (Severity: Major, Blocking: Yes, Axis: Scientific importance) The biological novelty of the "regulatory grammar" findings is not fully established. While the positional preferences of some TFs are interesting, the manuscript does not deeply explore the mechanistic basis of these preferences or their implications for gene regulation. The authors should either provide more mechanistic insights or frame these findings as descriptive observations.
  - **R1-M4** (Severity: Major, Blocking: Yes, Axis: Technical soundness) The robustness of the model to different sequence contexts and its generalizability to promoters not represented in the training set are not adequately addressed. The authors should perform additional validation on held-out chromosomes or on promoters from different genomic contexts to ensure the model is not overfitting.
- **Minor Comments**
  - **R1-m1** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Clarity) The description of the "regulatory grammar" is somewhat vague. The authors should define this term more precisely and provide a clear framework for how the model's predictions are interpreted in this context.
  - **R1-m2** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Reproducibility) The methods section lacks details on the exact hyperparameter search space and the criteria for selecting the final model. This information is crucial for reproducibility.
  - **R1-m3** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Readability) The main text is dense and could benefit from a more accessible explanation of the deep learning architecture and the in silico mutagenesis approach for a non-specialist audience.
  - **R1-m4** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Statistical rigor) The authors should report confidence intervals for the reported correlation coefficients and perform statistical tests to compare the performance of PARM with other models, rather than just reporting point estimates.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established**
  - R1-M1, R1-M2, R1-M3, R1-M4.
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria**
  - **Originality:** The work is original in its specific combination of a focused MPRA library with a lightweight CNN for promoter analysis. The approach is not entirely novel, as similar strategies have been used for enhancers, but its application to a comprehensive set of human promoters is a contribution.
  - **Scientific importance:** The findings are of moderate to high importance. The model itself is a useful resource, and the identified regulatory grammar, if validated, could have implications for understanding gene regulation. However, the lack of deep mechanistic insight limits the immediate impact.
  - **Interdisciplinary readership:** The work is primarily of interest to computational biologists and molecular biologists working on gene regulation. The methods and findings are unlikely to attract a broad readership beyond these fields.
  - **Technical soundness:** The technical approach is generally sound, but the major concerns outlined above need to be addressed. The lack of comprehensive benchmarking and systematic experimental validation weakens the current claims.
  - **Readability for nonspecialists:** The manuscript is not easily accessible to nonspecialists. The abstract and introduction are clear, but the main text and methods are highly technical and would be challenging for a reader without a background in deep learning and genomics.
- **Recommendation posture** Currently not established from the provided evidence. The manuscript has potential, but the major concerns regarding the validation of functional predictions, the completeness of benchmarking, and the depth of biological insight must be resolved before the claims are fully supported.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that PARM identifies "functional" TF binding sites is not fully supported by the evidence provided.
- The claim that PARM performs "similarly" to Enformer and Borgoi is not rigorously demonstrated.
- The claim that the model can "design purely synthetic strong promoters" is supported, but the practical utility and generalizability of this design process are not fully explored.
- The biological significance of the "regulatory grammar" findings is not fully established.
