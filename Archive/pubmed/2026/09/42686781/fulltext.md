# Truncated Gpr114-based ultrasound-hypersensitive gene circuit for controlled expression of therapeutics


## Abstract
  Sonogenetic holds great potential for modulating cellular functions and behaviors. However, developing hypersensitive ultrasound (US)-responsive receptors remains a substantial challenge. Here, we combine AlphaFold3 prediction with molecular dynamics simulations to identify a hypersensitive Gpr114 protein variant (T-Gpr114) that responds to short-pulse US stimulation for activation. We further design sonogenetic macrophages (MΦ) by fusing T-Gpr114 to a synthetic genetic circuit, this circuit initiates expression of the transcription factor ID3 upon short-pulse US treatment. In vivo assays using female tumor-bearing mice demonstrate that sonogenetic-MΦ suppress tumor growth with no obvious systemic toxicity, and exhibit enhanced anti-tumor efficacy relative to cells with constitutive ID3 expression. Single-cell RNA sequencing reveals that sonogentic-MΦ potentiate the recruitment recruitment of CD8+ T cells compared with constitutive ID3-overexpressing macrophages. In this work, this T-Gpr114-based US-hypersensitive gene circuit extends the scope of remote control over the activation of diverse cell types, thereby facilitating the programming of therapeutic cells.


## Introduction
  Cells integrate extracellular and intracellular cues to shape their functional states, and the genome encodes all cellular responses to these stimuli. Precise manipulation of transcriptional programs in response to specific stimuli enables the development of living cell-based therapeutics with programmable therapeutic functions1–3. To this end, researchers develop multiple strategies for controllable gene expression, including drug-inducible4, optogenetics5, radiofrequency radiation6, and magnetic fields7. Nevertheless, these technologies suffer from notable practical limitations. For instance, systemically administered chemical inducers lack tissue-specific targeting and frequently trigger undesired off-target effects4. Although optogenetics delivers superior spatiotemporal resolution, poor deep-tissue light penetration limits its in vivo applicability5. Similarly, radiofrequency radiation and magnetic field stimulation provide inadequate spatial and temporal precision for targeted cell manipulation6,7.

  Ultrasound (US) features inherent noninvasiveness and deep tissue penetration, rendering it an ideal tool for localized disease intervention8. Sonogenetic platforms built around US-sensitive receptors emerge as versatile noninvasive tools to modulate cellular behavior9–11. Researchers engineer a broad panel of US-responsive receptors, including prestin variants12, human TRPA113, Piezo family channels14, and bacterial MscL15 to enable precise regulation of cellular functions. Beyond its widespread use in neural modulation, sonogenetics also exhibits prominent therapeutic potential for cancer treatment. For example, Piezo1 effectively drives chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) transcription via the NFAT-dependent promoter16. Nevertheless, US-evoked nonselective calcium influx concurrently activates multiple intracellular signaling axes, which compromises the specificity of inducible gene transcription17. In addition, US parameters such as frequency, power, and stimulation duration generate local thermal bio-effects18. Such US-induced hyperthermia activates heat-shock protein (HSP) promoters to drive transient ectopic CAR expression. However, integrase-dependent Cre-lox systems exhibit irreversible activation, and persistent CAR expression leads to progressive T-cell exhaustion19. To address this drawback, prior work screens high-sensitivity HSP promoters from gene libraries and constructs sonogenetic EchoBack-CAR-T cells equipped with CAR signaling-derived positive feedback loops, which enable persistent CAR expression under focused US stimulation20. Unfortunately, HSP-inducible systems remain prone to nonspecific activation triggered by diverse cellular stressors21. Thus, establishing novel US-hypersensitive regulatory modules is essential to advance the specificity and precision of gene expression.

  Gpr114 belongs to the orphan GPCR family and relies on its endogenous tethered peptide agonist, the Stachel sequence (SS), for mechanical activation22. The SS resides at the extreme C-terminus of the highly conserved GPCR autoproteolysis-inducing (GAIN) domain and dynamically interacts with the transmembrane domain (TMD) under resting or stimulated conformational states23–25. Mechanical force (MF) stabilizes the active conformation of the tethered SS agonist, thereby triggering downstream G-protein signaling via the 7-transmembrane (7TM) domain24. Recent work confirms that the hydrophobic interaction motif (HIM) within the Gpr114 SS acts as a core “toggle switch” for transmembrane mechanosensing and is essential for receptor mechanical activation26. As a precisely tunable mechanical wave, US delivers localized mechanical stimulation to specific subcellular compartments of target cells8,27. In theory, Gpr114 possesses the structural capacity to sense US-derived mechanical stimuli. Nevertheless, the bulky N-terminal domain of native Gpr114 markedly impairs its intrinsic mechanosensitivity. Rational structural engineering of Gpr114 therefore stands to greatly enhance its responsiveness to mechanical US.

  In this work, we combine AlphaFold3 structural prediction with molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to engineer a US-hypersensitive Gpr114 variant (T-Gpr114) via targeted truncation and site-directed mutagenesis of native Gpr114. Using this optimized mechanosensor as a foundation, we further assemble a super-sensitivity sonogenetic integrated gene regulatory circuit (SSIGR). In this system, T-Gpr114 acts as the core mechanosensing module and couples functionally to synthetic genetic cascades consisting of the TEV protease cleavage system28 and the Tetracycline-controlled transactivator (tTA) circuit29. Short-pulse US stimulation induces conformational rearrangement of T-Gpr114, which in turn activates TEV protease. Cleaved TEV protease specifically removes the inhibitory linker segment and releases functional tTA from its inactive precursor, which drives robust transcription of downstream therapeutic transgenes. As a proof-of-concept application, we apply the SSIGR platform to selectively upregulate ID3 (a nuclear factor that endows macrophages with potent anti-tumor activity30) in engineered macrophages (MΦSSIGR-ID3). US-induced ID3 expression boosts phagocytic anti-tumor activity in MΦSSIGR-ID3. More importantly, elevated ID3 signaling strengthens their capacity to activate intratumoral CD8+ T cells and therefore potently inhibits tumor progression (Fig. 1). ## Results

### Optimization and design of an MF-controlled gene expression system
  In native Gpr114, a hydrophobic interaction motif (HIM) containing of five hydrophobic residues is essential for modulating the response of the SS to MF stimuli. This structural module senses the “toggle switch” and triggering downstream signaling pathways26 (Fig. 2A, B). However, the large N-terminal domain of native Gpr114 substantially constrains its mechanosensory sensitivity. To overcome this structural limitation, we combined AlphaFold3 structure prediction with MD simulations to screen Gpr114 variants with enhanced sensitivity to MF stimulation, following these steps (Fig. 2C): (1) Truncation mutants were conducted on existing protein sequences, and the outcomes of the protein structures were predicted; (2) Five hydrophobic residues within the HIM were selected to evaluate the interactions between the “toggle switch” and these residues under MF stimulation via MD simulation. This screening process yielded fourteen structurally stable Gpr114 variants validated by AlphaFold3 prediction (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Further analysis of Lennard-Jones short-range (LJ-SR) and Coulomb short-range (Coul-SR) interaction energies under tensile force loading in MD simulations identified a truncated Gpr114 mutant (T9, termed as T-Gpr114) with robust hypersensitivity to MF stimulation (Fig. 2D and Supplementary Fig. 1B–D). Molecular snapshots reveal that the hydrophobic residues within the HIM of T-Gpr114 exhibit strengthened binding interactions with the “toggle switch” residue W295 at a simulation time of 380 ps (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Notably, the SS-derived HIM of T-Gpr114 establishes stable interactions with W295, whereas this binding event is absent in native Gpr114 (Fig. 2E and Supplementary Fig. 2B). MD simulation further confirmed that the hydrophobic residue V56 in the T-Gpr114 HIM specifically interacts with the W295 toggle switch under tensile force. In contrast, the HIM hydrophobic residues of native Gpr114 fail to engage in stable interactions with the corresponding toggle switch residue W466 (Supplementary Fig. 2C, D).   To further characterize the capacity of T-Gpr114 to drive target gene expression in response to MF stimulation, we engineered an MF switch control system, designated MF-inducible TEV protease-based synthetic gene regulator (SSIGR). This system comprises a mechanosensory and genetic transducer component (Fig. 2F). The mechanosensory component is a membrane-tethered synthetic protein assembled from multiple mechanosensitive components, including T-Gpr114, TEV-N(the N-terminal region of TEV)31, TEV protease cleavage sequence (TEVseq)32, TetR-VP16 (a tetracycline-controlled transcriptional activator, tTA)33,34, and a fusion protein of β-arrestin2-TEV-C (the C-terminal region of TEV)29. The genetic transducer component contains a tetracycline response element (TRE) coupled to a pCMVmin promoter to initiate transcriptional activation (Fig. 2G). In the absence of MF stimulation, TEV-N and TEV-C remain dissociated and fail to form a functional protease. Upon MF exposure, T-Gpr114 undergoes conformational rearrangement driven by the binding between HIM hydrophobic residues and the “toggle switch” domain, which triggers T-Gpr114 activation. Activated T-Gpr114 further recruits β-arrestin2, thereby promoting the reassociation of TEV-N and TEV-C to restore intact TEV protease activity. The reconstituted functional TEV protease subsequently cleaves the TEVseq, ultimately triggering downstream transcriptional activation.

  To assess the spontaneous leakage of the split TEV system in the SSIGR system, we transfected human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293T cells with constructs encoding native gene circuit (SIGR, native Gpr114 as a mechanosensory component) and SSIGR components. Conventional split TEV systems are known to exhibit intrinsic basal leakage29. To address this issue, we incorporated a mutated TEV-C variant into the platform, which effectively minimizes basal background activity while preserving full reconstituted protease functionality29. No obvious luciferase reporter expression was detected in HEK293T cells transfected with individual or combined SSIGR components under MF-free conditions (Supplementary Fig. 2E). Notably, the SSIGR system exhibited significantly higher MF-triggered luciferase expression compared with the native SIGR system, with an ~5.77-fold upregulation of reporter activity (Fig. 2H), confirming the significantly higher activation efficiency of SSIGR.


### Short-pulse US as a remote trigger for controlling gene expression in living cells
  Given the rapid MF-inducible reporter gene expression driven by the SSIGR system, we hypothesized that short-pulse US could remotely control gene expression in living cells (Fig. 3A). To ensure sterility, the transfected HEK293T cells were subjected to short-pulse US treatment using a transducer placed at the bottom of the cell culture plate (Fig. 3B). The parameters of short-pulse US were used as shown in Fig. 3C. As expected, US stimulation triggered luciferase expression in the SSIGR system, reaching ~10.4-fold upregulation.   Optimizing US parameters is imperative for enabling precise and efficient control of the target gene expression in sonogenetics. We systematically tested a series of acoustic conditions to define the optimal performance of SSIGR system. Gradually increasing the total duration (Td) from 10 to 60 min (0.65 MHz, 0.28 W/cm2, 25% DC) revealed a time-dependent increase in reporter gene expression, reaching a maximum of 18.50-fold activation at 60 min (Fig. 3E). Screening duty cycles (DC) ranging from 5% to 25% (0.65 MHz, 0.28 W/cm2, 10 min duration) yielded progressive increases in gene activity, with 25% duty cycle producing 10.46-fold upregulation (Fig. 3F). Similarly, tuning acoustic intensity from 0.14 to 0.42 W/cm2 (0.65 MHz, 25% duty cycle, 10 min duration) drove intensity-dependent activation, peaking at 15.55-fold at 0.42 W/cm2 (Fig. 3G). Temperature profiling under the optimized pulsed-US condition (0.65 MHz, 0.28 W/cm2, 25% duty cycle, 10 min duration) showed no detectable temperature elevation upon sonication, ruling out thermal interference (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Identical US exposure caused negligible viability of engineered HEK293T cells (Supplementary Fig. 3B). These findings reveal that the optimized acoustic parameters support potent reporter gene induction in the absence of confounding thermal stress and overt cytotoxicity.

  Notably, US-triggered luciferase expression from the SSIGR system was reversible. Repeated administration of optimized US re-stimulation was sufficient to restore attenuated reporter activity (Fig. 3H). This observation highlights the sensitivity and reversible switching characteristics of the sonogenetic circuit. Subsequently, the temporal activation kinetics of the SSIGR system were profiled following optimized US stimulation. Reporter activity was significantly induced as early as 2 h post-stimulation, peaked at 12 h, and gradually declined to basal levels within 48 h (Fig. 3I), confirming the precise, controllability of the SSIGR circuit. Remarkably, compared with Ca2+ or ROS-dependent sonogenetic systems35,36, which typically trigger broad and poorly controllable downstream signaling, the engineered GPCR pathway confers higher activation efficiency and specificity under optimized US parameters (Fig. 3J, K). This functional advantage stems from the truncated GPCR configuration. Such structural optimization elicits targeted transcriptional responses and circumvents the widespread non-specific cellular activation commonly observed in conventional sonogenetic approaches. Taken together, these results demonstrate that the SSIGR system enables sensitive and robust responses to short-pulse US stimulation, allowing precision control of gene expression.


### Sonogenetics-controlled engineered MΦ cells with anti-tumor activity in vitro
  We next applied this system to remotely control the expression of nuclear factor ID3 in MΦ for cancer immunotherapy. Mechanistically, ID3 remodels the balance between inhibitory and activating receptors on macrophages to enhance phagocytic capacity and anti-tumor lymphoid responses by attenuating SIRPα-mediated inhibitory signaling30. To this end, we replaced the reporter gene with the ID3 coding sequence within the genetic transduction circuit to engineer ID3-inducible bone marrow-derived macrophages (termed MΦSSIGR-ID3), enabling short-pulse US-control ID3 expression (Fig. 4A, B). RT-qPCR assays confirmed the successful transduction and construction of the SSIGR system in MΦ cells (Supplementary Fig. 4A–C). Consistent with previous findings, bone marrow-derived macrophages intrinsically exhibit low ID3, high SIRPα, and low anti-tumor activity30. Similarly, MΦ cells displayed low ID3 and high SIRPα expression in the absence of US stimulation. In contrast, short-pulse US treatment (0.65 MHz, 0.28 W/cm2, 25% duty cycle, 10 min) triggered robust ID3 upregulation and SIRPα downregulation in MΦ cell (Fig. 4C). Time-course analysis further revealed that ID3 expression was induced at 2 h, peaked at 12 h, and declined by 48 h after US stimulation (Fig. 4D). In addition, this US condition had a minimal impact on the MΦ cell viability (Supplementary Fig. 4D).   In the context of cancer, the expression of ID3 can significantly enhance the anti-tumor activity of MΦ30. We found that ectopic expression of ID3 in MΦSSIGR-ID3 following short pulse US irradiation can increase the expression of various receptors related to macrophage activation and phagocytosis that recognizes carbohydrate antigens on the surface of tumor cells37, such as C-C chemokines (Ccl3, Ccl4, Ccl5) and interleukins (IL-12, IL-15, and IL-18), which are involved in the recruitment and activation of effector lymphocytes at the tumor site38–43 (Fig. 4E), These effects peak at 24 h post-US treatment (Supplementary Fig. 4E). Notably, MΦSSIGR-ID3 cells exhibit detectable production of Ccl3, Ccl4, Ccl5, Il-12, Il-15 and Il-18 even when treated with US alone, in the absence of tumor cells (Supplementary Fig. 4F). Co-culture with tumor cells further amplifies this US-triggered signaling cascade, resulting in markedly enhanced cytokine secretion, indicating that MΦSSIGR-ID3 combined with US treatment endows macrophages with augmented anti-tumor potential.

  Furthermore, Luminex ImageStream flow cytometry analysis revealed that untreated MΦSSIGR-ID3 cells exhibited limited phagocytic ability toward 4T1 tumor cells. However, short-pulse US irradiation-induced ID3 expression markedly potentiated the phagocytic capacity of MΦSSIGR-ID3 macrophages (Fig. 4F, G). Consistent with these quantitative results, imaging observations further validated robust phagocytic activity in US-treated MΦSSIGR-ID3 cells: extensive engulfment of CFSE-labeled 4T1 tumor cells by CD11b-positive MΦSSIGR-ID3 cells was clearly detected following pulsed US stimulation (Fig. 4H). Transwell migration assays demonstrated that MΦSSIGR-ID3 cells possessed comparable migratory capability to traverse the semipermeable membrane and contact 4T1 tumor cells relative to native macrophages. US stimulation under the current experimental conditions does not alter chemotactic properties of macrophages (Fig. 4I–K). These results indicate that the tumor-targeting tropism of MΦSSIGR-ID3 cells is not altered by genetic modification.


### Sonogenetics-controlled MΦSSIGR-ID3 inhibits tumor growth and lung metastasis
  To investigate the tropism of MΦSSIGR-ID3 toward tumors in vivo, DiR-labeled MΦSSIGR-ID3 were adoptively transferred into tumor-bearing mice, and their distribution was evaluated at various time points. The results demonstrated that MΦSSIGR-ID3 primarily accumulated in the liver, spleen, lung, and tumor tissues (Supplementary Fig. 5A–D). Notably, fluorescence could still be detected in tumor tissues up to 96 h post-transfer, indicating a strong tropism of MΦSSIGR-ID3 for tumors in vivo (Supplementary Fig. 5B). To further evaluate the spatial specificity and potential off-target activation of ID3, we established a bilateral subcutaneous tumor model in the same mouse, with one tumor designated for short-pulse US treatment and the contralateral tumor untreated (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Fig. 5E).   Inspired by the ability of short-pulse US to induce MΦSSIGR-ID3-mediated ID3 expression in tumor tissues, we further evaluated the in vivo anti-tumor efficacy of US-controllable MΦSSIGR-ID3 in a 4T1 breast tumor model. MΦSSIGR-ID3 were delivered into tumor-bearing mice via tail vein injection, followed by remote, noninvasive activation using short-pulse US (Fig. 5D). MΦID3-OE was established by lentiviral infection of a constitutive ID3 overexpression cassette (Supplementary Fig. 5F). After 15 days of treatment, the average tumor volume in the MΦSSIGR-ID3 + US group reached only 229.36 mm3, which was markedly lower than that of all control groups: Control (1533.10 mm3), MΦControl + US (1486.06 mm3), MΦSIGR-ID3 + US (816.37 mm3), MΦSSIGR-ID3 alone (1413.17 mm3), and MΦID3-OE (569.89 mm3) (Fig. 5E). Tumor tissues were dissected and weighed on day 15, which further confirmed that the MΦSSIGR-ID3 + US group exhibited the smallest tumor volume and weight among all groups (Fig. 5F, G). This potent anti-tumor effect can be attributed to US-induced local upregulation of ID3in MΦSSIGR-ID3 cells (Fig. 5H). Beyond primary tumor inhibition, the MΦSSIGR-ID3 + US group also showed remarkably reduced pulmonary metastatic nodules, which were further validated by histological H&E staining of lung tissues (Fig. 5I–K). These results demonstrate the potent therapeutic efficacy of the sonogenetic platform, in which US-triggered regional stimulation endows engineered macrophages with the capacity to efficiently suppress tumor growth and lung metastasis.

  Through unsupervised clustering analysis, we identified eight immune-related cell clusters with unique transcriptomic signatures, which were visualized via uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) and defined by specific marker genes (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Fig. 6A). UMAP visualization revealed that short-pulse US treatment significantly enriched in macrophage cluster regions. As expected, MΦ cells exhibited the highest overall ID3 abundance due to constitutive transgene expression (Fig. 6C). Further transcriptomic analysis confirmed that elevated ID3 expression potently suppressed SIRPα transcription in macrophages (Fig. 6D). Furthermore, MΦSSIGR-ID3 + US treatment increased the infiltration of CD8+ T cells within the tumor tissue, while CD8+ T cells decreased in the MΦID3-OE group (Fig. 6E, F). This finding suggests that the transient and efficient ID3 expression induced by MΦSSIGR-ID3 + US treatment in MΦ effectively enhances the expression of receptors involved in their activation and phagocytosis. Such modulation remodels the intratumoral distribution and abundance of CD8+ T cells, indicating successful reprogramming of the tumor immune microenvironment (Fig. 6G).

  We further investigated the intratumoral immune responses to elucidate the immune mechanisms driving suppressed tumor growth. Flow cytometry analysis of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes was performed on day 14 post-treatment (Supplementary Fig. 6B, C). Compared to other groups, mice treated with MΦSSIGR-ID3 + US exhibited a significantly increased population of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Fig. 6H, I and Supplementary Fig. 7A, B). Additionally, MΦSSIGR-ID3 + US treatment markedly increased the population of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells (CD8+ TNF-α+) (Fig. 6J and Supplementary Fig. 7C). However, compared to the MΦSSIGR-ID3 + US group, MΦID3-OE treatment not only decreased the infiltration ratio of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the tumor tissue but also reduced the number of functional CD8+ T cells. Immunofluorescence staining of tumor sections further confirmed this phenomenon (Supplementary Fig. 7D). These results demonstrate US-inducible MΦSSIGR-ID3 cells exhibit superior and sustained capacity to recruit cytotoxic T lymphocytes while circumventing the functional exhaustion associated with persistent ID3 overexpression. This functional advantage is likely attributed to the intermittent quiescent state of MΦ between discrete US stimulation cycles, which preserves macrophage immune function and sustains effective anti-tumor immunity.


### Sonogenetics-controlled MΦSSIGR-ID3 reduces immune-related adverse effects
  Alterations in MΦ differentiation and/or activity can have serious impacts on the homeostasis of the organism44,45. To systematically assess the in vivo biosafety and immunological impacts of different macrophage formulations, we performed serum biochemistry, routine hematology, and histological analysis of major organs. Key liver function markers, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and total bilirubin (TBIL), remained at basal levels in mice treated with MΦControl + US, MΦSSIGR-ID3, MΦSIGR-ID3 + US, and MΦSSIGR-ID3 + US. In contrast, mice receiving constitutive MΦID3-OE treatment exhibited significantly elevated ALT, AST, and TBIL levels, indicative of obvious hepatic toxicity caused by sustained systemic ID3 overexpression. By comparison, renal function-related indicators, including creatine kinase (CK), urea, and uric acid (UA), showed no abnormal elevation across all experimental groups (Supplementary Table 1), demonstrating negligible renal damage from all treatments. Consistent with the biochemical analysis, histological staining revealed prominent tissue damage in the liver and spleen of MΦID3-OE treated mice. Notably, no detectable histological lesions or adverse tissue alterations were observed in the liver and spleen of mice receiving US-controlled sonogenetic macrophage treatment, which was comparable to the control and MΦControl + US groups. In addition, histological examination of the heart and kidneys confirmed no obvious tissue injury across all treatment groups (Supplementary Fig. 8). Peripheral blood hematological profiling further evaluated systemic immune and hematological homeostasis. Relative to controls, the MΦID3-OE group exhibited disrupted hematological phenotypes, including increased leukocyte counts, excessive neutrophil activation, and altered lymphocyte and platelet profiles, indicative of systemic immune dysregulation (Supplementary Table 2). Collectively, these safety analyses demonstrate that constitutive ID3 overexpression in macrophages induces marked hepatic and splenic immunotoxicity and disturbs systemic immune homeostasis. In contrast, pulsed US-controlled sonogenetic ID3 expression enables precise, spatiotemporal gene activation, avoids systemic immunological side effects, and confers favorable in vivo biosafety.


## Discussion
  In this study, we established a sonogenetic regulatory platform driven by short-pulse mechanical US, which enables remote, non-invasive and high precise control of gene expression in living cells. Specifically, we screened and engineered an ultra-mechanosensitive protein variant T-Gpr114, and further integrated it with the TEV protease cleavage module and tetracycline-controlled transactivator (tTA) system to construct a sonogenetic gene expression regulatory circuit (SSIGR). Featuring high sensitivity, modularity, and good compatibility, the SSIGR system allows rapid and precise induction of target gene expression in response to short-pulse US stimulation. We demonstrated that engineered macrophages equipped with the SSIGR system can efficiently trigger the expression of the nuclear factor ID3 upon short-pulse US stimulation. US-induced ID3 upregulation significantly enhances the tumoricidal phagocytic capacity of macrophages, remodels the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, and activates systemic antitumor immune responses, ultimately achieving potent tumor therapeutic efficacy. This programmable sonogenetic strategy is adaptable for remote modulation of multiple cell types and provides a universal approach for controlling molecular functions in cell-based cancer immunotherapy.

  In the field of tumor therapy, sonogenetic cell therapies have attracted growing research attention due to the unique advantages of US, including noninvasiveness, superior specificity, and deep tissue penetration9,35,46. Previous studies have exploited US to regulate therapeutic gene expression in bacteria systems via thermally activated transcription factors TlpA47, or the leftward (PL) and rightward (PR) phage lambda promoters48,49. However, bacterial-based therapeutic platforms are still hampered by inherent biosafety concerns that limit their clinical translation. Alternatively, US has been applied to control chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) expression in mammalian cells through mechanically activated Piezo116 or HSP promoters19,20. Nevertheless, Piezo1-mediated activation relies on nonspecific calcium influx and generally requires microbubble assistance, severely restricting its in vivo applicability. Moreover, HSP promoter-dependent systems are prone to undesired activation induced by diverse cellular stress signals, resulting in poor induction specificity21. In contrast, structurally optimized SSIGR system based on engineered T-Gpr114 can specifically sense mechanical cues from low-intensity short-pulse US, achieving precise and specific target gene regulation. Compared with currently available sonogenetic tools, the structurally optimized T-Gpr114 endows the SSIGR system with substantially enhanced mechanosensitivity and induction specificity.

  Preliminary data indicate that the sonogenetically controllable SSIGR system is capable of rapidly upregulating the expression of the target gene. Experimental evidence further reveals demonstrates that short-pulse US stimulation can further enhance the upregulation of the target gene (Fig. 3E–G). Notably, short-pulse US stimulation offers the advantages of being non-invasive and repeatable, and its duration and intensity can be precisely controlled (Fig. 3H, I). This strategy can be extended to complex biological systems, such as the activation of macrophages, which involves changes in macrophage immune functions and enhances their anti-tumor therapeutic effects. Macrophages, serving as carriers of the SSIGR system, can efficiently induce the ectopic expression of the nuclear factor ID3 under US stimulation (Fig. 4C, D), thereby enhancing the phagocytic ability of macrophages (Fig. 4F–H). In an orthotopic breast cancer model, MΦSSIGR-ID3 + US showed significant anti-tumor effects (Fig. 5E–G, I–K). As shown in Fig. 6H–J, tumors treated with MΦSSIGR-ID3 + US exhibited a significant increase in CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, with a substantial rise in functional CD8+ T cells, indicating enhanced T cell activation. However, excessive activation of macrophages can impair their immune functions. Functional validation found that compared with the MΦSSIGR-ID3 + US treatment group, macrophages with constitutive ID3 expression had a significantly reduced ability to recruit T cells. Importantly, immune toxicity remains a concern in the clinical translation of macrophage activation-mediated immunotherapy50,51. Biosafety studies revealed that MΦSSIGR-ID3 + US administration did not cause significant tissue damage or weight changes in treated mice compared to MΦID3-OE. These studies demonstrate that MΦSSIGR-ID3 + US-induced cell killing is not only site-specific but also highly tunable, preventing adverse effects from non-specific and excessive activation.

  Despite the significant enhancement of anti-tumor efficacy and the favorable safety profile observed with SSIGR + US treatment in this study, several key developments are necessary for future clinical application. For example, there is a need for a pulse US-control device that integrates temperature monitoring, gene expression, and adjustable penetration depth to facilitate the expression of intratumoral cytokines or proteins for anti-tumor therapy. Additionally, to achieve optimal therapeutic effects in future clinical applications, further optimization of ultrasensitive mechanosensitive media is essential to enhance the expression levels of therapeutic genes.

  In summary, we have developed a highly modular sonogenetic control system that enables rapid output in various living cells, specifically designed for tumor immunotherapy. Functional validation demonstrate that this system can efficiently induce therapeutic gene expression in macrophages, resulting in robust tumor-suppressive effects. In conclusion, by integrating SSIGR with short-pulse US-mediated remote cell activation, we have achieved precise control of therapeutic interventions.


## Methods

### Ethical statement
  All animal experiments described in this work were performed in strict accordance with all relevant institutional ethical regulations for laboratory animal research. Eight-week-old female BALB/c mice were purchased from the Experimental Animal Center of the Fourth Military Medical University. Animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics and Use Committee of the Fourth Military Medical University (Permit Number 20250135). Female mice were selected for this study, as breast cancer predominantly occurs in females. According to institutional policies on tumor production, the upper tumor size limit of 2.0 cm was never exceeded in our animal experiments.

### Cell culture
  HEK293T and 4T1 cells were purchased from the China Center for Type Culture Collection. HEK293T cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. 4T1 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS. Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were generated from the bone marrow of BALB/c female mice. In brief, tibiae and femurs were isolated from eight-week-old BALB/c female mice. The bones were placed on ice and rinsed in sterile culture dishes. Under sterile conditions, the ends of each bone were cut off, and the bone marrow cavity was flushed with culture medium using a sterile syringe. After red blood cell lysis, the collected cells were washed and cultured in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS and 25 ng/mL M-CSF, allowing them to differentiate into macrophages over 7 days.


### Protein structures prediction and MD simulation
  AlphaFold 3 was used to generate the 3D protein structures based on the amino acid sequences of the native and variant proteins of Gpr11452. Molecular dynamics simulations were conducted using the Gromacs 2021.7 software. A force field was created for the generated protein structures using Gromacs. The simulation system was established with Packmol, creating a box measuring 9 nm × 9 nm × 50 nm. The generated protein structures were placed on the left side of the box, and water molecules were randomly inserted to fill the box. The system then underwent 50,000 steps of energy minimization to eliminate unfavorable contacts in the initial structure, followed by 100 ps of NVT (constant number of particles, volume, and temperature) equilibration and 100 ps of NPT (constant number of particles, pressure, and temperature) equilibration. Finally, a 380 ps NPT production simulation was performed. The temperature during the simulation was maintained at 37 °C, and the pressure was set at 1 bar (approximately one standard atmosphere). During the production simulation, positional restraints were applied to the first amino acid residue, and a pulling force was applied to the Stachel sequence in the z-direction at a loading rate of 1 N/s (spring constant 1.66 × 10−9 N/nm, velocity 1 × 109 nm/s). The interactions between the HIM in the Stachel sequence and the “toggle switch” were analyzed throughout the MD simulation process.


### Short-pulse US equipment
  The therapeutic US device used in this study is a low-power US therapy instrument developed by the Chongqing Ronghai Ultrasound Medical Engineering Research Center (Chongqing, China). The US pressure intensities were measured using a capsule hydrophone (NHA-0400, ONDA) in a custom-built plexiglas water tank. For sonication in vitro, cells were seeded in to a 6 or 24-well plate, and transducer was positioned at the center of the cell culture well, the sonication effect was confined to the intended cell population without excessive diffusion to non-target areas. In this study, cell samples were exposed to US at the intensities of 0.14, 0.28, 0.42 W/cm2 (ISPTA), respectively. For sonication in vivo, the focal zone dimensions of the ultrasound transducer ensure that the ultrasonic treatment is concentrated on the tumor site and the pressure intensities was 0.35 W/cm2 (ISPTA). Temperature changes were monitored using a near-infrared thermal imaging instrument (FLIR, C5, USA) during the ultrasonic treatment.


### Plasmid construction and transfection
  All plasmids used in this study were constructed by GenScript Biotech Co., Ltd (Nanjing, China). The DNA sequences are listed in Supplementary Data 1 file and the DNA file formats (.dna) are deposited in Source Data file. HEK293T cells were seeded in a 6-well plate to assess the reporter gene’s expression. After ~24 h, the cells were transfected with a mixture of plasmids comprising the SIGR system, which included Gpr114-V2 tail-TEV-N-TEVseq-tTA, β-arrestin2-TEV-C, and TRE-luciferase, using LipofectamineTM 3000 (Invitrogen) transfection reagent at a mass ratio of 0.5: 0.5: 2.5. Specifically, for each well, 3 μg of the above plasmid DNA was combined with 120 μL of Opti-MEMTM, followed by the addition of 20 μL of lipofectamin TM 3000. The resulting mixture was vortexed thoroughly to ensure homogeneity and then incubated at room temperature for 15 min before being added to the cells. The SSIGR system and Renilla luciferase plasmid (a transfection marker, was utilized to monitor transfection efficiency) were transfected into HEK293T cells using the same protocol.


### MF and short-pulse US-induced reporter gene expression
  To systematically verify the mechanosensitivity of the engineered SIGR and SSIGR gene circuits, 2 × 105 HEK-293T cells were seeded into a 6-well plate and transfected with the corresponding constructs following the aforementioned protocols. For mechanical force (MF) stimulation, transfected cells were subjected to continuous shaking at 100 rpm for 30 min in a 37 °C thermostatic oscillating incubator (Jinghong Co., Ltd, China). At 2 h post MF treatment, luciferase activity was measured in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. For pulsed US stimulation, engineered cells were exposed to pulsed US with gradient treatment durations and parameters, and luciferase expression were quantified using identical analytical procedures. Notably, mechanical shaking and pulsed US represent two biologically and physically distinct mechanical stimuli: mechanical shaking induces mild macroscopic bulk perturbation, whereas pulsed US applies rapid, localized acoustic radiation force directly to individual cells. Here, the shaking assay was used as a simplified proof-of-concept model to verify the intrinsic mechanosensing capacity of the synthetic gene circuits, rather than recapitulating the biomechanical intensity of US stimulation.


### Construction and characterization of genetically MΦSSIGR-ID3
  The constructs of T-Gpr114-V2 tail-TEV-N-TEVseq-tTA-Puro (GCPL#5045543), Native-Gpr114-V2 tail-TEV-N-TEVseq-tTA-Puro (GOSL#5045545), TRE-ID3-EF1a-β-arrestin2-TEV-C-Neo (GCPL#5045544), ID3-OE (GCPL#5045542) lentivirus packaging were produced by Genechem Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). To develop MΦSSIGR-ID3 cells that can ectopically express ID3 through pulse US remote control, the constructs were stably introduced into MΦ cells by lentiviral infection using the HitransG P kit (Genechem, China). MΦSSIGR-ID3 cells were obtained by selecting infected cells using G418 and puromycin.


### Cell viability test
  4T1 and MΦ cells were subjected to US treated (0.65 MHz, 0.28 W/cm2, 25% duty cycle, 10 min duration). After 24 h, the supernatant in the culture plate was removed, and 10% CCK-8 reagent in fresh culture medium was added to each well. The plates were then incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After gently shaking the plate for 60 s, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured for each well using a microplate reader (BioTek, neo2, USA).


### Phagocytosis assays of MΦSSIGR-ID3
  Before the phagocytosis assay, MΦSSIGR-ID3 cells were subjected to pulse US treatment (0.65 MHz, 0.28 W/cm2, 25% duty cycle, 10 min duration) for 10 min and then cultured at 37 °C. After 2 h, the treated cells were collected and stained with a CD11b antibody. Concurrently, 4T1 cells were harvested and stained with CFSE according to the protocol of the Cell Trace CFSE Cell Proliferation Kit. Subsequently, in a 6-well plate, 2 × 105 CD11b-labeled MΦSSIGR-ID3 cells were co-cultured with 2 × 105 CFSE-labeled 4T1 cells in culture medium derived from MΦSSIGR-ID3 cells. After 6 h, the cells were collected and analyzed using a Luminex ImageStream Mark II (USA).


### Chemotaxis assays of MΦSSIGR-ID3 cells in vitro
  A total of 2 × 104 MΦSSIGR-ID3 and MΦ cells, with or without US stimulation, were seeded into the upper chamber of a 24-well Transwell plate (8 μm, Corning) and cultured overnight. Meanwhile, 5 × 104 4T1 cells were plated in the lower chamber, which contained RPMI 1640 culture medium as a control. The system was incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. After incubation, the MΦSSIGR-ID3 cells and MΦ cells were washed, fixed with paraformaldehyde, and stained with crystal violet. Image were captured by Optical microscope (Nikon, T12-D-PD). Chemotaxis was assessed by counting the number of cells that migrated through the semipermeable membrane using Image J (Fijiversion 1.54, NIH).


### RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and real-time PCR
  Total RNA was extracted from cells at different stages of differentiation using an RNA isolation mini kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Subsequently, 1 μg of RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript II Q RT SuperMix following the manufacturer’s instructions. Gene expression was analyzed by SYBR-Green qPCR Master Mix using the Thermo ABI QuantStudio 6. The primers were obtained from TsingkeBiotechnologyCo., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and the sequence are listed in Supplementary Table 3.


### Immunoblotting analysis
  Protein samples were collected and separated on a 12.5% SDS-PAGE gel, followed by electroblotting onto a nitrocellulose membrane (1620115, Bio-Rad). The membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk (w/v) in TBST at room temperature for 2 h. It was then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against ID3, (ThermoFisher, Cat #PA5-100268, dilution ratio 1:1000). Subsequently, the membrane was incubated with an HRP-conjugated goad anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Proteintech, Cat# RGAR001, dilution ratio 1:3000) at room temperature for 1 h. The blot was developed using a fluorescence-based imaging system (LI-COR Odyssey Biosciences).


### Tumor model
  4T1 (1 × 106) tumor cells were implanted into the second left mammary fat pad of the BABL/c female mice. Before treatment, the average tumor volume was ~70 mm3. Tumor growth was monitored by external measurement with digital calipers, and the tumor volume = tumor length × tumor width2 × 0.5.


### Tumor treatment
  When tumor volume reached ~70–80 mm3, tumor-bearing mice were randomly allocated into experimental groups and intravenously administered 4 × 106 cells of MΦcontrol, MΦID3-OE, MΦSIGR-ID3, or MΦSSIGR-ID3, respectively. For in vivo US stimulation, medical ultrasound coupling agent was used to ensure efficient acoustic transmission between the transducer and the animal skin. The hair over the targeted tumor region was thoroughly shaved to minimize ultrasonic signal attenuation and maintain stable acoustic coupling. With the aid of a 3D positioning system, the tumor was precisely positioned within the focal center of the transducer, enabling accurate localized energy delivery to the tumor tissue. The tumor region was then exposed to pulsed US irradiation (0.65 MHz, 0.35 W/cm2, 25% duty cycle) for 10 min on days 2 and 4 after cell injection. On day 8 following the first administration, a second round of treatment was performed following the identical experimental procedure described above.


### Chemotaxis analysis in vivo
  DiR-labeled MΦ or MΦSSIGR-ID3 cells were used to assess tumor targeting in vivo using the IVIS Spectrum Imaging System (PerkinElmer, USA). The average radiance (photons/second/cm2/sr) of regions of interest was quantified using Living Image 4.3.1 software. Additionally, at 96 h post-injection, major organs from the mice, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and tumor were collected, and the DiR fluorescence intensity was examined using the IVIS Spectrum Imaging System.


### Immunostaining
  To confirm the in vivo expression of ID3 from MΦSSIGR-ID3 cells under pulse US treatment, engineered MΦSSIGR-ID3 cells were administered via tail vein injection to tumor-bearing mice bearing bilateral lesions. The left tumor was subjected to US irradiation (US+), whereas the contralateral right tumor received no US treatment (US−). Tumor tissues were then collected from mice and embedded in optimal cutting temperature (O.C.T.) compound and stored at −80 °C overnight. Subsequently, the tissues were sectioned into 10-micrometer-thick slices using a cryostat (Thermo Scientific) and mounted onto glass slides. The tumor tissue sections were heated in antigen retrieval buffer at 100 °C for 30 min, followed by blocking with 5% BSA for 30 min. The sections were then incubated with an anti-ID3 primary antibody (ThermoFisher, Cat #PA5-100268, dilution ratio 1:300) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with CoraLite488-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) (Proteintech, SA00013-2, dilution ratio 1:500) overnight at 4 °C. After washing three times with PBST, the sections were mounted in an anti-fade mounting medium containing DAPI and examined by 3DHISTECH Slide Converter (Hungary).


### Single-cell RNA-seq library construction and sequencing
  On day 14 following the indicated treatment, the tumor tissues were sorted for single-cell RNA-seq. Fresh tumor tissues were mechanically cut into 1–3 mm3 fragments, followed by enzymatic digestion with a mixed enzyme cocktail to generate single-cell suspensions. The cell suspensions were filtered through 40 μm cell strainers and rinsed 3 times with wash buffer to eliminate tissue debris. Cell viability was quantified via trypan blue staining using a TC20 automated cell counter (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA); only cell suspensions with viability exceeding 80% were retained for subsequent experiments. Single-cell RNA-seq libraries were constructed with the MobiCube® High Throughput Single Cell 3’RNA-Seq Kit (v2.1, MobiDrop) following the manufacturer’s protocol, and all library preparation procedures were completed at Novogene Co., Ltd (Beijing, China).


### scRNA-seq data processing and analysis
  For data preprocessing and quality control, the raw sequencing data included Read1, Read2, and the i7 index read. Quality filtering and preprocessing of raw reads were conducted using Fastp. The filtered reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome using MobiVision v2.1 under default parameters, and gene-cell expression matrices were generated for downstream analysis. Then, the expression matrix obtained from MobiVision was imported into the Seurat package (v4.3.0) in R for quality control and downstream analysis. Genes expressed in fewer than three cells were excluded, and cells with fewer than 200 detected genes were removed. Additionally, cells with mitochondrial gene content >5% or hemoglobin/red blood cell-related gene expression >5% were filtered out. Normalization was performed using the “LogNormalize” method. Using the Seurat (v4.3.0) “subset” function, major immune cell types, including T cells, B cells, NK cells, macrophages, monocytes, neutrophils, and dendritic cells, were extracted for further normalization, dimensionality reduction, and clustering. Immune subtypes were subsequently annotated based on the expression of subtype-specific markers. In subsequent analyses, each sample was independently subjected to cell clustering and visualization using the Seurat package (v4.3.0) in R. The expression levels of target genes across different cell types or clusters were extracted and visualized using box plots to assess differential expression patterns. Additionally, the abundance of each cell population was quantified per sample to facilitate comparative analysis of immune microenvironment composition among samples.


### Flow cytometry
  On day 14 following the indicated treatment, the tumor-tissue were isolated from the mice and cut into small pieces, then the tissue was digested with collagenase V (2 mg/ml, Gbico) in HBSS solution at 100 rpm for 2 h in a 37 °C thermostatic oscillating incubator (Jinghong Co., Ltd, China). Cells were then passed through a 70-micrometer cell strainer (Absin, China). The collected cells were treated with red blood cell lysis buffer (BioLegend, Cat #420301) for 5 min. Subsequently, the samples were centrifuged at 400 × g for 10 min to remove the lysed red blood cells from the supernatant. The cell pellet was rinsed with PBS and resuspended in cell staining buffer (BioLegend, Cat #420201), a panel of fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies was used to label cell surface markers: CD45 (BioLegend, Cat #103132, clone# 30-F11, used at 0.25 µg /test), CD3 (BioLegend, Cat #100204, lone# 17A2, used at 1.0 µg /test), CD8a (BioLegend, Cat #162304, clone# S18018E, used at 0.5 µg /test), CD4 (BioLegend, Cat #100516, clone# RM4-5, used at 0.25 µg /test), TNF-α (BioLegend, Cat #506344, clone# MP6-XT22, used at 0.25 µg /test) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were stained for 30 min at room temperature and washed with cell staining buffer. After that, the samples were run on a BD flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter CytoFLEX, USA) and analyzed using Agilent NovoExpress software.


### Immunotoxicity assay
  Blood routine parameters, serum biochemical indices, and histological (H&E) staining of major organs were assessed to evaluate the biosafety of the MΦSSIGR-ID3 + US combination therapy. Tumor-bearing mice were randomly assigned to six groups (n = 3 per group): control, MΦcontrol + US, MΦSIGR-ID3 + US, MΦSSIGR-ID3 alone, MΦSSIGR-ID3 + US, and MΦID3-OE. Mice were euthanized 15 days after different treatment. Orbital blood samples were collected for hematological and biochemical analysis. Major organs were harvested and subjected to H&E staining, followed by histological examination using a Saiviewer 2.0.1 software.


### Statistics & reproducibility
  All data and figures in this study were analyzed and generated using AlphaFold3, GraphPad Prism 8.0.1, and Adobe Illustrator 2023. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (S.D.). We did not use any sample, size calculation; the sample size was chosen based on published literature and sample size was chosen depending on the technique and based on our experience. No data were excluded from the analyses. The data used for analysis were from three or more in triplicate or more unless. Unless otherwise specified, all experiments were performed in biological replicates. Replicates reported in the figures. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using unpaired two-tailed t-tests. When comparing more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed, followed by Tukey’s post hoc multiple comparisons. Statistical methods are fully described in the figure legends. Statistical significance was defined as a P value less than 0.05 and the exact P values are described in the figures. The experimenters were not blinded during the experiments or analysis except histological analysis. Blinding was not feasible in cases because the same researcher carried out both sample handling and subsequent data quantification. Blinding was performed when analyzing histological data, the person analyzing the histological slides was not aware the group type. For the establishment of an initial observation in any tumor growth experiments, the measurement was conducted by a second experimenter unaware of the information.
