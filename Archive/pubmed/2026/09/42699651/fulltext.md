# Single cell transcriptomic analysis by deep learning identifies novel
microglial transcription regulations in Alzheimer’s
Disease


## Abstract
  Artificial Intelligence (AI) has been increasingly applied to investigate genetic irregularities associated with Alzheimer’s Disease (AD). However, its potential to uncover deeper, more detailed molecular and cellular mechanisms remains underexplored, primarily due to limitations in integrating large-scale data and capturing the complex, cell-type-specific dynamics involved in AD pathology. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has emerged as a powerful tool in transcriptomics, offering high-resolution, cell-specific insights into complex biological systems. Despite this advancement, a significant gap remains in identifying both common and cell-type-specific transcriptomic signatures that define AD-related cellular and molecular processes. To address this, we propose a deep learning framework leveraging a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) to classify AD versus control nuclei using scRNA-seq data from the Religious Orders Study/Memory and Aging Project (ROSMAP). We focus on microglial subclusters, particularly those representing homeostatic and activated states, to train the MLP model for optimal classification performance. We utilize the predicted embeddings from the MLP to model a disease progression trajectory for each of the datasets. Our model demonstrates strong performance in both classification and disease trajectory inference. To enhance interpretability, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) are applied to identify key AD-associated genes. Based on the most salient genes implicated in AD, we built transcription gene regulatory networks, revealing novel transcription factors and regulons for AD pathogenesis. These regulons highlight profound impacts of dysregulations of proteostasis, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress responses, and circadian rhythm on synaptic plasticity and neuronal survival in AD, offering a more holistic approach to drug target discovery compared to conventional single-target strategies, potentially leading to greater efficacy in slowing or reversing disease progression. This work demonstrates the transformative potential of AI in elucidating the molecular mechanisms of AD, offering improvements over traditional methods and uncovering novel insights into disease pathogenesis and potential therapeutic targets.


## INTRODUCTION
  Alzheimer's Disease (AD) represents one of the most challenging neurodegenerative disorders, characterized by its complex, multifactorial nature involving diverse genetic and molecular alterations1. Despite decades of intensive research aimed at elucidating its underlying mechanisms, the molecular pathways driving disease onset, progression, and clinical manifestation remain only partially understood2. Understanding the intricate cellular composition and behavior in AD-affected brains is paramount for unraveling the disease's biological foundations, necessitating sophisticated approaches to dissect cellular heterogeneity at increasingly refined resolutions. In recent years, significant advances have been made in transcriptomic technologies, particularly RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), enabling deeper insights into gene expression dynamics3.

  In contrast to bulk RNA-seq studies which average global gene expression across thousands of cells4, scRNA-seq enables the detailed scrutiny of individual cells, revealing the remarkable diversity, heterogeneity, and specialized functions within cellular populations. The superior resolution of scRNA-seq data has transformed the ability to analyze individual cell types with high precision, facilitating the identification of subtle yet biologically significant differences in cellular states and their functional implications. The integration of scRNA-seq with Machine Learning (ML) and Deep Learning (DL) techniques has marked a paradigm shift in disease modeling, offering novel insights into the molecular basis of health and disease5,6. When coupled with advanced computational methods, scRNA-seq provides a wealth of information that can transform disease prediction by capturing cellular heterogeneity and high-resolution gene expression patterns. However, scRNA-seq data pose two major analytical challenges – batch effects and high sparsity. To address these issues, a variety of computational methods have been developed7-10. Despite these advancements, there remains a scarcity of comprehensive, end-to-end integrative pipelines that effectively translate scRNA-seq data into clinically actionable insights. However, for complex diseases like AD, there remains a need for a comprehensive framework that can not only preprocesses scRNA-seq data but also isolate functionally relevant microglial subsets, constructs predictive models to distinguish AD from cognitively normal (CN) individuals, interpret classification outcomes to identify key molecular drivers, and perform downstream analyses for biological validation. Such an end-to-end pipeline would bridge the gap between single-cell analysis and the discovery of meaningful biomarkers for clinical application in AD.

  To address this, we present a comprehensive and interpretable framework for analyzing AD using scRNA-seq data building upon our prior work11, where we developed an interpretable deep learning framework for understanding AD using bulk RNA-seq data. In our previous work, we extensively benchmarked several DL architectures and chose MLP for the classification component of the framework due to its superior performance for similar tasks as prior study demonstrated12. By using model interpretation method i.e. the Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values13 for feature extraction, we identified the most salient genes implicated for disease progression, and termed them as “index genes”, as they are the genes warranting in-depth downstream investigations for understanding of disease mechanisms and facilitating the discovery of novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Numerous prior studies14-16 have shown that the microglial module plays a major role in the development and progression of AD. In this work, we specifically focused on the microglia and identified microglial subsets that represent two extremes of the AD continuum: homeostatic (healthy) and activated (disease-associated) states. By isolating and studying these distinct populations, we aim to gain a deeper understanding of how microglial heterogeneity contributes to AD progression. We applied the same framework as it used for the bulk RNAseq data, by training the MLP model to separate the two states, then modeling the disease progression for the whole microglia dataset as pseudotemporal trajectory. We interpreted the model by the same model interpreting methods, then built gene regulatory networks (GRNs) based on the extracted features. By leveraging an integrated workflow, we identified novel transcription factors (TFs) and regulons implicated in AD pathogenesis and provided a holistic approach for studying AD etiology to facilitate target and biomarker discovery through the application of artificial intelligence. These TFs and their associated regulons also provide novel biological insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying AD and warrant further experimental investigation to evaluate their potential as biomarkers or therapeutic targets.


## MATERIALS AND METHODS

### Single cell RNA-seq data
  In this study, we utilized the single nuclei RNA-seq (snRNAseq) dataset17 from an extensively characterized cohort: the Religious Orders Study and Memory and Aging Project (ROSMAP)18, downloaded from synapse with accession ID: syn31512863. The details of raw snRNAseq data processing steps including raw data preprocessing, filtering, integration, clustering, and cell type annotation have been reported earlier in our previous work for investigation of single nuclei transcriptomics of multiple datasets19. In short, integration of all the nuclei was accomplished in multiple steps. Reference-based integration was employed for the integration of all subjects in Seurat20 in three batches. Reciprocal Principal Component Analysis (RPCA) was applied to find the anchors before integration by SCTransform21. The final Seurat object was integrated together from the three batches using the atomic sketch integration method20 by selecting and storing 50,000 representative cells (“atoms”) from each dataset in the sampling step. The processed dataset comprised 1,317,970 cell samples (nuclei) with 38,391 genes (features) from the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) region of 436 subjects. Of these, 56,352 nuclei were identified as microglia and thoroughly assessed in the original publication. Within this work, the microglia cluster was further clustered and annotated by using the FindMarker function from Seurat20 (v4.0) to identify the subset associated with homeostatic or activated (disease-associated microglia, or DAM) states. The marker genes used for annotating DAM are SPP1, APOE, LPL, and CTSB, and for homeostatic cluster are P2RY12, CX3CR1, and TMEM119. We also obtained the differentially expressed gene (DEG) list and their fold changes from an additional snRNAseq dataset of the same cohort of the same brain region, the MIT ROSMAP Single-Nucleus Multiomics Study22 (https://github.com/mathyslab7/ROSMAP_snRNAseq_PFC/).


### Phenotypical data
  All the clinical and pathological data for the ROSMAP cohort were obtained from the Rush Alzheimer’s Disease Center Research Resource Sharing Hub (https://www.radc.rush.edu/), upon approval of data-usage agreement. The detailed definitions of phenotypic measurements including clinical evaluation and postmortem neuropathological quantifications of the ROSMAP cohort are reported in our previous work11 together with their possible values, and the characterizations were summarized in Bennett et al.18

  For the Deep Neural Network (DNN) classification, we trained MLP models using neuropathologically confirmed AD subjects and normal controls (CN), the two termini of the AD spectrum to maximally differentiate the two groups. Data collected from ROSMAP including cogdx, braaksc and ceradsc were used to define the class label for AD, control (CN) and OTHER groups. Specifically, the AD group was defined by the nuclei from subjects meeting the criteria cogdx = 4, braaksc ≥4 and ceradsc ≤2. The CN group comprised the nuclei from subjects characterized by cogdx = 1, braaksc ≤3 and ceradsc ≥3. The OTHER category encompassed all remaining nuclei, including subjects of mild cognitive impairment, with mixed diagnoses with other dementias and comorbidities such as cardiovascular pathologies.

  To further capture the heterogeneity of the cell states, only cells within the activated state from the AD subjects and those within the homeostatic state from the CN subjects were included in the classification model. Total number of cells used in the training for each model, their diagnosis classifications and split by training/test sets and the model configurations are reported in Table 1.


### AI framework
  Our framework is designed to systematically investigate microglial diversity in AD through a series of interconnected steps: rigorous preprocessing tailored to scRNA-seq data, identification of disease-relevant microglial subpopulations, training of classification models to predict diagnostic status, and application of explainable ML techniques to uncover gene-level contributions to classification decision. In addition, we integrate gene network and pathway-based analyses to interpret the biological significance of our findings. The overall workflow of the proposed approach is shown in Figure 1.


### Imputation
  Imputation methods have been shown to improve downstream analyses such as clustering, cell-type classification, trajectory analysis, and GRN inference by mitigating sparsity and dropout effects in scRNA-seq data23. We conducted a comprehensive literature review to identify suitable strategies for handling dropout events in scRNA-seq data, a rapidly evolving field with a wide array of methodological approaches. We evaluated six different imputation approaches ranging across five categories of approaches: Network Based (NE24), Smoothing Based (MAGIC25), Model Based (scImpute26 and SAVER27), Data Reconstruction Based (ALRA28), and Deep learning-based (ScNTImpute29). To assess the effectiveness of each imputation approach in recovering the underlying cellular structure, we followed the similar method used in literature24. We first applied Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to reduce the dimensionality of the imputed gene expression matrices to the top 50 principal components. For ALRA, these 50 components captured 78.54% of the total variance in the Human Innate T cell dataset and 85.98% in the Hrvatin dataset. For the imputed matrices generated by the other methods, we also retained 50 principal components to ensure methodological consistency across approaches. Following dimensionality reduction, we performed unsupervised clustering using the Louvain community detection algorithm30, which identifies communities in a k-nearest neighbor graph constructed from the PCA-reduced data. This pipeline allowed us to evaluate how well each imputation method preserved or enhanced the biological heterogeneity of the original datasets. Due to ALRA’s superior performance and scalability, as well as its ability to distinguish between true biological and technical zeros, we selected ALRA as the preferred imputation method for downstream classification tasks.


### Deep learning
  The proposed deep learning framework consists of two major components, supervised classification (deep learning) and unsupervised dimensionality transformation. For the DNN classification, we trained MLP models using AD nuclei from the AD-related activated microglial state and CN nuclei from the homeostatic, non-activated state. The genes that exhibited zero expression across all nuclei were removed to avoid introducing noise from uninformative features. To ensure proper training and comparability, we performed feature-wise z-transformation on the gene expression values, recording the mean and standard deviation (SD) for each feature. Given that multiple nuclei originated from the same subject, we stratified the data into training and testing sets based on subject IDs rather than individual cell IDs, to prevent data leakage. More details about the total number of nuclei used in the training for each model, their diagnosis classifications and split by training/test sets, and the model configurations are discussed in Experiment and result section.

  After the MLP model was trained, the remaining nuclei (OTHER nuclei and cross-cluster AD/CN nuclei) were scaled using the mean and SD values from the AD-CN nuclei used in the training process and added back to the dataset. Eventually a manifold representation was generated using the unsupervised dimensionality transformation technique UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection)31. A forward pass through the previously trained neural networks was performed for all the nuclei in the whole cohort. We used the “umap.UMAP.fit_transform” function from the Python UMAP library to project the high-dimensional representations from the final MLP layer into a three-dimensional space for visualization.


### Model validation
  We derived an index, the Severity Index (SI), similarly as our previous work11, i.e. the pseudotime for staging the progression of AD from normal state in the homeostatic microglia (cluster_HM) to terminal disease in DAM (cluster_AM) based on the pseudotemporal trajectory in the UMAP embedding. SI can also be viewed as the pseudotime needed for traveling from the start of the trajectory (homeostatic state in control subjects) to the current position the cell is located at along the continuum. Pseudotime was derived for each cell first, by applying the method of inferring pseudotimes from the function ‘slingPseudotime’ as implemented in the R package Slingshot32. The average pseudotime for each subject in each cluster was then determined as SI for each subject within the cluster. To evaluate the models, SIs were then linearly correlated with all the AD clinical and pathological biomarkers individually in each cluster, in both datasets, including the covariates postmortem interval (PMI), apoe4 allele count, age at death, sex, and years of education, using a linear regression framework consistent with our previous work11.


### Model interpretation
  We utilized the SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)33 framework to interpret the MLP models, with the goal of identifying gene features that drive the classification outcomes. This approach builds upon our previous work11 with additional improvement. Instead of calculating SHAP values only for two diagnostic categories AD and CN, we extended the analysis to evaluate SHAP importance scores for each feature across all three categories: AD, CN, and OTHER. This analysis was performed separately within each of the two clusters (Cluster_AM and Cluster_HM). The detailed steps we followed for the SHAP calculation can be found in Algorithm 1 in the Supplementary Information.

  Eventually, we sorted the genes by their final SHAP value score difference between the two clusters used to train the model (i.e. SHAPAD_AM – SHAPCN_HM) to identify the most influential genes for the classification model while making predictions. The cutoff to extract genes making significant contributions to the classification was set as 1.5 * interquartile range (IQR) above the third quartile or below the first quartile of the distribution ( > q0.75 + 1.5 * IQR or < q0.25 − 1.5 * IQR, Tukey's fences34). These gene were eventually identified as the “index genes” in each dataset that explains AD vs control classification.


### Gene regulatory network and regulon Analysis
  Based on the extracted genes, we ran GRN analysis following the protocols of pySCIENC35 to infer GRNs and identify the key transcription factors (TFs) that drive cell state. Co-expression modules are inferred using a regression per-target approach by GRNBoost236. The indirect targets are pruned from these modules using cis- regulatory motif discovery by cisTarget37. Lastly, the activity of these regulons is quantified via an enrichment score for the regulon’s target genes by AUCell38. For comparison, the whole microglia data matrix before and after imputation was used respectively for inferring the GRNs and identifications of the regulons. Differentially expressed genes and their fold changes in an independent ROSMAP snRNAseq data were obtained from previously published data22 where each gene was assessed with their association of variables measuring AD pathology, cognition, or cognitive resilience, with locally multiple testing corrected P < 0.05. Functional and pathway enrichment analysis for each regulon was performed using Metascape39.


## RESULTS

### Imputation
  To choose the best method for imputation, we evaluated the performances of six different methods on the benchmark datasets Human Innate T cell dataset (2,005 cells × 13,260 genes) (GSE124731)40 and the Hrvatin dataset (65,539 cells × 19,155 genes) (GSE102827)41. The imputation performance was evaluated based on the cell clustering performance using several metrics (RI, ARI, MI, NMI, AMI, FMI)42, with the results reported in Table 2. More details about the evaluation metrics for the clustering task are given in Evaluation Metrics section in supplementary material. While all methods successfully completed imputation on the Human T cell dataset, NE, scImpute, and ScNTImpute failed to process the Hrvatin dataset due to its substantially higher dimensionality. These failures reflect the limited scalability and computational inefficiency of the respective methods when applied to large and complex datasets. Among the methods tested, ALRA consistently achieved the best performance across all evaluation metrics on both datasets. Based on this, ALRA was selected as the imputation method for all subsequent analyses.


### Classification
  The microglia subset consisted of 56,352 samples (nuclei) and 34,438 features (genes). Using the unsupervised clustering methodology, two major subpopulations of microglia were identified: Cluster_AM (21,737 cells), which exhibited transcriptional signatures of activated microglia, and Cluster_HM (33,169 cells), which represented homeostatic microglia. Furthermore, a total of 561 nuclei from 18 subjects representing other neuropathologies and/or symptoms of dementia (i.e. not AD diagnosis) were removed from the analysis. To build a classification model, an MLP was trained using AD nuclei from Cluster_AM (4,591 cells) and CN nuclei from Cluster_HM (4,250 cells), which represent activated and homeostatic microglial states with the designated disease states respectively. The preprocessing steps including gene filtering and feature-wise z-transformation, subject-based train-test splitting were applied here prior to model training. After filtering the genes with zero expression, there were total 28,829 genes that were used to train the model. The MLP architecture consisted of 32x16x1, with a dropout rate of 0.3 applied after each hidden layer. The details about the number of AD and CN subjects, number of AD and CN nuclei in training and testing as well as the classification result on the test data are shown in Table 1. In the final trained model, we achieved superior results for all the metrics (accuracy = 90.4%, sensitivity = 92.3%, and specificity = 88.8%, with area under the curve (AUC) = 96.2), demonstrating the classification model’s strong performance.


### Model the whole disease spectrum as a pseudotemporal trajectory
  AD is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder, where different nuclei even within the same diagnostic group may reflect varying stages along the disease spectrum. Given the high dimensionality of single-cell RNA sequencing data, direct interpretation of this progression from raw gene expression profiles is challenging. Here, we have used UMAP to obtain the pseudotemporal trajectory.

  To better capture and visualize the progression continuum, we used the trained MLP models to project nuclei from both extremes, as well as intermediate states, into a shared low-dimensional representation. As previously described, the MLP model was trained on AD nuclei from Cluster_AM and CN nuclei from Cluster_HM. The remaining nuclei, CN and OTHER from Cluster_AM, and AD and OTHER from Cluster_HM were z-transformed using the mean and SD derived from the training nuclei (AD in Cluster_AM and CN in Cluster_HM).

  Before this normalization step, genes removed during the initial preprocessing of the training data were also excluded from the corresponding remaining nuclei to maintain consistency. These transformed nuclei were then passed through the trained MLP model, and the outputs from the final prediction layers were used to construct 3D UMAP embeddings. The resulting UMAP visualizations capture the continuum of disease states, enabling interpretation of microglial activation along a trajectory that reflects AD progression.

  The UMAP visualizations generated from the MLP model embeddings reveal a clear trajectory capturing the progression from CN to AD nuclei (Figure 2A). The model was trained using AD nuclei from Cluster_AM and CN nuclei from Cluster_HM. When projecting all remaining nuclei including CN, AD, and OTHER nuclei from both clusters into the learned embedding space, we observe that the CN nuclei from Cluster_AM and AD nuclei from Cluster_HM are located at opposite ends of the trajectory (dark blue and dark red respectively). Intermediate nuclei, such as OTHER and cross-cluster CN/AD, lie along the continuum where the spectrum transits from dark blue to dark red, spanning blue, cyan, green, yellow, and orange, suggesting smooth disease progression. These consistent observations indicate that the MLP model captures a biologically meaningful spectrum, potentially reflecting the underlying disease continuum from healthy to pathological states in microglial cell populations.

  When the progression along the trajectory was quantitatively summarized to each individual split by the clusters, we can see that the trajectory delineates disease progression accurately, with SIs correlating neuropathologies such as amyloid or neurofibril tangles in each cluster respectively and in combination (Figure 2A and C, Table 3). In general, the gene expression within homeostatic microglia resides within a relatively stable state, while the SI most significantly correlates with the neuropathologic measurements within the DAM cluster.


### Dysregulated gene networks and functional annotations
  Based on the interpretation of the MLP model, we identified 4,594 “index genes” contributing the microglia activation process in AD, many of which are implicated in the microglia-neuron crosstalk in neurodegeneration (Supplementary Table 1 and 2). From their expression profiles after imputation, we carried out gene regulatory network analysis and identified 11 TFs dysregulated in AD, with their targets reported in Table 4. We also compared the results obtained for the regulons using the raw data before imputation (Supplementary Table 3). Although there are more regulons identified (41) from the unimputed data, most of them (28) consists of less than 30 target genes, indicating a more diverse yet less stable regulatory landscape in the raw data. We report herein the three most prominent regulons (CREB3, DBP, and POLR2A) conserved both before and after imputation, with reasonably large size and high AUCell activity as high-confidence core regulators. The targets of these TFs are enriched in the functions terms (Figure 3) implicated in neurodegeneration and diseases, including neuron projection morphogenesis (GO:0048812), neuronal system (Reactome: R-HSA-112316), and pathways of neurodegeneration - multiple diseases (KEGG pathway: hsa05022) including Alzheimer’s disease (KEGG pathway: hsa05010), especially from the regulons regulated by CREB3 (Figure 3A, Supplementary Table 4), DBP (Figure 3B, Supplementary Table 5), and POLR2A (Figure 3C, Supplementary Table 6).

  We further illustrate the dynamic changes in GRNs using heatmaps of gene expression profiles derived from an independent snRNA-seq dataset generated from the same brain region and cohort in a previous study22. Because the DEG analysis was performed independently by the original study authors, the resulting expression patterns of these DEGs provide an orthogonal validation of our findings. We observed the regulated genes are significantly enriched (P < 2.2e-16) in the DEGs in microglia, with 45 out of the 145 genes for CREB3 regulon, and 64 out of the 175 genes for DBP regulon differentially expressed in at least one of the clinical or neuropathological traits (Figure 4A and B). These traits have been extensively characterized in the ROSMAP cohort and provide us valuable information to thoroughly assess how the regulons mediate clinical and neuropatholgies in AD spectrum. The vast majority of the DEGs are downregulated in microglia associated with higher neuropathology and worse cognitive function. CREB3 and DBP both function as transcription activators and were observed to be downregulated in microglia within the independent ROSMAP snRNA-seq data. Although this downregulation did not pass the adjusted P value cutoff (adj.P < 0.05) in the DEG analysis, the finding suggests these genes play a nuanced role in gene regulation that is not easily captured by conventional DEG analysis. Interestingly, we also identified a regulon associated with the DNA-directed RNA polymerase POLR2A, with a few of the target genes linked to sex chromosome (KDM5C, KDM5D, and NLGN4Y), besides the targets implicated in AD (i.e. GRIN2A, PSMB2, PSMC6, PSMD13, ATG13, NCSTN). POLR2A is trending upwards for the AD traits in the snRNAseq data, with one of its target genes, PTPRG significantly upregulated in microglia associated with all AD traits (Figure 4C). This corroborates the role of microglia subpopulation Mic_PTPRG in modifying neuronal connections in the brain during AD progression43, and implicates the transcription mechanism in sex dimorphism in AD44.


## DISCUSSION
  In this study, we presented a comprehensive and interpretable deep learning framework tailored to snRNA-seq data from the ROSMAP cohort, a well-characterized clinical datasets for AD. Building upon our previous work using bulk RNA-seq data, this study advances our understanding by enabling more granular insights at the cellular level, with a specific focus on microglial cells, which play a central role in neuroinflammation and AD pathology. Bulk RNA-seq studies have indicated that microglia undergo significant changes with aging, exhibiting transcriptomic profiles enriched for disease-related genes45-47. Gene expression profiles further suggest the presence of multiple states of microglial activation in neurodegenerative disease contexts, which may explain the complex and sometimes contradictory roles microglia play in AD pathogenesis48. Different activation states of microglia are thought to emerge over the course of AD, where homeostatic microglia contribute to maintaining brain health and protecting neurons, while activated microglia can mediate neuroinflammatory responses, potentially exacerbating disease pathology.

  Despite these insights, how distinct microglial subsets contribute to the onset and progression of AD remains incompletely understood. Therefore, it is critical to identify and characterize microglial subpopulations to elucidate their specific roles in AD pathophysiology49. We started our investigations by imputation of the dataset due to high sparsity of the data, which impeded some of the downstream analysis results such as GRN inference50. ALRA was chosen as the imputation method in our study after our extensive benchmark analysis across different imputation methods, as well as supported by literature23, which shows improvement for many of the downstream tasks for scRNAseq data analysis, which include clustering, classification, trajectory analysis, and GRN inference with SCIENIC. Through unsupervised clustering of microglial transcriptomes before imputation, we identified distinct subpopulations that represent extreme cell states - homeostatic and disease-associated (activated) microglia. By training MLP models on these refined subsets, rather than the entire microglial population, we achieved improved classification performance, demonstrating the utility of stratifying cells based on biologically meaningful heterogeneity. In our previous work11, we compared MLP with several machine learning and deep learning approaches such as Convolution Neural Network (CNN), Recurrent Neural Network (RNN), Support Vector Machine (SVM) and Random Forest models and concluded MLP provided strong performance for classification of bulk RNA sequence data. Based on these findings and literature report12, we selected MLP as the core predictive model for the current work. However, we do believe there is a need to identify the appropriate MLP configuration given different datasets, we thus conducted comprehensive experiments on different MLP configurations and achieved the desirable performance using the current architecture.

  For trajectory inference, rather than relying solely on traditional unsupervised pseudotime or trajectory methods which can be sensitive to noise, we adopted a supervised strategy. This approach was designed specifically to identify molecular signatures associated with the microglial activation process that is characteristic of neurodegenerative diseases such as AD. Our results further reveal consistent pseudotemporal trajectories within the whole dataset, reinforcing the notion of AD as a progressive continuum rather than a binary state, and highlighting the broader applicability of our framework in modeling complex neurodegenerative processes. Beyond achieving high classification performance (> 90% accuracy) in distinguishing the two training subclusters (activated_AD vs. homeostatic_CN), we observed a continuous, progressive trajectory when additional subclusters were projected onto the same UMAP space (Figure 2), and the metric derived from the progression (i.e. the severity index or SI) quantitively correlates with neuropathological and clinical traits (Table 3). This finding demonstrates that our model extends beyond simple classification. Instead, it captures the intrinsic, dynamic progression of neurodegenerative disease across the transcriptomic landscape in a nonlinear manner, highlighting its potential to support novel analytical approaches.

  A key strength of this study lies in the interpretability of our models. By leveraging SHAP, we provided both global (i.e. AD vs CN by group difference) and local (the absolute value within a specific state_diagnosis group) feature importance for the classification task (Supplementary Table 1), offering a transparent view into the gene-level drivers of AD pathology. We were able to identify novel TFs and regulons implicated in AD based on the extracted genes in our trained models. This not only enhances the biological credibility of the model but also aids in the discovery of candidate genes and molecular pathways relevant to disease onset and progression. We further demonstrate that the SHAP-derived gene set (index genes) can be used to construct gene networks that provide novel biological insights. Conventional DEG analysis relies on predefined statistical significance and fold-change thresholds, which may overlook subtle but biologically meaningful transcriptional changes. This limitation is particularly relevant for TFs and regulatory genes, whose modest expression changes can nevertheless have substantial downstream effects on cellular programs51. As a result, the index genes complement traditional DEG analyses and enable the identification of regulatory mechanisms that may otherwise remain undetected. We compared the index genes identified in this study with those from the microglia module reported in our previous publication11. Due to substantial differences in data modality (bulk vs. single-cell), preprocessing methods (cqn vs. Seurat), and analytical focus (whole tissue vs. microglia-specific clusters), the overlap between the two gene sets was limited. This suggests that gene regulatory modules derived from snRNA-seq data are not readily recapitulated in bulk tissue analyses, which primarily reflect signals from dominant cell populations at a global level. In contrast, snRNA-seq enables the detection of cell-type-specific and potentially transient regulatory mechanisms within individual cell populations.

  Our observation from the regulon comparison derived before and after imputation indicates that imputation led to a marked compression of regulon activity distributions, with most TFs showing reduced mean AUCell scores, while a small subset of dominant regulators (e.g., DBP and CREB3) retained high activity. This was accompanied by loss of intermediate regulatory programs and emergence of a simplified regulatory landscape after imputation (Supplementary Table 3). Leveraging raw data led to diverse regulatory module discovery, while in the imputed data, we validate the findings from raw data and emphasize dominant programs reported herein.

  From the GRN analysis, we identified the TF CREB3 and its regulon as a key regulator in AD. The broader CREB family is essential for learning and long-term memory formation in areas like the hippocampus. Dysfunction of CREB signaling is well-documented in other neurodegenerative conditions and cognitive deficits52. CREB3 proteins are located in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and are involved in the unfolded protein response (UPR), a cellular stress response pathway. CREB3 transcription factors act as signaling hubs that maintain Golgi homeostasis, controlling a suite of essential cellular processes like secretion, protein modification, and trafficking that ultimately regulate membrane composition and expansion. Their activity is especially crucial in the central nervous system (CNS), where the vulnerability of neurons to Golgi stress and fragmentation is a major factor in neurodegenerative diseases53 including AD. In contrast to its potential negative role in AD pathogenesis, recent research suggests that a gain-of-function variant of the CREB3 gene may be protective against amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS)54. This highlights the distinct and complex roles of different CREB3 family members in different neurodegenerative diseases. Understanding the TF and its gene networks will therefore provide novel strategies for the development of effective therapeutics for AD and other neurological disorders.

  We also identified DBP and its regulon at the intersection of circadian biology and AD pathology. DBP plays a role in governing daily gene expression rhythms in the brain and has newly appreciated functions in regulating inflammatory responses55. Evidence from transcriptomic studies indicates that DBP and related clock genes are altered in AD brains, generally trending downward or losing rhythmicity as disease progresses56. Network analyses further connect DBP to key AD pathways (such as APOE regulation and immune gene networks)57. Diminished DBP may contribute to circadian desynchrony in AD, with lots of synaptic signaling genes such as GRIK2, GRIA2, and GRIA4 significantly downregulated by its diminished expression. While DBP is not a traditional AD risk gene, these findings suggest it is a context-dependent modulator of disease processes, potentially influencing when and how strongly certain pathways (immune, metabolic, synaptic) are active in the AD brain.

  The POLR2A regulon was found to contain several critical target genes including genes previously implicated in AD (GRIN2A, PSMB2, PSMC6, PSMD13, ATG13, and NCSTN) as well as genes located on the sex chromosomes KDM5C, KDM5D, and NLGN4Y. Given the known sex differences in AD prevalence and the enrichment of female AD cases in many clinical cohorts, including those analyzed in this study, we cannot exclude the possibility that the identification of KDM5C, KDM5D, and NLGN4Y was influenced in part by sex imbalance within the pooled dataset. At the same time, several observations suggest that these findings may reflect biologically meaningful signals rather than solely a sampling artifact. First, KDM5C and KDM5D are gametologs that have retained functional copies on both sex chromosomes and have been implicated in sex-specific gene regulation. Second, all three genes KDM5C58, KDM5D59, and NLGN4Y60 have been independently associated with AD in previous studies. Third, commonly used sex-marker genes such as XIST and RPS4Y1 were not identified among the index genes thus excluded from the regulon, suggesting that the model is not simply capturing global sex differences in sampling bias. These genes warrant further investigation through sex-stratified analyses and independent validation studies as candidate contributors to sex-related heterogeneity in AD.

  Molecular changes in AD-affected brains are known to be both specific and regional. The findings present within this work reveal several novel regulatory networks present in the DLPFC from a relatively homogenous white population. It is yet to be seen if the same regulatory mechanisms persist in other brain regions affected earlier by AD neuropathology such as entorhinal cortex. We anticipate our analysis framework could be applied when more high-quality molecular data are profiled for different brain regions to help address the question. With the development of model-based experimental systems such as human cell-based iPSC or organoid and animal models, the reported regulatory mechanisms warrant in-depth investigation, such as perturbations of the candidate TFs for downstream effects to facilitate the pursuit of early diagnosis tools and/or effective treatments of AD.

  Our study has several limitations that present opportunities for future refinement. The proposed deep learning framework captures a continuous pathological gradient and prioritizes microglial regulators, but the present analysis is based on DLPFC snRNA seq data from the ROSMAP cohort. Evaluation in independent cohorts, such as those from SEA-AD61 and other AD cohorts with comparable transcriptomic profiles can help in assessing the robustness and generalizability of the identified severity associated signatures. Such validation will require careful harmonization because available datasets often differ in brain region, cohort composition, sample processing, sequencing protocol, and preprocessing strategy. Applying this framework to independent AD cohorts with comparable single nucleus transcriptomic profiles would help determine whether the identified microglial regulatory programs are reproducible across datasets and would strengthen their biological interpretation. Future applications to cell types other than microglia will also be important to determine whether the proposed framework generalizes beyond microglia and can identify cell type specific regulatory programs across broader AD related cellular contexts. Future studies may also use additional trajectory inference or RNA velocity-based analyses to further examine the disease associated continuum captured by the Severity Index and refine the understanding of microglial state transitions during AD progression. Finally, CREB3, DBP, and POLR2A were consistently identified before and after imputation and were associated with clinical traits and relevant biological pathways. These findings support their prioritization as candidate regulators of microglial activation in AD. However, because these regulons were inferred computationally, future studies using alternative GRN inference methods and experimental validation approaches, such as RNAscope would further strengthen confidence in the inferred regulatory interactions. Together, these future directions will help determine the reproducibility, biological validity, and broader applicability of the proposed framework.

  In summary, our interpretable deep learning approach effectively captures microglial heterogeneity in scRNA-seq data and yields robust predictive models. We identified novel gene regulatory networks implicated in AD that otherwise undetectable from conventional analysis methods, which warrants in-depth study of their roles in AD, specifically in microglia activation. These findings underscore the potential of combining single-cell resolution with interpretable machine learning to uncover meaningful patterns in complex diseases like AD and pave the way for future applications in other neurodegenerative disorders. The current work is an extension of our previous publication11 aimed at exploring its applicability to single-cell RNA-seq data to better resolve gene regulatory networks at single-cell resolution. Its current application represents an initial proof of concept demonstrating its potential utility. As presented in the paper, single-cell data introduces additional challenges that require careful preprocessing, including customized batch correction and imputation approaches. As advances continue in the analytic methods and tools for raw data processing, artificial intelligence, and network biology and the expansion of transcriptomic and multi-omics datasets, we anticipate further refinement of the framework to achieve improved performance and broader applicability.
