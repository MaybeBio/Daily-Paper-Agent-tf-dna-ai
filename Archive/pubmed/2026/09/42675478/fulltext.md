# AI-guided design of plectin-1-targeted 68Ga-radiotracers reveals EP300-mediated membrane relocalization of plectin-1 in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma


## Background
  Plectin-1 (PLEC) is a membrane-associated biomarker implicated in the progression of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) and is an attractive target for molecular imaging. However, peptide-based radiotracers targeting PLEC remain limited, and the mechanisms linking plectin-1 relocalization to aggressive biology have yet to be fully elucidated.


## Methods
  In the present study, we performed stepwise in silico screening to identify membrane-associated metastatic drivers in PDAC. Then, we performed convolutional neural network-assisted docking to guide the design of 68Ga-labeled NOTA-conjugated plectin-1-targeted monomeric (FZPN) and dimeric (FZPN-dimer) radiotracers. Radiochemical characterization, surface plasmon resonance, cellular uptake, blocking, pharmacokinetic, microPET/CT, biodistribution, and preliminary toxicity studies were then undertaken in PANC-1 models. Mechanistic studies assessed the subcellular localization of PLEC, its interaction with EP300, acetylation, and the functional role of lysine 1310 (K1310).


## Results
  PLEC emerged as the top membrane-enriched, metastasis-linked, and prognostically adverse candidate for PDAC. Analysis demonstrated that the [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer exhibited high affinity for PLEC, favorable hydrophilicity and stability, and significantly higher cellular uptake and tumor accumulation than the monomeric tracer, with receptor-specific blockade in vitro and in vivo. In PANC-1 xenografts, tumor uptake of the dimer reached 3.8 ± 0.5%ID/g at 30 min after injection and remained higher than that of the monomer at all imaging time points. Mechanistically, PDAC tissues exhibited membrane-enriched PLEC; EP300 interacted with PLEC, acetylation was increased in PDAC cells, and the deacetylation-mimetic PLEC-K1310R mutant redirected plectin-1 away from the plasma membrane and reduced cell migration.


## Conclusions
  Collectively, our findings show that [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer represents a promising PLEC-targeted PET radiotracer for molecular imaging in PDAC. EP300-mediated acetylation at K1310 appears to drive the membrane relocalization of PLEC and promote the migration of tumor cells, providing biological support for PLEC-targeted imaging and a rationale for further translational development.

## Background
  Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is one of the most aggressive malignancies, characterized by rapid local invasion, early metastasis, and extremely poor clinical outcomes despite advances in systemic therapy [1–3]. In addition to oncogenic driver mutations, an accumulating body of evidence indicates that the aberrant regulation of protein subcellular localization and membrane-associated signaling networks plays a critical role in the progression, invasion, and metastatic dissemination of PDAC [4, 5]. Molecular imaging has emerged as an important strategy for non-invasively characterizing these biological processes. Established PDAC imaging targets, including integrins and fibroblast activation protein (FAP), have demonstrated great value in assessing angiogenesis, cell-matrix interactions, and stromal remodeling. However, these targets primarily reflect the tumor microenvironment rather than the malignant epithelial compartment itself. In contrast, plectin-1 (PLEC), a cytoskeletal linker protein, has emerged as a promising tumor cell-associated biomarker for cancer detection, diagnosis, and targeted therapy across multiple malignancies, including pancreatic, prostate, and ovarian cancers [6–9]. Notably, Shin et al. were the first to report that PLEC undergoes aberrant cell-surface localization in PDAC cells while exhibiting minimal levels of expression in normal pancreatic tissue and pancreatitis [10]. This unique expression pattern distinguishes PLEC from conventional stromal imaging targets and makes it an attractive candidate for tumor-specific molecular imaging. PLEC-targeted imaging may therefore provide a complementary biological readout for tumor cell membrane remodeling, invasive behavior, and metastatic potential, while also offering translational opportunities for peptide-based molecular imaging and theragnostic development. Furthermore, elucidating the biological functions of PLEC during the progression of PDAC may facilitate the development of novel imaging biomarkers and targeted therapeutic strategies.

  Current PLEC-targeted imaging probes are predominantly based on fluorescent probes or macromolecular carriers [11–13]. In contrast, radiolabeled peptides offer several advantages for molecular imaging, including high sensitivity, quantitative whole-body imaging, excellent tissue penetration, rapid blood clearance, and low background activity, rendering them particularly suitable for positron emission tomography (PET) [14, 15]. These favorable pharmacokinetic properties have established peptide-based radiotracers as an attractive platform for translational molecular imaging and theragnostic applications. However, despite these advantages, small-molecule or peptide-based plectin-1-targeted radiopharmaceuticals labeled with clinically relevant positron emitters, such as gallium-68, remain limited [16]. Furthermore, existing PLEC-targeted probes rely on empirically driven structural modifications, with quantitative analysis of ligand-receptor binding modes and artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted design remaining unexplored. This significantly increases the cost and uncertainty of research and development. In the present study, we used an integrated convolutional neural network (CNN) scoring function to perform an in-depth analysis of the binding conformation between the plectin-targeting peptide (PTP) (sequence: KTLLPTP) and PLEC. Based on this analysis, we developed 68Ga-labeled and NOTA-conjugated plectin-1-targeted radiotracers in monomeric (FZPN) and dimeric (FZPN-dimer) formats. These probes were designed to systematically evaluate the impact of bivalent interactions on PLEC-targeting efficiency and in vivo imaging performance, thereby offering a feasible approach for the AI-driven development of PLEC radiotracers.

  The mechanisms underlying PDAC invasion and metastasis are complex and involve dynamic interactions between tumor cells and the surrounding microenvironment [17, 18]. Proteins localized at the plasma membrane play critical roles in these processes by mediating cell adhesion, migration, signal transduction, and interactions with the immune system [19]. Of these molecules, PLEC has been implicated in promoting the proliferation, growth, invasion, and metastatic dissemination of tumor cells [20, 21]. Previous studies have shown that the expression of PLEC is positively correlated with the malignant potential of pancreatic cancer. Bausch et al. reported that higher levels of PLEC are associated with increased tumor aggressiveness in PDAC [22]. In addition, Shin et al. demonstrated that PLEC regulates the migration and invasion of tumor cells and that the co-incubation of PLEC-negative PDAC cells with PLEC-enriched exosomes significantly enhanced their migratory and invasive capacities [10]. Consistently, the genetic depletion of PLEC markedly suppressed tumor growth in orthotopic PDAC mouse models in immunocompetent and immunodeficient settings. Despite these findings, the mechanisms by which PLEC is regulated in PDAC and how it exerts its pro-metastatic functions remain poorly understood. In particular, whether the subcellular distribution of PLEC undergoes dynamic remodeling during malignant progression and the molecular mechanisms governing this process have yet to be fully elucidated.

  PLEC primarily localizes to the cytoplasmic cytoskeletal network and the inner side of membrane-associated adhesion structures [23]. However, in PDAC, PLEC can undergo aberrant subcellular relocalization from intracellular or sub-membranous compartments to the tumor cell surface, thereby creating an accessible target for molecular imaging. We observed that PLEC progressively shifted from a diffuse intracellular pattern toward membrane enrichment as tumor grade increased in PDAC. Given that PLEC overexpression and abnormal cell-surface localization have been linked to PDAC migration, invasion, and malignant progression, this form of membrane relocalization is unlikely to be merely a passive consequence of increased expression but rather may represent an actively regulated pathological process. Since intracellular protein membrane remodeling can be regulated by post-translational modifications, lipid signal-dependent recruitment, or vesicular trafficking, we hypothesized that the post-translational modification of PLEC may also drive its redistribution from the cytoplasm to the plasma membrane in PDAC cells. Elucidating this mechanism may explain how elevated levels of PLEC promote the invasion and metastasis of PDAC and provide a mechanistic basis for PLEC-targeted molecular imaging.

  In this study, we developed novel PLEC-targeted molecular imaging probes and systematically evaluated their structural optimization and imaging performance. In addition, we investigated dynamic changes in the subcellular localization of PLEC during tumor progression and explored the molecular mechanisms underlying the role of PLEC in tumor invasion and metastasis. Collectively, this work provides experimental evidence and mechanistic insights supporting the development of PLEC-targeted diagnostic strategies and their potential clinical translation (Fig. 1).

## Methods

### Pathway analysis
  Section 1 of the Supplementary Materials and Methods provides full experimental details of membrane-related genes identified through pathway analysis.


### Materials, instrumentation and precursor synthesis
  The PLEC-targeting radiotracers used in this study include the monomer FZPN and the dimer FZPN-dimer. Detailed information relating to the chemical reagents, instrumentation, and precursor synthesis is provided in the Supplementary Materials and Methods.


### Synthesis and radiolabeling
  The precursor solution (1.0 mM) was prepared in advance. For radiolabeling, 20 µL of the precursor solution was mixed with 80 µL of sodium acetate buffer (1.5 M), followed by the addition of 800 µL of 68GaCl₃ eluate (74–111 MBq, corresponding to 2–3 mCi). The reaction mixture was adjusted to pH 3.8–4.2 and incubated at 95℃ for 10 min. After completion of the reaction, an aliquot of the reaction mixture was analyzed by radio instant thin-layer chromatography (radio-TLC) to determine radiochemical purity. Radio-TLC was performed using ammonium acetate/methanol (1:1, v/v) as the mobile phase.


### Molecular docking studies
  The crystal structure of human PLEC used for molecular docking was downloaded from the RCSB Protein Data Bank. Specifically, we used PDB ID: 2ODU, chain A, which represents an experimentally resolved X-ray crystal structure of an N-terminal fragment of the plakin domain of human PLEC. This PDB entry is annotated as a PLEC fragment comprising residues 300–530, and the prepared receptor coordinates used for docking contained chain A residues (approximately 303–520). Therefore, docking analysis was performed against this defined plakin-domain fragment of PLEC, rather than against full-length PLEC or a full-length model predicted by AlphaFold.

  During receptor preparation, water molecules were removed, and hydrogen atoms were added. The ligand structures PTP and NOTA-FZPN were converted into SDF format. The docking grid center was set at x = 36.5, y = 18.0, z = 11.0 Å in the coordinate system of the 2ODU crystal structure. Molecular docking was performed individually with CNN to simulate the interaction between the PLEC protein and the ligands. Rescore was selected as the CNN Scoring Mode. The docking results were analyzed by PyMOL (version 2.4.0, Schrödinger LLC, USA), focusing on the three-dimensional binding modes and binding kinetics between PLEC and each ligand.


### In vitro experiments
  The hydrophilicity of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-Dimer was determined by measuring the partition coefficients (log P and log D7.4) in ultrapure water or phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4)/n-octanol (1:1, v/v) systems. Approximately 37 kBq of the radiotracer was added to 2 mL of the biphasic mixture and vortex-mixed for 5 min, followed by centrifugation at 15,000 × g for 5 min to facilitate phase separation. Aliquots (100 µL) from each phase were then collected, and radioactivity was quantified using a γ-counter. All measurements were performed in quintuplicate.

  The in vitro stability of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer was evaluated by incubating approximately 37 kBq of the radiotracer in physiological salineat 37 °C. At predetermined time points (30, 60, 120, and 180 min), aliquots were collected for radio-HPLC (high-performance liquid chromatography) analysis. The resulting supernatant was collected and analyzed by radio-HPLC. All experiments were repeated five times.

  Cellular uptake studies were performed using PANC-1 cells. In brief, cells (5 × 104 per well) were seeded into 24-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. The culture medium was then replaced with 1 mL of serum-free medium, followed by incubation with approximately 37 kBq of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN monomer or dimer at 37℃ for the indicated time (n = 5 per group). After incubation, the cells were washed with PBS and lysed with 1.0 M NaOH. Cell-associated radioactivity was subsequently measured using a γ-counter. To evaluate binding specificity, blocking experiments were performed in parallel by co-incubating cells with 5 µg of unlabeled Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer (molecular weight: 2781 Da) dissolved in 200 µL of PBS (final concentration: 9.21 µM) for 60 min prior to measurement.


### In vivo experiments
  Pharmacokinetic studies were performed in male Kunming (KM) mice (4–6 weeks old, 18–20 g; n = 5 per group) to evaluate the blood clearance of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer. Each mouse was intravenously injected via the tail vein with 3.7 MBq of the radiotracer (approximately 120 pmol). Blood samples were collected from the tail vein into pre-weighed capillary tubes at 1, 3, 5, 10, 15, 30, 60, and 90 min after injection. The radioactivity of each sample was measured using a γ-counter, and the activity concentration was expressed as percentage of the injected dose per gram of blood (%ID/g). Pharmacokinetic parameters were calculated using DAS 2.0 software.

  The in vivo stability of the radiotracer was evaluated in mice (n = 5). Each mouse received an intravenous injection of 3.7 MBq of the radiotracer in 200 µL of saline. Urine samples were collected at 30 min after injection, diluted with water, and passed through a 0.22 μm membrane filter. The samples were subsequently analyzed by radio-HPLC.


### Micro-PET/CT imaging
  Micro-positron emission tomography/computed tomography (microPET/CT) imaging studies were performed in BALB/c nude mice bearing PANC-1 xenograft tumors. Mice were intravenously injected via the tail vein with 3.7 MBq of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN or [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer (200 µL saline; n = 3 per group). All imaging studies were performed using an Inveon microPET/CT scanner (Siemens Medical Solutions, Knoxville, TN, USA). Mice underwent a 5-min CT scan followed by a 10-min static PET acquisition at 30, 60, and 90 min after tracer injection. Mice were anesthetized with inhaled isoflurane approximately 5 min before each imaging session. To assess tumor-specific uptake, blocking studies were performed by co-injecting the radiotracer with 200 µg of the corresponding unlabeled Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer (approximately a 400-fold molar excess), and micro-PET/CT images were acquired at 30 and 60 min after injection.


### Biodistribution studies
  Biodistribution studies were conducted in PANC-1 tumor-bearing mice. Animals were intravenously injected via the tail vein with 3.7 MBq of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer (200 µL saline; n = 3 per group). At 30, 60, and 90 min after injection, mice were euthanized and major organs and tissues, including blood, heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, stomach, intestines, bladder, muscle, bone, brain, thyroid and tumor, were harvested and weighed. The radioactivity of each sample was measured using a γ-counter. The measured counts were decay-corrected to the time of injection, and tissue uptake was expressed as %ID/g. Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0; GraphPad Software). Detailed protocols for immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry staining analyses in PANC-1 tumors are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

  BALB/c nude mice were randomly divided into two groups (n = 3 per group) and intravenously injected via the tail vein with either saline or 3.7 MBq of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer in a total volume of 100 µL. Animals were monitored for 14 days in terms of their general condition and vital signs. At the end of the observation period, blood samples were collected via cardiac puncture. A portion of the blood was transferred into EDTA (ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid)-anticoagulated tubes for complete blood count analysis. The remaining samples were collected in non-anticoagulant tubes, allowed to clot at room temperature, and centrifuged to obtain serum for biochemical analysis, including blood urea nitrogen (BUN), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and uric acid (UA) levels. Following euthanasia, major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys, were harvested, fixed, and processed for paraffin embedding. Tissue sections were then prepared and stained with hematoxylin and eosin for histopathological evaluation.


### Mechanisms and functional roles of PLEC subcellular localization
  Detailed experimental procedures for investigating the mechanisms and functional roles of PLEC subcellular localization are provided in Sect.  4 of the Supplementary Materials and Methods.


### Image and statistical analysis
  PET images were reconstructed and quantitatively analyzed using Inveon Research Workplace (Siemens Medical Solutions, Knoxville, TN, USA). Regions of interest (ROIs) in tumors were manually delineated on fused PET/CT images. The concentration of radioactivity within each ROI was expressed as MBq/mL and converted to the percentage of injected dose per gram of tissue (%ID/g) by normalizing to the injected radioactivity, assuming a tissue density of 1 g/mL. All imaging parameters were kept constant within each experiment. For IF, ≥ 100 cells per condition from more than three independent experiments were analyzed. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were carried out with a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. Comparisons between multiple groups (where applicable) were carried out with one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test. Normality was assessed by the Shapiro-Wilk test. Exact n, statistical tests, and p values are reported in the figure legends and p < 0.05 was considered significant.


## Results

### Membrane-associated drivers linked to metastasis and outcome in PDAC
  Given that the primary objective of this study was to identify a clinically translatable molecular imaging target for PDAC, we first adopted a biology-guided candidate selection strategy rather than directly applying machine learning to all genome-wide transcriptomic features. Four complementary gene sets were integrated to define candidates with biological, clinical, and imaging relevance: differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were used to ensure tumor specificity; survival-associated genes were included to capture potential prognostic value; metastasis-related genes were incorporated to enrich for markers linked to aggressive tumor behavior; and membrane-associated genes were prioritized because molecular imaging probes require targets that are potentially accessible at or near the tumor cell surface. This intersection strategy was designed to generate a focused and biologically interpretable candidate pool while reducing irrelevant transcriptomic features and limiting overfitting risk in downstream modeling.

  Using this filtering framework, six candidate genes were identified: PLEC, AXL, MET, MSLN, CEACAM6 and MUC1 (Fig. 2A). Random forest and LASSO regression were then applied to the expression profiles of these six pre-selected candidates as complementary prioritization approaches within this restricted feature space. In the LASSO model, feature selection was performed according to the optimal penalty parameter, and PLEC was retained among the selected variables (Fig. 2B). Random forest analysis further ranked PLEC as the most important candidate based on Mean Decrease Accuracy and Mean Decrease Gini metrics (Fig. 2C). The overlap between the LASSO- and random forest-selected candidates identified PLEC as the common prioritized marker (Fig. 2D). ROC (receiver operating characteristic) analysis showed that PLEC expression exhibited favorable discriminatory performance for PDAC, with an AUC (area under the curve) of 0.83 (Fig. 2E). We next evaluated the prognostic relevance of PLEC in two independent cohorts. In the Cancer Genome Atlas pancreatic adenocarcinoma (TCGA-PAAD) cohort, patients with high levels of PLEC expression showed a trend toward poorer overall survival compared with those with low levels of PLEC expression (HR = 1.50, Cox p = 0.050; log-rank p = 0.050; n = 89 per group) (Fig. 2F). This association was further supported in the GSE32676 cohort, in which high PLEC expression was associated with worse overall survival (HR = 1.57, Cox p = 0.048; log-rank p = 0.045; n = 31 in the high-expression group and n = 32 in the low-expression group) (Fig. 2F). Collectively, these results suggest that PLEC expression may have potential diagnostic and prognostic relevance in PDAC, and robustly nominate PLEC as a membrane-enriched, metastasis-linked, and prognostically adverse gene in PDAC.

### Molecular docking studies
  Given the current lack of reported structural analyses of PLEC interactions with peptides or probes, we next performed molecular simulations of the PTP and PLEC domains using an integrated three-dimensional (3D) CNN scoring function (Fig. 3E). CNN scoring played a pivotal role in screening candidate conformations for binding sites close to the intrinsic state: the preferred docking conformation stably embedded within the charge-complementary groove of PLEC (Fig. 3A), resolving hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions with key amino acid residues such as Glutamic acid 355, Glutamine 347, and Glutamic acid 340 (Fig. 3C and D). This provided a reliable structural template for subsequent probe optimization.

  Building upon this, we constructed NOTA-FZPN (Fig. 3F) by introducing the NOTA chelator via a connecting arm to the PTP-exposed end while preserving the PTP-recognized pharmacophore. CNN docking results revealed that this monomer probe maintained a binding orientation and interaction network (Glutamine 347 and Glutamic acid 340) that was highly consistent with PTP within the PLEC binding pocket, whereas NOTA and the linker arm caused minimal interference at the protein-peptide interface (Fig. 3B). Building upon this CNN-refined binding model, we further designed dimeric PLEC probe skeletons (Fig. 3G). These achieved bivalent binding by spatially ordering 2–4 PTP units around a common radiolabeled metal chelation core, laying the structural foundation for subsequent in vivo and in vitro evaluations of 68Ga-labeled plectin-1-targeted radiotracers.


### Radiolabeling and stability
  The chemical structures and mass spectra of precursors are summarized in Supplementary Methods (Supplemental Fig. S1-S2). The radiochemical purity of the labeled compounds was assessed by radio-TLC, and all radiotracers showed purities > 95%; thus, no further purification was required. The retention time of the labeled compound was consistent with that of the corresponding unlabeled peptide determined by UV-HPLC under identical chromatographic conditions (9.18 min for NOTA-FZPN-dimer precursor vs. 9.08 min for the [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer; Fig. 4A and B, Supplemental Fig. S3-S5), thus confirming successful radiolabeling without detectable alteration of the molecular structure. Binding affinity to the PLEC protein was further evaluated by surface plasmon resonance, which demonstrated the high affinity of the NOTA-FZPN-dimer with a dissociation constant (KD) of 1.074 nM (Fig. 4C).

(C) Binding affinity to the PLEC protein of NOTA-FZPN-dimer. (D) In vitro stability of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer. (E) In vivo stability of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer. (F) Partition coefficients of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer

  In vitro stability studies further demonstrated that [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer remained highly stable, with a radiochemical purity exceeding 95% after incubation in physiological saline for 180 min (Fig. 4D). In addition, the radiochemical purity of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer remained > 97% at 30 min after injection into mice (Fig. 4E). The octanol-water partition coefficients of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer were log P = − 2.85 ± 0.41 and log D7.4 = − 2.32 ± 0.14, indicating high hydrophilicity (Fig. 4F). Collectively, these findings demonstrate the favorable in vitro and in vivo stability of the radiotracer and support its further evaluation in preclinical studies.


### Cellular uptake and receptor specificity
  In vitro cellular uptake studies demonstrated that [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer was associated with a significantly higher uptake in PANC-1 cells at 1 h compared with [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN (2.26 ± 0.56% vs. 0.84 ± 0.28%; p < 0.05; Supplemental Fig. S7). Blocking experiments further revealed a marked reduction in tracer uptake following co-incubation with a 500-fold excess of the corresponding unlabeled ligand, with the 1-h uptake decreasing to 1.05 ± 0.26% and 0.44 ± 0.19%, respectively (Supplemental Fig. S7-S8). These findings further support the PLEC-specific binding of both radiotracers.


### Imaging, biodistribution studies, and safety
  Pharmacokinetic analysis revealed that [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer exhibited rapid initial distribution, with distribution and elimination half-lives of T1/2α = 0.265 min and T1/2 β = 19.951 min; furthermore, more than 90% of the injected radioactivity was cleared from circulation within 60 min (Fig. S9).

  To evaluate the in vivo imaging performance of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer, microPET/CT imaging was performed in BALB/c nude mice bearing PANC-1 xenograft tumors. As shown in Fig. 5A, [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer exhibited rapid tumor uptake within 30 min, reaching 3.8 ± 0.5%ID/g and providing clear tumor-to-background contrast. Tumor uptake gradually decreased to 3.0 ± 0.4%ID/g by 60 min and 1.7 ± 0.4%ID/g by 90 min after injection. Physiological uptake was also observed in several non-target organs, including the kidneys, heart, and liver, with radioactivity levels decreasing over time. In blocking studies, the co-injection of an excess of unlabeled ligand significantly reduced tumor uptake at 60 min from 3.0 ± 0.4%ID/g to 0.90 ± 0.17%ID/g (Fig. 5B), thus supporting receptor-specific binding. For comparison, [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN also exhibited rapid tumor uptake, with values of 1.8 ± 1.1%ID/g, 1.1 ± 0.9%ID/g, and 0.5 ± 0.4%ID/g at 30, 60, and 90 min, respectively (Fig. 5C). At all time points, the tumor uptake of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer was markedly higher than that of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN (Fig. 5D-E), indicating the superior tumor-targeting performance of the dimeric tracer, consistent with the in vitro cellular uptake results.

  To further evaluate the in vivo tumor-targeting capability of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer, ex vivo biodistribution studies were performed in PANC-1 tumor-bearing mice. At 30 min after injection, the tumor uptake of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer reached 3.601 ± 0.85%ID/g. Tumor uptake gradually decreased over time, with values of 2.471 ± 0.77%ID/g at 60 min and 1.774 ± 0.63%ID/g at 90 min after injection (Fig. 5F). Both radiotracers showed the highest accumulation in the kidneys, consistent with the PET imaging results, indicating that renal clearance was the primary excretion pathway. Uptake in other non-target organs, including the liver, heart, stomach, and intestines, remained relatively low at all of the examined time points. Overall, [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer exhibited favorable pharmacokinetic properties with rapid systemic clearance. Although physiological liver uptake was also observed, tumor uptake remained higher than normal liver background throughout the imaging period, thus providing favorable tumor-to-liver contrast for PDAC imaging. These findings confirm the effective and specific targeting of PLEC-expressing tumors by [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer in vivo. Immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry analyses further confirmed high PLEC expression in PANC-1 tumor tissues ( Supplemental Fig. S10).

  Toxicity evaluation of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer in BALB/c nude mice failed to identify any significant differences in hematological parameters when compared between the treated and control groups (Supplemental Fig. S11A-B). Furthermore, histopathological examination of hematoxylin and eosin stained (H&E)-sections from major organs failed to detect any abnormalities (Supplemental Fig. S11C). These findings suggest that [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer was tolerated well tolerated in mice at the tested dose.


### EP300-dependent acetylation of PLEC at K1310 drove membrane localization and cell migration
  To determine whether PLEC undergoes subcellular relocalization in PDAC, we first performed immunohistochemistry on paired tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissues. In non-tumor pancreatic tissue, PLEC exhibited a broad distribution across the nucleus, cytoplasm, and plasma membrane. In contrast, PDAC tissues exhibited a prominent membrane-enriched PLEC signal (Fig. 6A), suggesting disease-associated spatial remodeling. To identify upstream regulators potentially responsible for this shift, we subjected immunoprecipitated PLEC to mass spectrometry. The interactome was enriched for cytoskeletal/adhesion proteins and notably, the histone/lysine acetyltransferase EP300 (p300) (Fig. 6B). Reciprocal co-immunoprecipitation in PANC-1 and MIA PaCa-2 cells confirmed a physical interaction between PLEC and EP300 (Fig. 6C). Given that lysine acetylation can drive protein relocalization, we compared PLEC acetylation in normal pancreatic ductal epithelium in comparison with PDAC cell lines. After normalizing for equivalent PLEC pulldown, pan-acetyl-lysine immunoblotting revealed higher PLEC acetylation in PDAC lines than in HPDE cells (Fig. 6D). Targeted mass spectrometry mapped a major acetylation site to lysine 1310 (K1310) on PLEC. To test the functional relevance of this site, we generated a deacetylation-mimetic mutant (PLEC-K1310R). Relative to wild-type PLEC, confocal immunofluorescence showed that K1310R redirected PLEC from the plasma membrane to the nucleocytoplasmic compartment in PANC-1 and MIA PaCa-2 cells (Fig. 6E). Functionally, K1310R impaired cell motility, as evidenced by reduced wound closure and reduced transwell migration compared with wild type (Fig. 6F-G).

## Discussion
  Previous studies demonstrated that the expression of PLEC increases progressively during the development of PDAC [24]. Specifically, the expression rate of PLEC in pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PanIN) lesions was reported to be 0%, 3.85%, and 60% in PanIN I, II, and III, respectively, but reached 100% in invasive PDAC [25]. This progressive upregulation during disease progression suggests that PLEC is closely associated with pancreatic tumorigenesis and malignant transformation. Consistent with these findings, our diagnostic performance analysis demonstrated that PLEC alone could distinguish PDAC from non-tumor pancreatic tissues with high levels of accuracy, highlighting its potential as a promising biomarker for the detection of pancreatic cancer and as an attractive target for molecular imaging. However, radiotracers based on PLEC-targeting antibodies may be limited by relatively slow pharmacokinetics, sub-optimal tumor penetration, and prolonged circulation times in vivo. To overcome these limitations, we designed and synthesized two novel peptide-based PLEC-targeted radiotracers: [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer and [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN. The two tracers were developed to maintain high binding affinity while improving in vivo stability and imaging performance, thereby providing promising candidates for the detection of PDAC by molecular imaging.

  In this study, we combined CNN-driven molecular docking with bivalent ligand engineering, providing a generalizable design approach for radiotracers targeting PLEC. By precisely identifying key recognition residues between PTP and PLEC through CNN analysis, we rationally incorporated NOTA chelating groups while preserving the conformational stability of the pharmacophore. This approach enabled the structural optimization of the 68Ga-labeled monomeric radiotracer and provided a rational basis for the construction of its dimeric counterparts. Based on the NOTA-FZPN and PLEC binding model, we further constructed a representative dimeric radiotracer to evaluate whether a bivalent architecture could enhance PLEC targeting compared with the monomeric radiotracer. Dimers exhibit markedly distinct behaviors from monomers in terms of affinity and in vivo distribution, indicating that AI-derived binding patterns not only enabled rational modification of single ligands but also provided a structural rationale for bivalent design. The successful application of this strategy for PLEC-targeted imaging demonstrated that advancing deep learning scoring functions to the early stages of radiotracer design has the potential to accelerate the discovery and cross-modal optimization of radiotracers for additional tumor-associated targets.

  Cell-binding assays demonstrated that [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN and [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer specifically bound to PANC-1 cells. Blocking studies further supported the high binding affinity and receptor specificity of these radiotracers for PLEC. Pharmacokinetic analysis provided additional insights into their in vivo distribution and clearance profiles. Notably, [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer exhibited favorable pharmacokinetic characteristics, including good in vivo stability and rapid clearance from non-target tissues. These properties highlight its potential utility as a PLEC-targeted probe for PET imaging. MicroPET/CT imaging performed in PANC-1 tumor-bearing model demonstrated that [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN and [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer achieved clear and specific tumor accumulation as early as 30 min after injection, resulting in favorable tumor-to-background contrast. Notably, the dimeric tracer [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer consistently exhibited higher tumor uptake than the monomeric tracer [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN in PANC-1 tumors. This difference was maintained across multiple imaging time points, from 30 to 90 min after injection, indicating the superior tumor-targeting capability of the dimeric construct. The consistently higher tumor uptake of the dimeric tracer than that of the monomeric tracer may be associated with its bivalent architecture and enhanced bivalent interactions, which may improve target engagement and tumor-associated retention.

  The ex vivo biodistribution data were consistent with the micro-PET/CT imaging results, demonstrating the preferential accumulation of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer in tumor tissue with favorable tumor-to-background contrast. In addition, no observable toxicity was detected during the study, indicating the good in vivo tolerability of the radiotracer. Compared with previously reported PLEC-targeted radiotracers, including [99mTc]Tc-labeled PTP-based SPECT probes [26] and the recently reported monomeric PET tracer [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-PTP [16], [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer incorporated a dimeric peptide architecture designed to enhance PLEC targeting. Consistent with this design strategy, the dimeric tracer exhibited higher cellular uptake, greater tumor accumulation, and improved tumor-to-muscle contrast than its monomeric counterpart. These results suggest that [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer expands the current repertoire of PLEC-targeted PET radiotracers and provides a promising platform for further optimization and clinical translation.

  Over recent years, PLEC has emerged as a membrane-associated biomarker with biological significance and potential clinical relevance [27, 28]. Shin et al. previously demonstrated that the suppression of PLEC expression significantly reduced the proliferative capacity of pancreatic cancer cells. To further evaluate the clinical significance of plectin-1, we performed survival analysis using data from the TCGA database. Analysis revealed that high levels of PLEC expression were significantly associated with a shorter overall survival (OS) in patients with pancreatic cancer. These findings support the potential value of PLEC for the molecular stratification, risk assessment, and targeted imaging of PDAC. Moreover, the consistent association between elevated PLEC expression and poor survival outcomes across independent cohorts suggests that PLEC may play an important role in the invasive and metastatic progression of PDAC.

  PLEC has been increasingly recognized as a key gene associated with metastatic progression and poor prognosis in PDAC [29]. Nevertheless, the mechanisms governing the dynamic relocalization of PLEC and its functional regulation remain incompletely understood. Previous studies have shown that PLEC-integrin β4 interaction contributes to PLEC anchorage and cell-surface/exosome-associated relocalization in PDAC [30, 31]. This canonical interaction predominantly involves the N-terminal actin-binding domain (ABD) of PLEC and defined regions within the cytoplasmic domain of integrin β4. In contrast, K1310 is located outside of this classical ABD-integrin β4 binding interface, suggesting that K1310 acetylation is unlikely to directly constitute the β4-binding site. Rather, our findings raise the possibility that K1310 acetylation acts as a distal regulatory event that modulates PLEC membrane relocalization, potentially by conformational remodeling, vesicular trafficking, or the altered accessibility of β4-associated anchoring complexes. Whether K1310 acetylation directly affects PLEC -integrin β4 binding remains to be clarified in future studies.

  From a functional perspective, the membrane localization of PLEC appears to play an important role in regulating the migratory behavior of PDAC cells. As a cytoskeletal linker protein that connects intermediate filaments with adhesion complexes, membrane-enriched PLEC may facilitate cell-matrix interactions and cytoskeletal remodeling, thereby promoting tumor cell migration and invasion. In the present study, the K1310R mutation markedly suppressed wound-healing and transwell migration, indicating that the pro-migratory function of PLEC depends on acetylation-driven membrane localization rather than merely on its expression level. These findings suggest that PLEC functions not only as a biomarker but also as a functional regulatory node involved in the progression of PDAC, thus providing a mechanistic explanation for its association with a poor prognosis and metastatic phenotypes. Collectively, our results delineate a previously unrecognized signaling axis in which EP300-mediated acetylation of PLEC at the K1310 residue drives its relocalization to the plasma membrane, thereby promoting cell migration and invasion in PDAC. This mechanism provides new insights into the metastatic biology of PDAC and may offer a potential target for future therapeutic intervention.

  This study has several limitations that need to be considered. Although [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer demonstrated promising potential as a PLEC-targeted PET radiotracer in preclinical models, additional studies are now required before clinical translation can be considered. Although favorable tumor-to-background contrast was achieved, physiological liver uptake and relatively prominent renal accumulation were observed, representing important considerations for future clinical application. In addition, tumor retention gradually decreased over time, indicating the need for further optimization of the tracer to prolong tumor residence time, reduce hepatic and renal background activity, and improve tumor-to-liver and tumor-to-kidney contrast, particularly for the detection of hepatic metastases. Another limitation is that only one dimeric format was synthesized and evaluated; therefore, we are unable to determine whether the linker length and spatial arrangement used in the present study were optimal. A systematic structure–activity relationship (SAR) study comparing dimers with short, medium, and long linkers will be necessary to validate the optimal conformational requirements for multivalent PLEC targeting. Finally,with regard to future theragnostic translation, although [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer demonstrated favorable tumor visualization for PET imaging, the relatively limited tumor retention may restrict prolonged radiation delivery for future targeted radionuclide therapy applications. Further engineering of the tracer, including linker optimization, structural modification, and strategies to enhance tumor retention, will be required. At the same time, the observed renal accumulation represents a potential dose-limiting factor for therapeutic translation, and comprehensive dosimetry studies will be necessary to evaluate the feasibility and safety of future theragnostic applications.


## Conclusions
  In this study, we developed two novel peptide-based plectin-1-targeted radiotracers, [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer and [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN. Of these, [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-FZPN-dimer demonstrated superior PLEC-targeting capability and PET/CT imaging performance in cellular and tumor models, thus supporting its potential as a promising radiotracer for molecular imaging in PDAC. Furthermore, we provide additional mechanistic insights into the pro-invasive role of PLEC in PDAC. From the perspective of post-translational modification and subcellular localization, we identified EP300-mediated K1310 acetylation as a key mechanism driving the membrane localization of PLEC and the promotion of tumor cell migration. These findings provide a foundation for future mechanistic studies and the development of targeted therapeutic strategies.
