# Transcriptomic trajectory of hepatocyte ballooning reveals a lipotoxic point of no return and initiates oncogenic regulatory rewiring in MASLD


## Background
  Hepatocyte ballooning is a defining histological marker of progressive steatohepatitis, but its molecular basis remains poorly defined.


## Objective
  To identify a molecular signature and mechanistic trajectory underlying hepatocyte ballooning across the MASLD-to-hepatocellular carcinoma spectrum.


## Methods
  Liver microarray data from 43 patients stratified by histological ballooning score were combined with machine-learning feature selection and publicly available single-nucleus RNA sequencing data (87,244 hepatocyte nuclei) to derive a gene signature, tested in an independent bulk transcriptomic cohort (GSE130970, n = 75); pseudotime, transcription-factor regulon and cell–cell communication analyses characterised the underlying biology, with in vitro validation in palmitic acid-treated Huh7 cells and prognostic testing in 510 TCGA-LIHC patients.


## Results
  An eight-gene signature (SPP1, MFSD2A, PRAP1, ANXA2, KRT8, MACROH2A2 (H2AFY2), JUN and FABP4) characterised ballooned hepatocytes and discriminated moderate-to-severe from absent ballooning with an AUC of 0.768 (95% CI 0.654–0.883). Six of these genes formed a Hepatocyte Ballooning–Hepatocyte injury (HBHi) score that was highest in nuclei from cirrhotic (F4) liver. Pseudotime analysis resolved a four-stage molecular collapse culminating at pseudotime ≈ 25, marked by loss of the chromatin stabiliser MACROH2A2, co-induction of KRT8 and SPP1, and replacement of the HNF4A/PPARA-driven programme by the stress factors JUN, ATF3 and EGR1. Ballooned hepatocytes transmitted pro-fibrogenic signals to stellate cells and macrophages via FN1–CD44 and FN1–(ITGAV + ITGB1) axes; high KRT8, JUN and SPP1 expression was each associated with poorer survival in hepatocellular carcinoma.


## Conclusions
  Hepatocyte ballooning is a transcriptionally active, stepwise process with a lipotoxic point of no return and prognostic relevance extending to hepatocellular carcinoma.


## Introduction
  Metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is the most common chronic liver disorder worldwide. A meta-analysis of 92 population-based studies encompassing more than 9.3 million individuals estimated global prevalence at 30.05% overall, rising from 25.26% in 1990–2006 to 38.00% in 2016–2019 [1], and the burden is projected to continue increasing [2]. A subset of patients with simple steatosis progresses to metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH), which may in turn advance to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [2,3]. In this paper the term MASH is used interchangeably with the earlier designation NASH where required for consistency with cited datasets and literature.

  Hepatocyte ballooning (HB) is a cardinal feature of MASH and refers to a form of hepatocyte degeneration characterised by cell enlargement, swelling, rounding and a distinctive reticulated cytoplasm [3]. Ballooned cells are typically larger and rounder than normal hepatocytes and display a pale, reticulated cytoplasm that gives them a “spider web” appearance on haematoxylin and eosin staining [4]. Histopathological studies have shown co-localisation of steatotic hepatocytes and areas of perisinusoidal fibrosis with ballooned hepatocytes, implicating HB in MASH progression [4,5]. The presence of hepatocyte ballooning has been linked to greater severity of liver disease and a higher likelihood of hepatic, cardiovascular and chronic kidney complications [4], and a higher frequency of liver-related death has been observed in patients whose biopsies show ballooning, Mallory–Denk bodies or fibrosis [6].

  Because hepatocyte ballooning correlates with inflammatory activity, fibrosis progression and adverse clinical outcomes, it represents a key pathological transition within the MASLD–MASH continuum [7]. Both of the principal histological scoring systems grade ballooning semi-quantitatively: the NASH Clinical Research Network NAFLD Activity Score (NAS) scores hepatocellular ballooning on a 0–2 scale [8], and the SAF (Steatosis–Activity–Fibrosis) system incorporates ballooning into a composite activity score [9]. Despite this diagnostic importance, hepatocyte ballooning remains defined principally by morphological criteria, and the molecular mechanisms linking the phenotype to malignant progression are incompletely understood [4,6,7,10].

  Here we address this gap through an integrative analysis combining bulk transcriptomics, machine-learning feature selection and single-nucleus RNA sequencing to reconstruct the molecular trajectory of hepatocyte injury across the healthy–MASLD–MASH–HCC spectrum. We identify transcriptional programmes associated with hepatocyte ballooning, resolve a continuous hepatocyte disease axis, and characterise hepatocyte ballooning as a transcriptionally governed state that links metabolic liver injury to hepatocellular carcinoma.


## Materials and methods

### Microarray data processing and weighted gene co-expression network analysis
  Hepatic microarray data generated previously by our group [11] were re-analysed. Following quality filtering, 43 samples were retained and the 10,000 most variable probe sets were carried forward. A co-expression network was constructed with WGCNA [12] using a topological overlap matrix–based adjacency computed at a soft-thresholding power of five, and modules were detected by dynamic tree cut. Closely related modules were merged at a dissimilarity threshold of 0.25. Module–trait associations were calculated as Pearson correlations between module eigengenes and clinical variables.


### Differential expression analysis
  Differential expression was assessed with limma using linear modelling and empirical Bayes moderation [13]. The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied to control the false discovery rate. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as |log2FC| > 0.5 and FDR < 0.05. The comparison was made between patients with a hepatocyte ballooning score (HBS) of 1 or 2 (n = 19) and those with an HBS of 0 (n = 24).


### Feature selection and stability analysis
  LASSO logistic regression was performed with the glmnet package, and the penalty parameter was selected by 10-fold cross-validation [14]. To assess the stability of gene selection, 10,000 bootstrap iterations were performed and genes with a bootstrap selection frequency above the 50th percentile were retained. HSPC157 and C10orf116 (ADIRF) were excluded despite high selection frequencies because neither has a documented biological function in hepatocyte injury.


### Independent validation and clinical translation
  Bulk transcriptomic data from an independent cohort (GSE130970, n = 75) were normalised [15]. Samples were scored using a ridge-penalised logistic regression model built exclusively on the final eight genes [14]. GSE130970 was not used at any stage of feature selection or model fitting. The area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve (AUC) was estimated with bootstrapping to generate a 95% confidence interval. A nomogram was constructed with the rms package [16]. Clinical net benefit was evaluated by decision curve analysis using the rmda package [17]. Agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes was assessed with B = 200 bootstrap calibration repetitions.


### Single-nucleus RNA sequencing processing and integration
  Two publicly available single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) datasets, GSE189175 [[18], [19], [20]] and GSE202379 [21], were processed with Seurat v5 [22]. Nuclei with fewer than 200 or more than 6000 detected genes, or with more than 25% mitochondrial reads, were removed. Computational doublets were identified and removed with scDblFinder [23]. Datasets were integrated by reciprocal principal component analysis (RPCA) and clustered using a shared nearest-neighbour graph. The hepatocyte population was subclustered at a Leiden resolution of 0.4, yielding 11 subpopulations.


### Selection of six genes for single-nucleus analysis
  The signature was narrowed to six genes (JUN, ANXA2, KRT8, SPP1, MFSD2A and MACROH2A2) for single-nucleus analysis on two criteria: detection in more than 10% of hepatocyte nuclei across both datasets (Supplementary Fig. S2B) and published evidence of hepatocyte-specific function [[24], [25], [26], [27], [28], [29]]. FABP4 was excluded because it is expressed predominantly in macrophages and adipocytes, giving subthreshold nuclear detection in hepatocytes [30]. PRAP1 was excluded because of sparse nuclear capture, consistent with its predominantly cytoplasmic localisation.


### Trajectory inference and the HBHi score
  Pseudotime trajectory inference was performed on the hepatocyte subset with Monocle 3 [31]. The trajectory root was anchored to the centroid of the healthy hepatocyte cluster. Gene expression kinetics along pseudotime were smoothed with generalized additive models. The Hepatocyte Ballooning–Hepatocyte injury (HBHi) score was defined as the first principal component (PC1) of the expression matrix of the six signature genes computed exclusively within snRNA-seq hepatocyte nuclei. The relationship between HBHi score and pseudotime was assessed by Spearman rank correlation.


### Metabolic pathway activity analysis
  KEGG metabolic pathway activity was scored for each nucleus with the scMetabolism package [32] using VISION-based enrichment. Metabolic scores were mapped onto the pseudotime axis and trends visualised with GAM smoothing.


### Gene regulatory network analysis
  Regulatory networks were inferred with pySCENIC [33,34]. Co-expression modules were inferred with GRNBoost2 and pruned against the RcisTarget database using the hg38 500 bp and 10 kb motif rankings. Regulon activity was scored per nucleus with AUCell, and GRNBoost2 edge weights were used to represent regulatory strength. The relationship between regulon activity and the HBHi score was assessed by Pearson correlation.


### Cell–cell communication analysis
  CellChat [35] was applied to the integrated snRNA-seq dataset using the human CellChat ligand–receptor database. Communication probabilities were computed for all cell-type pairs across all disease stages, and the sender, receiver, mediator and influencer roles of each cell type were assessed. Significant ligand–receptor pairs were identified by permutation testing (n = 100) at p < 0.05.


### TCGA-LIHC survival analysis
  RSEM-normalised expression and matched clinical survival data were obtained for 510 patients with HCC from the TCGA-LIHC cohort [36] via the UCSC Xena browser (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/?cohort=GDC+TCGA+Liver+Cancer+%28LIHC%29). For each signature gene, patients were dichotomised at the median expression value. Kaplan–Meier curves were plotted for overall and disease-free survival, groups were compared by log-rank test, and hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals were obtained by Cox proportional hazards regression. All hazard ratios reported are univariable. Analyses were performed with the survival and survminer packages.


### Cell culture, treatment, RNA extraction and RT-qPCR
  Huh7 hepatocellular carcinoma cells (JCRB Cell Bank, JCRB0403; RRID: CVCL_0336) were cultured in DMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 11965092) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# A5670501) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 15140122) at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Cells (5 × 104 per well) were seeded in 12-well plates and treated with 150 μM palmitic acid (PA; Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# P0500) conjugated to fatty-acid-free bovine serum albumin (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# A2153) for 24 h or 48 h. Cells treated with the vehicle alone (0.1% DMSO; Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# D2650) served as controls. Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 15596026) [37] and reverse transcribed with the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 4368814) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RT-qPCR was performed with SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 4309155) using 18S rRNA as the reference gene, and relative expression was calculated by the 2^−ΔΔCT method [38].


### Protein extraction and western blotting
  Huh7 cells (1 × 105 per well) were seeded in 6-well plates, treated with 150 μM PA for 24 h or 48 h as described above, and lysed in 150 μL ice-cold RIPA buffer (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# R0278) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Cat# 11836153001) and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# P5726). Protein concentration was determined by the Bradford assay (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Cat# 5000006) [39]. Equal amounts of protein were resolved by SDS-PAGE, transferred to 0.45 μm PVDF membranes (Merck Millipore, Cat# IPVH00010) and blocked in 5% BSA in TBS containing 0.1% Tween-20 (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# P1379). Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against osteopontin/SPP1 (Cat# A1499), cytokeratin 8/KRT8 (Cat# A1024) and β-actin (Cat# AC004; loading control), all from ABclonal Technology, followed by HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (ABclonal Technology, Cat# AS014) or HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (ABclonal Technology, Cat# AS003) as appropriate. Blots were developed with an ECL western blotting substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 32106) on a chemiluminescence documentation system. Full details of all reagents, antibodies, cell lines and software are given in the Key Resources Table (Supplementary Table S1).


### Quantification and statistical analysis
  All computational and statistical analyses were performed in R (v4.3.1) and Python (v3.10). Key software versions were: WGCNA v1.73, limma v3.58.1, glmnet v4.1.10, rms v6.7.1, rmda v1.6, Seurat v5.4.0, scDblFinder v1.16.0, Monocle v3-1.4.26, scMetabolism v0.2.1, pySCENIC, AUCell v1.24.0, RcisTarget v1.20.0, CellChat v1.6.1, survival v3.8.3 and survminer. Variables were compared across ballooning categories with the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s post-hoc correction and Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment. Continuous clinical trait associations were tested by one-way ANOVA. Spearman rank correlation was used for trajectory data, and Pearson correlation for module–trait and regulon associations. RT-qPCR data are presented as mean ± SD of three independent experiments and compared by unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. CellChat interactions were considered significant on permutation testing at p < 0.05. TCGA survival statistics were obtained by log-rank test and Cox regression. Significance levels are denoted *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001. Exact sample sizes, tests and p values are given in the figure legends.


## Results

### Identification of a hepatocyte ballooning gene signature from the liver tissue transcriptome
  Ballooned hepatocytes are widely recognised as the histological feature that most specifically distinguishes benign steatosis from progressive steatohepatitis and fibrosis. Ballooning has, however, historically been interpreted as a morphological observation rather than as a transcriptionally altered cellular state. We examined liver microarray data from 43 patients categorised by histological hepatocyte ballooning score (HBS) from our previously published cohort [11] (Supplementary Fig. S1A, Supplementary Table S2). Patients were categorised as HBS 0 (no ballooning; n = 24) or HBS 1 + 2 (moderate and severe; n = 19). Patients without ballooning were largely free of fibrosis, the great majority being at fibrosis stage 0 and the lowest levels of portal and lobular inflammation (Supplementary Fig. S1B–C). Alanine aminotransferase and aspartate aminotransferase were significantly elevated in the moderate (adjusted p = 2.6 × 10−5) and severe (adjusted p = 1.8 × 10−7) groups relative to HBS 0 (Supplementary Fig. S1D).

  WGCNA [12] applied to the normalised expression matrix identified the MEblack module (1238 genes), whose eigengene was more strongly correlated with the hepatocyte ballooning score than that of any other module (r = 0.57). MEblack was also correlated with the NAFLD activity score (r = 0.62), steatosis grade (r = 0.58), lobular inflammation (r = 0.49), fibrosis stage (r = 0.46), portal inflammation (r = 0.41), AST (r = 0.43) and ALT (r = 0.37; Fig. 1A; Supplementary Table S3). Differential expression analysis with limma [13] comparing HBS ≥ 1 with HBS 0 identified 21 significant DEGs at |log2FC| > 0.5 and FDR < 0.05 (Fig. 1B; Supplementary Table S5).   LASSO regression [14] with 10,000 bootstrap iterations was applied to the 20 candidates and selected SPP1, MFSD2A, PRAP1, ANXA2, KRT8, MACROH2A2 (H2AFY2), JUN and FABP4 (Fig. 1D). HSPC157 (a pseudogene) and C10orf116 (ADIRF) also scored highly but were excluded because neither has an established role in hepatocyte injury.   The panel was evaluated in an independent bulk transcriptomic cohort (GSE130970, n = 75) [15] using ridge regression. The model achieved an AUC of 0.768 (95% CI 0.654–0.883). Model weights were used to construct a nomogram [16] converting the eight-gene profile into a risk score for grade 1 + 2 ballooning (Fig. 1E). The three genes contributing most to the score were MACROH2A2, FABP4 and ANXA2. Decision curve analysis [17] indicated superior net clinical benefit relative to treat-all and treat-none strategies across thresholds from 0 to 0.92, with net benefits of 0.517, 0.373 and 0.133 at the 20%, 50% and 80% thresholds respectively. Bootstrap calibration (B = 200) gave a low mean absolute error of 0.046 (Supplementary Fig. S1E).


### Pseudotime trajectory analysis resolves a four-stage molecular collapse
  To determine the cellular origin of this transcriptional programme, the snRNA-seq datasets GSE202379 [21] and GSE189175 [[18], [19], [20]], spanning the healthy–MASLD–MASH–HCC spectrum, were integrated. Six genes (JUN, ANXA2, KRT8, SPP1, MFSD2A and MACROH2A2) were retained because they were detected in more than 10% of hepatocyte nuclei in both datasets and each has supporting literature for a role in hepatocyte injury and MASH. These six genes were used for all subsequent single-cell analyses (trajectory scoring, HBHi score and regulatory network analysis). FABP4 was excluded because its expression is predominantly in liver macrophages and adipocytes rather than hepatocytes [30] and nuclear detection was low and inconsistent. PRAP1 was excluded because nuclear transcript counts were very low across all hepatocyte clusters in both datasets, consistent with its primarily cytoplasmic localisation (Supplementary Tables S7–S8).

  After integration, five major liver cell types were recovered: hepatocytes, stellate cells, endothelial cells, cholangiocytes and immune cells (Supplementary Fig. S2A). The hepatocyte subset was isolated and re-clustered, yielding 11 subpopulations (Supplementary Fig. S2B), and all six signature genes were confirmed to be detected within these clusters by dot plot.

  Trajectory inference with Monocle 3 [31] ordered hepatocytes along a pseudotime axis spanning healthy to HCC (Fig. 2A). The defined disease stages fell along this axis in the expected chronological order (Fig. 2A, lower panels). The HBHi score correlated with pseudotime (Spearman’s ρ = 0.40, p < 2.2 × 10−16; Fig. 2D; Supplementary Fig. S3A).   A progressive decline in MFSD2A expression was observed. Because Mfsd2a marks periportal hepatocytes that repopulate the liver during injury and regeneration [26], its loss indicates a breakdown of hepatic spatial zonation (Fig. 2E). A transient increase in JUN and ANXA2 was noted, consistent with activation of AP-1 stress signalling [27] and a membrane injury response [28]. The chromatin stabiliser MACROH2A2 [29] declined sharply at pseudotime ≈ 25, at which point the structural (KRT8 [25]) and pro-fibrogenic (SPP1 [24]) programmes were simultaneously activated. Late-trajectory hepatocytes occupied a state characterised by loss of normal hepatocyte identity and structural collapse relative to healthy hepatocytes.


### Identification of a lipotoxic tipping point along the MASLD spectrum
  To characterise the functional transition accompanying hepatocyte injury and ballooning, metabolic activity was analysed along the disease trajectory. scMetabolism [32] was used to score metabolic pathway activity in each nucleus. Early in pseudotime, hepatocytes retained normal functionality, including macronutrient and xenobiotic metabolism (Fig. 3A). As disease progressed along the pseudotime axis, these pathways declined in a coordinated manner. Fatty acid degradation capacity fell sharply around the 30th percentile of the trajectory, the point at which the rising HBHi injury burden overtook it (Fig. 3B). ### Signature genes are acutely induced by palmitic acid–driven lipotoxicity in vitro
  Having identified a lipotoxic tipping point along the pseudotime trajectory, we asked whether this transcriptional response could be induced experimentally by acute lipotoxic stress alone. Huh7 cells [40] were treated with 150 μM palmitic acid (PA), a saturated fatty acid that induces endoplasmic reticulum stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, reactive oxygen species production and apoptosis in hepatocytes [41,42]. RT-qPCR revealed a time-dependent induction of JUN, KRT8 and SPP1 transcripts in PA-treated cells relative to vehicle controls. JUN and KRT8 were already significantly upregulated at 24 h (1.6-fold, p < 0.01, and 2.3-fold, p < 0.01, respectively) and rose further by 48 h (3.5-fold and 5.1-fold, both p < 0.001). SPP1 was unchanged at 24 h and significantly induced only at 48 h (2.4-fold, p < 0.01; Fig. 3D). At the protein level, Western blot analysis confirmed increased expression of osteopontin (SPP1) and KRT8 in PA-treated cells (Fig. 3D). Together these findings indicate that acute lipotoxic stress is sufficient to activate the hepatocyte ballooning gene programme in vitro.


### Stress transcription factors replace the metabolic regulatory programme
  The HBHi trajectory identified a metabolic-to-stress transition at pseudotime ≈ 25, but the transcription factors orchestrating this switch were unknown. pySCENIC [33,34] was therefore used to map transcription factor regulon activity across the snRNA-seq hepatocyte subset and to correlate it with the HBHi score.

  Two opposing groups of transcription factors emerged (Fig. 4A). Factors whose activity increased with HBHi — led by EGR1 (r = 0.56), JUN (r = 0.52) and ATF3 (r = 0.45) — constituted a stress-driven arm. Factors whose activity decreased — HNF4A (r = −0.40), PPARA (r = −0.38) and NR1H4 (r = −0.33) — constituted a collapsing protective arm. Along the pseudotime axis, CEBPB, FOS, ATF3 and JUN rose progressively while HNF4A, NR1H3 and RXRA declined, confirming a coordinated regulatory switch (Fig. 4B).   We next asked whether these HBHi-correlated transcription factors directly regulate the six signature genes. Regulatory weights were mapped between correlated factors and their downstream targets (Fig. 4C). On the stress side, ATF3 directly targeted JUN with the strongest regulatory weight in the network (weight = 5.2), and EGR1 also directly regulated JUN (weight = 2.7), indicating that stress factors amplify one another (Fig. 4C, D, 4G). On the protective side, SOX5 sustained MACROH2A2 (weight = 3.2) and NR1H4 sustained MFSD2A (weight = 2.5), both confirmed at single-cell level (Fig. 4C, E, 4F). As the HBHi score increased, both protective axes progressively lost activity, withdrawing two key brakes on healthy hepatocyte identity.

  Collectively these findings describe a two-arm transcriptional collapse: a stress arm (ATF3/EGR1 → JUN) that drives injury forward, and a protective arm (NR1H4 → MFSD2A, SOX5 → MACROH2A2) that progressively loses its hold on healthy hepatocyte identity along the HBHi trajectory.


### Ballooned hepatocytes send pro-fibrogenic signals to surrounding cells
  CellChat [35] was applied to model ligand–receptor communication across all cell types at every disease stage. Cell type proportions shifted significantly with disease progression, with the HB_high hepatocyte subpopulation expanding progressively and most markedly in advanced NASH and HCC (Supplementary Fig. S4). Key ligand–receptor interactions from HB_high hepatocytes to stellate cells and macrophages included FN1–CD44 and FN1–(ITGAV + ITGB1), indicating active extracellular matrix remodelling, and LAMC1–(ITGA1+ITGB1) and LAMC1–(ITGA9+ITGB1), indicating basement membrane reprogramming (Supplementary Fig. S5).


### Ballooning-associated genes are prognostic in hepatocellular carcinoma
  We next asked whether the genes activated at the Catastrophic Nexus carry prognostic weight in human liver cancer. Expression and survival data from 510 patients with HCC in the TCGA-LIHC cohort [36] were analysed. Expression levels were dichotomised at the median for each gene and outcomes evaluated by Kaplan–Meier curves and Cox proportional hazards regression (Supplementary Fig. S6). High KRT8 expression was associated with worse overall survival (log-rank p = 0.038; HR = 1.5, p = 0.04). High JUN expression similarly predicted worse overall survival (log-rank p = 0.02; HR = 1.5, p = 0.021). High SPP1 expression carried the strongest prognostic signal of all genes tested (log-rank p = 1.6 × 10−5; HR = 2.2, p = 2.4 × 10−5). Conversely, high MFSD2A expression — the gene lost earliest along the trajectory — trended toward better disease-free survival (log-rank p = 0.1; HR = 0.75), consistent with its role as a marker of healthy hepatocyte zonation. All hazard ratios reported are univariable.


## Discussion
  This study investigated the molecular basis of hepatocyte ballooning in MASLD/MASH and placed it within the disease continuum from healthy liver to hepatocellular carcinoma. Using an integrative approach combining bulk transcriptomics, machine learning, single-nucleus RNA sequencing and cell–cell communication analysis, our findings define hepatocyte ballooning as a transcriptionally active and dynamic cellular state rather than a passive morphological observation.

  An eight-gene signature (SPP1, MFSD2A, PRAP1, ANXA2, KRT8, MACROH2A2, JUN and FABP4) was identified from microarray data and evaluated in an independent cohort (AUC = 0.768). This level of discrimination is clinically informative though not definitive, and it illustrates the value of combining multiple markers rather than relying on a single gene. Each gene has a supported biological role. SPP1 (osteopontin) is induced by hedgehog pathway activation and promotes fibrosis progression in steatohepatitis by activating hepatic stellate cells [24]. KRT8 aggregates form Mallory–Denk bodies, a structural hallmark of ballooned hepatocytes [25]. MACROH2A2, a chromatin-stabilising histone variant, is a new addition in this context; macroH2A variants regulate epigenetic plasticity and the silencing of cancer-promoting expression programmes [29], and its loss may therefore permit de-repression of stress and oncogenic genes, although this remains a hypothesis that our data do not directly test. AP-1 dimers containing c-Jun regulate steatohepatitis and PPARγ signalling in dietary models of fatty liver disease [27]. The three genes contributing most to the nomogram score were MACROH2A2, FABP4 and ANXA2, pointing to chromatin instability, lipid handling and membrane stress as central features of ballooned hepatocyte identity. The HBHi score, derived from six of these genes, was highest in cirrhotic (F4) liver, indicating that the ballooning transcriptional state is most pronounced once structural liver damage is advanced.

  Pseudotime trajectory analysis placed hepatocytes along a continuous axis from healthy to HCC and recovered the expected chronological ordering of disease stages. Four sequential events were identified. First, in early disease, hepatocytes maintained normal metabolism and MFSD2A was highly expressed, marking periportal identity [26]; loss of MFSD2A was therefore an early indicator that hepatic spatial organisation had begun to break down. Second, JUN and ANXA2 were transiently activated, reflecting AP-1-mediated stress responses [27] and membrane injury signalling [28]. Third, at approximately pseudotime 25, three events occurred concurrently: MACROH2A2 expression fell sharply, KRT8 was induced, and SPP1 was activated. This coincidence of chromatin de-repression, cytoskeletal injury and fibrogenic signalling defines what we term the Catastrophic Nexus, marking the transition from a recoverable injury state to structural and pro-fibrogenic collapse. Fourth, late-stage hepatocytes entered a state characterised by loss of normal hepatocyte identity. This ordered sequence offers a more granular and mechanistic account of MASH progression than histological staging alone, and may provide a framework for defining intermediate disease states not captured by current scoring systems.

  The regulatory network analysis identified two opposing groups of transcription factors. The protective arm — HNF4A, PPARA and NR1H4 (FXR) — was highly active in healthy hepatocytes and declined progressively as the HBHi score rose. The stress arm — EGR1, JUN and ATF3 — became increasingly active in parallel. Within the stress arm, ATF3 directly drove JUN expression (regulatory weight = 5.2) and EGR1 also targeted JUN (weight = 2.7), creating a self-reinforcing loop that is difficult to interrupt once activated. On the protective side, SOX5 sustained MACROH2A2 and NR1H4 maintained MFSD2A in healthy cells; as these regulators lost activity, their downstream targets were coordinately lost. This two-arm switch, in which the stress arm drives forward while the protective arm progressively fails, offers a mechanistic account of why late-stage MASH tends toward irreversibility. Notably, EGR1 transcript levels were sparse across disease stages while its regulon activity rose sharply in HCC, illustrating the value of regulon-based inference over raw transcript counts for identifying functionally active transcription factors.

  Cell–cell communication analysis showed that heavily ballooned hepatocytes were the dominant signal senders across all disease stages, and that the HB_high hepatocyte subpopulation expanded progressively as disease advanced toward HCC. Key ligand–receptor interactions from ballooned hepatocytes to stellate cells and macrophages included FN1–CD44, FN1–(ITGAV + ITGB1) and LAMC1–(ITGA1+ITGB1), indicating extracellular matrix remodelling and basement membrane reprogramming driven by injured hepatocytes. These findings are clinically relevant because they indicate that ballooned hepatocytes are not passive bystanders but active drivers of fibrogenesis in neighbouring cells, consistent with reports that hepatic osteopontin expression correlates with fibrosis stage in patients with steatohepatitis [24] and with the broader landscape of intercellular signalling described in NASH liver [43]. The signalling axis between ballooned hepatocytes, stellate cells and macrophages may represent a therapeutic target that could be addressed independently of resolving ballooning itself. We note that all cell–cell communication findings here are computationally inferred and require experimental confirmation.

  Huh7 cells treated with 150 μM palmitic acid showed a time-dependent induction of JUN, KRT8 and SPP1 mRNA — significant for JUN and KRT8 by 24 h and for all three by 48 h — and western blotting confirmed increased SPP1 and KRT8 protein. These results indicate that acute lipotoxic stress alone is sufficient to activate the core signature genes, subject to the limitations of the model discussed below.

  High expression of SPP1 (HR = 2.2, p = 2.4 × 10−5), KRT8 (HR = 1.5, p = 0.04) and JUN (HR = 1.5, p = 0.021) was each associated with worse overall survival in TCGA-LIHC (n = 510). Conversely, high MFSD2A expression trended toward better disease-free survival (HR = 0.75), consistent with its role as a marker of healthy hepatocyte identity and periportal zonation. These associations suggest that the ballooning transcriptional state shares molecular features with oncogenic progression, which is biologically plausible given that c-Jun is required for the early stages of chemically induced hepatocellular carcinoma in mice, where it antagonises p53-dependent apoptosis [44]. These findings are nonetheless observational and derived from HCC patients rather than MASH patients; they do not establish that hepatocyte ballooning causes HCC. All hazard ratios are univariable and, given the number of genes tested, the associations for KRT8 and JUN in particular should be regarded as provisional.


### Limitations
  This study has several important limitations. First, the discovery cohort was small (n = 43); although the signature was evaluated in an independent cohort (GSE130970, n = 75), larger and more ethnically diverse prospective cohorts are needed to establish clinical utility. Second, the bulk microarray data used for signature discovery lack single-cell resolution, and co-expression patterns observed at bulk level may mask cell-type-specific effects or reflect shifts in cell type proportions between samples. Third, the publicly available snRNA-seq datasets were collected at different centres and processed with different protocols; RPCA integration was used to reduce batch effects, but residual technical variation cannot be excluded. Fourth, the in vitro validation used Huh7 cells, a hepatocellular carcinoma line with substantially altered metabolism [40] that lacks primary hepatocyte polarity and cannot reproduce paracrine signalling. Acute palmitic acid treatment models only the immediate cellular response to lipotoxicity and does not replicate the chronic, multi-year trajectory observed in patients; in particular, sustained silencing of MACROH2A2 and the progressive collapse of HNF4A and NR1H4 are long-term processes that cannot be reproduced in short-term culture. Fifth, the TCGA-LIHC survival analysis was performed in HCC patients rather than MASH patients; the prognostic value of the HBHi score in the MASH-to-HCC transition specifically remains to be tested in longitudinal cohorts capturing disease progression from its earliest stages. Sixth, all cell–cell communication findings are computationally inferred ligand–receptor interactions and require experimental validation.


### Conclusions
  This study provides a molecular model of hepatocyte ballooning as a progressive, transcriptionally active state. The eight-gene signature and HBHi score offer tools for objective, transcriptomics-based staging that may complement histological scoring. The Catastrophic Nexus — defined by the simultaneous loss of MACROH2A2, induction of KRT8 and activation of SPP1 at pseudotime ≈ 25 — and its upstream drivers ATF3 and EGR1 may define a therapeutic window. Intercepting this transition before irreversible fibrosis or malignant transformation occurs could represent a new strategy for MASH treatment. Future work should focus on prospective validation of the HBHi score in MASH cohorts, functional studies to confirm the causal roles of the identified transcription factors, and preclinical testing of interventions targeting the FN1–CD44 and FN1–ITGB1 signalling axes.
