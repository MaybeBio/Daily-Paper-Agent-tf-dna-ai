# Potential of RAD51C as a hub gene and therapeutic target in bladder cancer: insights from bioinformatics and experimental analysis


## Background:
  The global incidence of tumors is rising sharply. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) represent the mainstream of immunotherapeutic approaches. Despite the remarkable clinical benefits of immunotherapy in various tumors, ICIs resistance remains an urgent challenge to be addressed. The transcription factors cAMP response element-binding protein 1(CREB1) and c-Jun converge to regulate oncogenesis and immune modulation.


## Methods:
  Based on the ‘CREBP1CJUN_01’ gene set from the Molecular Signatures Database and single-cell RNA sequencing data, we identified 116 CREB–c-Jun transcription factor target-related differential genes (CR.Sig) and refined to 20 CREB–c-Jun transcription factor target-related important feature genes (Hub-CR.Sig) through multi-algorithm machine learning. This Hub-CR.Sig enabled prognostic risk modeling, molecular subtyping of bladder cancer, and somatic mutation stratification.


## Results:
  Among these 20 signatures, RAD51C emerged as a top-ranked driver that was correlated with advanced T/N/M stages, associated with poor survival outcomes and is highly expressed in bladder cancer tissues. Functional assessment revealed that RAD51C knockdown suppressed bladder cancer cell proliferation, invasion, migration, and DNA repair capacity in vitro, while also reducing tumor formation in vivo. RNA sequencing analysis implicated the enrichment of JAK-STAT signaling pathways. Meanwhile, we identified an additional type of RAD51C+ fibroblast that was significantly associated with the depth of tumor invasion.


## Conclusions:
  The study establishes the Hub-CR.Sig as an immunotherapy response classifier and nominates RAD51C targeting as a promising therapeutic strategy for bladder cancer.


## Introduction
  The global incidence of tumors is rising sharply, primarily attributed to environmental factors and physicochemical stimuli such as smoking[1]. Conventional cancer immunotherapies still center on chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapy, while immunotherapy has emerged as a novel therapeutic strategy for patients with advanced malignancies in recent years. Currently, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) represent the mainstream of immunotherapeutic approaches, with PD-1, PD-L1, and CTLA4 as the core targets, and their therapeutic efficacy varies drastically across different tumor types[2]. Moreover, due to interindividual heterogeneity among patients with the same tumor, the therapeutic responses to immunotherapy are also inconsistent.

  Despite the remarkable clinical benefits of immunotherapy in various tumors, ICIs resistance remains an urgent challenge to be addressed[3,4]. At present, most relevant studies focus on identifying reliable predictive biomarkers in a single tumor type to predict patients’ therapeutic responses to immunotherapy. Biomarkers are typically identified through the use of microarrays and next-generation sequencing technologies due to their convenience and cost-effectiveness[5]. These technologies measure the abundance of proteins and genes in intact tissues or organs and quantify the average expression levels across cells. On the other hand, single-cell sequencing captures cell-to-cell variability, detects cell heterogeneity, and facilitates the discovery of biomarkers and therapeutic targets under pathological conditions[6]. Numerous new biomarkers and gene signatures have been detected in the field of immunotherapy[7,8]. Currently, very few biomarkers have been successfully translated into clinical practice. Furthermore, identifying valid biomarkers in a single tumor type fails to adequately reflect the features of pan-cancers[9]. We therefore aim to explore specific biomarkers in a cohort of tumor types to distinguish a particular tumor subgroup.

  Transcription factors such as cAMP response element binding protein 1 (CREB1) and c-Jun play a pivotal role in orchestrating oncogenesis and immune modulation, thereby providing a strategic avenue for precision immunotherapy. CREB1 is a leucine zipper transcription factor that binds to cAMP-responsive elements and mediates transcriptional programs critical for tumorigenesis and tumor progression[10,11]. CREB1 activation contributes to cancer cell plasticity and promotes pro-tumorigenic cell states[12–14]. c-Jun, serving as the DNA-binding subunit of the AP-1 complex, complex, is the first identified oncogene, with its alterations driving oncogenic transformation[15]. c-Jun not only dynamically controls cancer cell growth and metastasis but also mediates immune evasion by activating the death of T cells[16,17]. The overexpression of c-Jun in chimeric antigen receptor T cells increases both the population and functional capacity of cells, enhancing antitumor potency[18]. Critically, CREB and c-Jun signaling pathways may drive oncogenesis and immune evasion in bladder cancer via molecular crosstalk and functional cooperation[19,20].

  Building upon the established biological synergy between CREB1 and c-Jun, we leveraged the Molecular Signatures Database to curate the experimentally validated ‘CREBP1CJUN_01’ gene set. This authoritative resource identified high-confidence target genes that are co-regulated by CREB1 and/or c-Jun, similar to how RELA’s regulatory network was determined through its interacting partners. With this molecularly annotated gene ensemble as our basis, we obtained a bladder cancer-specific functional signature by intersecting these targets. Utilizing malignant cell transcriptomes derived from single-cell RNA sequencing data, we established 116 CREB–c-Jun transcription factor target-related differential genes (CR.Sig) and refined to 20 CREB–c-Jun transcription factor target-related important feature genes (Hub-CR.Sig) for subsequent mechanistic investigation in bladder cancer.

  This study aims to mechanistically elucidate the coordinated regulatory network of Hub-CR.Sig in bladder cancer pathobiology. Our current study, through the integration of multi-omics analysis, aims to enhance the clinical application of CREB–c-Jun network biology for diagnostic purposes. Identify hub genes and their interactions with other transcription factors, as tools and therapeutic targets in bladder cancer.

### Analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing data
  Single-cell RNA sequencing data were analyzed using Seurat v4.0.6[30]. Doublet detection and quality control were performed using the Scrublet R package[31]. Cells with fewer than 300 detected genes per cell or mitochondrial gene counts exceeding 20% of the total reads were excluded. Data normalization and standardization were performed using principal component analysis (PCA). Batch effect correction was performed using the Harmony R package. Dimensionality reduction and visualization was conducted using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). Differential expressed genes in specific cell types were identified using the “FindMarkers” function with thresholds of |logFC| ≥ 0.25 and q-value < 0.00001. Differential expressed CREB–c-Jun transcription factor target-related genes were enriched for Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways using the clusterProfiler R package. GO terms and KEGG pathways with P-value < 0.05 and q-value < 0.25 were considered statistically significant.


### Single-sample gene set enrichment analysis
  “CREB–c-Jun transcription factor target-related gene score” was calculated using single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA). This was based on the ‘CREBP1CJUN_01’ gene set (containing 264 CREB-c-Jun transcription factor target-related genes) from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB). The ssGSEA algorithm from the R package Gene Set Variation Analysis was applied to compute the enrichment score of this gene set for each single-cell expression matrix. This score represents the overall activation level of the CREB–c-Jun transcription factor target-related genes in each cell, with a higher score indicating stronger pathway activity. For single-cell data (e.g., GSE115978 and GSE123813), this score was used to compare the enrichment of the CREB-c-Jun pathway across different cell subtypes.


### Construction of machine learning-based prognostic risk model
  The five ICI RNA-Seq datasets mentioned above were used for maching learning. Batch effects among the five ICI RNA-Seq datasets were removed using the ComBat method. The merged dataset was then randomly divided into two subsets: a training set (80%, n = 618) and a validation set (20%, n = 154). Six machine learning algorithms – namely, bagged trees, Bayesian methods, learning vector quantization, Boruta, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), and random forest – were employed to construct prognostic risk models.


#### Bagged trees
  Core principle: Generates multiple training subsets through bootstrap sampling, with each subset constructing an independent decision tree model. Results are integrated via voting or averaging to reduce the overfitting risk of a single decision tree and enhance model stability.

  Screening criterion: The variable importance score of each gene across all decision trees is calculated. Genes ranked in the top 30% based on this score are selected as candidate genes by this algorithm.


#### Bayesian
  Core principle: Based on Bayes’ theorem, the posterior probability is computed by combining prior probability with data likelihood to assess the association strength between genes and prognostic outcomes. This method is suitable for feature selection in high-dimensional data with small sample sizes.

  Screening criterion: A gene is considered a candidate if its 95% confidence interval for the posterior probability does not include 1, indicating a statistically significant association (P < 0.05) between gene expression and patient survival risk.


#### Learning vector quantization
  Core principle: A supervised neural network algorithm that defines prototype vectors to represent different categories (high-risk/low-risk). By measuring the distance between gene expression vectors and the prototype vectors, sample classification and feature importance ranking are achieved.

  Screening criterion: Based on the contribution of genes to the classification boundary, a weight coefficient is calculated. Genes with an absolute weight coefficient >0.1 are selected as significant contributors to prognostic classification.


#### Boruta
  Core principle: A feature selection algorithm based on random forest, which compares the importance of original gene features with randomly shuffled “shadow features” to identify truly effective genes whose importance significantly exceeds that of the shadow features.

  Screening criterion: Using the default significance test (P < 0.05), genes that are confirmed as “Confirmed” after multiple iterations of verification are selected as candidate genes. “Tentative” and “Rejected” genes are excluded.


#### Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator
  Core principle: Adds an L1 regularization term to the loss function to shrink the coefficients of unimportant genes to zero, achieving both feature selection and model regularization. This method is suitable for high-dimensional reduction of gene expression data.

  Screening criterion: The optimal regularization parameter λ (lambda.min) is determined through cross-validation (five-old). Genes with non-zero coefficients in the model are retained as candidate genes, while those with zero coefficients are considered to have no prognostic predictive value.


#### Random forest
  Core principle: Combines bootstrap sampling with random feature selection (each tree uses only a subset of features) to construct multiple decision trees. Ensemble learning reduces variance and improves model generalizability. Gene importance is calculated based on the decrease in Gini coefficient or out-of-bag error.

  Screening criterion: The mean decrease in Gini coefficient is computed for each gene. Genes with a mean decrease Gini > 0.01 are selected as those that significantly contribute to node purity improvement.

  Genes consistently identified by at least three machine learning algorithms were designated as Hub-CR.Sig. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was conducted using the R survival package to evaluate differences in overall survival between high-score and low-score groups.


### Prognostic evaluation of the bladder cancer risk model
  Cox regression analyses, both univariate and multivariate, were conducted incorporating the prognostic risk score and a selection of clinical variables, following a rigorous process of variable selection and clinical relevance assessment. Variables with P-value < 0.10 were screened for Cox univariate analysis. The outcomes of both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were presented visually through forest plots to demonstrate the distribution of risk score and clinical features. Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, also called sensitivity curves, were generated based on risk score and overall survival using the R survivalROC package. Decision curve analysis (DCA) plots were generated using the ggDCA R package.


### Classification of bladder cancer subtypes
  Bladder cancer subtypes were identified based on Hub-CR.Sig using the R package ConsensusClusterPlus. The Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) algorithm was applied to analyze expression matrices from the TCGA-bladder cancer database, providing insights into potential immunotherapy responses. Differences in TIDE immune scores across subtypes were assessed using Mann-Whitney U tests (Wilcoxon Rank Sum Test). Masked Somatic Mutation data obtained from TCGA were utilized as Single Nucleotide Polymorphism data in bladder cancers and pre-processed using VarScan. Mutational profiles were visualized using the maftools R package.


### Construction of regulatory network
  The transcription factors that regulate Hub-CR.Sig were analyzed using ChIPBase. The miRNAs targeting Hub-CR.Sig were identified through TarBase. The drug-target interactions involving Hub-CR.Sig were predicted utilizing the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database. The regulatory networks involving mRNA-transcription factor, mRNA-miRNA, and mRNA-drug interactions for Hub-CR.Sig were visualized with Cytoscape.


### RAD51C prognostic analysis
  Clinical correlations between RAD51C expression and clinicopathological variables were assessed through the comparisons of RAD51C expression across different groups (bladder cancer tissues versus normal tissues and bladder cancer cluster 1 versus cluster 2 in TCGA-bladder cancer). The prognostic significance was assessed using Kaplan-Meier analysis to determine overall survival rates. The association between RAD51C and immune cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME) was examined using ssGSEA and CIBERSORT algorithms.


### Gene set enrichment analysis
  The TCGA-BLCA dataset was divided into high RAD51C expression and low RAD51C expression groups based on the median RAD51C expression. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the limma R package, with differentially expressed genes defined by adjusted P-value < 0.05 and fold change >2 or <−2. GSEA was performed using clusterProfiler (v4.10.0), with significant enrichment determined by adjusted P-value < 0.05 and q-value < 0.05.


### RAD51C expression analysis
  Transcriptomic data and clinicopathological information were obtained from TCGA. RAD51C expression differences between tumor and normal tissues were compared using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, while RAD51C expression differences between paired samples were compared using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. ROC curves were plotted based on RAD51C gene expression and patient survival status to evaluate the predictive power of RAD51C for survival outcomes. RAD51C gene expression differences across different T stages and N stages were compared using one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal-Wallis test. RAD51C gene expression differences between different M stages were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or t-test. Analysis was performed using R (version 4.2.1) with ggplot2 (v3.4.4), stats (v4.2.1), car (v3.1-0), pROC (v1.18.0), survival (v3.3.1), and survminer (v0.4.9).


### Animal experimentation
  All mice enjoy basic physiological welfare, that is, they were free from hunger and thirst; they enjoy environmental welfare, with comfortable living quarters; they enjoy hygiene welfare (SPF), reducing the incidence of diseases and injuries; behavioral welfare, ensuring the freedom of their natural behaviors; and psychological welfare, reducing the fear and anxiety of the animals. The experimental design strictly adhered to the principles of animal welfare ethics review and conformed to the 3 R principles. After the tumor observation period ended, the mice were anesthetized with intraperitoneal injection of ethylene bromide and sacrificed by cervical dislocation. The tumors were removed and labeled with the group and ear tag numbers. The work has been reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines (Animals in Research: Reporting In Vivo Experiments)[32].


#### Xenograft model tumorigenesis assay
  Ten male BALB/c-nude mice were purchased from ** University Animal Experiment Center (IACUC Approval No. **). Mice were randomly divided into two groups and injected subcutaneously with 5 × 106 5637 cells transfected with negative control shRNA or shRNA targeting RAD51C. The tumor size was monitored every 5 days. Mice were anesthetized on day 40, and tumor tissues were dissected, weighed, and fixed for staining.


#### Syngeneic tumorigenesis assay
  Fourteen male C57BL/6 mice were purchased from ** University Animal Experiment Center (IACUC Approval No. **). Each mouse received an in situ injection of 1–2 × 105 MB49 cells into the bladder. The mice were sacrificed 1 week and 2–3 weeks after injection respectively, and their bladder tissues were collected for fixation.


### Tissue staining procedures

#### Hematoxylin and eosin
  After dewaxing and rehydration, tissue sections were stained with Harris hematoxylin (HHS32; Sigma-Aldrich) for 5 min. The tissue was differentiated in 1% acid ethanol and subsequently counterstained with eosin Y (HT110116; Sigma-Aldrich) for 2 min. Following this, the tissue sections were dehydrated and mounted using neutral balsam.


#### Masson
  The tissue sections underwent dewaxing, rehydration, and fixation procedures. Subsequently, they were immersed in Bouin’s solution (HT10132; Sigma-Aldrich) at room temperature for 1 h. After that, the sections were stained with Weigert’s iron hematoxylin for 5 min and Ponceau red (P7170; Sigma-Aldrich) for 10 min, differentiated in 1% phosphomolybdic acid aqueous solution (P4869; Sigma-Aldrich), stained with aniline blue solution (415 049; Sigma-Aldrich) for 5 min, and subsequently dehydrated and mounted.


#### TUNEL
  Tissue sections were processed using the In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit (11 684 817 910; Roche) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, tissue sections were subjected to Proteinase K digestion for 15 min, followed by incubation with the TUNEL (FITC Fluorescein) reaction mixture at 37°C for 1 h. Subsequently, the sections were incubated with panCK primary antibody (BX50143; Bailing) at 4°C overnight in the dark to label the tumor cell regions. Then, universal secondary antibody was added and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Following incubation, the sections were stained with a fluorescent dye with an emission wavelength of 690 nm at room temperature for 15 min in the dark. For nuclear counterstaining, the sections were incubated with DAPI (ab104139; Abcam) at room temperature for 10 min and protected from light. Subsequently, after Mayer’s hematoxylin counterstaining, the tissue sections were dehydrated and mounted.


#### IHC
  After performing antigen retrieval in citrate buffer at 95°C for 20 min, the tissue sections were incubated with primary antibodies targeting Ki67 (9129; CST), and RAD51C (31 678-1-AP; Proteintech, Wuhan), followed by the secondary antibody used was horseradish peroxidase-conjugated rabbit IgG (K4003; Agilent Dako). Tissue sections were processed using DAB substrate (SK-4105; Vector Labs,) and counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin, followed by dehydration and mounting. The staining intensity was classified as follows: 0 for negative, 1 for pale yellow (weak), 2 for brown yellow (positive), and 3 for brown (strong). The percentage of positive cells was scored on a scale of 1–4, corresponding to <25%, 26%–50%, 51%–75%, and >75% positivity, respectively. The final IHC score was determined by combining the intensity and percentage scores and calculated by multiplying the intensity score by the percentage score.


#### mIHC staining
  All paraffin samples were dewaxed for transparency, antigen repair and blocking, and then stained overnight successively with RAD51C (31 678-1-AP; Proteintech) and α-SMA (14 395-1-AP; Proteintech) antibodies. Following incubation with each primary antibody, the samples were incubated with the corresponding secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h and labeled with the appropriate fluorescent dye. After completion of all staining procedures, the samples were subjected to nuclear staining with DAPI for 15 min and finally mounted in neutral resin. All slides were scanned at 40× magnification with a Leica SCN400 slide scanner (Leica Biosystems, Wetzlar, Germany), then imported into the image analysis software APTime®, and a digital image was captured.


### Western blot
  Tissue and cell samples were collected, incubated in radio immunoprecipitation assay lysis buffer (RIPA) lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) containing protease inhibitor (Roche), and then centrifuged at 12 000 × g at 4°C for 15 min to collect the supernatant. Protein samples were applied to 6% and 12% SDS-PAGE for electrophoresis and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% skimmed milk at room temperature for 1 h, probed with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight, and then incubated with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1–2 h. Anti-RAD51C (84 537-1-RR; Proteintech), anti-BCL-2 (12 789-1-AP; Proteintech), anti-BAX (50 599-2-Ig; Proteintech), anti-BRCA1 (22 362-1-AP; Proteintech), anti-BRCA2 (29 450-1-AP; Proteintech), anti-ATM (27 156-1-AP; proteintech), anti-p-ATM (ET1705-50; HUABIO, Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China), anti-CDK7 (27 027-1-AP,proteintech), anti-p-H2A.X (ET1602-2; HUABIO), and anti-β-actin (A5441; Sigma-Aldrich) primary antibodies were used in this study. Protein bands were illuminated using ECL Western Blotting Substrate (Millipore). Band intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.


### Cell culture and transfection
  The human bladder carcinoma cell line 5637 was purchased from Procell system (Wuhan, Hubei, China). Bladder cancer cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco) and 1% penicillin and streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich) at 37°C under 5% CO2. For cellular transfection, plasmids containing shRNA sequences (shRNA1: GTTGGGATATCTAAAGCAGAAttcaagagaTTCTGCTTTAGATATCCCAAC; shRNA2: CAGAAGAGAATGTCTCACAAAttcaagagaTTTGTGAGACATTCTCTTCTG; shRNA3: AGCATACCCAGGGCTTCATAAttcaagagaTTATGAAGCCCTGGGTATGCT) were obtained from MiaoLing Plasmid Platform (Wuhan, China).


### Cell counting kit-8 assay
  Cells were seeded in 96-well plates with triplicate wells per group. After 24, 48, and 72 h of culture, cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) solution was added to each well. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader to generate growth curves.


### Colony formation assay
  Bladder cancer cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 200 cells/mL. After colony formation, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 1% crystal violet to count colonies.


### Transwell invasion assay
  Bladder cancer cells were seeded in the upper chamber pre-coated with Matrigel at a density of 5 × 104 cells/mL. The upper chamber was filled with 300 μL serum-free medium, while the lower chamber was filled with complete medium supplemented with 10% FBS. After 24 h of incubation, non-migrated cells on the upper surface of the upper chamber were removed, while cells invaded through the membrane to the lower surface were fixed and stained with crystal violet. Invaded cells were quantified using ImageJ software.


### Wound healing assay
  Bladder cancer cells were seeded in six-well plates at a density of 4 × 105 cells/mL. When cells reached 90% confluence, a straight scratch was created in the monolayer using a sterile 1 mL pipette tip. After 48 h of incubation, the wound area was examined. Cell migration rate was calculated as [(scratch width at 0 h—scratch width at 48 h)/scratch width at 0 h] × 100%. Quantitative analysis was conducted by delineating the wound area using ImageJ software and calculating the wound area.


### Comet assay
  Bladder cancer cells were trypsinized and pipetted into a cell suspension containing 10 000 cells in 10 μL PBS. Slides were pre-warmed, coated with 0.5% normal-melting-point agarose, covered with coverslips, and solidified at 4°C for 10 min. Cell suspension mixed with 75 μL 1.0% low-melting-point agarose was layered onto the initial normal-melting-point agarose, solidified, and then covered with a third normal-melting-point agarose layer. Following coverslip removal, slides were immersed in RIPA lysis buffer for 150 min and subjected to alkaline unwinding in 1× Tris-Borate-EDTA buffer electrophoresis buffer for 20 min and then electrophoresed at 25 V for 30 min at 4°C. Slides were dried, neutralized in Tris-HCl for 15 min, stained with ethidium bromide under light-protected conditions for 20 min, and visualized using fluorescence microscopy.


### Dual-Luciferase reporter assay
  Cell transfection: HEK-293 T cells were seeded in 24-well plates (5 × 104 cells/well) and cultured to 70%–80% confluence. Cells were co-transfected with reporter plasmid (0.5 μg/well), pRL-TK (0.02 μg/well), and target plasmid(0.2 μg/well) using Lipofectamine 3000 following the manufacturer’s protocol. Luciferase activity detection: At 48 h post-transfection, cells were lysed with passive lysis buffer for 15 min. Firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were measured sequentially using Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System and GloMax 20/20 Luminometer.


### RNA sequencing and bioinformatic analysis
  Total RNA was extracted from transfected bladder cancer cells using TRIzol reagent. RNA purity and concentration were assessed with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), while integrity was evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The sequencing libraries were prepared using the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit. The sequencing was conducted using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform by oebiotech (Shanghai, China), a system known for its high throughput and flexibility, capable of delivering up to 200 billion reads and 6 Tb of data within 2 days, as per industry standards. Gene expression levels were quantified in terms of FPKM values. PCA was performed using R software (v3.2.0). Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2. Genes with q-value < 0.05 and fold change >2 or <−2 were screened as differentially expressed genes. Differentially expressed genes were functionally enriched for GO terms, KEGG pathways, and Reactome pathways. GSEA was conducted by ranking genes based on differential expression.


### Statistical analysis
  All bioinformatics data processing and analyses in this study were performed using R software (Version 4.2.2). Continuous variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Unless otherwise specified, correlations between different molecules were calculated using Spearman correlation analysis, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all results. Data were reported as mean ± SEM. Statistical analysis was performed using Graphpad Prism 8.4. Student t-test was used for the comparison between two groups while one-way ANOVA was for the comparison between three groups. Statistical significance was set at P-value < 0.05.

## Discussion
  As a globally prevalent chronic malignant disease, cancer has become a core public health challenge threatening human health and quality of life. Currently, a comprehensive cancer treatment system has been established, based on chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapy, with immunotherapy emerging as a new core strategy. Among them, ICIs targeting PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 have become first-line treatments for advanced tumors due to their unique advantage of reactivating anti-tumor immune response and achieving long-term survival benefits, demonstrating significant clinical efficacy in various solid tumors such as melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer, and bladder cancer[3,33]. However, immunotherapy resistance (including primary and acquired resistance) remains a critical bottleneck restricting its clinical translation[34,35]. Approximately 60%–80% of cancer patients are unresponsive to ICIs or experience disease progression after treatment, and the underlying mechanisms are complex and not yet fully elucidated. It is currently widely recognized that TME remodeling is one of the key inducements of drug resistance[36,37]. Tumor cell immune escape, immunosuppressive remodeling of the TME, T cell exhaustion, and abnormal activation of signaling pathways are all key factors driving immunotherapy resistance. Therefore, in-depth analysis of the molecular mechanisms of immunotherapy resistance, identification of novel therapeutic targets with clinical translational potential, and subsequent reversal or improvement of drug-resistant phenotypes have become urgent scientific issues in the field of tumor immunology and translational medicine.

  As core nodes of gene expression regulatory networks, transcription factors precisely regulate the transcriptional activation and silencing of downstream genes by specifically binding to the promoter or enhancer regions of target genes, playing a crucial regulatory role in the entire process of tumor initiation, progression, invasion, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance, and have always been a research hotspot in the oncology field[38–40]. Abnormal activation of signaling pathways mediated by transcription factors has been confirmed to be closely related to the resistance of various immunotherapeutic drugs, becoming an important research direction to break through the dilemma of drug resistance[41–44]. Transcription factors are classified into multiple families based on their distinct structural domains. Members of different families mediate signal pathway transduction through unique domains, construct complex gene regulatory networks, and participate in the regulation of tumor malignant phenotypes[45]. Both c-Jun and CREB belong to the bZIP family of transcription factors and are typical inducible transcriptional activators with low basal expression levels under physiological conditions. Their activities can be activated by various extracellular signals such as growth factors, cytokines, and stress signals. C-Jun is mainly phosphorylated and activated through the JNK/p38/ERK MAPK signaling pathway[46], while CREB relies on phosphorylation at Ser133 (regulated by kinases such as PKA, ERK, and CaMK). After activation, they recruit CBP/p300 coactivators to form functional complexes, thereby regulating the expression of downstream target genes[47]. In recent years, a growing number of studies have confirmed that c-Jun and CREB play key roles in regulating tumor immunotherapy resistance, and their abnormal activation can promote tumor immune escape through multiple molecular pathways, significantly impairing the therapeutic efficacy of ICIs[48–52].

  Specifically, c-Jun mediates immunotherapy resistance through multiple mechanisms: on the one hand, c-Jun can activate the JNK-c-Jun signaling axis to upregulate PD-L1 expression and enhance the binding ability between tumor cells and PD-1 on the surface of T cells, thereby inhibiting the anti-tumor activity of T cells[53]; on the other hand, c-Jun can directly regulate the transcription of ectonucleotide pyrophosphatase/phosphodiesterase 1 (ENPP1), inhibit the activation of the cGAS-STING innate immune pathway by hydrolyzing 2’3’-cGAMP via ENPP1, reduce CD8⁺ T cell infiltration, and ultimately mediate cancer immunotherapy resistance[54]. In addition, c-Jun can also regulate PD-L1 glycosylation modification through the TGF-β1/c-Jun/STT3A pathway, enhance PD-L1 protein stability and ligand-binding capacity, promote nasopharyngeal carcinoma immune escape and reduce ICIs sensitivity[55], indicating that its mechanism of regulating immune resistance is universal across tumor types. CREB also plays an important role in regulating immunotherapy resistance, with its core mechanisms focusing on TME remodeling and T cell function inhibition: studies have found that PD-L1 in tumor-derived extracellular vesicles can activate the CREB signaling pathway, induce T cell lipid metabolism reprogramming and senescence, inhibit T cell proliferation and effector function, and ultimately lead to PD-L1 inhibitor resistance, while blocking CREB signaling can reverse the T cell senescence phenotype and significantly enhance immunotherapeutic efficacy[51]. Meanwhile, CREB can drive tumor cell metabolic reprogramming by regulating key genes of glycolysis and glutamine metabolism, create an immunosuppressive microenvironment, and further exacerbate tumor immune escape by upregulating PD-L1 expression and immunosuppressive cytokine secretion[51].

  Given the critical regulatory roles of c-Jun and CREB in immunotherapy resistance, this study aims to systematically explore the expression patterns, regulatory networks, and correlations with immunotherapeutic responses of downstream target genes of c-Jun and CREB in various tumors, using immunotherapy-related single-cell sequencing datasets and bioinformatics analysis methods. Through successive screening and validation, this study ultimately focuses on the role and molecular mechanisms of core target genes in bladder cancer, and in-depth explores their regulatory pathways in bladder cancer immunotherapy resistance, so as to provide a theoretical basis and experimental foundation for developing combined therapeutic strategies targeting the CREB–c-Jun-target gene axis and improving the efficacy of bladder cancer immunotherapy.

  In the current study, we screened 116 CR.Sig based on single-cell sequencing dataset and further selected hub signature genes using machine learning algorithms from a pan-cancer database. Multiple machine learning algorithms, including bagged trees, Bayesian, LQV, Boruta, LASSO, and random forest, have been extensively applied in the field of oncology for feature selection and predictive modeling[56–61]. These algorithms resolve tumor heterogeneity, map spatial transcriptomic drivers of metastasis, predict microenvironment-driven drug resistance and therapeutic susceptibilities, and decode resistance mechanisms through multi-omics data integration. The application of these algorithms enables dynamic risk stratification, early cancer detection, and identification of critical therapeutic targets. In our study, we established a selection threshold requiring genes to be consistently identified by at least three distinct machine learning methodologies. The intersectional implementation of these approaches revealed 20 Hub-CR.Sig with significant therapeutic implications.

  Employing these identified Hub-CR.Sig, we successfully constructed a prognostic risk model for bladder cancer and confirmed their predictive value for patient outcomes. Remarkably, bladder cancer is a heterogeneous carcinoma primarily classified into non-muscle-invasive and muscle-invasive subtypes with the latter constituting the majority of cases[62,63]. In line with the classification of bladder cancer, we utilized cell clustering to separate bladder cancers into two distinct clusters exhibiting significant differences in TIDE immunoscores, mutation frequencies, and druggable target categories. These Hub-CR.Sig are therefore critically important, not only for their robust prognostic capability in bladder cancer but also for their utility in elucidating tumor subtypes and underlying molecular characteristics, including immunogenicity and mutation landscapes, which are essential for understanding disease heterogeneity and guiding therapeutic strategies.

  Among these Hub-CR.Sig, RAD51C surfaced as a principal signature gene, demonstrating exceptional prominence across multiple algorithms. In the TCGA database, we found that RAD51C expression was increased in bladder cancer samples, which was negatively correlated with prognosis, and significantly higher in T3/T4 stage tumors. The significant upregulation of RAD51C in bladder tissues was also verified in clinical samples by IHC and WB experiments.

  The RAD51 family member RAD51C is involved in DNA damage repair, thereby acting as a tumor suppressor[64,65]. However, beyond its canonical tumor-suppressing function, RAD51C demonstrates an oncogenic duality in cancer pathobiology, mainly through driving therapy resistance[66]. RAD51C functions as a cancer predisposition gene in various types of cancer, such as breast, ovarian, and prostate malignancies[67,68]. RAD51C critically regulates cancer cell behavior and modulates therapeutic responses across cancers. Reduced RAD51C enhances the sensitivity to Olaparib, a therapeutic agent for cancer, and promotes apoptosis in gastric breast cancer cells[66]. In non-small cell lung cancer, RAD51C serves as an independent predictor of shortened overall survival and demonstrates significant oncogenic driver potential, characterized by elevated expression in tumor tissues. RAD51C knockdown amplifies cytotoxicity induced by DNA-damaging agents and potentiates cancer cell apoptosis[69]. Similarly, RAD51 is highly expressed in oral squamous cell carcinoma, where genetic knockout suppresses tumor proliferation[70]. However, the specific pathobiological functions of RAD51C in bladder cancer remain undefined.

  Here, our functional validation demonstrated that RAD51C knockdown profoundly suppressed bladder cancer progression both in vitro and in vivo. In vitro transfection of bladder cancer cells with sh-RAD51C significantly impaired cellular metabolic activity evaluated via CCK-8 assay and abolished long-term proliferative capacity evaluated via colony formation assay. Transwell invasion assay and wound healing assay further demonstrated that RAD51C knockdown concurrently inhibited cellular invasion and migration potential. Crucially, comet assays revealed severe DNA repair deficiency in RAD51C-silenced bladder cancer cells. DNA repair defects trigger genomic instability and may lead to mutation accumulation and impaired cellular proliferation, invasion, and other malignant capabilities. Consistent with in vitro findings, in vivo observations showed that RAD51C knockdown profoundly suppressed xenograft tumor growth, attenuated proliferation, elevated apoptosis, and remodeled tumor stroma through collagen deposition. WB results deciphered RAD51C depletion triggered pro-apoptotic reprogramming by increasing the ratio of BAX/BCL-2[71], enlarged DNA damage by upregulating p-H2A.X and p-ATM[72], and disrupted cell cycle progression by suppressing CDK7[73]. Conversely, elevated BRCA1/BRCA2 levels may represent a compensatory repair mechanism and could suggest adaptive homologous recombination activation.

  When investigating the potential mechanisms of RAD51C in bladder cancer via GSEA, we found that the RAD51C high-expression group was significantly enriched in several less common gene sets. The “Blanco Melo Bronchial Epithelial Cells Influenza A Del Ns1 Infection Dn” gene set is derived from transcriptomic studies of bronchial epithelial cells infected with influenza A virus lacking the Ns1 protein, with core functions involved in virus-induced cellular stress responses, DNA damage repair, and the regulation of immune inflammation[74]. The “Wu Apoptosis By Cdkn1a Via Tp53” gene set focuses on the TP53-mediated Cdkn1a (p21)-dependent apoptotic pathway, and its core genes are implicated in cell cycle arrest and the regulation of DNA damage-induced apoptosis[75]. The “Tang Senescence Tp53 Targets Dn” gene set contains senescence-related target genes regulated by TP53, with core functions including the inhibition of cellular senescence and the maintenance of cell proliferation[76]. These three gene sets are consistent with the most well-characterized function of RAD51C in promoting damage repair; thus, high RAD51C expression in bladder cancer may help cancer cells resist oxidative stress in the TME, facilitate DNA damage repair and anti-apoptosis, and thereby promote tumor cell survival.

  Notably, the “Croonquist Il6 Deprivation Dn” gene set includes genes with downregulated expression following blockade of the IL-6 signaling pathway, whose core functions involve inflammatory responses, cell proliferation, and the regulation of the STAT3 pathway[77]. The IL-6/STAT3 pathway is a key driver of bladder cancer progression, and its aberrant activation can promote tumor invasion and immune evasion[78]. The enriched association between RAD51C and this gene set suggests that the damage repair gene RAD51C may exhibit functional synergy with the IL-6/STAT3 pathway[79], which collectively drives the malignant phenotype of bladder cancer cells. This finding provides a novel insight that the functions of RAD51C may not be restricted to tumor cells themselves but also extend to other immune cells within the TME.

  Based on the aforementioned findings, we hypothesize that RAD51C, as a recombination repair gene, exerts a crucial regulatory role in tumorigenesis and progression. However, knockout of RAD51C in bladder cancer cells may result in deficiencies in global genomic repair function, or at least partial impairment, thereby inducing genomic instability. This genomic instability is presumably one of the key mechanisms contributing to the significant attenuation of the malignant phenotype in bladder cancer cells. Consequently, the biological consequences associated with genomic instability in tumor cells warrant further in-depth investigation.

  In the IHC of clinical samples, we specifically observed high expression of RAD51C in fibroblasts. Similarly, in xenograft tumor samples, Masson staining also revealed an abnormal increase in collagen fiber accumulation in tumor tissues of the RAD51C knockout group. All the above observations suggest that the expression of RAD51C in tumor cells appears to be closely associated with fibroblasts in the TME. First, we performed mIHC staining and found colocalization of RAD51C with fibroblasts in 26 clinical samples, which is a particularly interesting finding. In addition, we also noted that RAD51C+ fibroblasts were correlated with clinical T stage. These findings further highlight the importance of the RAD51C gene in bladder cancer.

  As a core component of the TME, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) can mediate ICIs resistance through multiple pathways, including regulating immune cell activity, remodeling the extracellular matrix (ECM), and secreting immunosuppressive factors[80–82]. Tumor cells may release certain cytokines to induce changes in fibroblast function, thereby participating in the occurrence and development of ICIs resistance. CAFs mainly include inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs), myofibroblastic CAFs (myCAFs), and other subtypes.

  MyCAFs are the most common subtype of CAFs in solid tumors such as bladder cancer, lung cancer, and colorectal cancer[83,84]. They are polarized by TGF-β secreted by tumor cells, which activates the SMAD2/3 pathway and non-classical PI3K/AKT and MAPK pathways in CAFs. The core markers of myCAFs are α-SMA, FAP, SMA, and PDGFRβ[80,85]. A key characteristic of myCAFs is their strong contractility and ECM secretion capacity, making them the main contributors to tumor stromal sclerosis. Collagen fibers secreted by myCAFs can form a dense matrix barrier through cross-linking, thereby hindering immune cell infiltration[86–88].

  iCAFs are polarized by IL-6 and IL-1α/β secreted by tumor cells, which activate the JAK-STAT3 pathway in CAFs[83,89–91]. IL-6 secreted by tumor cells can specifically bind to the IL-6 R on the surface of CAFs, activate STAT3 phosphorylation, and further induce the high expression of markers such as IL-6 and CXCL12[90–93]. iCAFs are the main source of immunosuppressive factors in the microenvironment and the core subtype mediating immune cell recruitment and functional inhibition, a function that has been validated in multiple centers in solid tumors such as bladder cancer and lung cancer[94,95].

  This study aims to initially explore the changes in fibroblasts after RAD51C knockout in bladder cancer cells. After RNA sequencing, we found that multiple pathways, including JAK-STAT, were significantly upregulated in the knockout group. As master orchestrators of cell fate, JAK-STAT cascades critically coordinate proliferation dynamics, lineage specification, programmed cell death, and immunological functions[96]. Activated JAK-STAT signaling further orchestrates immune evasion, providing mechanistic rationale for immunotherapy[97]. We speculate that the activated JAK-STAT pathway in tumor cells may also have a certain promoting effect on the transformation of fibroblasts into iCAFs. This step was not completed, which is a limitation of our study.

  In this study, for the first time, we classified bladder cancer into two subtypes by leveraging this hub gene set and identified potential clinical therapeutic agents. Moreover, we uncovered the specific expression of the core gene RAD51C in bladder cancer, particularly in CAFs. This finding warrants further investigation into its underlying mechanism and potential clinical significance. Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. First, the clinical sample size was relatively small, consisting of only 26 cases. In the future, we plan to increase the sample size to validate our findings and minimize research bias resulting from the limited sample size. Second, the exploration of the molecular mechanism lacks depth. In subsequent research, we will focus on the downstream pathways and target genes of RAD51C. Additionally, we will explore the pivotal role of this gene within the regulatory network of potential drugs and molecular compounds, aiming to bridge the gap between basic research and clinical translation. Our research is expected to lay a novel foundation for the diagnosis and treatment of bladder cancer based on its emerging clinical molecular characteristics.


## Conclusion
  Collectively, our current study discovered Hub-CR.Sig, provided a robust prognostic and subtype-classification framework for bladder cancer, and identified hub signature gene RAD51C as an important bladder cancer driver. Our study demonstrates the high therapeutic potential of targeting Hub-CR.Sig, particularly RAD51C, for precision intervention in bladder cancer. Due to resource limitations, we have not included an external validation cohort temporarily. However, we recognize the importance of external validation cohorts. In the future, we will prioritize collecting prospective clinical samples or waiting for the release of larger-scale bladder cancer immunotherapy cohorts to perform adequate external validation.
