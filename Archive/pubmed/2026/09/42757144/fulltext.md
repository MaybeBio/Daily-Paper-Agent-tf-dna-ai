# BAN-SDBPred: Improving
Single-Stranded and Double-Stranded
DNA-Binding Protein Prediction Using an Attention Network with Bilinear
Convolution and Adaptive Sampling Strategy


## Abstract
  DNA binding proteins play essential roles in numerous biological mechanisms. The DBPs can be either single-stranded binding (SSBs) or double-stranded binding (DSBs) to a DNA molecule. The in-depth identification of SSBs and DSBs has been a hot topic in bioinformatics and is involved in the drug discovery process. Traditional experimental methods failed to characterize the types of DBPs because of high cost and time constraints. While computational prediction of novel SSBs and DSBs has made significant progress, there are still challenges remaining in enhancing overall prediction performance. Methods: Here, we develop a novel BAN-SDBPred (Bilinear Attention Network for Single and Double Stranded DNA-Binding Protein Prediction) method. BAN-SDBPred leverages the evolutionary features by protein language model-based Evolutionary Scale Modeling 2 (ESM2), ProtT5, and a histogram of oriented gradient-based residue pairwise energy content matrix (RECM-HOG)-transformed energy estimation features from sequence alone. Then, the adaptive neighborhood-based sampling (ANBS) algorithm was adopted to solve the imbalance issue. Compared to other deep learning models, the bilinear attention network (BAN) learns the local and global enriched features from the sequences. Extensive experimental results anticipate that BAN-SDBPred outperforms the existing predictors in terms of all performance measures, such as Acc, F1, MCC, etc., on the training and independent test data. Our designed model has significant advantages in discriminating SSBs and DSBs from DBPs with an improved Acc of 2%, Precision of 4.5%, F1 of 21%, MCC of 9%, and area under curve (AUC) of 20%, respectively. We expect this research will help to predict large-scale novel SSBs and DSBs in particular and other binding problems in general. All data and models are available at 10.5281/zenodo.18718092.


## Introduction
  DNA-binding proteins (DBPs) mediate protein-DNA interactions and serve as essential mediators in cellular processes including gene expression regulation, DNA repair, replication, and recombination. DBPs reflect the combined effects of intermolecular forces i.e., electrostatic forces, van der Waals, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic effects with protein structural properties. −   Past studies have proven that DBPs are closely associated with for example deadly diseases such as cancer (liver, lung, and breast cancer), chronic diseases (asthma, HIV AIDS), and antibiotics. DBPs are categorized into two functional groups from broad spectrum: double stranded DBP (DSBs) and single stranded DBP (SSBs). −   The fundamental difference between these two classes of DBPs is due to their biological function, structural-based specificity of DNA, and interaction mechanism. DSBs are involved in numerous cellular-based functions like DNA repair, transcription factors, or maintaining genomic stability. In contrast, SSBs are essential for DNA repair, recombination, and replication processes and are capable of binding with high affinity to single-stranded DNA. ,  Therefore, understanding the binding activity of DBPs and their functional type is critical for advance precision medicine, antibody–antigen interactions, and drug discovery. Conventional-based experimental techniques such as X-ray crystallography, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM), and filter binding assays have been used to investigate the binding affinity of DBPs as SSBs and DSBs. However, these laboratory-based methods require huge amounts of time, money, and high resources to identify the DBPs’ functions. −    To overcome these shortcomings, computational techniques can provide an alternative solution to highlight as a powerful, reliable, efficient, and cost-effective method to predict SSBs and DSBs −   as experimental techniques.

  Over the past decade, a number of researchers have made an effort to develop computational methods, including machine learning (ML) −   and deep learning (DL) approaches, for predicting DBPs and their functional types. These predictors often work on inputs to sequence- and 3D structure-based methods. Applications of evolutionary sequence-based methods such as Wang et al., Sharma et al., Xiong et al., Xiong et al., Dey et al., Sharma et al., SDBP-Pred, and CNN-Pred only required amino acid protein sequences with various feature extraction techniques, including evolutionary information obtained from PSI-BLAST, and other methods use secondary structure, amino acid composition (AAC), quasi residue couple (QRC), and physicochemical properties −      for better identification of SSBs. Other feature extraction methods are the pseudoposition-specific scoring matrix (PsePSSM), N-gram features (NGR), and texture descriptors (TD). Sang et al. used hidden Markov models (HMMs) and converted the obtained matrices into fixed-length feature vectors based on AAC, autocovariance transformation (ACT), and cross-covariance transformation (CCT). Thus, sequence-based computational methods are still required for the accurate and genome-wide prediction of SSBs.

  The feature extraction, classification algorithm, and selection of the best features are crucial to the accurate computational prediction of the SSBs. Recently, four accurate benchmarked predictors, Wang et al., proposed a prediction method using amino acid composition, dipeptide composition, and position-specific scoring matrices (PSSMs) as feature descriptors and with support vector machines (SVM) and random forests (RF) as the classification algorithms. Thereafter, SDBP-Pred proposed a feature extraction approach on the basis of PSSMs in aggregation with a K-segmentation method with the notion of a consensus sequence, prior to SVM-based classification of the obtained features’ performance. Likewise, Sharma et al. employed hidden Markov models (HMMs) together with PSSM profiles to construct feature representations for the same prediction task, using monogram and bigram schemes for feature generation, and then applied SVM, RF, and K-nearest neighbors (KNN) classifiers to predict SSBs and DSBs. More recently, CNN-Pred was introduced, which extracted evolutionary features through monogram and bigram profiles derived from PSSM and implemented 1D convolutional neural network (CNN) classifiers to identify SSBs and DSBs.

  Although the above traditional state-of-the-art predictors and the CNN-based predictor CNN-Pred have achieved substantial performance improvement, most of them still rely on either hand-crafted sequence descriptors or single-view evolutionary PLM-based embeddings, which limit their ability to capture discriminative global or local dependencies and to remain robust under severe class imbalance. To overcome these limitations, we propose BAN-SDBPred, a multimodal deep learning framework that robustly discriminates SSBs from DSBs using sequence information. BAN-SDBPred integrates complementary global and local representations by combining evolutionary-scale embedding (ESM2) with a residue energy contact matrix profile encoded via the histogram of oriented gradients (RECM-HOG), as visualized in Figure  . These heterogeneous features are fused through a bilinear attention network (BAN), which facilitates local as fine-grained and global as long-range fusion among multiple modalities, which enables BAN-SDBPred to capture both global dependencies and local binding motifs. Furthermore, the ANBS strategy, along with focal loss, is explicitly applied to balance the classes between DSBs and SSBs, which improves minority-class detection while maintaining overall calibration and generalization. Experimental performance highlights that BAN-SDBPred attains new higher performance by outperforming four existing SSB methods on independent test data sets, as illustrated via Figure  . Extensive ablation studies demonstrate that the strong performance is due to the fusion between ESM2 global embeddings and RECM-HOG local descriptors, with ANBS class balancing, making BAN-SDBPred a generalizable protocol for predicting the binding function of DBPs. BAN-SDBPred outperforms the existing PLM-based predictors in three aspects:By utilizing a bilinear attention network, global evolutionary embeddings (ESM2) are integrated with local structural features (RECM-HOG) to capture the long-range sequence dependencies and local residue interactions simultaneously.The bilinear attention fusion layer biologically magnifies the cross-modal interaction between conserved residues and structural motifs, enabling the layer to learn more interpretable feature insights to bind DNA–protein.By integrating ANBS to address the impact of extreme class imbalance, BAN-SDBPred consistently improves generalization and achieves higher performance over several multimodal protein predictors while retaining interpretability via SHAP analysis. This incorporation of established techniques in a synergistic framework signifies a conceptual advance beyond previous PLM-based SSB/DSB predictors, offering both an improved accuracy and biological interpretability.

### Benchmark Data Sets
  The collection of a data set that is standardized and widely recognized plays a key part in the process of comparing, developing, evaluating, and benchmarking the effectiveness of models in predicting single-stranded binding (SSBs) and double-stranded DNA-binding proteins (DSBs). Standard benchmark data sets are used to assess the quality of research and are key to making reliable predictors. In order to be comparable with previous work, the data sets followed the recent standard in the literature. Two data sets were used: Uniprot1065, which was employed to train the model, and PDB401, which was considered an independent test set to assess the generalization capability of our method.

  The training data set was taken from UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot and contained, initially, 2,136 DSB and 339 SSB sequences. The CD-HIT was used to remove the sequence redundancy, with the sequence identity threshold of 0.7, to obtain the final training set with 873 of DSBs and 183 of SSBs. An independent test data set (PDB401) was obtained from the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/) by using PISCES (http://dunbrack.fccc.edu/Guoli/PISCES.php), a web server that selects NMR and X-ray crystal structures with resolution > 3 Å. Sequence similarity between training and test sets was limited to <30%, and minimum sequence length was 40 amino acids. The CD-HIT toolkit was used to identify the nonredundant protein sequences, thereby obtaining a final independent test set of 124 DSBs and 41 SSBs as represented with an imbalance ratio in Table  . In this research, the DSBs are taken as the positive class and SSBs as the negative class while computing the evaluation measures. To overcome the class imbalance problem in the training data set, a neighborhood selection and adaptive synthesis-based sampling technique called ANBS was employed iteratively to augment the minority class.


### Architectural
Overview of the Proposed Model
  BAN-SDBPred utilizes a dual-encoder-based design combining ESM2 evolutionary embeddings and RECM-HOG structural feature encoders. The RECM-HOG captures local interaction patterns, while ESM2 captures long-range global evolutionary dependencies. A bilinear attention network fuses these complementary modalities through high-order cross-modal interactions, and pooling is applied for a unified representation. To handle class imbalance, we applied ANBS during training. Finally, fully connected layers perform the binary classification of SSBs and DSBs. ##### RECM-HOG Transformation

##### Residue Energy Contact Matrix (RECM)
  Protein sequence stability quantification requires analysis of the interaction between amino acid residues at two levels, intra- and inter-residue contacts. Although energy-based potentials calculated from 3D structures reflect these interaction energies, they cannot be used for proteins without resolved structures, such as intrinsically disordered proteins or sequences without defined tertiary folds. To overcome this limitation, predictive methods have been proposed, which estimate pairwise amino acid interaction energies from primary sequences alone, without any structural constraints, but their performances for fully disordered proteins are suboptimal. In this work, we employ the Residue Energy Contact Matrix (RECM) to encode amino acid interaction propensities, utilizing predicted contact energy parameters, as shown in Table S2 of the Supporting Information: Note S3. The RECM parameters were derived through least-squares optimization, fitting pairwise residue interaction energies from 785 tertiary-structured proteins against 674 corresponding primary sequences. This square matrix shows the self-contact energies on its diagonal and the predicted contact energies between different standard amino acids in the other positions. For a target protein sequence of length L, RECM creates a feature representation that is 20 rows by L columns, changing the input sequence into a set of 20-dimensional vectors that show how different amino acids interact with each other. Formally, the RECM representation can be expressed in eq    RECM=[E1,1E1,2⋯E1,20E2,1E2,2⋯E2,20⋮⋮⋮⋮EL,1EL,2⋯EL,20]L×20 1 where the matrix element E(k, L) denotes the energy contribution at position L for each of the 20 standard amino acid types, and k represents the residue position in the protein sequence. For a protein sequence of length L, the RECM matrix  R∈RL×20  is generated as described in eq  . To capture local residue interaction patterns, we calculate gradient magnitudes and orientations and then divide them into a 3 × 3 spatial grids for HOG transformation. Each cell contains a 9-bin orientation histogram, and concatenating all cells constitutes a fixed length 81-dimensions RECM-HOG feature vector. This method preserves local energy interaction patterns yet generates a uniform feature size for the downstream classifiers.

### Adaptive Neighborhood-Based Sampling (ANBS)
Algorithm
  In this paper, we used an effective Adaptive Neighborhood-Based Sampling (ANBS) to address the severe class imbalance problem in the data set. Such an approach is based on an iterative process that incrementally increases the minority class until a balanced configuration is achieved. At each iteration, the distance-based algorithm calculates the Euclidean distances for each distance-measure pair among the majority and minority sets. Then, it selects for oversampling those minority samples that lie closest to the majority class boundary. Such high-density minority samples are synthesized, and subsequently, more and more synthetic samples are generated to increase the representation of the minority class. The Euclidean distance between two feature vectors, p1 = (p 1x , p 1y , ···, p 1n ) and p2 = (p 2x , p 2y , ···, p 2n ), is defined in eq  : d(p1i,p2i)=∑i=1n(p1i−p2i)2 10 Although traditional oversampling methods such as SMOTE are often employed to address class imbalance, they typically rely on heuristic neighborhood selection or simply replicate minority samples. These approaches could unintentionally lead to overfitting and bias the data distribution, especially in complex biological data sets. The proposed ANBS algorithm combines the adaptive synthesis idea of ADASYN ,  by selectively synthesizing samples close to the distributional boundaries of the minority class. By focusing on these ambiguous boundary points, while as much as possible on distributing diversity among synthesized instances, ANBS can greatly reduce the sample redundancy and improve the representational power of the training set. In addition, the algorithm has a manageable balancing term through a parameter target ratio (max_ratio) to ensure the final class distribution. Unlike traditional oversampling methods, ANBS is more capable of handling the heterogeneity of data and therefore produces better model generalization. This technique is suitable for classification problems where the amount of data is small and class imbalance is extreme. The parameters were set as follows: max_ratio = 1.0 (indicates that the majority-to-minority class ratio should not exceed 1:1) and iter_max = 50 (maximum number of iterations to reach the balance). A constant random seed (random state = 42) was used to make these experiments reproducible. With these configurations, the training had a balanced representation of DSBs and SSBs, while maintaining some diversity in the minority class. The pseudocode of the ANBS algorithm is shown in Algorithm 1.

  The ANBS Algorithm 1, repeatedly balancing the distribution of classes by adding augmenting samples to the minority class and removing majority samples close on both sides of the decision boundary, was applied on training data only to prevent data leakage. No resampling or augmentation was applied to the separate test set, so the evaluation metrics represent an unbiased evaluation of the model performance. The algorithm follows a three-step process: (1) boundary majority samples are selected based on their proximity to minority samples using Euclidean distance as described in line 3, (2) these selected samples are then removed from the majority set as indicated in line 4, and (3) new minority samples are generated using a modified ADASYN scheme that focuses on boundary proximal samples (line 5). The iteration continues until the defined class ratio threshold is achieved, and then, termination occurs.

  The complexity of the ANBS algorithm is derived and based on iterations. Let N and P be the initial cardinalities of the majority and the minority class samples, respectively, where each sample is represented by a d-dimensional feature vector. The algorithm runs for at most T iterations but may be terminated early if the desired class balance is achieved. For a given iteration t, the computational cost is dominated by two main steps. First, the pairwise Euclidean distances for all majority samples N  t  with all minority samples P  t  are computed with a complexity of O(N  t  · P  t  · d). Second, the sorting of these distances for fetching the K nearest neighbors for the boundary classifier needs O(P  t  log P  t ). The overall per-batch complexity is thus summarized in eq  : O(Nt·Pt·(d+log⁡Pt)) 11 Under the assumption that, in the worst case, the sample size does not shrink significantly between iterations, the total complexity for T iterations is represented by eq  . O(T·N·P·(d+logP)) 12 In practice, both the feature dimensionality d and the maximum number of iterations T are taken as fixed constants. In addition, the algorithm’s boundary-aware pruning and merging gradually shrink N  t  and restrict the growth of P  t , thus resulting in sublinear scaling in practical runs. This guarantees realistic efficiency for small-to-medium-scale high-dimensional data sets, as found in the identification of SSBs from DSBs, as visualized in Figure  . To study the influence of ANBS on the distribution in the feature space, we performed 3-D UMAP ,  dimensionality reduction on the fused representations of ESM (2560-dim global) and RECM-HOG (81-dim local). The plots visualized in Figure   represent the imbalanced original training set, the training set after applying ANBS, the validation set, and the test set. In the original training samples, the SSBs minority are sparse and partially overlapping, and the DSBs exist in dense clusters; both have them. With ANBS, the resampled training data exhibits significantly improved balance across classes and better class-wise mixing without corrupting the intrinsic manifold structure. This indicates that ANBS generates minority samples and maintains biologically meaningful feature relationships, leading to improved generalization.


### Bilinear
Attention Network for Feature Fusion
  To capture the pairwise interaction between global evolutionary semantics and local structural motifs of DNA-binding proteins efficiently, we utilize the Bilinear Attention Network (BAN) module as the feature fusion module in symmetry within our proposed BAN-SDBPred framework. This allows fine-grained high-order interaction of the representations obtained from the evolutionary-scale modeling (ESM) encoder and the local RECM-HOG descriptor. This bilinear attention module not only fuses local and global features efficiently but also enables biologically meaningful interpretation by emphasizing interactions between conserved residues and structural motifs relevant to DNA-binding specificity. Formally, let the ESM2 encoder produce a hidden representation of the input protein sequence as formulated in eq    Hs=[h1s,h2s,...,hNs]∈Rds×N 13 where h  j   s  denotes the j–th contextual substructure embedding, and N represents the number of such substructures with dimensionality d  s . Similarly, the RECM-HOG encoder produces a local motif representation in eq    Hi=[h1i,h2i,...,hMi]∈Rdi×M 14 where h  m   i  corresponds to the m–th local motif feature with dimensionality d  i , and M is the number of motifs.

  To project both representations into a common latent space of dimension d, two learnable projection matrices are introduced via eq  : U∈Rds×d,V∈Rdi×d 15 For each pair (j, m) of substructure and motif features, a bilinear interaction score is computed in eq    Ij,m=qT[σ(UThjs)⊙σ(VThmi)] 16 where σ(·) denotes the ReLU activation function, ⊙ represents the Hadamard (element-wise) product, and  q∈Rd  is a trainable weight vector. Each entry I  j, m  quantifies the cross-modal dependency between the j–th evolutionary substructure and the m–th local motif, highlighting biologically informative interactions. A compact joint representation is then obtained via bilinear pooling over all pairwise interactions through eq    f=∑j=1N∑m=1M((UThjs)⊙(VThmi)) 17 yielding a d-dimensional feature vector that contains integrated global and local information. This is a parameter-efficient operation since the projection matrices U and V are shared across the interaction computation and pooling step.

  The BAN can also be generalized to a multihead version in which multiple interaction maps are generated simultaneously, each with a separate weight vector q, while optionally sharing U and V, as depicted in Figure  . This multihead structure enables the model to learn more diverse interaction patterns.

  The fused representation f then goes through fully connected layers for binary classification between SSBs and DSBs, as visualized via Figure  . By directly capturing higher-order interactions between evolutionary context and local structural motifs, BAN not only boosts prediction accuracy but also provides improved biological interpretability, modeling the synergy between global sequence constraints and local binding determinants in DNA–protein recognition.

### Feature Importance and Interpretability Analysis
of the BAN-SDBPred Model Using SHAP
  To analyze the impact of each feature on the predictions of our proposed BAN-based BAN-SDBPred model, we used SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) as a posthoc interpretability method. SHAP values attribute an importance value for each input feature based on Shapley values from cooperative game theory, representing the marginal contribution of a feature to the model output for each prediction. Formally, for a prediction instance x, the SHAP explanation model g is an additive function of binary variables represented through eq    g(z′)=ϕ0+∑i=1Mϕizi′ 27 where z ′ ∈ (0,1) M  indicates feature presence, ϕ0 is the baseline output, and ϕ i  denotes the Shapley value for feature i. The Shapley value is computed in eq   as ϕi=∑S⊆F\(i)|S|!(|F|−|S|−1)!|F|![fS∪(i)(xS∪(i))−fS(xS)] 28 where F is the complete feature set, S is a feature subset excluding i, and f  S (x  S ) is the model’s prediction using only subset S. This formulation captures the average marginal contribution of feature i across all possible feature combinations.

  The kernel explainer-based analysis was done on the independent test set with respect to the best-performing model that combines ESM global embeddings and RECM-HOG local descriptors. A background data set consisting of 50 randomly sampled instances was used for the baseline approximation. The SHAP values were aggregated over the test set to provide global feature importance lists. Figure   represents that both ESM2-based evolutionary embeddings and RECM-HOG-based local structural motifs play important roles in making the classification decisions. The highest-scoring features, including embeddings of conserved residues derived from ESM2 and solvent accessibility patterns encoded by RECM-HOG, distinguished strong discriminative capabilities between SSBs and DSBs.

  Analysis of SHAP values shows that embeddings for conserved residues in helix-turn-helix and zinc-finger motifs contribute most strongly to the discrimination between SSBs and DSBs. Interestingly, embedding captured by ESM2 of these conserved sites corresponds to residues already reported contributing to DNA binding, including arginine and lysine residues that interact with the phosphate backbone. However, RECM-HOG local motifs represent regions enriched in hydrophobic and charged amino acids, aligning with known structural motifs involved in DNA-binding specificity. Furthermore, the bilinear attention maps indicate that cross-modal interactions between these conserved residues and local structural motifs are emphasized, suggesting that the network captures biologically meaningful interactions, such as contact interfaces within DNA-binding domains, which enhances interpretability and aligns with known mechanistic insights; therefore, SHAP-based interpretability thereby enhances the transparency and utility of the BAN-SDBPred framework.


## Results

### Performance Analysis of
Single Features on
5-Fold-CV and Independent Testing (without ANBS)
  To examine the predictive performance of single global and local feature representations, ESM2, ProtT5, CPSR, and RECM-HOG with five different deep learning models (ResNet, LSTM, RNN, CNN, and BAN) were extensively tested on a 5-fold CV test and then validated on an independent test to determine the generalization ability before applying the ANBS technique. Eight common metrics were used for performance evaluation, including accuracy (Acc), precision (Pre), sensitivity (Sen), specificity (Spe), F1-score (F1), Matthew’s correlation coefficient (MCC), and area under the ROC curve (AUC), as represented in Table  .

  In the 5-fold CV test, ESM2 is the best-performing single feature among architectures. For BAN, ESM2 attains the highest overall CV performance with Acc of 97.6, Pre of 97.2, Sen of 100, Spe of 86.5, F1 of 98.6, MCC of 0.917, and AUC of 0.976. ResNet achieved very similar performance with ESM2, attaining an Acc of 97.6, Pre of 97.2, Sen of 100, Spe of 86.5, F1 of 98.6, MCC of 0.917, and AUC of 0.971, indicating that ESM2 embeddings obtained a highly separable representation regardless of the convolutional models used. By contrast, ProtT5 as a single feature achieved slightly lower 5-fold CV performance for ResNet with ProtT5, achieving an Acc of 96.2, Sen of 98.3, Spe of 86.5, F1 of 97.7, MCC of 0.867, and AUC of 0.980, while BAN with ProtT5 achieved an Acc of 94.8, Sen of 97.1, Spe of 83.8, F1 of 96.9, MCC of 0.818, and AUC of 0.968. For local handcrafted features, the performance was poor and varied across the models. CPSR with BAN achieved a CV Acc of 91.0, Pre of 90.6, Sen of 99.4, Spe of 51.4, F1 of 94.8, MCC of 0.659, and AUC of 0.909, whereas ResNet by CPSR only achieved an Acc of 85.9 with an MCC of 0.552. RECM-HOG performed similarly as a single feature: BAN with RECM-HOG achieved an Acc of 91.0, a Sen of 96.6, an Spe of 64.9, an F1 of 94.7, an MCC of 0.669, and an AUC of 0.932, and ResNet with RECM-HOG reached 88.2 in Acc, 93.3 in F1, 0.527 in MCC, and an AUC of 0.898. However, persistent models were unstable with handcrafted features; for example, LSTM with the CPSR feature obtained just 62.3 in Acc and 0.381 in MCC, and LSTM with the RECM-HOG feature obtained 65.1 in Acc with 0.422 in MCC. Overall, the 5-fold CV performance of ESM2 single-feature embedding is better than that of ProtT5, ProtT5 performs better than RECM-HOG, and CPSR performance is poorer than that of all single features, as depicted in Table   and Figure  , with BAN and ResNet exploiting these encodings most effectively.

  The independent/blind test set is important for testing the model’s generalization ability on new data and provides a realistic evaluation of the model’s performance when deployed in real scenarios. For ESM2, BAN still achieved the highest and well-balanced performance in the single-feature scenario, reaching Acc of 80.6, Pre of 87.7, Sen of 86.3, Spe of 63.4, F1 of 87.0, MCC of 0.489, and AUC of 0.825. CNN with ESM2 also demonstrated a high level of generalization (Acc 81.2, Pre 82.5, Sen 95.2, Spe 39.0, F1 88.4, MCC 0.435, AUC 0.804), whereas ESM2-based ResNet and RNN obtained slightly lower Acc between 74.6 and 78.8 and MCC below 0.40. These results demonstrate that ESM2 is the best single feature in a blind test performance to validate, while BAN and CNN perform the best and are balanced for sensitivity and specificity and while models with ProtT5 achieved consistently lower independent performance than ESM2 embedding. BAN with ProtT5 achieved 72.7 Acc, 89.1 Pre, 72.6 Sen, 73.2 Spe, an F1 of 80.0, an MCC of 0.406, and an AUC of 0.817, while the corresponding models, including ResNet, RNN, LSTM, and CNN models, achieved Acc performances from 72.1 to 73.3 and MCC from 0.367 to 0.418. Thus, the ESM2 and ProtT5 relatively outperformed by approximately 5% to 8% in Acc and were noticeably superior in MCC across all models, as shown in Table   and Figure  , indicating that ESM2 encodings provide a more precise and generalized representation for identifying DSBs from SSBs.

  Independent testing further highlighted the limitations of CPSR and RECM-HOG descriptors. Even in their robust models’ architecture with BAN, CPSR obtained an Acc of 75.2, Sen of 96.0, Spe of 12.2, MCC of 0.148, and AUC of 0.685, while RECM-HOG achieved performance in terms of Acc, Sen, Spe, MCC, and AUC of 67.3, 81.5, 24.4, 0.063, and 0.586. For all models with CPSR and RECM-HOG, sensitivity tended to achieve unbalanced sensitivity and specificity, whereas sensitivity was as high as more than 95%, but specificity was as low as less than 15%, suggesting these models would produce a large number of false positives or false negatives on unseen data, and the decision boundaries were very weak.   Therefore, the results of the 5-fold CV and independent test on the single feature descriptors indicate that evolutionary embeddings significantly surpass local handcrafted descriptors in all evaluation matrices. The best representation is ESM2. The most stable and best-performing representation is ESM2 combined with the BAN model; it achieves the best overall accuracy, F1, MCC, and AUC on both 5-fold CV and independent tests, while ProtT5 presents competitive but consistently worse generalization. CPSR and RECM-HOG provide useful local information but fail to achieve robust performance across architectures and data sets. In conclusion, the combination of ESM2 with BAN represents the optimal single-feature setting for achieving the best performance. In the next section, we explore feature fusion (hybrid encodings) to exploit complementary global and local information for further improving DSBs/SSBs prediction.


### Performance Analysis of Dual Features Fusion
on 5-Fold-CV and Independent Testing (without ANBS)
  After identifying ESM2 as the best single-feature encoding, we systematically evaluated whether global evolutionary embeddings and local handcrafted descriptors could be more complementary. Four dual-feature fusions, ESM2 + CPSR, ESM2 + RECM-HOG, ProtT5 + CPSR, and ProtT5 + RECM-HOG, were evaluated on the same five models without applying class balancing through the ANBS. The purpose was to analyze whether feature fusion can improve complementary discriminative patterns.

  On the 5-fold CV test, the ESM2-based fusion consistently achieved better performance than the single feature. BAN with ESM2 + CPSR achieved Acc of 97.6, Pre of 97.2, Sen of 100, Spe of 86.5, F1 of 98.6, MCC of 0.917, and AUC of 0.956, matching the ESM2-only configuration in accuracy but offering marginally improved architectural stability. BAN with ESM2 + RECM-HOG obtained the same CV performance (Acc 96.7, Pre 97.2, Sen 98.9, Spe 86.5, F1 98.0, MCC 0.883, AUC 0.965), which indicates that the fusion approach effectively captures local structure without losing the discriminative global motifs. Among other models, RNN with ESM2 + CPSR and ESM2 + RECM-HOG both achieved 97.2 Acc with F1 of 98.3, MCC of 0.900, and AUC of 0.966 and 0.972, respectively, demonstrating that traditional models benefit substantially from dual-feature inputs. CNN with ESM2 + RECM-HOG similarly achieved 96.7 Acc, F1 98.0, MCC 0.883, and AUC 0.938, confirming the robustness of this combination across diverse models. ProtT5-based combinations highlighted some improvements over ProtT5 only but were still outperformed by the ESM2 combinations. The BAN with ProtT5 + CPSR achieved the best CV Acc of 96.2, Pre 96.1, Sen 99.4, Spe 81.1, F1 97.8, MCC 0.865, and AUC 0.977, and the BAN with ProtT5 + RECM-HOG also obtained competitive results with 94.8 Acc, F1 96.9, MCC 0.815, and AUC 0.976. CNN with ProtT5 + RECM-HOG obtained 96.7 Acc with MCC 0.882 and AUC 0.969, and ResNet with ProtT5 + RECM-HOG obtained 96.2 Acc with MCC 0.865 and AUC 0.960, as illustrated in Table   along with Figure  . These results highlighted that local features’ fusion contributes very little when fused with ProtT5 and contributes optimally when fused with an ESM2-based model, implying that the quality of the global features embedding dominates the discriminatory power of the fused feature representation.

  Independent test results validated the 5-fold CV approaches while achieving promising generalization with fusion strategies. BAN with ESM2 + RECM-HOG obtained the best independent performance among all dual-feature fusions, with an Acc of 83.6, Pre of 89.4, Sen of 88.7, Spe of 68.3, F1 of 89.1, MCC of 0.566, and AUC of 0.879. This is a highlighted enhancement from BAN with ESM2 only (Acc 80.6, MCC 0.489, AUC 0.825), illustrating that the local interaction motifs of RECM-HOG fusion with the evolutionary global context of ESM2 boost generalization. CNN with ESM2 + RECM-HOG also achieved strong generalized performance (Acc 83.6, Pre 88.2, Sen 90.3, Spe 63.4, F1 89.2, MCC 0.552, AUC 0.847), and RNN variations with ESM2 + CPSR and ESM2 + RECM-HOG both obtained the best result of 84.2 in Acc with F1 of 90.2 and MCC of 0.538. In contrast, BAN with ESM2 + CPSR obtained 84.2 in Acc with Pre of 84.5, Sen of 96.8, Spe of 46.3, F1 of 90.2, MCC of 0.538, and AUC of 0.861 and achieved a slightly lower MCC than the RECM-HOG fusion despite the same Acc due to a lower Spe of 46.3 versus 68.3. This performance is surprising, showing that CPSR’s compositional features, when combined with ESM2, cause more false positives than RECM-HOG’s descriptors. Similarly, ResNet with ESM2 + CPSR and ESM2 + RECM-HOG achieved an accuracy from 78.8 to 79.4 but with a lower MCC from 0.422 to 0.427 compared to BAN and CNN, as shown in Table   and visualized in Figure  , and this implies that convolutional models without an attention architecture struggle to optimally obtain dual-feature performance.

  ProtT5 fusions’ performance is worse according to the independent test results. BAN with ProtT5 + CPSR achieved 78.2 Acc, 85.5 Pre, 85.5 Sen, 56.1 Spe, 85.5 F1, 0.416 MCC, and 0.818 AUC, and BAN with ProtT5 + RECM-HOG achieved 73.9 Acc, 87.9 Pre, 75.8 Sen, 68.3 Spe, 81.4 F1, 0.399 MCC, and 0.837 AUC. The corresponding CNN and RNN models obtained Acc in the range from 71.5 to 81.2 with MCC from 0.340 to 0.463, consistently underperforming ESM2 fusions by 5% to 10% in Acc and 0.10 to 0.17 in MCC. This indicates that ESM2 + RECM-HOG is the best dual-feature fusion without class balancing and performs better than ProtT5-based fusions and all single-feature encodings on the independent test set with respect to Acc, MCC, F1, and AUC measures.

  BAN with ESM2 + RECM-HOG (Acc 83.6, MCC 0.566, AUC 0.879) outperforms BAN with ESM2 alone (Acc 80.6, MCC 0.489, AUC 0.825) by 3% in Acc, 0.077 in MCC, and 0.054 in AUC, achieving a 15.8% relative MCC increase, as seen in Table   and depicted in Figure  . This highlights that RECM-HOG’s local structural descriptor provides nonredundant features with the BAN model. ESM2 + RECM-HOG’s superiority over ESM2 + CPSR (MCC 0.566 vs 0.538) further confirms that the RECM-HOG-based local descriptor is better than compositional descriptors; it captures residue interaction insights not explicitly highlighted by evolutionary descriptors. Feature fusion benefits varied substantially across architectures of BAN, and CNN consistently outperformed dual features, while ResNet and LSTM underperformed single-feature baseline descriptors. Additionally, AUC and average precision (AP) graphs are visualized to analyze the 5-fold CV and independent test performance of BAN without ANBS sampling as represented in Figure  . Furthermore, a comprehensive performance analysis of each evaluation measure through cluster heatmap is provided in the Supporting Information: Note S2: Tables S1, S2, S3, S4, S5, S6, S7, and S8.

  Therefore, the BAN with ESM2 + RECM-HOG fusion representation is consistently more informative than the single feature and most generalized on the BAN and CNN models. ESM2 consistently outperforms ProtT5, while RECM-HOG outperforms CPSR across all fusions. Notably, its superior performance in independent tests demonstrates the power and robustness of BAN for identifying DBPs. ### Performance Analysis of Single Features on
5-fold-CV and Independent Testing (with ANBS)
  ANBS was conducted only on training data, repeatedly synthesizing SSB samples near the boundary and removing the majority of DSB samples close to the decision boundary until a desired class ratio was achieved. All of the same four single-feature encodings, including ESM2, ProtT5, CPSR, and RECM-HOG, were tested over all models with ANBS-resampled training to determine if class balancing could reduce overfitting and generalize better.

  On the 5-fold CV test with ANBS, global embeddings still achieved robust performance, whereas local descriptors obtained inconsistent results. ResNet with ESM2 achieved CV Acc of 95.8, Pre of 97.2, Sen of 97.7, Spe of 86.5, F1 of 97.4, MCC of 0.851, and AUC of 0.968, representing a slight decrease from the without-ANBS baseline performance of 97.6 in Acc and 0.917 in MCC, but also with a better balance of sensitivity and specificity. ResNet with ProtT5 also achieved 96.2 in Acc with an MCC of 0.865 and an AUC of 0.966, again showing that ANBS achieves strong CV results for pretrained model encodings. Conversely, LSTM with ESM2 obtained only 19.3 in Acc with a Sen of 2.3 and an MCC of 0.064, indicating that traditional models are not performing optimally with ANBS-generated synthetic samples. RNN and CNN with ESM2 still achieved good CV performance, with Acc from 96.7 to 97.2 and MCC from 0.883 to 0.900 again, highlighting that convolutional models’ architectures are more effective with ANBS than LSTMs. For local features with ANBS, they underperform substantially. ResNet with CPSR performance dropped to 78.8 on the 5-fold CV test in Acc (85.9 without ANBS) with an MCC of 0.510 and AUC of 0.905, while ResNet with RECM-HOG obtained 88.2 in Acc with an MCC of 0.562 and AUC of 0.886, very close to its performance without ANBS. BAN with CPSR was 91.0, and after sampling it achieved 68.9 on the CV test in Acc, and BAN with RECM-HOG was 88.2, and after sampling it achieved 88.7 in Acc, as seen in Table  , indicating that attention-based fusion can slightly improve the noise introduced by ANBS on handcrafted features. However, the LSTM model with CPSR failed entirely (Acc <20%), further confirming that ANBS synthetic samples failed to improve the performance of traditional models.

  The results of the independent test were employed to check generalization with ANBS resampling. For ResNet with ESM2, it obtained an Acc of 78.8, a Pre of 89.4, an Sen of 81.5, an Spe of 70.7, an F1 of 85.2, an MCC of 0.485, and an AUC of 0.849, with a slight change in Acc and a better Spe of 70.7 than without ANBS and a higher AUC of 0.849 with ANBS than without ANBS, which is 0.747, which indicates that ANBS successfully reduced false positives in ResNet by balancing training class distributions. Similarly, CNN with ESM2 has a better Acc of 84.9 and MCC of 0.569 than 81.2 Acc and 0.435 MCC without applying ANBS sampling, indicating the generalization improvement, as illustrated in Table   and visualized through Figure  . However, RNN + ESM2 also outperformed in terms of Acc, with 83.0 and an MCC of 0.497, and LSTM underperformed at 26.7 in Acc and MCC of 0.078, indicating that not all models improved their performance from ANBS resampling. ProtT5 has slightly improved in specificity but has limited overall performance. ResNet with ProtT5 achieved an Acc of 74.6, Spe of 73.2, MCC of 0.431, and AUC of 0.834, achieving a better performance increase in Spe of 73.2 vs 63.4 without ANBS but comparable Acc. CNN with ProtT5 obtained an Acc of 77.6, Spe of 68.3, MCC of 0.455, and AUC of 0.822, and RNN achieved an Acc of 81.8, Spe of 31.7, MCC of 0.452, and AUC of 0.870, improving performance approximately 1–10% of their non-ANBS. These performances indicate that the overfitting of ProtT5 to the imbalanced data is less than that of non-ANBS; thus, ANBS has an effect on this single-feature encoding to enhance the predictive performance and generalization.

  For handcrafted features, ANBS yielded results that were dependent on the model. ResNet with CPSR obtained an Acc of 46.7, a low Sen of 33.9, an Spe of 85.4, and an MCC of 0.183, achieving less performance than 72.7 Acc without ANBS. This indicates that the compositional structure required for generalization is not retained in the CPSR samples generated by the ANBS. ResNet with RECM-HOG achieved performance in terms of Acc, MCC, and AUC of 62.4, 0.056, and 0.510, with lower performance. CNN and RNN with handcrafted features also performed less well, whereas BAN with CPSR and without ANBS obtained 35.8 in Acc, and BAN with RECM-HOG achieved 61.2 in Acc, indicating that the models are not improved enough with the ANBS distribution in local descriptors.

  The comparison of the best single-feature-based models, both within and outside of ANBS, demonstrates that performance is highly discriminative. For ResNet with ESM2, ANBS obtained an Spe from 46.3 to 70.7 and AUC from 0.747 to 0.849 with a stable Acc of 78.8, indicating an effective reduction of false positives, whereas for the other models, it achieved slight performance: CNN with ESM2 achieved an MCC of 0.550 vs 0.435, and LSTM with ESM2 achieved an Acc of 26.7 vs 77.6 without ANBS. For all single features, the best performance with ANBS was achieved by CNN with ESM2 in terms of Acc 81.2, MCC 0.550, and AUC 0.904, which slightly improved MCC and AUC over the non-ANBS BAN, as depicted in Figure  . Furthermore, a comprehensive performance analysis of each evaluation measure through cluster heatmap is provided in the Supporting Information: Note S2: Tables S1, S2, S3, S4, S5, S6, S7, and S8.

  Conclusively, the ANBS performance is partially on single-feature representations. It is more effective on global feature embeddings rather than handcrafted feature descriptors. ANBS mainly achieved performance in specificity and MCC for convolutional models based on ESM2, while it tends to have worse performance for traditional models and when using handcrafted features, which motivated us to evaluate dual feature fusion for better performance in the next section.

### Ablation Test
  To evaluate the performance of various deep learning models for fair comparison with BAN-SDBPred framework, the same data set splits and parameter settings are employed in training and independent testing for the identification of the SSBs and DSBs. The BAN-SDBPred model’s contribution, a comprehensive and systematic ablation test, is also applied in terms of single features and multiple models across BAN to validate the effect of structured analysis and the most important architectural single feature impact on the predictive performance for SSBs, whereas BAN-SDBPred is architectural-based on dual feature fusion.

  Especially, with the effect of ANBS, we considered experiments without ANBS as a baseline of our method. In particular, Tables   and  report the results for all single- and two-feature models without ANBS, whereas the equivalent results for the models trained on the data after using ANBS are reported in Tables   and . To further validate ANBS, we conducted comparative experiments against widely used imbalance-handling methods, including SMOTE, and assessed the sensitivity of ANBS hyperparameters (max_ratio and iter_max). The results are summarized in Tables S3 and S4 in the Supporting Information: Note S3. Table S3 shows that ANBS improves independent test accuracy (Acc) from 83.6% (without sampling) to 84.2%, F1-score from 89.1% to 89.6%, and MCC from 0.566 to 0.571, outperforming SMOTE in most metrics. Table S4 demonstrates that BAN-SDBPred performance is robust across a range of ANBS hyperparameters, with optimal results achieved at max_ratio = 1.0 and iter_max = 50.

  These ablation studies confirm that ANBS enhances minority-class detection and overall generalization without overfitting, particularly when combined with ESM2 + RECM-HOG feature fusion in the BAN model. The systematic evaluation of ANBS, both in comparison to standard oversampling and via hyperparameter sensitivity, demonstrates its critical role in achieving the higher performance reported for BAN-SDBPred. Therefore, the ablation study clearly indicates that the entire original BAN-SDBPred structure is important for its best performance. The developed model outperformed all other models (ResNet, LSTM, RNN, and CNN) and single- and dual-feature fusion in CV and, more importantly, in independent tests. Conclusively, our systematic ablation test confirms that the proposed framework of the BAN-SDBPred model is optimal and discriminative.

These show BAN-SDBPred’s improvements over existing predictors in Acc, F1, MCC, and AUC for independent tests, highlighting the consistency and generalization benefits of proposed model.

  Conclusively, the proposed BAN-SDBPred outperforms all existing benchmarked models with all evaluation metrics by a large margin. This superior performance of BAN-SDBPred was achieved from a unique pipeline: ESM2 global embeddings and RECM-HOG local descriptors with ANBS class balancing and BAN dual feature fusion. The results indicate that BAN-SDBPred has a higher potential to precisely and reliably predict SSBs and should be considered a benchmark for future investigation.


## Discussion
  The features employed in BAN-SDBPred are complementary information to improve the prediction performance for both SSB and DSB. From a biological point of view, the ESM2 embeddings capture global evolutionary information, enabling the model to distinguish conserved residues and motifs important for DNA binding (e.g., helix-turn-helix and zinc-finger motifs). These global patterns are actually long-range dependencies between residues and are crucial to discriminating between single-stranded and double-stranded DNA binding proteins. ProtT5 is an alternative sequence-level representation also capturing evolutionary information and was used to validate the superiority of ESM2. Local descriptors, such as RECM-HOG, represent residue interaction energies and spatial orientation patterns of local structural motifs and energy landscapes that are significant to DNA binding specificity. CPSR captures basic compositional and physicochemical characteristics contributing additional information about the sequence of amino acids and their chemical characteristics.

  From a computational perspective, global and local features are fused in BAN-SDBPred to exploit the long-range dependencies and local interaction patterns. ESM2 embeddings provide a dense, high-dimensional representation that captures evolutionary relationships, while RECM-HOG complements this by preserving local pairwise energy interactions in a fixed dimensional vector. This dual representation improves the discriminative power of the model, as demonstrated in our ablation studies and independent test performance, and explains why the fusion of these heterogeneous features consistently outperforms single-feature models. Moreover, by incorporating ANBS into these features, we achieve a balanced training, mitigating the bias toward majority DSB class yet preserving biologically meaningful patterns for better generalization and interpretability.

  Although BAN-SDBPred demonstrated strong repeated cross-validation performance, the independent test results indicated that generalization remains more challenging than internal validation. This gap may be partly attributed to the limited size and diversity of the benchmark data sets, particularly the relatively small number of SSB samples in the independent test set. Although CD-HIT redundancy reduction and low training test sequence similarity were applied to reduce redundancy-related bias, hidden family level homology and taxonomic imbalance cannot be completely excluded. Therefore, the present results should be interpreted with an appropriate caution. Future work will focus on constructing larger and more taxonomically diverse external data sets, performing family wise or cluster-wise validation, and improving probability calibration to further enhance model generalization.


## Conclusion
  The computational identification of DNA-binding proteins is important for accelerating the drug discovery process. Traditionally, the wet-lab experimental methods for detecting and exploring SSBs are challenging in terms of biasness toward huge data, time and labor-consuming, and costly. To address these challenges, several traditional computational predictors have achieved substantial performance, most of them still rely on either hand-crafted sequence descriptors or single-view evolutionary profiles, which limit their ability to capture discriminative global or local dependencies and to remain robust under severe class imbalance. In this study, we proposed a novel DL-based predictor BAN-SDBPred, predicting the SSBs and DSBs solely from sequence information. The proposed BAN-SDBPs use the deep representation from PLMs (ESM2 and ProtT5), physicochemical features (CPSR), and compressed energy-estimation-based features (RECM-HOG). The imbalance issue hampers the prediction performance of a model. Therefore, the ANBS sampling strategy was adopted to solve the imbalance problem. Then, several DL models, including CNN, ResNet, RNN, LSTM, and the BAN model, were developed and evaluated to assess the effectiveness of the proposed feature representations and sampling strategy. The ablation study shows that the designed BAN model significantly improved the overall prediction of SSBs and DSBs using PLMs and RECM-HOG hybrid features. This demonstrates the benefit of multimodal features and attention-based architectures for precise DBP prediction.

  Despite these advantages, there are still limitations: the model might not capture all rare sequence motifs or structural variants, and extreme class imbalance might impact the generalization; we acknowledge that the benchmark data sets used are relatively old. Comparative experiments against SMOTE and sensitivity analysis of ANBS hyperparameters further confirm that the proposed sampling strategy robustly improves minority-class detection and contributes significantly to the performance of BAN-SDBPred. Future directions include developing larger protein language models, incorporating structural predictions, enhancing interpretability through explainable AI, curating an independent test set of recently annotated proteins to further validate the predictive performance of BAN-SDBPred on novel sequences, and creating a public web server to facilitate high-throughput DBP screening and community access.
