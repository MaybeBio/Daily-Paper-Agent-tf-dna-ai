# Creating a non-native bio-amidation activity in asparagine synthetase via biosensor-guided directed evolution for the synthesis of (S)-2-aminobutyramide


## Abstract
  The direct asymmetric synthesis of chiral amides remains a focal point in green manufacturing, particularly for (S)-2-aminobutyramide whose traditional production is often hampered by low atom economy and arduous chiral resolution steps. Here, we report the creation of a non-native bio-amidation activity in Escherichia coli asparagine synthetase A (AsnA) for the proof-of-concept one-step biosynthesis of (S)-2-aminobutyramide, a critical intermediate for anti-epileptic drugs. To overcome the bottleneck of high-throughput screening, we engineered the AmiC–AmiR transcription factor into a highly sensitive biosensor (RC-4-Y83H) by remodeling its ligand-binding pocket, achieving a 23-fold induction response specifically toward (S)-2-AB. Utilizing this biosensor to screen saturation mutagenesis libraries, we identified an evolved variant (K77L/E120L/R255W) that showed a reversed substrate preference between the two substrates tested, converting (S)-2-aminobutyric acid to (S)-2-AB with retention of configuration. Molecular dynamics simulations and free-energy landscape analysis reveal that the mutations fundamentally remodel the active site, inducing a 180° flip of the substrate binding mode. This structural reorientation positions the non-native carboxyl group in a catalytically productive geometry relative to ATP, a conformation inaccessible in the wild-type enzyme. As a proof of concept, this work does not aim to deliver an industrially competitive biocatalyst; instead, it shows that the specificity of a native amide-forming enzyme can be reprogrammed toward a non-native alpha-amino acid, and it provides a generalizable, biosensor-guided framework for such reprogramming.


## Introduction
  Chiral amide moieties are indispensable functionalities in critical sectors such as pharmaceuticals, advanced materials, and agriculture, owing to the specific biological activities conferred by their chiral architecture.1–3 Consequently, the synthesis of optically pure amides has long represented a significant challenge in chemical and biological manufacturing that requires urgent resolution.4–7 (S)-2-Aminobutyramide ((S)-2-AB), a pivotal pharmaceutical chiral intermediate, is essential for the synthesis of various therapeutic agents, exhibiting significant efficacy in anti-epileptic, antidepressant, and antibacterial applications.8,9 Notably, the production of first-line broad-spectrum anti-epileptics, such as Levetiracetam and Brivaracetam, relies heavily on the synthesis efficiency and cost control of their core chiral intermediate, (S)-2-AB, which currently represents a major bottleneck limiting drug accessibility.10 The classical industrial method relies on the synthesis of (±)-2-aminobutyramide from a (±)-2-aminobutyric acid precursor, followed by chiral resolution of the racemate using highly enantiopure tartaric acid11 (Fig. 1A). However, this conventional approach is inherently limited by a maximum theoretical yield of 50%, and the mandatory use of resolving agents and catalysts significantly complicates downstream product isolation and purification.

  In recent years, biocatalysis has been widely adopted for amide synthesis due to its superior atom economy and mild reaction conditions.12 Nitrile hydratases are among the most prevalent industrial enzymes, catalyzing the hydration of nitriles to amides. However, their application in α-aminoamide synthesis is severely restricted by the inherent instability of α-aminonitrile substrates and the generally poor stereoselectivity of native enzymes.13,14 Similarly, while d-aminopeptidase can resolve racemic 2-aminobutyramide mixtures to yield high-purity (S)-2-AB, the widespread adoption of this method is constrained by its theoretical maximum conversion rate of 50% and the additional operational costs involved15 (Fig. 1A). Previous studies have shown that the synthesis of (S)-2-aminobutyric acid ((S)-2-ABA) is highly mature and has been successfully industrialized,16–19 highlighting the strategic advantages of the asymmetric synthesis of (S)-2-AB. Although direct asymmetric synthesis via the Strecker reaction can theoretically achieve high yields20 (Fig. 1B), the harsh reaction conditions and expensive chemical catalysts required significantly increase the overall production cost of (S)-2-AB. The solution to these challenges lies in developing novel enzymatic pathways capable of synthesis from a single chiral source. A one-step amidation of the precursor (S)-2-ABA to (S)-2-AB, catalyzed by a single enzyme, would effectively circumvent the yield limitations and stereoselectivity issues inherent in kinetic resolution. However, although enzymes such as lipases, amide bond synthetases, and carboxylate reductases are known to catalyze the conversion of carboxylic acids to amides, they are constrained either by reaction conditions or by substrate specificity, and thus generally cannot produce the primary amides required for the biosynthesis of (S)-2-AB.21–25 Therefore, a green and simple production method for this compound remains elusive.

  Asparagine synthetase naturally catalyzes the ATP-dependent synthesis of asparagine from aspartic acid and an ammonia donor.26,27 Mechanistically, this reaction parallels our target transformation, and the substrate l-asparagine shares significant structural homology with (S)-2-AB (Fig. 1C). However, wild-type AsnA (WT) from E. coli exhibits no detectable activity toward (S)-2-ABA. Based on this, we proposed to engineer asparagine synthetase to catalyze the non-native synthesis of (S)-2-AB from (S)-2-ABA. While the construction of screening libraries for target enzymes is a well-established practice, the engineering process is frequently limited by the lack of sufficiently sensitive and rapid screening methodologies.28 Consequently, developing effective high-throughput screening strategies is crucial to accelerate directed evolution and obtain variants with the desired catalytic properties.29,30 Biosensors based on transcription factors have proven to be a powerful strategy for identifying high-performance enzymes. By enabling the rapid interrogation of vast libraries for specific targets, this approach has been widely applied in constructing novel synthetic pathways and optimizing engineering processes.31–35

  In this study, we developed a highly specific biosensor for the intracellular detection of amides based on the AmiC–AmiR system. In its native host, this system is induced by acetamide and propionamide, whereas butyramide functions as a competitive inhibitor. We first optimized the sensor's sensitivity to inducers by modulating the expression ratio of the regulatory proteins AmiC and AmiR. To reprogram the sensor for the specific detection of the target product, we subsequently implemented rational engineering targeting the ligand-binding domain (LBD) of AmiC, thereby constructing a precise transcriptional regulation system. Assisted by structure-based semi-rational design and directed evolution, we generated a mutant library of l-asparagine synthetase A (AsnA) from E. coli. Using the optimized biosensor (AmiC–AmiR–Y83H), we successfully identified mutants capable of catalyzing the conversion of (S)-2-ABA to (S)-2-AB, thus establishing a novel biocatalytic framework and providing a proof-of-concept enzymatic route for the direct asymmetric synthesis of optically pure (S)-2-AB.


## Results and discussion

### Construction and design of the amide biosensor
  The amide-regulated transcription anti-termination system (AmiC–AmiR) from Pseudomonas aeruginosa comprises a ligand-binding negative regulator (AmiC), a terminator-binding positive regulator (AmiR), and a generic Rho-independent transcription termination sequence (TS).36,37 Mechanistically, in the absence of an inducer, constitutively expressed AmiC and AmiR form a heterodimeric complex. This sequestration prevents AmiR from interacting with the nascent mRNA leader sequence, allowing the formation of a terminator structure upstream of the amidase gene, thereby repressing expression. Upon amide binding, the AmiC–AmiR complex dissociates; the released AmiR then destabilizes the terminator stem-loop, permitting transcriptional read-through and amidase expression. To adapt this system for our study, we integrated the AmiC–AmiR genetic circuit into the pEV-GFP plasmid38 (previously constructed in our laboratory), replacing the downstream amidase gene with green fluorescent protein (GFP) to serve as a fluorescent reporter, and transformed the construct into E. coli for characterization (Fig. 2A).

  To establish a sensor with robust detection functionality, we first validated the orthogonal activities of AmiC and AmiR. Using acetamide as an inducer, we confirmed that AmiR alone is sufficient to execute anti-termination, whereas AmiC functions as the sensory module governing ligand recognition. Recognizing that the stoichiometric ratio of regulator proteins profoundly influences sensor performance, we constructed three plasmid variants with distinct transcriptional architectures and evaluated their detection capabilities using 5 mM acetamide (Fig. 2B). In the first configuration, AmiC and AmiR were expressed as a fusion protein under a single LacI promoter. This design failed to respond to the inducer, likely due to protein misfolding inherent to the fusion strategy. In the second configuration, an RBS was inserted between amiC and amiR to enable tandem expression, which yielded a 4-fold fluorescence increase upon induction. Notably, these mutants produce elevated levels of background fluorescence, this observation is tentatively attributed to cross-interference in the expression of AmiC and AmiR. In the third configuration, we decoupled the expression of the two regulators: AmiR was controlled by an inducible LacI promoter, while AmiC was expressed under constitutive promoters of varying strengths (J23100 > J23104 > J23108 > J23105 > J23103) (https://parts.igem.org/Promoters/Catalog/Anderson, Fig. 2C and Table S3). The optimal signal-to-noise ratio was achieved using the strong J23100 promoter, resulting in a 6-fold increase in fluorescence.

  To further amplify sensor sensitivity, we sought to fine-tune the translation initiation rates of the regulatory proteins. We constructed a library by introducing simultaneous degenerate mutations (NNK) into the ribosome binding sites (RBS) of both AmiC and AmiR within the best-performing plasmid background (Fig. S2A). Variants displaying high fluorescence intensity under low-acetamide conditions were isolated (Fig. S2B). The top-performing variant, designated RC-3, achieved a maximum dynamic range of 10-fold (Fig. 2D). Finally, we profiled the substrate specificity of the optimized AmiC–AmiR system (RC-3) against a panel of effector molecules, confirming that the sensor currently retains a narrow specificity profile restricted to short-chain amides (Fig. 2E). Our primary objective in the next phase is the engineering of the optimized AmiC–AmiR protein.


### Engineering biosensor specificity for (S)-2-aminobutyramide
  To reprogram the RC-3 for the specific detection of the target product, (S)-2-AB, we employed a structure-guided protein engineering strategy. Based on the crystal structure of AmiC (PDB ID: 1QO0),39 we identified residues within a 5 Å radius of the substrate-binding pocket and subjected them to mutagenesis using the Combinatorial Active-site Saturation Test (CAST).40,41 The targeted residues were partitioned into five libraries: group 1 (Tyr83, Met84, Ser85, Arg88), group 2 (Tyr104, Thr106, Phe107, Tyr108, Glu109), group 3 (Tyr150, Tyr152, Phe153), group 4 (Val206), and group 5 (Thr233) (Fig. 3A). Screening was performed with (S)-2-AB as the sole inducer to identify variants with enhanced fluorescence (Fig. S3). After eliminating false positives that exhibited fluorescence even in the absence of inducer, three mutants (Y83H, Y83T, Y83C) were found to produce positive fluorescence signals in response to (S)-2-AB (Fig. 3B). Among them, RC-3-Y83H showed pronounced fluorescence enhancement upon addition of 5 mM (S)-2-AB, with an induction fold of approximately 12. However, this sensor variant also displayed elevated basal fluorescence. We therefore optimized the ribosomal binding sites (RBS) of both AmiC and AmiR in RC-3-Y83H to fine-tune their expression ratio. In this round of screening, we investigated how the relative expression levels of AmiC and AmiR affect sensor performance. Variants with distinct functional characteristics were selected from the library, including those with high fluorescence but high leakage, low fluorescence with no response, and moderate fluorescence with low leakage. Predictive analysis indicated that higher AmiC expression generally reduced overall fluorescence and increased tolerance to higher inducer concentrations, likely due to free AmiC sequestering a fraction of the ligand. Conversely, higher AmiR expression enhanced sensitivity, but excessive AmiR led to constitutive GFP expression (Fig. 3C and S4).

  The optimized sensor RC-4-Y83H exhibited fluorescence enhancement at (S)-2-AB concentrations as low as 0.005 mM, and reached saturation at 25 mM with an induction fold of 22. Its fluorescence intensity correlated closely with inducer concentration. In contrast, the wild-type RC-3 showed no detectable fluorescence even at high (S)-2-AB concentrations (Fig. 3D). To assess the specificity of RC-4-Y83H, we tested potential interfering compounds present during (S)-2-AB synthesis as well as several common amides (Fig. 3E). The results demonstrated that RC-4-Y83H not only improved detection of short-chain amides compared to the wild-type (induction fold increased from 11 to 20 for acetamide and from 9 to 18 for propionamide), but also gained responsiveness to bulkier amides such as butyramide, (S)-2-AB, and acrylamide. No significant fluorescence above background was observed for structurally related metabolites like l-2-aminobutyric acid, l-aspartic acid, or l-asparagine.

  In summary, through iterative optimization and engineering of the AmiC–AmiR system, we developed an in vivo sensor capable of detecting (S)-2-AB. By modulating the AmiC and AmiR expression ratio (https://www.denovodna.com/software/design_rbs_library_calculator, we obtained sensors with different dynamic ranges: RC-4-Y83H (0.05–5 mM), RC-5-Y83H (5–100 mM), RC-6-Y83H (0.005–0.5 mM), and RC-7-Y83H (0.5–100 mM) (Fig. S3). RC-4-Y83H achieved a maximum induction fold of 23 for (S)-2-AB and significantly broadened the spectrum of detectable amides. This work establishes a solid foundation for further engineering of amide-responsive biosensors.

  To elucidate the molecular basis of the observed functional shift, we modeled the mutant structure using AlphaFold3 (ref. 42) and performed molecular docking to generate the RC-Y83H and (S)-2-AB complex. Structural analysis indicates that residues Y83 and T233 function as a steric gate at the entrance of the AmiC ligand-binding pocket. In the wild-type protein, the bulk of these residues restricts the entry of amides with larger substituents. The mutation of Tyr83 to a histidine (Y83H) reduces the side-chain volume, effectively enlarging the pocket entrance to accommodate bulkier substrates like (S)-2-AB (Fig. 3F). Furthermore, the docking model suggests that the imidazole ring of His83 forms a novel hydrogen bond with the α-amino group of (S)-2-AB. This interaction likely enhances the stability of the ligand within the binding pocket, providing a structural rationale for the higher fold-induction observed for (S)-2-AB compared to butyramide. Armed with this validated biosensor, we proceeded to screen enzyme libraries for the production of (S)-2-AB (Fig. S5).


### Design and initial screening of AsnA mutant libraries
  Structural analysis of wild-type Escherichia coli asparagine synthetase A (AsnA) (PDB ID: 12AS).43 Reveals a stringent substrate recognition mechanism: the α-carboxyl and amino groups of the native substrate, aspartate, are anchored by a network of polar residues within the active site pocket. This specific coordination sequesters these groups away from the ATP binding site, while orienting the β-carboxyl group toward the catalytic center for activation (Fig. 4A). This observation suggests that the spatial orientation of the substrate within the active site is the primary determinant of regiospecific activation. To test whether the wild-type enzyme possesses promiscuous activity toward our target substrate, we expressed and purified WT AsnA and performed in vitro catalytic assays with (S)-2-aminobutyric acid (S)-2-ABA). Liquid chromatography analysis confirmed the absence of product formation (Table S1). Molecular docking of (S)-2-ABA into the WT active site indicated that it adopts a binding mode analogous to l-aspartate, with the α-carboxyl group anchored in the conserved polar pocket distal to ATP (Fig. 4B). We hypothesized that this non-productive binding conformation prevents the activation of the carboxyl group, thereby rendering WT inactive toward (S)-2-ABA.

  Guided by these structural insights, we sought to remodel the active site to force a reorientation of the substrate. We identified 12 residues (D46, S72, A74, K77, R100, Q116, D118, E120, Y218, D219, S251, R255) located within 5 Å of the ligand that potentially govern substrate positioning (Fig. 4B). To enable high-throughput screening, we employed a dual-plasmid system: one plasmid for the inducible expression of AsnA variants and the other carrying the optimized AmiC–AmiR–Y83H biosensor.

  Detailed inspection of the WT and (S)-2-ABA docking model revealed that residues K77, G293, and R255 form direct interactions with the ligand's carboxyl group (Fig. 4B), imposing significant constraints that restrict substrate mobility. Consequently, we targeted these “anchor” residues in our initial engineering phase. We first performed single-site saturation mutagenesis on K77, G293, and R255. The libraries were screened on agar plates supplemented with 10 mM (S)-2-ABA, 10 mM NH4+, 10 mM Mg2+, and 2 mM l-arabinose. However, this first round of screening failed to yield variants with enhanced fluorescence, suggesting that single mutations were insufficient to disrupt the stable non-productive binding mode.


### Directed evolution and biochemical characterization of an active mutant
  Proceeding to combinatorial mutagenesis, we simultaneously targeted the key anchor residues. This approach yielded mutant M1 (K77L/R255W), which exhibited a distinct gain-of-function phenotype, showing a 1.9-fold increase in fluorescence intensity compared to the negative control. Using M1 as a template, we applied an Iterative Saturation Mutagenesis (ISM) strategy based on the CAST to further refine the active site.44 Screening of the second-generation library identified mutant M2 (containing the mutations K77L/E120L/R255W), which displayed the highest activity with a 2.5-fold fluorescence enhancement (Fig. 4C).

  To validate the catalytic activity of the evolved variant, M2 was over-expressed and purified for in vitro characterization. LC-MS analysis of the reaction mixture definitively confirmed the conversion of (S)-2-ABA into high-purity (S)-2-AB. Chiral analysis confirmed that (S)-2-ABA was converted into (S)-2-aminobutyramide with >99% enantiomeric excess (ee). Under maximum-sensitivity conditions, no reaction product was detected for (R)-2-ABA above the assay limit of detection LOD (S/N = 3, LOD(R)-2AB = 0.089 mM), which corresponds to a specific activity below 1.55 × 10−5 U g−1 (Fig. 4D and S8). Between the two substrates tested under sensitive conditions (4 mg mL−1 enzyme, 24 h), M2 exhibits a clear preference switch relative to the WT. WT (M2) gave no detectable product from (S)-2-ABA (l-Asp). No peak was observed above the assay LOD (S/N = 3, LOD(S)-2AB = 0.1 mM, LODl-Asn = 0.12 mM), which corresponds to a specific activity below 1.74 × 10−5 U g−1 (WT for (S)-2ABA) and 2.2 × 10−5 U g−1 (M2 for l-Asp). While acquiring synthetic activity for (S)-2-AB (1.1 U g−1) (Table 1). Additionally, the evolved enzyme exhibited a shift in optimal pH to 8.5 and an optimal temperature of 40 °C (Fig. S9 and S10).


### Molecular mechanism of substrate reorientation
  To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which the M2 mutant catalyzes the amidation of (S)-2-ABA, we built a structural model of the M2 mutant from the experimental crystal structure of E. coli AsnA (PDB 12AS)43 with the K77L/E120L/R255W substitutions introduced in Schrödinger and performed molecular docking with (S)-2-ABA (Fig. 5A). Comparing the results with the wild-type (WT) docking with l-Asp, the docking score for the M2-(S)-2-ABA was −3.69 kcal mol−1, which is higher than the −5.63 kcal mol−1 observed for the WT-l-ASP. This indicates that substitutions of hydrophobic amino acids reduce the substrate affinity of the mutant, which appears to be unfavorable for the amidation of (S)-2-ABA. This prediction is agreement with the experimental kinetic data, particularly the increase in the Km value from 1.64 mmol L−1 (WT-l-aspartate) to 9.58 mmol L−1 (M2-(S)-2-ABA) (Table 1). Structurally, the K77L and R255W substitutions eliminate the hydrogen bond network that anchors the α-carboxyl group of the substrate in the WT enzyme, causing the carboxyl group of (S)-2-ABA to no longer be firmly fixed by R255 (Fig. 4A and 5A). Nevertheless, key hydrogen bonds between the amino group of (S)-2-ABA and the residues S72, Q116, and D118 are retained, ensuring the stable positioning of the substrate within the binding pocket. Most notably, (S)-2-ABA undergoes a 180° flip within the binding pocket of M2 (Fig. 5A and B). This reorientation significantly reduces the distance between the carboxyl carbon of the substrate and the α-phosphate of ATP, narrowing it from 7.8 Å in the WT to 4.7 Å in M2 (Fig. 5B). Overall, we successfully achieved substrate flipping at the cost of a slight reduction in substrate affinity, and this structural rearrangement constitutes the key driving force enabling asparagine synthetase to catalyze the production of (S)-2-AB.

  To further investigate how the M2 mutations dictate the catalytic trajectory, we performed extensive Molecular Dynamics (MD)45 simulations to monitor the behavior of (S)-2-ABA within the ligand-binding pockets of both the WT and mutant enzymes (Fig. S12). Meanwhile, the distance between the substrate carboxyl carbon and the α-phosphate group of ATP, as well as the rotational orientation of the substrate, were tracked during the simulations. The results demonstrate that the median distance between the carboxyl carbon of the substrate and the α-phosphate of ATP in M2 is 7 Å, approximately 2 Å shorter than in the WT. Furthermore, in the M2 trajectory, this distance remained within the reactive threshold (<5.5 Å) for approximately 25% of the simulation time (Fig. 5D), suggesting that the carboxyl group of (S)-2-ABA adopts a conformation proximal to ATP for a significant fraction of the time, which is highly likely to facilitate the activation of the substrate's carboxyl group. An acute angle signifies a conformation where the carboxyl group faces ATP (productive), while an obtuse angle indicates it faces away (non-productive). In the M2 simulation, this angle remained consistently acute, clustering predominantly around 50°. In contrast, the WT simulation showed a consistently obtuse angle, clustering around 120° (Fig. 5E). Based on these dynamic profiles, we propose that the hydrophobic enclosure created by the L77, L120, and W255 mutations induces the inversion of (S)-2-ABA. Additionally, the steric bulk introduced by the tryptophan at position 255 likely acts as a wedge, forcing the substrate into a closer proximity with ATP.

  Finally, we analyzed the Free Energy Landscape to determine the energy required to maintain various conformations during the catalytic process (Fig. 5F). The data indicate that in the WT, the most stable conformation for (S)-2-ABA corresponds to a distance between the carboxyl carbon of the substrate and the α-phosphate of ATP of 9 Å and an orientation angle of 120°, which positions the reactive carboxyl group away from ATP. In stark contrast, the global energy minimum for M2 corresponds to a conformation where the carboxyl group is oriented toward and in close proximity to ATP. This confirms that the mutations in M2 have fundamentally remodeled the active site energy landscape, stabilizing a “flipped” substrate binding mode. This complete reversal of substrate orientation is the direct mechanistic basis enabling the engineered asparagine synthetase to catalyze the conversion of (S)-2-ABA to (S)-2-AB. Notably, the conformation in which the carboxyl group of (S)-2-ABA is proximal to ATP is consistently observed in the energy landscape of the wild-type (WT) (Fig. 5F). To investigate whether these emerging conformations are sufficiently stable to support the activation of the carboxyl group by ATP, we adopted the substrate conformation unique to M2—specifically, the conformation where the substrate's carboxyl group is proximal to ATP—aligned it to the WT, and performed molecular dynamics simulations. The results showed that the substrate underwent a flip within the binding pocket within an extremely short time (<1 ns) (Fig. S11), stabilizing ultimately at the polar end distal to ATP. This demonstrates that such a conformation cannot be sustained in the wild-type to support the catalysis of (S)-2-AB. Although the M2 variant shows a reversed substrate preference, its absolute catalytic efficiency remains significantly lower than that of the wild-type enzyme toward its native substrate. Furthermore, both the distance distribution (Fig. 5D) and the energy landscape (Fig. 5F) of M2 reveal that the substrate still adopts a substantial number of catalytically unfavorable conformations during the simulation trajectory. This dynamic flexibility indicates that while the primary steric hindrance has been removed to allow the 180° substrate flip, the newly formed hydrophobic pocket (L77/L120/W255) lacks the precise hydrogen-bonding network required to rigidly lock the α-carboxyl group in the optimal attack geometry. Addressing this bottleneck in future studies will require iterative optimization of secondary-shell residues to restrict the conformational ensemble, or the exploration of alternative amide synthetase scaffolds more naturally amenable to α-carboxyl activation.


## Conclusion
  This study successfully engineered E. coli asparagine synthetase A (AsnA) through biosensor-guided directed evolution to acquire non-native amidation activity, enabling the proof-of-concept direct conversion of (S)-2-ABA into (S)-2-AB, a key chiral intermediate for antiepileptic drugs. Initially, a highly sensitive and specific biosensor (RC-4-Y83H) for (S)-2-AB was developed based on the AmiC–AmiR transcription factor, achieving micromolar detection and a 23-fold induction response toward the target product. Utilizing this sensor to screen saturation mutagenesis libraries, a key variant (K77L/E120L/R255W) was identified, which reversed the substrate preference between the two substrates tested and synthesized (S)-2-AB with retention of configuration. Molecular dynamics simulations and free-energy landscape analysis revealed that the mutations disrupt the polar “anchor” network present in the wild-type enzyme, inducing a 180° flip of the substrate within the active site. This repositions the α-carboxyl group into a catalytically productive geometry close to ATP, fundamentally shifting the enzyme's function from native asparagine synthesis to non-native amide formation. While the absolute catalytic turnover leaves room for further evolutionary optimization, the (S)-2-AB biosensor-HTS platform developed in this work and the fundamental “substrate flip” mechanism established here provide a robust, generalizable paradigm for the future design of “new-to-nature” catalysts for diverse green chemical transformations. We emphasise that M2 is an early proof-of-concept variant: its activity (kcat = 0.047 min−1; 1.1 U g−1) remains far below what practical or industrial application would require, and it is not intended as an industrial biocatalyst. Its main limitation is that the re-engineered active site permits the productive flipped conformation but does not yet rigidly lock the alpha-carboxyl group in the optimal attack geometry. Future work could close this gap through further iterative CAST/ISM optimisation of second-shell residues, ATP-regeneration systems (for example polyphosphate kinase), whole-cell biocatalysis, or the exploration of amide-synthetase and ligase scaffolds more naturally suited to alpha-carboxyl activation.


## Materials and methods

### Materials
  All chemical reagents were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai, China), Aladdin (Shanghai, China), and Macklin (Shanghai, China). PrimeSTAR Max DNA Polymerase and Dpn I were purchased from Takara Bio Inc. (Japan). ABclonal MultiF Seamless Assembly Mix was acquired from ABclonal Technology (Wuhan, China). DNA Clean-up Kit and Plasmid Mini Kit were obtained from Cwbio (Wuhan, China). Oligonucleotides were synthesized and sequenced by Azenta (Suzhou, China).


### Plasmid and strain construction
  Escherichia coli strain JM109 was used as the host for biosensor construction and high-throughput enzyme screening, while E. coli BL21(DE3) was employed for protein overexpression. The previously constructed plasmid pEV-GFP served as the biosensor vector.38 All plasmids were constructed using Gibson assembly and standard molecular cloning techniques. The purified fragments were seamlessly joined using DNA Assembly Mix Ultra EG24204S (BestEnzymes Biotech Co., Ltd, China). Plasmids and primers used in this study are listed in Tables S1 and S2, respectively.

  A biosensor for (S)-2-aminobutyramide detection was constructed, comprising the transcription factor pair AmiC–AmiR, a transcription termination sequence (TS), and GFP as the reporter. The codon-optimized genes encoding AmiC–AmiR and terminator sequence were commercially synthesized and inserted downstream of the constitutive promoters LacI and Pveg in pEV-GFP to generate plasmid RC-0. Using RC-0 as the backbone, RBS001 was inserted between AmiC and AmiR to construct RC-1. In RC-2, AmiC was placed downstream of the constitutive promoter J23100, and AmiR was placed downstream of LacI, with a transcription terminator introduced at the C-terminus of AmiC via overlap PCR. To enhance the detection sensitivity of RC-2 toward (S)-2-aminobutyramide, promoters of varying strengths were used to regulate AmiC expression and integrated into RC-2 by primer-based cloning.


### Fluorescence analysis
  Transformed cells harboring the sensor were grown overnight at 37 °C on LB agar plates supplemented with 100 µg mL−1 kanamycin and 10 mM acetamide. Fluorescent colonies were visualized under blue light. For liquid-phase fluorescence assays, single colonies containing the sensor were inoculated into 96-deep-well plates containing 600 µL of LB medium with 100 µg mL−1 kanamycin. After 8 h of growth at 37 °C with shaking at 300 rpm, 1% (v/v) of the seed culture was transferred into 5 mL of fresh LB medium containing different concentrations of acetamide (0–100 mM). Following incubation at 37 °C with shaking at 400 rpm for 18 h, GFP fluorescence intensity (excitation 495 nm, emission 525 nm) and OD600 were measured using a BioTek Synergy H1 multimode microplate reader.


### Optimization of the AmiC/AmiR expression ratio
  Random mutations were simultaneously introduced into the ribosomal binding sites of AmiC and AmiR using primers containing the degenerate codon “NNK.” Using RC-2 as the template, the fragment containing the mutated RBS region was amplified with primers AmiR-RBSNNK-F and AmiC-RBSNNK-R, while the backbone was amplified with primers AmiC-RBSNNK-F and AmiR-RBSNNK-R. PCR products were treated with DpnI and purified using a DNA Clean-up Kit. The two fragments were assembled by seamless assembly and transformed into E. coli JM109. Transformants were plated on LB agar containing 5 mM acetamide and 100 µg mL−1 kanamycin and incubated at 37 °C for 12 h. Highly fluorescent colonies, identified visually under blue light, were inoculated into 96-well plates with or without 5 mM acetamide and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Fluorescence and OD600 were measured as described, and fluorescence values were normalized to cell density (OD600). The top 10 transformants were selected for further validation. Each was inoculated into a test tube containing 3 mL of LB medium with 100 µg mL−1 kanamycin and grown at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm for 12 h. Then, 1% (v/v) of the seed culture was transferred into 5 mL of LB medium with or without 5 mM acetamide. After 24 h of growth at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm, the transformants exhibiting the highest fluorescence were selected for subsequent analysis.


### Engineering of AmiC and mutant library construction
  Based on a CAST-guided semi-rational strategy, an AmiC mutant library was constructed. Residues within 5 Å of the ligand were grouped according to their sequential distances. The library was generated by whole-plasmid PCR using primers containing the degenerate codon “NNK,” followed by Gibson assembly. The ligation products were transformed into E. coli JM109 to generate the AmiC mutant library. Transformants were plated on LB agar containing 10 mM (S)-2-AB and 100 µg mL−1 kanamycin. After 12 h of growth at 37 °C, screening was performed as described above. Mutants displaying higher fluorescence in the presence of (S)-2-AB were selected for further characterization.


### High-throughput screening of amide synthetase
  A dual-plasmid high-throughput screening system based on sensor RC-Y83H was established for in vivo detection of amide synthetase activity. The sensor plasmid carries a high-copy ColE1 origin and a kanamycin resistance marker, while the enzyme expression plasmid, under arabinose-inducible control, contains a medium-copy p15A origin and a chloramphenicol resistance marker.

  The AsnA random mutagenesis library was constructed using the same method described for the AmiC library. The AsnA library was transformed into E. coli JM109 competent cells already harboring the sensor plasmid. Transformants were plated on LB agar containing 10 mM (S)-2-ABA, 10 mM ATP, 10 mM NH4Cl, 10 mM MgCl2, 2 mM l-arabinose, 100 µg mL−1 kanamycin, and 34 µg mL−1 chloramphenicol, and incubated at 37 °C for 12 h. Colonies showing high fluorescence were inoculated into LB medium supplemented with arabinose and substrate and grown at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm for 24 h. After normalization of fluorescence to cell density, the mutants with the highest fluorescence were selected for sequencing and further analysis.


### Over-expression and purification of recombinant proteins
  The coding sequences of WT and its mutants were cloned into pET-24a with a C-terminal Strep-tag II. E. coli BL21(DE3) was transformed with the resulting plasmids and grown in LB medium containing 100 µg mL−1 kanamycin at 37 °C. When the OD600 reached 0.6, protein expression was induced by adding IPTG, and the temperature was lowered to 25 °C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation (8000×g, 5 min, 4 °C) and lysed by sonication in 10 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). The lysate was clarified by centrifugation (10 000×g, 30 min, 4 °C). Enzyme purification was performed using an ÄKTA purifier (GE Healthcare, UK). The soluble fraction was loaded onto a 5 mL strep trap XT column equilibrated with 10 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) and eluted with 50 mM d-biotin. Eluted proteins were concentrated, aliquoted, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C. Protein concentration was determined by the Bradford method, and purity was verified by SDS-PAGE (Fig. S11).


### Enzymatic activity and kinetic analysis
  The specific activity of WT was determined using l-Asp as the substrate. The 500 µL reaction mixture contained 0.01 mg mL−1 purified WT, 20 mM l-Asp, 10 mM ATP, 10 mM NH4Cl, and 10 mM MgCl2. For mutant M2 (K77L/E120L/R255W), activity was measured using (S)-2-ABA as the substrate in a reaction mixture containing 1 mg mL−1 purified M2, 20 mM (S)-2-ABA, 10 mM ATP, 10 mM NH4Cl, and 10 mM MgCl2. Reactions were conducted at 40 °C for 10 min and terminated by adding 1 mL of acetonitrile. Product formation was quantified by high-performance liquid chromatography. One unit of enzyme activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to produce 1 µmol of product per minute.

  Kinetic parameters (Km, kcat) of WT and M2 were determined under their respective standard assay conditions with substrate concentrations of 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 mM. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 7.

  The limit of detection (LOD) and limit of quantification (LOQ) of the analytical method were calculated based on the standard deviation of the response and the slope of the calibration curve, in accordance with the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) Q2 guidelines. The values were derived using the following equations:LOD = 3.3σ/SLOQ = 10σ/Swhere σ is the standard error of the intercept derived from the linear regression analysis of the calibration curve, and S is the slope of the linear regression line.


### Analytical methods
  Concentrations of l-Asp and (S)-AB were measured by HPLC (Agilent 1260, USA). Reaction mixtures were subjected to online derivatization with a reagent consisting of 53.6 mg mL−1 o-phthalaldehyde (OPA), 100 mM N-acetylcysteine (NAC), and 100 mM sodium borate-sodium hydroxide buffer (pH 9.0) in a 1 : 1 ratio. Derivatized samples were analyzed using a Diamonsil C18(2) column (5 µm, 250 mm × 4.6 mm, Dikma Technologies, China) at a detection wavelength of 336 nm. The mobile phase composition and gradient elution program are provided in Table S4. The flow rate was 0.8 mL min−1, and the column temperature was maintained at 40 °C.

  Molecular weights of reaction products were determined by LC-MS using a Waters ACQUITY UPLC system equipped with a Spherisorb Amino (NH2) column (3 µm, 4.6 mm × 250 mm, Waters). Acetonitrile was used as the mobile phase with gradient elution at 25 °C (Table S4). The flow rate was 0.3 mL min−1, and the injection volume was 5 µL. Eluted samples were introduced into a Waters MALDI SYNAPT Q-TOF mass spectrometer via ESI-positive ion mode. Operating parameters were as follows: capillary voltage, 3.5 kV; cone voltage, 30 V; ion source temperature, 100 °C; desolvation temperature, 400 °C; desolvation gas flow, 700 L h; cone gas flow, 50 L h; collision energy, 6/20 eV; mass range, 20–2000 m/z; detector voltage, 1800 V.


### Homology modeling
  Structural analysis of AmiC and AmiR was conducted using a previously reported crystal structure (PDB ID: 1QO0).39 WT AsnA was modelled using AlphaFold3 (ipTM/pTM = 0.90/0.92). Because structures with up to 99% sequence identity are available in the PDB (e.g., 11AS and 12AS) and the positions of ATP and Mg2+ are known from related asparagine synthetase structures (e.g., 3REU), the model is highly reliable: the first three N-terminal residues have pLDDT values of 70 to 90 (confident), while all remaining residues have pLDDT above 90 (very high). The RC-Y83H structure was predicted via AlphaFold3, yielding high-confidence models with ipTM/pTM scores of 0.89/0.90. Triple mutations (K77L/E120L/R255W) were introduced into the AsnA homodimeric framework using the Schrödinger 2024-3 package. Subsequently, both WT and mutant models were processed using the Schrödinger's Protein Prep package: hydrogens were added, and protonation states were assigned via PROPKA at pH 8.0. Following an initial restrained minimization (RMSD cutoff of 0.3 Å), a second round of local minimization was performed on all residues within 3 Å of sites 77, 120, and 255 to refine the binding pocket environment.


### Molecular docking
  Molecular docking was performed using the Schrödinger 2024-3 suite. Ligands (ammonia, 2-S-ABA, and l-aspartic acid) were retrieved from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and prepared via LigPrep at pH 8.0, maintaining their original chirality. For the homodimeric protein, docking was conducted independently for each chain. Grid generation and docking utilized the OPLS_2005 force field, with the grid center positioned between the ATP and Arg255 (inner box: 10 Å × 10 Å × 10 Å; outer box: 18 Å × 18 Å × 18 Å). A sequential docking strategy was employed: ammonia was docked first, followed by a second grid generation that incorporated the predicted ammonia position (as only one binding pose was identified). Subsequent substrates were docked using the Glide with XP precision module of the Schrödinger 2024-3 software suite. For each substrate, the two highest-scoring poses were retained. Finally, the poses exhibiting the lowest docking energy and optimal orientation relative to ammonia, ATP, and residues 77, 120, and 255 were selected for subsequent MD simulations.


### Molecular dynamics simulations
  Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using GPU-accelerated GROMACS 2024.4 with the Amber 14SB force field.45 For ligand preparation, protonation states were predicted by PROPKA.46 Ligand charge was calculated by Multiwfn package based on Restrained Electrostatic Potential (RESP) charge. And the topology file was constructed by Sobtop package based on GAFF force field.

  Final simulations were carried out at 300 K and atmospheric pressure for 500 ns with a 2 fs time step. Periodic boundary conditions and the particle mesh Ewald method (cut-off radius 12 Å) were applied to treat electrostatic interactions. The protein was solvated in a TIP3P water box with a minimum distance of 12 Å from the box boundary, and 0.15 mol L−1 NaCl was added to neutralize the system. Temperature and pressure were controlled using the C-rescale and V-rescale algorithms, respectively. All the trajectory analysis (e.g. RMSD, RMSF, distance, angle) was performed using GROMACS tools, and visualization was conducted with PyMOL and VMD. Each simulation was performed 3 times.
