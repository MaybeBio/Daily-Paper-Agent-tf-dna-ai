# An MRI-Based Intratumoral and Peritumoral 2.5D Deep Learning Model for Predicting P53-Mutated Hepatocellular Carcinoma: A Two-Center Study


## Introduction
  Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common primary liver cancer with high mortality. TP53 is the most frequently mutated tumor-suppressor gene in HCC, and P53 mutations are associated with poor differentiation, vascular invasion, and lower survival. Preoperative noninvasive detection of P53-mutated HCC is critical for treatment planning and prognostic assessment. This study aimed to develop and validate a deep learning (DL) model for predicting P53 mutation status in HCC.


## Methods
  In this retrospective two-center study, 373 patients with pathologically confirmed HCC were enrolled from two institutions (Center 1: n=320; Center 2: n=53). The Center 1 cohort was randomly partitioned into a training cohort (n=256) and an internal test cohort (n=64). Patients from Center 2 constituted an independent external test cohort (n=53). We employed a 2.5D ResNet34 architecture to process three-channel dynamic contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (DCE-MRI). Separate deep learning models were constructed for intratumoral and peritumoral regions using both single-phase and multiphase (arterial, portal venous, and delayed) fusion sequences. Significant clinical variables were selected to build a Clinical model, which was subsequently integrated with the optimal deep learning model to create a Combined model. Model performance was assessed by the area under the curve (AUC), calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA).


## Results
  Multiphase fusion models consistently outperformed their single-phase models. The peritumoral (5mm) fusion model (IntraPeri5mm_Fusion) outperformed the intratumoral fusion model, achieving AUCs of 0.881 (95% CI: 0.836–0.926), 0.772 (95% CI: 0.651–0.893), and 0.757 (95% CI: 0.624–0.891) on the training cohort, internal test cohort, and external test cohort, respectively. The Combined model yielded further incremental improvement, with AUCs reaching 0.886 (95% CI: 0.842–0.930), 0.805 (95% CI: 0.691–0.918), and 0.793 (95% CI: 0.668–0.918) across the respective cohorts.


## Conclusions
  The proposed 2.5D DL model based on DCE-MRI effectively predicts P53-mutated HCC. Integrating peritumoral features with clinical variables yields the best performance, offering a promising noninvasive tool for prognostic stratification and individualized therapeutic planning.


## Introduction
  Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common primary liver cancer, with high incidence and mortality rates. 1  In addition, survival from liver cancer is lower in children and adolescents. 2  Despite recent advances in research and therapy, patients with HCC still have poor overall survival (OS): 49.3%, 35.3%, 26.6%, and 19.5% at 1, 2, 3, and 5 years after diagnosis, respectively. 3  Whole-genome sequencing studies have identified TP53 as the most frequently mutated tumor-suppressor gene in HCC. 4  Targeted exome sequencing has further shown a strong association between TP53 mutations and hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection, a major risk factor for HCC. 5  HCCs with P53 mutations demonstrate poor histological differentiation 6  and a high tendency for vascular invasion, 7  leading to lower 5-year OS. 8  P53 mutation is also an independent risk factor for HCC recurrence. 9  These mutations significantly influence HCC progression and treatment response. Currently, P53 mutation status is determined exclusively through invasive tissue sampling with subsequent immunohistochemistry or genetic sequencing. This approach precludes preoperative risk stratification, carries procedural risks, and is subject to sampling bias from intratumoral heterogeneity. A noninvasive predictive tool would enable preoperative identification of P53-mutated HCC, thereby informing surgical planning, guiding adjuvant therapy selection, and advancing personalized treatment strategies. Consequently, preoperative noninvasive detection of P53-mutated HCC is critical for treatment planning and prognostic assessment.

  Previous studies have reported that certain conventional imaging features are associated with P53-mutated HCC. These features include rim arterial phase hyperenhancement (APHE), 10  dilated vasculature in the arterial phase (AP) of dynamic computed tomography (CT), a lower relative enhancement ratio in the hepatobiliary phase, 8  and lower apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values. 11  However, these conventional imaging features are often subtle, overlapping, and subject to considerable inter-observer variability, making reliable preoperative differentiation of P53 mutation status challenging in clinical practice. Moreover, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data may be limited by subjectivity and technical constraints, reducing their ability to fully capture tumor heterogeneity. Recently, deep learning methods that use imaging data have shown substantial promise in multiple aspects of HCC evaluation. These applications include identifying subtypes, 12  predicting disease risk, 13  forecasting early recurrence, 14  and assessing post-treatment response. 15  At the core of deep learning is the construction of convolutional neural network (CNN) models that perform high-performance classification on large datasets. With the progressive refinement of multicenter, large-sample data and advancements in model interpretability research, the integration of deep learning into routine clinical practice is anticipated. This integration should improve the precision of HCC diagnosis and treatment. 16

  Peritumoral tissue encompasses the region adjacent to the tumor, including contiguous normal and potentially compromised structures. This microenvironment plays a key role in HCC progression, invasiveness, treatment response, and prognosis.17,18 The interaction between tumor cells and the peritumoral microenvironment underscores the importance of this region in driving HCC progression. Systematic analysis of cellular and molecular changes in peritumoral tissue provides crucial insights into tumor-host interactions, treatment efficacy, and patient prognosis. In this study, we constructed a 2.5D deep learning model based on dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI (DCE-MRI) to predict P53 mutation status in HCC. We also systematically compared the predictive performance of intratumoral and peritumoral models and incorporated a transformer fusion strategy. This innovative approach is critical for advancing personalized diagnosis and treatment in patients with HCC.


## Materials and Methods

### Patient
  This retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2024) and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hospital. The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

  We consecutively enrolled 373 patients with pathologically confirmed HCC from two institutions via retrospective review of electronic medical records. From January 2020 to February 2021, we randomly split 320 HCC patients from Center 1 into a training cohort (n = 256) and an internal test cohort (n = 64) in an 8:2 ratio. This fixed patient list was used for all subsequent model development and evaluation. Repeated random splitting or cross-validation was not performed because an independent external cohort from Center 2 was used for validation. All eligible patients during the study period were included; therefore, no a priori sample size calculation was performed. Additionally, from September 2018 to February 2023, we enrolled 53 patients from Center 2 who constituted the external test cohort. The comprehensive methodology of this study is illustrated in Figure 1. All enrolled cases met predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, ensuring the final dataset contained complete imaging data. The largest tumor diameter (LTD) was determined by measuring the maximum diameter of the largest lesion visible on T2WI sequences. Relevant clinical variables, including alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) levels and age, were extracted from the clinical database and verified at enrollment.Figure 1.Study flowchart of the enrolled patients

  All patient data were fully de-identified prior to analysis. Personal identifiers, including names, medical record numbers, dates of birth, and other protected health information, were removed or anonymized to ensure that the identity of any individual could not be ascertained in any way.


### Detection of P53 Mutation Status
  The patients were divided into two groups: P53-mutated (P53+) HCC and non-P53-mutated (P53-) HCC. A tumor was classified as P53-mutated if ≥10% of neoplastic cells showed positive nuclear immunostaining for P53.8-11 Representative p53 immunohistochemistry images are shown in Supplementary Figure S1.


### Magnetic Resonance Imaging Protocol
  At Center 1, preoperative liver MRI was performed on seven different scanners, as shown in Supplementary Table S1. At Center 2, preoperative MRI was performed with a 3.0T system (Achieva, Philips) using a 16-channel abdominal coil. Sequence parameters are listed in Supplementary Table S2.


### Imaging Preprocessing and ROI Segmentation
  An abdominal radiologist with 6 years of experience outlined the entire tumor using ITK-SNAP, and a senior abdominal radiologist with 15 years of experience revised the contours on every transverse slice. To assess reproducibility, 30 HCC cases were re-segmented one week later. Inter- and intra-reader ICCs were 0.88 (95% CI: 0.53–0.99) and 0.92 (95% CI: 0.23–0.99), respectively. While the point estimates indicated good to excellent agreement, the wide confidence intervals (particularly for intra-reader agreement) suggest variable consistency across different radiomic features. For discrepant delineations between the two radiologists, consensus was reached through joint review and discussion, with the senior radiologist’s assessment serving as the final arbitration when agreement could not be achieved. All volumes were resampled to 1 mm isotropic voxels and their intensities were normalized to [0, 1] to reduce inter-scan variability. The intratumoral region was extracted from the region of interest (ROI) mask. The peritumoral region was created by expanding the ROI outward by 5 mm from the tumor edge (Supplementary Figure S2). A multi-channel 2.5D DL model was constructed. First, the slice with the largest tumor area was identified and designated as the target slice (n). Two additional slices, n − 2 and n + 2, were selected, yielding a three-slice input stack.


### Single-Modality DL Model Construction
  We used a 2.5D ResNet34 initialized with ImageNet-pretrained weights. The model input consisted of three slices: the slice with the largest tumor area (n), together with the n − 2 and n + 2 slices. After global average pooling, each ResNet34 backbone generated a 512-dimensional feature vector, which was passed to a fully connected classifier for single-phase prediction. No manual feature selection was applied; discriminative imaging features were learned end-to-end through backpropagation.

  A weighted cross-entropy loss was used to counteract class imbalance. The class weights were calculated based on the ratio of positive and negative samples. We used the Adam optimizer (β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999) with an initial learning rate of 1 × 10-4. Training began with a linear warmup to increase the learning rate, followed by cosine annealing to smoothly reduce it. The regularization method involved adding dropout with a rate of 0.5 before the fully connected layer and adding L2 weight decay of 1e-5 in the convolutional layer. During training, data augmentation included random rotations (±15°), horizontal and vertical flips, scaling (±10%), Gaussian noise, and brightness/contrast adjustments.

  We independently trained intratumoral and peritumoral models for each of the three contrast-enhanced phases, including the arterial phase, portal venous phase (PVP), and delayed phase (DP), resulting in six distinct single-phase deep learning models.


### Fusion DL Model Construction
  We used the ResNet34 feature-extraction layer, previously trained on each single-modal model, as the pretrained model for the fusion model. This layer remained trainable during fusion to be optimized through multimodal joint learning. For the fusion model, the AP, PVP, and DP feature vectors were treated as three modality tokens. A Transformer-based fusion module with multi-head self-attention was used to model cross-phase dependencies and adaptively weight information from different enhancement phases. The fused representation was then connected to a binary classifier to generate the final DL probability. A detailed explanation of the fusion strategy is provided in Supplementary Material S1 and Supplementary Figure S3.


### Combined Model
  Clinical variables were evaluated using univariate statistical analysis. Multicollinearity among selected variables was assessed before model construction, and continuous variables were standardized. Based on this procedure, AFP and LTD were identified as the final clinical features and used to build the Clinical model. The best-performing deep learning model was then combined with the Clinical model, and the integrated features were fed into a logistic regression classifier to construct the final Combined model. The overall workflow is shown in Figure 2.Figure 2.The overall workflow of the study


### Statistical Analysis
  Continuous variables were compared with the Mann-Whitney U test (for non-normally distributed data) or the independent-samples t-test (for normally distributed data), and categorical variables using the χ 2  test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to evaluate the predictive performance of P53 mutations, and the DeLong test was used to compare AUCs between models. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p-value less than 0.05. The reporting of this study conforms to the TRIPOD+AI statement. 19

  To improve interpretability, Grad-CAM heatmaps were generated for the IntraPeri5mm_Fusion DL model. Grad-CAM was calculated from the final convolutional block of each phase-specific ResNet34 backbone and overlaid on the AP, PVP, and DP images. As shown in Figure 5, representative true-positive, true-negative, false-positive, and false-negative cases were selected to demonstrate the model behavior at the sample level. In the correctly classified positive case, the high-activation areas were mainly located within the tumor and adjacent peritumoral region, suggesting that the model captured biologically relevant imaging information around the lesion. In the true-negative case, the activation was relatively weak or distributed away from the key tumor region, consistent with the negative prediction. The false-positive and false-negative examples further illustrate potential sources of model error, including heterogeneous enhancement patterns and incomplete attention to the most informative tumor-peritumoral areas. ## Discussion
  In this study, we established a multi-channel 2.5D model based on DCE-MRI images of primary tumors and analyzed the performance differences in predicting P53 mutations in HCC between intratumoral and peritumoral models. We also built a multi-phase fusion model with a transformer strategy and found that the peritumoral fusion model achieved the best AUCs (training cohort: 0.881; internal test cohort: 0.772; external test cohort: 0.757), indicating that multi-phase MRI and peritumoral heterogeneity offer valuable information for molecular subtyping of HCC. By integrating the Clinical model and the IntraPeri5mm_Fusion DL model, we constructed and validated a Combined model that achieved excellent AUCs of 0.886 in the training cohort, 0.805 in the internal test cohort, and 0.793 in the external test cohort. We developed a nomogram to visualize the Combined model and confirmed its clinical utility through DCA.

  Multiple studies on microvascular invasion (MVI) in HCC have shown that multi-phase MRI captures tumor heterogeneity and vascular distribution better than single-phase images, providing richer information on tumor aggressiveness.20,21 The Fusion DL model integrating AP, PVP, and DP phases consistently outperformed single-phase models across all cohorts, with AUCs ranging from 0.723 to 0.881. The arterial phase alone achieved suboptimal AUCs (0.734, 0.666, 0.629), likely because early enhancement patterns are insufficient to capture the full spectrum of P53-associated heterogeneity. The portal venous and delayed phases contribute complementary information on contrast washout and retention kinetics, which the transformer mechanism effectively integrates through multi-head self-attention and adaptive weight allocation. This end-to-end joint optimization is more flexible than simple concatenation, enabling dynamic feature fusion and improved classification performance. In a multitask deep-learning model that predicted MVI and recurrence-free survival (RFS) in HCC from preoperative MRI, a transformer architecture also extracted key features from multiple sequences, yielding accurate predictions. 22

  In deep learning research, most work focuses on 2D and 3D models. 2D models require less data, train and infer faster, and incur lower annotation costs, but they only provide single-level information. For CT or MRI data that exist in 3D space, applying 2D methods to 3D tasks may lose key structural information; although 3D models preserve full 3D information, they need large datasets to learn complex spatial features. Insufficient data can cause poor performance or overfitting. A 2.5D image retains adjacent 2D slices, keeps the same pixel height and width as the 2D image, and provides contextual information around the target slice. 23  In this study, we constructed a multi-channel 2.5D DL model that takes the target slice (n) with the largest tumor area and its adjacent slices (n − 2 and n + 2) as input. Stacking these 2D slices forms a 2.5D image. Previous studies have shown that 2.5D DL models perform well across datasets, outperform 3D models, and are especially effective for predicting early postoperative HCC recurrence when combined with clinical features. 24  Another study that used deep-learning features to predict radiation pneumonitis in lung cancer found 2.5D DL models significantly outperformed 2D and 3D DL models. 25  Notably, a recent 2D DL model using multiphase DCE-MRI achieved high accuracy for P53-mutated HCC prediction in a single-center cohort. 26  However, this approach did not leverage 2.5D contextual information or peritumoral features, which our study addresses. Recently, 2.5D DL models have been increasingly applied to HCC tasks, including prediction of MVI and prognosis, 27  evaluation of postoperative responses to transarterial chemoembolization (TACE), 28  and forecasting histopathologic grade. 29  These studies also demonstrate that the 2.5D approach excels at capturing contextual information and offers promising potential for medical image segmentation and detection. Our previous 2D deep learning and radiomics model for P53-mutated HCC prediction achieved AUCs of 0.519–0.779, 30  while the current 2.5D fusion model substantially outperformed these prior approaches (AUCs 0.723–0.802) and effectively predicted P53-mutated HCC. The Combined model achieved AUCs of 0.886, 0.805, and 0.793 in the training, internal test, and external test cohorts, respectively, with incremental improvements observed across all cohorts compared to the IntraPeri5mm_Fusion DL model alone. This indicates that AFP and LTD provide complementary information that enhances predictive performance and stabilizes generalizability across institutions. The Combined model effectively captures both complex imaging features and clinical context, thereby improving overall predictive performance and clinical utility.

  Preoperative P53 prediction offers actionable clinical value in three aspects. First, predicted P53-mutated HCC may warrant wider resection margins or anatomical resection given their elevated risk of MVI and early recurrence. Second, high-risk P53-mutated cases identified preoperatively may benefit from intensified adjuvant therapy, such as transarterial chemoembolization or systemic treatment. Third, for patients with borderline resectability, P53 status prediction informs shared decision-making by stratifying biological aggressiveness beyond conventional staging. DCA supports this framework, showing the Combined model yielded the highest net benefit across threshold probabilities of 10–40%. Importantly, the model integrates routinely acquired preoperative DCE-MRI and basic clinical variables without additional cost, facilitating clinical adoption.

  The tumor microenvironment significantly influences tumor progression and treatment response, especially the peritumoral region, which plays a crucial role in the early recurrence of HCC. 31  Currently, few studies have used peritumoral deep-learning techniques to predict P53-mutated HCC. In a recent ultrasound-based peritumoral radiomics study for predicting P53 mutation status, peritumoral features accurately reflected HCC biology, suggesting that peritumoral radiomics will remain important. 32  However, that study used a small single-center dataset (n = 153) and ultrasound radiomics only. Compared with that study, we enrolled 373 patients from two centers. We used DCE-MRI to build a 2.5D deep-learning model and validated it in an external test cohort to ensure better reproducibility. The IntraPeri5mm_Fusion model achieved AUCs of 0.881, 0.772, and 0.757 in the training, internal test, and external test cohorts, respectively, consistently outperforming the Intra_Fusion model (0.802, 0.727, 0.723). This performance gap demonstrates that peritumoral features contain independent predictive information beyond intratumoral characteristics. The selection of a 5-mm peritumoral margin was based on established literature demonstrating that this distance captures the tumor-host interface microenvironment most relevant for HCC microvascular invasion 33  and recurrence prediction. 34  When the TP53 gene mutates, it promotes the proliferation of hepatocellular carcinoma cells 35  and leads to poor tumor differentiation, which increases the risk of vascular invasion. 6  The tumor becomes more invasive and infiltrates surrounding normal tissue. This explains why peritumoral models have good predictive ability for evaluating P53-mutated HCC. A DCE-MRI radiomics study that differentiated vessels encapsulating tumor clusters (VETC) and MVI in HCC also found the peritumoral model outperformed the intratumoral model. 36

  This study had several limitations. First, because the study was retrospective, selection bias was unavoidable. Second, because all eligible patients from the study period were included, no formal prospective sample size calculation was performed. The relatively limited cohort size may reduce statistical power and should be considered when interpreting subgroup and model-comparison results. Third, insufficient data precluded assessment of the Combined model’s ability to stratify prognostic risk in HCC. Future prospective validation should assess whether P53-mutated directed stratification improves RFS and OS outcomes compared to standard care. Fourth, MRI data were acquired with different scanners and parameters, reducing comparability across imaging datasets and potentially limiting model generalizability. Therefore, additional prospective studies with larger cohorts will be necessary to confirm model stability. Fifth, P53 mutation status was determined by immunohistochemistry rather than direct DNA sequencing. While this threshold is widely adopted, it serves as a surrogate marker that may not capture all mutation types. Future validation against direct TP53 sequencing is warranted to ensure generalizability across the full spectrum of TP53 alterations.


## Conclusions
  In summary, our study introduced a novel approach to develop and evaluate a 2.5D deep learning model using DCE-MRI for noninvasive preoperative prediction of P53-mutated HCC and compared the predictive performance of intratumoral and peritumoral models. Combining DL models with Clinical models enhances clinical applicability and value, thereby promoting personalized treatment strategies.
