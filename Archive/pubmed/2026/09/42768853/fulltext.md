# Assessment of the Targeted Therapeutic Potential of Naringenin‐Benzyl Piperazine Derivatives on Hypoxia‐Induced JAK/STAT Pathway in Breast and Ovarian Cancers


## ABSTRACT
  Naringenin, a prominent member of the flavanone family of polyphenols, is widely distributed in cherries, tomatoes, and citrus fruits, such as grapefruits, as well as in medicinal plants. In vitro and in vivo investigations have shown that naringenin, naringenin‐loaded nanoparticles, and its derivatives, whether administered singularly or in conjunction with other anticancer agents, can effectively counteract carcinogenic agents and inhibit the advancement of various malignancies, including colon cancer, lung neoplasms, ovarian cancer, and breast cancer. In this study, the effects of naringenin‐benzylpiperazines (3b, 3f, and 3g) were investigated on breast ductal carcinoma MCF7 (ER‐α+), human breast adenocarcinoma SK‐Br, human triple‐negative breast metastatic adenocarcinoma MDA‐MB‐231, ovarian cancer NIH‐OVCAR‐3, and human ovarian cancer SKOV‐3 cells. The IC50 values of 3b, 3f, and 3g were found to be between 31.39 and 73.60 µM, 29.11 and 86.91 µM, and 23.53 and 88.63 µM, respectively. The data obtained indicate that compound 3f may be a molecule with the potential to trigger mitochondrial apoptosis in cancer cell lines in a dose‐dependent manner by modulating hypoxia, angiogenesis, and JAK/STAT signaling pathways. In addition, the potential therapeutic value of the compound and its interaction mechanisms with molecular targets have been investigated through theoretical (in silico) calculations.


## Introduction
  As per reports by the World Health Organization (WHO), cancer is still a major threat to millions of people [1]. One of the most effective ways to fight cancer is chemotherapy, which began in the 1940s with cytotoxic agents in general and continued in the 1960s with naturally occurring chemicals such as anthracyclines or alkaloids extracted from Vinca [2]. Currently, fruit‐based anticancer and therapeutic agents, such as flavonoids and their derivatives, have been reported to exhibit noticeable effects in suppressing tumor formation and cancer cell growth [3]. Flavonoids are a wide range of naturally occurring polyphenols that are present in many fruits and vegetables that people frequently eat. These phytochemicals are classified into various subclasses, including anthocyanidins, flavonols, isoflavones, flavanones, flavan‐3‐ols, and flavones [4]. Flavonoids inhibit metastasis, angiogenesis, and proliferation, increase apoptosis, and disrupt several signal transduction pathways in the context of carcinogenesis [3].

  Naringenin is mainly found as aglycones; however, glycosylated and neohesperidoside forms are also present. It is a product of the hydrolysis of naringin or narirutin. The hydrophobic molecule naringenin (1) has a molecular weight of 272.25 g/mol (C15H12O5) and is soluble in organic solvents such as dimethyl sulfoxide and ethanol (Figure 1). This significant phytochemical, which is a member of the flavanone group of polyphenols, is mostly present in citrus fruits, such as grapefruit, lemon, cherries, and tomatoes, as well as foods made from some medicinal plants. Current evidence suggests that naringenin, as a herbal medicine, has important pharmacological properties, including antioxidant, neuroprotective, anti‐inflammatory, hepatoprotective, and anti‐cancer activities [5, 6, 7]. Data obtained from in vitro and in vivo studies on various cancer types, such as lung, breast, ovarian, colon, and pancreatic cancers, show that the application of pure naringenin, naringenin derivatives, naringenin‐loaded nanoparticles, and the combination of naringenin and anticancer agents results in the inactivation of carcinogens. In a variety of ways, naringenin prevents the spread of cancer, including the induction of apoptosis, cell cycle arrest, inhibition of angiogenesis, and modification of various signaling pathways, such as the PI3K/Akt, Wnt/β‐catenin, TGF‐β, and NF‐κB pathways [8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. Naringenin's hydrophobic and crystalline nature, along with its poor water solubility, low oral bioavailability, and instability, are the primary hindrances to its effective medical application. To overcome this physicochemical problem, naringenin derivatives or nanocarrier formulations are being developed [15]. To date, many naringenin derivatives have been synthesized, and biological activity studies have demonstrated potent anticancer activity against various cancers [16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. Naringenin derivatives, obtained by incorporating various potential bioactive molecules into naringenin, are novel and unique drug candidates. In our previous studies, the developed naringenin‐cycloaminoethyl derivatives (2) were specifically tested on human breast (MCF‐7) cervix (HeLa) cancers (Figure 1) [18]. In our subsequent research, we synthesized and evaluated naringenin‐benzyl piperazine compounds (3a–3h) on three‐dimensional (3D) Grade IV glioblastoma tumor spheroids to determine their capacity to selectively target the JAK/STAT pathway. All of the molecules showed strong anticancer effects on Glioblastoma tumors, but two derivatives in particular were found to suppress the HIF1‐α/VEGF/MMP9 axis, directly inhibiting the JAK2/STAT3 axis in the JAK/STAT pathway and suppressing tumor angiogenesis more potently than the currently licensed JAK2/JAK3 inhibitor ruxolitinib [17].

  In this study, the naringenin‐benzylpiperazine derivatives with promising anticancer potential synthesized were tested on three different types of breast tumors and two different types of ovarian cancer. Breast tumors are the most common, lethal cancers among women. Breast cancer is divided into three main subtypes based on the presence or absence of molecular markers for estrogen or progesterone receptors and human epidermal growth factor 2 (ERBB2; formerly known as HER2): hormone receptor‐positive/ERBB2‐negative (70% of patients), ERBB2‐positive (15%–20%), and triple‐negative (tumors lacking all three standard molecular markers; 15%). Studies have shown that naringenin has a high binding capacity to estrogen receptors (ERs), particularly ER‐α [8, 22], and that naringenin can suppress cells in a time‐ and dose‐dependent manner in breast cancer cell lines. Another study also noted that naringenin showed time‐dependent inhibition of the growth of human breast cancer cells (HTB26 and HTB132) and reduced colony formation while simultaneously promoting early and late apoptosis and necrosis [23]. In triple‐negative breast cancer cell lines, adjuvant therapy combining naringenin with copper and 2,2′‐bipyridine was more effective than naringenin alone [24]. Another synthetic naringenin derivative, 7‐O‐butyl naringenin, has been demonstrated to inhibit key cell growth pathways, such as MAPK, ROS generation, and elevated p38 levels [25].

  Ovarian cancer is the most lethal cancer and the fifth leading cause of cancer‐related death in women, especially in developed countries. There are three types of ovarian cancer: germ cell, sex cord stromal, and epithelial. Second, epithelial ovarian cancer originates from the epithelium of the ovary and fallopian tubes and is the most common type of ovarian cancer. Due to vague symptoms in the early stages and lack of access to appropriate screening techniques, more than 70% of patients are diagnosed in advanced stages of the disease, making treatment very challenging and contributing to the low 5‐year survival rate in ovarian cancer patients [26, 27]. Similar to other cancers, natural products are gaining attention in the treatment of ovarian cancer to address the drawbacks of conventional treatment strategies. In this context, in an in vitro study, Mundlia et al. combined naringenin with pectin to obtain a conjugate with increased solubility and compared the therapeutic effects of the conjugated compound with pure naringenin in the treatment of ovarian cancer. The cytotoxicity of the naringenin‐based conjugate on OVCAR‐5 cells was determined to be higher than that of pure polyphenol application in a concentration‐dependent manner [28].

  As can be seen from the literature findings, although there are few studies on it, both breast cancers and ovarian cancers respond to naringenin treatments, which has led us to consider our own developed and synthesized naringenin‐benzyl piperazine derivatives as potential targets.


## Results and Discussion

### Chemistry
  Compounds 3a–3c were prepared starting from naringenin in two steps. 7‐[2‐(Bromo)ethoxy]−5‐hydroxy‐2‐(4‐hydroxyphenyl)–2,3‐dihydrochromen‐4‐one was obtained from naringenin and 1,2‐dibromoethane in acetone using a synthetic method based on a little adjustment of published methods [29, 30]. Next, a series of benzyl piperazines in acetonitrile and 7‐[2‐(bromo)ethoxy]–5‐hydroxy‐2‐(4‐hydroxyphenyl)–2,3‐dihydrochromen‐4‐one were converted into compounds 3a–3c using basic media by the nucleophilic substitution reaction (Figure 2).

  1H NMR, 13C NMR, FT‐IR, and HR‐MS spectroscopy were used to confirm the structures of each synthesized compound. The spectral data of the synthesized compounds are given in the Supporting Information. For 3b (Figure 3), 1H chemical shift at 11.99 ppm were ascribed for 5‐O. Chemical shifts between 7.27 and 7.24 were associated with 2′ and 6′, while 7.19–7.04 ppm were associated with protons 2⁗, 4⁗, 5⁗, and 6⁗. H‐3′ and H‐5′ were assigned to the doublet at 6.78 ppm, whereas H‐6 and H‐8 were assigned to the at 5.91–5.84 and 6.04–5.97 ppm, respectively. H‐2 was observed at 5.28 ppm as m coupling. 4.08 ppm was observed for H‐1″, 3.48 ppm for benzylic protons, 3.11–2.98 ppm for H‐3, and 2.81 ppm for 2″ and 2.76–2.71 ppm for another H3. Protons in the piperazine ring were measured at 2.64 and 2.54 ppm, with 2.30 ppm providing as a singlet for CH3. 13C‐values are compatible with compound 3b. Especially 62.93 ppm for the benzylic carbon, 65.88 ppm for 1″, 56.95 ppm for 2″, 53.26 ppm, and 52.60 ppm for piperazine carbons, 21.32 for the methyl carbon, and 43.18 for C‐3. Furthermore, FT‐IR revealed the presence of functional groups in the structure, and HRMS performed as anticipated. The combination of spectroscopic data supported the production of the chemicals.


### Evaluation of Antiproliferative Activity and IC50 Values of Naringenin‐Benzyl Piperazine Compounds in Ovarian and Breast Cancer Cell Lines
  In the first step of the study, the cytotoxic and antiproliferative effects of molecules 3b, 3f, and 3g on various breast and ovarian tumors were determined. The results are presented in Figure 4. The study indicated that the examined derivatives exhibited notable antiproliferative and cytotoxic activities across the tested cancer cell lines, showing statistically significant results, particularly in a dose‐dependent manner. MCF7 and SKOV 3 cells were generally more affected by all synthesized naringenin derivatives, followed by SK‐Br cells, OVCAR‐3 cells, which have a multidrug‐resistant tumor phenotype, and MDA‐MB‐231 cell lines, which also have a strong MDR phenotype. According to the study, the IC50 values of the 3b, 3f, and 3g derivatives were found to be between 31.39 and 73.60 µM, 29.11 and 86.91 µM, and 23.53 and 88.63 µM, respectively.


### Quantification of Apoptosis and Necrosis Using Annexin V/PI Staining by Image‐Based Cytometry
  For further research, it is important that targeted compounds exhibiting antiproliferative effects induce apoptotic death in cancer cells. Otherwise, necrotic death can release many harmful molecules within cancer cells, triggering intense inflammation and potentially leading to genometastasis. Therefore, in the second step of the study, we used the Annexin V/PI dual staining method with an image‐based cytometer to investigate whether the anti‐proliferative effect, determined by the MTT assay, was necrotic or apoptotic. The cytotoxic effects of 3b, 3f, and 3g on healthy human skin keratinocyte cells (HaCaT) were also analyzed (Figure 5).

  Our cell cytometry results were generally consistent with our MTT test results. In our study, all three molecules produced a potent killing effect on both breast tumor and ovarian cancer cells. The highest apoptotic effects were detected in the MCF7, SKBR, and SKOV‐3 cell lines, while all molecules had a relatively low effect on the triple‐negative breast tumor MDA‐MD‐231, which has very strong resistance mechanisms, and the multidrug‐resistant OVCAR‐3 cell line. A general analysis of the molecules revealed that 3b and 3g triggered more potent necrotic death in cancer cell lines, while 3f had the highest apoptotic effect on all cell lines except the SKBR cell line.

  The compounds were also analyzed for their effects on healthy human cells, thus investigating their selective anticancer activity. In the study, 3b and 3g induced 20% and 21% mortality in HaCaT cells, respectively. This mortality, as well as the number of necrotic and apoptotic cells, was found to be significantly different compared to the control. Therefore, due to their toxicity to healthy cells, these two molecules were unable to pass this stage. Furthermore, 3f exhibited a very low mortality rate of 12.2%, and this difference was not statistically different compared to the control across all percentages of live, dead, and apoptotic cells. Due to the low‐energy binding rates observed in docking studies, the dose‐dependent strong anticancer effect on cancer cells as determined by our MTT assay, the fact that this effect was primarily apoptosis‐mediated in the Tali cell cytometer, and the fact that it exhibited a selective anticancer effect without cytotoxicity on healthy cells, further studies were conducted with 3f.


### Elucidating the Molecular Mechanism: Effect on Gene and Protein Expression in Pro‐Survival and Apoptotic Signaling
  In this context, the following sections of the study analyzed the molecular effects of 3f on hypoxia, angiogenesis, Jak/stat, PI3K/AKT, and apoptosis pathways, which are the most important mechanisms of cancer in breast and ovarian cancer cell lines. Gene activation in these pathways was determined using quantitative real‐time PCR (qRT‐PCR), and gene expression was calculated. Holistic analyses were applied to these data to determine the effects of the 3f molecule on each cancer cell line, including the most significant genes, vector strengths, major components, and major component groups. Heatmap analysis, group distributions in principal component analysis and eigenvectors affecting this distribution, distribution of control and treatment groups in the coordinate system under the effect of the first three principal components, and volcano plot analysis, performed using gene expression values determined by qRT‐PCR after 3f application at an IC50 dose for 48 h in the human ovarian cancer SKOV‐3 cell line, are given in Figures S24–S26. The most significant components determined by least‐squares discriminant analysis using gene expression values determined by qRT‐PCR are presented in Figure 6. Furthermore, gene expression patterns and independent t‐test results obtained from the application of the 3f molecule to all cancer cell lines are presented in Table 1.

  In the study, 3f produced a statistically significant effect on genes involved in all pathways examined in all cell lines, with a few exceptions. In a hierarchical classification analysis using gene expression values after IC50 dose application in all cancer cells, the control and drug‐treated groups were located in completely separate branches. Examining the effects of 3f on cancer cell lines, the strongest effects were found on VEGF, caspase 3, and SOCS3–7 molecules, particularly in the MCF7 cell line. In the SK‐BR cell line, it specifically induced BAX and SOCS3 genes, significantly reducing VEGF gene expression, and simultaneously decreasing IL6 levels compared to MCF7. In MB‐231, 3f application produced a strong increase in the expression of the anti‐apoptotic BCL‐2 gene, but a significant decrease in VEGF‐R2 and HIF1 genes. Although an increase in VEGF signaling was detected in OVCAR3 and SKOV3 compared to control, a significant increase in mitochondrial apoptosis genes, in particular, indicated a strong anticancer effect. Our protein findings paralleled our gene expression findings and supported our results (Figure 7).

  Our cell migration analysis also revealed that angiogenic capacity was very low and cell migration was absent in cancer cell lines, particularly MCF7 and SKOV, treated with 50 μM 3f, followed by the SKBR cell line. This finding parallels the decrease in VEGF gene and protein expression in these cells. The lowest effect was observed in the MB‐231 and OVCAR‐3 cell lines, which are also highly resistant cancer types (Figure 8).

  When all the data were considered together, it was determined that 3f exerts a particularly effective inhibition on the HIF1‐α/VEGF/MMPs axis in tumor cell lines. HIF‐1 is a transcription factor that activates genes involved in processes such as angiogenesis (formation of new blood vessels), cell growth and survival, invasion, and metastasis. It has also been identified to play an important role in glucose metabolism, immune evasion, and resistance to cancer therapies [31]. HIF‐1 is overexpressed in many types of cancer, including breast cancer, and high levels are associated with poor prognosis [32, 33, 34]. It promotes tumor growth by increasing the production of growth factors and supporting cell survival [35]. HIF‐1 also facilitates metastasis by increasing cell motility and invasiveness and promoting the degradation of the extracellular matrix, which allows cancer cells to spread to surrounding tissues [31, 36]. VEGF is a key regulator of angiogenesis, which is essential for tumor growth and metastasis. HIF‐1 activates VEGF expression, thereby promoting blood vessel formation and providing tumors with the nutrients and oxygen they need for growth. MMPs are enzymes that degrade the extracellular matrix and enable cancer cells to invade surrounding tissues and metastasize to distant sites [31]. Previous studies have shown that HIF‐1 activates the expression of several MMPs, including MMP‐2 and MMP‐9, which are associated with increased invasiveness and metastasis in breast cancer [37]. Therefore, the HIF1‐VEGF axis is one of the most important targets in breast cancer [38, 39, 40]. 3f specifically affected many of the genes in this pathway in almost all cell lines. Furthermore, the gene expression findings were supported by both Western blot protein levels and cell migration analysis. These results indicate that the 3f molecule has therapeutic potential for the hypoxia‐related angiogenesis signaling pathway.

  Another important pathway involved in cell proliferation and angiogenesis in breast cancer is the JAK/STAT pathway. The JAK/STAT signaling pathway plays a crucial role in breast cancer, particularly in hormone receptor‐positive tumors. This pathway is involved in various cellular processes, including cell growth, survival, and immune response [41]. This activation can be triggered by a variety of factors, including hormones such as prolactin and cytokines such as IL‐6 [42]. Studies have shown that JAK/STAT signaling can promote resistance to chemotherapy in breast cancer cells [43, 44]. This resistance may limit the effectiveness of conventional treatments. JAK/STAT signaling is crucial for regulating immune responses, including those involved in recognizing and eliminating tumor cells [42]. However, in breast cancer, this pathway can be hijacked by tumor cells to evade the immune system and promote tumor growth. Therefore, the JAK/STAT pathway is a therapeutic target in cancer treatments. Our qRT‐PCr results indicate that 3f exerts a significant inhibitory effect, particularly on JAK2 and STAT3, while also decreasing their phosphorylation levels. Furthermore, increased SOC expression following 3f administration acts as a potent inhibitor of JAK receptors. Taken together, these findings suggest that JAK2, in particular, may be a target for 3f.

  The PI3K/AKT pathway regulates fundamental cellular processes, including proliferation, survival, metabolism, and angiogenesis [45]. In breast cancer, this pathway is often dysregulated due to mutations or amplifications in genes such as PIK3CA, leading to increased tumor growth, metastasis, and treatment resistance [46, 47]. Inhibiting PI3K/AKT signaling is a promising therapeutic strategy. Several inhibitors targeting PI3K, AKT, or mTOR (a downstream effector) have been developed. However, challenges remain, including intrinsic and acquired resistance and adverse effects [45]. Inducing apoptosis in cancer cells is a key target of many therapies [48]. Natural products and targeted therapies that activate apoptotic pathways are being investigated [49, 50]. Apoptosis markers can also be used to monitor treatment response [51]. Consequently, both PI3K/AKT and apoptosis signaling pathways play critical roles in breast cancer. Targeting these pathways holds promise for improving treatment outcomes, but further research is needed to overcome challenges and develop personalized treatments. The 3f molecule regulated the PI3K/AKT pathway in all cell lines, a difference that was statistically significant. Although it remained relatively low, particularly in the MB‐231 and OVCAR‐3 cell lines, it triggered a highly effective apoptosis process in other cancer cell lines.


### Molecular Docking Studies
  Molecular docking is an in silico modeling method that predicts how a protein receptor and a ligand interact in 3D space. Molecular docking results can provide researchers with preliminary information on which constructs to focus on before moving on to in vitro experiments. Molecular docking saves both time and resources because it does not require experimental effort. However, molecular docking remains a theoretical and speculative prediction because it cannot fully reflect real biological dynamics such as protein flexibility and the effects of the aquatic environment. Molecular docking studies were performed as a theoretical framework to demonstrate possible binding modes and potential interaction sites of 3f with hypoxia, angiogenesis, JAK/STAT, PI3K/AKT, and apoptosis pathway proteins, which are among the most important mechanisms in breast and ovarian cancer cell lines.

  In this context, 3f was docked with the key proteins involved in hypoxia‐associated angiogenesis signaling, IL‐6‐dependent JAK/STAT signaling, PI3K/AKT, and caspase‐dependent mitochondrial apoptosis signaling. The predicted binding energies of 3f with targeted proteins are presented in Table 2, and the 3D view and interactions between 3f and selected proteins are presented in Figures 9, 10, 11. All 2D and 3D views of the interactions between target proteins and 3f are given in Figures S6–S32. These figures indicate that 3f exhibits strong hydrogen bonding and carbon–hydrogen bonding interactions. The oxygen atoms in the 3f structure serve as hydrogen bond acceptors, while the H atoms bonded to the oxygen serve as hydrogen bond donors. Due to the double bonds in the 3f structure, π–anion, π–π, π–sulfur, π–δ, and π–alkyl interactions were formed. Also, weak alkyl interactions were formed. All of these interactions are effective in binding the compounds to that region. Apart from these, unfavorable donor–donor interactions caused an increase in the binding energy.

  Immunoblot test results showed that 3f effectively inhibited the HIF1‐α/VEGF/MMP axis in tumor cell lines. Molecular docking simulations also theoretically supported these in vitro findings by calculating that 3f could exhibit affinity for these proteins with low binding energy. 3f interacted with Hypoxia‐inducible factor‐1α, Vascular endothelial growth factor, and matrix metallopeptidase‐9 proteins with binding energies of 8.1, 8.4, and 9.9 kcal/mol, respectively. 3f interacted with amino acids Arg17, Thr263, and Val264 in the HIF1‐α protein via four hydrogen bonds, while it interacted with other amino acids mostly via alkyl interactions. Unfavorable donor–donor interaction with Val264 reduced the stability of this binding (Figure 9A). It was observed that the 3f molecule interacts with amino acids in the VEGF protein via hydrogen bonding, carbon–hydrogen bonding, and π–donor hydrogen bonding (Figure 9B), while it mainly forms π interactions with amino acids in the MMP9 protein (Figure 9C).

  qRT‐PCR results showed that 3f had a significant inhibitory effect on another important pathway related to cell proliferation and angiogenesis in breast cancers, especially on JAK2 and STAT3 from the JAK/STAT pathway proteins. Molecular docking analyses also provide a theoretical basis for these findings; 3f has the lowest binding energy with JAK2 (−10.3 kcal/mol), while the binding energy with STAT3 is −7.9 kcal/mol. 3f interacted with amino acids in the JAK2 protein through three hydrogen bonds and one C–H bond, as well as pi and alkyl interactions. These interactions can stabilize the binding of 3f to the JAK2 protein (Figure 10A). Although 3f forms four hydrogen bonds and one C–H bond with amino acids in the STAT protein, unfavorable donor–donor interactions with Leu438 caused an increase in the binding energy (Figure 10B).

  Experimentally, the 3f molecule regulated the PI3K/AKT pathway in all cell lines; docking results determined that 3f had the lowest binding energy to AKT serine/threonine kinase and Phosphoinositide 3‐kinase proteins, respectively. This is also consistent with docking predictions showing that 3f can bind to related proteins with low binding energy. 3f interacted with amino acids in both P13K and AKT proteins through multiple hydrogen bonds and C–H bonds. Additionally, pi and alkyl interactions contribute to stable protein binding (Figure 11A,B).

## Conclusion
  In summary, Naringenin‐benzyl piperazine derivatives were synthesized and tested on selected breast tumors (MCF7 (ER‐α+), SKBR, MDA‐MB‐231) and ovarian cancer cells (NIH‐OVCAR‐3 and SKOV‐3). In all cancer types, all three derivatives showed potent antiproliferative and cytotoxic activities; however, their effects were more pronounced on MCF7 and SKOV‐3 cells than on other cancers. The apoptotic effects of the molecules on cells were investigated using MTT. While the highest apoptotic effect was observed in MCF7, SKBR, and SKOV‐3 cell lines, this effect was found to be relatively lower in MDA‐MD‐231 and OVCAR‐3 cell lines. Among the synthesized molecules, 3f was found to have the highest apoptotic effect in all cell lines except the SKBR cell line. Due to the higher mortality rates of 3b and 3g, the study was continued with 3f. The molecular effects of 3f on hypoxia, angiogenesis, JAK/STAT, PI3K/AKT, and apoptosis pathways were analyzed in all cancer lines. Findings at the gene, protein, and cellular levels support the idea that compound 3f may play a regulatory role in the hypoxia‐associated angiogenesis signaling pathway. Combined, the data revealed that 3f exhibited a significant inhibitory effect, particularly on JAK2 and STAT3, while also reducing their phosphorylation levels. Furthermore, 3f was found to regulate the PI3K/AKT pathway in all cell lines and triggered a highly effective apoptosis process in all cancer cell lines except MB‐231 and OVCAR‐3. Furthermore, in this study, the biological findings regarding the interaction of 3f with target proteins (JAK2, STAT3, HIF‐1α, VEGF, MMP9, PI3K, and AKT) were attempted to be explained at the molecular level through molecular docking. The values for compound 3f seem to be within the permissible range when compared to ADMET predictions.

  In summary, the remarkable in vitro efficacy of compound 3f against breast and ovarian cancer cell lines indicates its potential as a novel therapeutic strategy; therefore, the aim is to support and validate these findings with in vivo studies.


## Materials and Methods

### Chemistry

#### General
  All compounds were obtained from Acros, Merck, and Sigma‐Aldrich and utilized without purification. Distilled solvents (dichloromethane and methanol) were used for column chromatography. Column chromatography and TLC analysis were performed on silica gel (Merck, 60 mesh) and silica gel plates (Merck, 60F‐254), respectively; UV light (254 or 366 nm) was used for visualization. Melting points were determined using an electrothermal melting point analyzer. IR spectra were obtained using a PerkinElmer FT‐IR spectrophotometer. NMR data were acquired with a Varian Mercury Plus 400 MHz spectrometer, operating at 400 MHz for 1H NMR and 100 MHz for 13C NMR. The following abbreviations were used in 1H NMR spectra: s = singlet, bs = broad singlet, d = doublet, dd = doublet of doublets, t = triplet, and m = multiplet. High‐resolution mass spectra (HR‐MS) were measured as m/z (rel.%) with the AB SCIEX 4600 Q‐TOF (Ab Sciex, USA).


#### Synthesis of 7‐[2‐(Bromo)ethoxy]–5‐hydroxy‐2‐(4‐hydroxyphenyl)–2,3‐dihydrochromen‐4‐one
  7‐[2‐(Bromo)ethoxy]−5‐hydroxy‐2‐(4‐hydroxyphenyl)−2,3‐dihydrochromen‐4‐one was synthesized by a substitution reaction of 1,2‐dibromoethane with (R,S)‐Naringenin [(±)−5,7‐Dihydroxy‐2‐(4‐hydroxyphenyl)−2,3‐dihydro‐4H‐chromen‐4‐one] that we used previously [17, 18] (Figures S33–S36).


#### Synthesis of 7‐{2‐[4‐(Benzyl)piperazin‐1‐yl]ethoxy}–5‐hydroxy‐2‐(4‐hydroxyphenyl)–2,3‐dihydrochromen‐4‐one Derivatives

The reaction was completed after 16 h, as monitored by TLC, and the solvent was then evaporated. ### Biological Assays

#### Cell Lines and Cell Culture
  The synthesized molecules were applied to human breast tumor ductal carcinoma MCF7 (HTB‐22–ATCC) (ER‐α+), human breast tumor adenocarcinoma SK‐Br (HTB‐30–ATCC) (ER‐α−, ERBB2+), and human triple‐negative breast metastatic adenocarcinoma MDA‐MB‐231 (HTB‐26 ATCC) cells, which are currently the most common breast cancer phenotypes worldwide, as well as to NIH‐OVCAR‐3 (HTB‐161–ATCC) cells, which are ovarian cancer cells with two different phenotypes: ovarian CA, adenocarcinoma, progressive, multidrug‐resistant, and human metastatic ovarian cancer SKOV‐3 (HTB‐77 ‐ ATCC). Human skin keratinocyte cell line HaCaT was used as a healthy cell line. Breast tumor cell lines were cultured in high‐glucose DMEM medium containing 10% FBS, 2 mM l‐glutamine, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Ovarian cancer cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium, which contained the same supplements. Healthy HaCaT cells were cultured in DMEM: F12 medium containing the same supplements. The cells were grown under sterile conditions in incubators at 37°C and 5% CO2. Cells were passaged in 75‐mL flasks at 48 and 72 h, depending on the cell density, which was checked regularly throughout the culture process.


#### Determination of Antiproliferative Effects and IC50 Values of Naringenin‐Benzyl Piperazine Derivatives in Breast Tumors and Ovarian Cancers
  The anti‐proliferative and cytotoxic effects of the compounds were determined using the MTT assay. Breast tumor and ovarian cancer cell lines were inoculated into 96‐well plates at 2000–5000 cells/well, based on their proliferative capacity and logarithmic proliferation rate, using 180 µl of the appropriate growth medium for each cell type as described in the previous section. After inoculation, cells were allowed to adhere to the bottom of the wells overnight (approximately 12 h) to determine whether they had normal morphology. Then, 20 µL of each compound was applied at seven different concentrations, ranging from 5 to 200 µM, prepared by serial dilution. The control group received the same amount of vehicle in which the drug was dissolved. Following incubation, the cells were incubated at 37°C and 5% CO2 for 48 h. At the end of these periods, 20 µL of MTT (3‐(4,5‐Dimethylthiazol‐2‐yl)–2,5‐Diphenyltetrazolium Bromide) solution (at a concentration of 5 mg/mL) was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for 4 h. The formed formazan crystals were dissolved with ultrapure dimethyl sulfoxide (200 µL/well) and read at 492 nm using a microplate reader (Bionoy, ENZO, USA). In the analysis of the results, cell viability in the control group was assumed to be 100%, and drug activities were calculated as the relative percentage of cell viability. IC50 values were determined using probit analysis with SPSS 20 software.


#### Cytotoxicity Test by Cell Cytometry
  The cytotoxic effects of compounds on cancer and healthy cell lines (HaCaT) were calculated using a Tali Cytometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). For this analysis, breast tumor, ovarian cancer, and healthy cells were seeded in 25 cm2 sterile cell culture flasks, and after 18 h of incubation, 50 µM concentrations of molecules and vehicle (Control: final concentration 0.1% molecular grade DMSO) were applied. 48 h after application, all cells were trypsinized, and the cell viability, necrotic (dead), and apoptotic cells were determined using the Tali Apoptosis Kit according to kit protocols.


#### Wound‐Healing Assay
  The effect of molecules on cell migration, an indicator of invasion in breast tumors and ovarian cancer cells, was investigated using a wound healing assay. Tumor cells were seeded in 25 cm2 cell culture flasks and allowed to incubate until 90% confluency. Wounds were created in these cells using a 200‐µL yellow pipette tip at three different sites. The flasks were rinsed with PBS to eliminate dead cells, and then the vehicle and IC50 doses of the molecules were pipetted into the flasks using the respective serum‐free medium used for each cell. Cell migration and anti‐angiogenic activity were determined using the wound closure rate according to the protocol established by Zaim et al. [17].


#### Total RNA Isolation, cDNA Synthesis by Reverse Transcription, and qRT‐PCR
  Tumor cells were seeded in 25‐mL flasks as indicated in the previous section to test the effects of the 3f and control groups on mRNA expression. Drug applications were then performed. After a 48‐h incubation, the cells were collected from each well, and eight replicates were run for the control and experimental groups. Total RNA was obtained from tumor cells using a PureLink RNA purification kit in conjunction with TRIzol. The amount and purity of the isolated RNA were measured using an Optizen NanoQ spectrometer (Mecasys, South Korea) and equalized with distilled water. A High Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit was used to generate cDNA. The mRNA expression levels were determined by qRT‐PCR on a QuantStudio 5 real‐time PCR apparatus (Life Technologies, USA) with Power Cyber Green Gene Expression Master Mix. The primers and qRT‐PCR conditions were listed in Table 3 Gene expression was determined as a relative fold change using the 2−ΔΔCt  technique, with GAPDH and β‐actin mRNA levels serving as internal controls [17].


### Immunoblot Assay
  Protein expression was employed to evaluate the outcome of gene expression in cell cultures and support the qRT‐PCR results. Following the application of 3f in breast tumors and ovarian cancers, the medium was removed from the flasks, and protein was isolated from the cells according to the RIPA Lysis Buffer System (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) kit protocol. Protein quantities were equalized using a Qubit Fluorometer (Invitrogen) and, following denaturation, loaded onto NuPAGE gels. In this study, ready‐made kits from Thermo Scientific were employed, and blotting and transfer to membranes were carried out using the iBlot (Life Technologies) system with prepared membranes and the kit. After blotting, JAK2, phospho‐JAK2, Stat3, phospho‐Stat3, HIF1‐alpha, VEGF, MMP9, BCL‐2, BAX, cleaved caspase 3 proteins, and β‐actin (as a control) were treated with their specific primary antibodies. Then the antibodies were labeled with appropriate secondaries and visualized with ChemiDoc chemiluminescence imaging, and protein amounts were given with actin correction compared to the control using the software available in the device [52].


### Statistical Analysis
  In the project, the difference between the means of relative gene expressions, live cell ratios determined by the MTT test, and secondary apoptosis analysis results in the control and treatment groups was determined using the independent t‐test, Mann–Whitney U‐test, one‐way ANOVA, TUKEY HSD test, and SPSS 20 software in multiple comparisons outside of the two groups, depending on whether the data were numerical or not and whether the variances were homogeneous. MetaboAnalyst software was also used to create a heat map, a volcano plot, principal component analysis (PCA), and a most significant component analysis using the relevant R statistical packages. The significance criterion for the analyses was p ≤ 0.05.


### Molecular Docking Studies
  Molecular docking, which studies ligand‐protein interactions at the atomic level, allows us to better understand biological processes and discover how a ligand interacts with target proteins at their binding sites. Molecular docking was performed to determine the affinity of the 3f to proteins present in the JAK/STAT and hypoxia pathways. The 3D crystal structures (pdb format) of the potential target proteins were downloaded from the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org). After removing water and residues from the structure, adding Kollman charges and polar hydrogens, they were prepared for docking. The 3f were prepared in pdb format by minimizing their energy using molecular mechanics (MM2) in the Chem 3D program. The protein and ligand were converted to pdbqt format, and docking was performed using AutoDock Tools 1.5.6 [53]. Finally, the lowest‐binding energy pose among the nine docking positions was chosen and interpreted. The data were visualized in 2D and 3D format using PyMOL [54] and BIOVIA Studio [55]; the amino acids in the binding site were identified, and the interactions were interpreted.


### ADMET Analyses
  ADMET analyses allow us to evaluate drug potential by theoretically determining the pharmacokinetic properties, drug‐like structures, physicochemical properties, and medicinal chemical interactions of molecules. For this purpose, molecular structures were converted to SMILES format using ChemDraw, and their ADMET properties were determined using the ADMETlab3 program (https://admetlab3.scbdd.com/) [56].
