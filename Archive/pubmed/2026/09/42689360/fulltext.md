# Iron(II) and Copper(II) Ions Modulating Binding Interaction of Sugar‐Based Silatrane With DNA: Analytical and Theoretical Validation


## ABSTRACT
  Research on molecular systems capable of interacting with DNA through binding, modification, or cleavage remains important in medicinal chemistry. In this study, we report the design and synthesis of a new D‐glucamine‐based Schiff base containing a triazolyl silatrane probe (6) and its Fe(II) and Cu(II) complexes, followed by investigation of their interactions with calf thymus DNA (CT‐DNA). The synthesized ligand and its metal complexes were characterized by elemental analysis, FT‐IR, UV–vis spectroscopy, ESI‐MS, and NMR spectroscopy (1H and 13C for the free ligand). DNA‐binding properties were evaluated by UV–vis absorption spectroscopy, ethidium bromide (EtBr) displacement fluorescence assays, and molecular docking studies. The combined experimental and computational results indicate that ligand 6 interacts predominantly with CT‐DNA through groove binding, whereas complexation with Fe(II) or Cu(II) enhances DNA‐binding affinity and modulates the interaction toward a mixed groove‐binding/intercalative mode. The higher binding constants, enhanced fluorescence quenching, and favorable docking interactions observed for the metal complexes support this metal‐induced modulation of DNA‐binding behavior. Overall, the findings demonstrate that metal complexation can tune both the strength and mode of DNA binding, highlighting a potential strategy for designing silatrane‐based metal complexes with DNA‐targeting applications.


## Introduction
  The universe has always been full of some wonder secrets that make us ponder, like what makes each species unique and differently shaped. The exact reply to this statement is DNA, the head biomolecule, which encodes genetic information and gives instructions for protein synthesis [1]. Including these practices, DNA finds numerous applications in material science as nanomachines, biosensors, and bone‐guiding scaffolds [2, 3, 4]. The modes of binding that characterize the interactions of new molecules with DNA are covalent, electrostatic, groove, and intercalative binding [5]. Because of these binding interactions, the structural changes induced in double‐stranded DNA by new molecules result in a disruption of the transcription and replication events, ultimately leading to apoptosis and cell death [6]. Hence, DNA is a key molecular target for plethora of antibiotics and antitumor drugs in the cell [4]. The knowledge of the interaction mode of newly synthesized molecules with DNA is not only crucial for their efficient sensing in pathological samples but also aids in designing new as well as potent DNA‐targeted drugs [5, 6, 7]. Understanding the interaction of newly synthesized metal complexes with DNA is an important first step toward the rational development of biologically active coordination compounds and provides valuable insight for future medicinal chemistry investigations. Recent studies have demonstrated that transition‐metal complexes can effectively modulate DNA‐binding affinity and binding mode through changes in coordination geometry, electronic structure, and ligand environment, making them promising candidates for the development of DNA‐targeted therapeutic agents [8]. Numerous transition‐metal complexes have been reported to interact with DNA through groove binding, intercalation, or mixed binding modes, depending on the nature of the coordinated ligand and metal ion [9]. Moreover, a designed molecule that shows non‐covalent interactions with DNA, particularly groove binding, electrostatic binding, and partial intercalative binding, which is common in the literature, however, a molecular system having a change in the mode of these bindings on account of metal ion complexation is very uncommon and rare [10, 11]. Such type of investigations play an indispensable role in the development of novel chemotherapeutic drugs [12, 13, 14].

  In pharmaceutical industries, drug‐designing by a combination of selective biological active organic moieties is a great inspiration, as it can offer enhanced pharmacokinetic properties [15]. D‐glucamine has attracted considerable interest in the scientific community as it is apt to biodegrade, safer, and an environment‐friendly hydrophilic group. It is an amino sugar and is economically synthesized without producing any harmful waste [16]. The biological background of D‐glucamine is similar to that of glucose, and it can be used as a substrate for glycolysis in cells and can provide energy for cellular processes. It shows anti‐inflammatory properties, owing to its ability to inhibit prostaglandin synthesis [17, 18]. Furthermore, phenyl pyrimidine derivatives possessing D‐glucamine are used to explore novel anticancer properties of the LCs [18]. Besides, silatranes are a genre of tricyclic cage compounds that enclose a higher‐coordinated silicon atom having a transannular dative bond (Si←N) to nitrogen. Recent studies have further demonstrated that functionalized silatrane derivatives possess attractive structural features and biological properties, making them useful scaffolds for the design of metal‐responsive functional molecules and supramolecular receptors [19, 20]. Moreover, numerous derivatives of silatrane have been synthesized which are known for their remarkable pharmacological activities, which include antioxidant, antiviral, anti‐inflammatory, anticancer, anticonvulsant, antitumor, and treatment of alopecia [21]. Furthermore, triazoles are also one of the most imperative moieties, which have been researched, and still its scope is inexorable in pharmacokinetic society [22]. Triazole compounds' distinctive characteristics, such as the formation of hydrogen bonds, dipole–dipole interactions, as well as π‐stacking interactions, have made them more momentous in the arena of medicinal chemistry because of their enhanced solubility, which allows them to bind with the biological target with robust affinity. Triazoles, particularly the 1,2,3‐triazole moiety, are the basis for several currently used medications, including the anti‐HIV agent TSAO, the antibacterial agent Tazobactam, the antibiotic Cefatrizine, the anticancer agent CIA, and the anti‐TB agent I‐A09 [23, 24, 25]. Schiff bases serve an instrumental role in medicinal chemistry due to its manifold activities, including antioxidant, anticancer, antifungal, antiviral, antibacterial, anti‐HIV, anti‐inflammatory, and herbicidal properties [26, 27, 28]. Hence, the conjugation of these flexible moieties will not only provide structural diversification but can also produce new molecules with wonderful applications.

  Metal complexes are reckoned to be less poisonous in contrary to free ligands and serve as effective chemotherapeutic agents to heal multifarious diseases owing to their facilitation in altering ligand oxidation states, coordination number, and exchange reaction rates in order to escalate their efficiency with the minimal side effects [29, 30]. The chelation of, specifically Cu(II), Fe(II), and Fe(III) harmful ions might be regarded as an extra mechanism of silatranes' antioxidant activity since traces of these soluble transition metal ions, can trigger the metal‐catalyzed free radical production process. Conventionally, the tools used for the identification of nucleic acids are staining, radio labeling, probing, tagging, and hybridization [31, 32]. Recent studies have demonstrated that the DNA‐binding behavior of metal complexes is strongly influenced by ligand architecture, donor atoms, coordination geometry, and the nature of the coordinated metal ion. In particular, sugar‐derived ligands improve aqueous solubility and provide multiple hydrogen‐bonding sites, while triazole and silatrane moieties contribute additional π‐conjugation [19] and donor centers capable of enhancing non‐covalent interactions with DNA. Furthermore, metal coordination can significantly modify the electronic properties [8] and molecular geometry of the ligand, thereby altering its binding affinity and interaction mode with DNA. These structure–activity relationships [9] have motivated the design of multifunctional ligands capable of exhibiting tunable DNA‐binding behavior through metal complexation [30, 33, 34]. In this regard, current research efforts have been focused on the development of new silatrane‐based systems with improved biological and sensing properties. In our previous studies, organosilatrane‐based receptors were successfully developed for molecular recognition and sensing applications, demonstrating the versatility of the silatrane scaffold as a functional platform [20]. However, the influence of metal‐ion complexation on the DNA‐binding behavior of D‐glucamine‐based triazolyl silatrane systems has not yet been explored.

  Therefore, in the present work, we designed and synthesized a novel D‐glucamine‐based Schiff base containing a triazolyl silatrane moiety and investigated how Fe(II) and Cu(II) complexation modulates its interaction with CT‐DNA. The DNA‐binding behavior of the free ligand and its metal complexes was systematically evaluated using spectroscopic techniques and supported by molecular docking studies, with the aim of providing new insights into metal‐assisted modulation of DNA recognition and expanding the biological potential of silatrane‐based molecular systems.


## Results and Discussion

### Synthesis and Characterization of Compounds (2–6)
  As indicated in Scheme 1, the reaction commences with the synthesis of terminal alkyne (2) by O‐propargylation of 6‐hydroxynicotinaldehyde (1) in the presence of propargyl bromide and anhydrous potassium carbonate using DMF (dimethylformamide) solvent by employing Williamson ether synthesis [35]. The O‐propargylated aldehyde (2) was then allowed to undergo an acid‐catalyzed condensation reaction with D‐glucamine (3) to give an important class of compounds, D‐glucamine‐based alkyne‐terminated Schiff base (4). Alkyne terminated Schiff base was then exposed to a robust ligation with 3‐azidopropyltriethoxy‐silane (3‐AzPTES) in THF/Et3N solvent system and catalytic amount of [CuBr(PPh3)3] [Copper (I) catalyzed azide‐alkyne cycloaddition (CuAAC)] [36] resulting into triazole allied organo‐triethoxysilane (5) and in this, azide group of 3‐AzPTES comes out to be a fragment of the triazole core. The final step involved the transesterification of organo‐triethoxysilane (5) with triethanolamine (TEA) in the presence of a catalytic amount of KOH, affording the corresponding D‐glucamine‐based triazolyl silatrane (6). The detailed synthetic procedures for all compounds are provided in the Experimental Section (cf. Supporting Information).

  Although silatrane formation can be achieved without added base in some cases, the catalytic amount of KOH employed in the present study accelerated the transesterification process under the optimized reaction conditions. The absence of any decomposition products and the spectroscopic characterization of compound 6 confirm that the silatrane framework remained intact throughout the reaction.

  The structural embodiment of all the synthesized compounds (4–6) has been fully specified by FT‐IR, 1H NMR, 13C NMR, and mass spectra, cf. Figures S1‐S11. In FT‐IR spectra, all the compounds display absorption bands in the region 3400–3550 cm−1 corresponding to the stretching of ─OH group. The appearance of bands at 2194 cm−1(νC≡C), 3256 cm−1 (νC≡CH), and 1080 cm−1(νC─O─C) confirm O‐propargylation of compounds (1) to form compound (2), that is, terminal alkyne and disappearance of band corresponding to ─CHO at 1660 cm−1 and emergence of new bands at 1617 cm−1(νC═N) confirm the formation of Schiff base containing terminal alkyne (4). Similarly, the vanishing of bands at 2105 and 3257 cm−1 correlating to C≡C group and the occurrence of new bands at 1503 cm−1(νN═N) and 1007 cm−1(νSi─O) authenticate the cyclic formation of alkynyl moiety into triazole unit in compounds (5). The atrane formation is ratified by the emergence of bands at 544 cm−1 that match with the asymmetric vibrations of the Si←N bond (6).

  In 1H‐NMR, Schiff base (HC─N) proton is identified consistently in the prepared compound (4) and emerges around 8.46–8.54 ppm which completely supports the desired synthesis. The proton of the alkyne‐terminated Schiff bases (4) emanates from 2.81–2.83 ppm to 6.81–6.83 ppm upon cyclization with 3‐AzPTES upon formation of 1,2,3‐triazole‐linked organotriethoxysilane (5). The ethoxy part of the silanes exhibits a triplet around 1.46–1.49 ppm and a quartet in the range 3.50–3.67 ppm. The two triplets emerge in the range 2.67–2.78 ppm and 3.54–3.64 ppm in 1H‐NMR spectrum of organosilatrane (6). In the 13C‐NMR spectrum of the compounds (2–6), carbon signals pertaining to aromatic carbon shows up around 113–155 ppm, and Schiff base linked carbon were spotted in the range 156–158 ppm. The acetylinic carbons ─C≡CH (4) at 70.18 ppm and 78.76 ppm shift to 114.66–128.76 ppm and 131.03–131.88 ppm on switching into organotriethoxysilane (5). The ethoxy group‐defining carbons, OCH2CH3 and OCH2, are found at 17.4–18.9 ppm and 57.3–59.0 ppm, respectively. These carbons then move toward 52.7–53.7 ppm and 55.8–57.8 ppm, which are attributed to OCH2 and NCH2 carbons of the final organosilatrane, respectively (6).


### Synthesis and Characterization of Complex 6‐Fe(II)/Cu(II) Cl2
  The iron and copper complexes were prepared by dissolving compound 6 with solid anhydrous FeCl2/CuCl2 salt in 25 mL solvent in equimolar ratio and allowed to stir at room temperature for 6 h cf. Scheme 2. The color of solid compound 6 and its 6‐Fe/Cu(II) complexes [Fe(L)2Cl2] and [Cu(L)2Cl2], where L = compound 6]; was yellow solid for compound 6, dark brown solid for 6‐Fe(II) complex, and dark green solid for 6‐Cu(II) complex.

  ESI‐MS analysis further confirmed the formation of the metal complexes. The spectrum of the Fe(II) complex exhibited a molecular ion peak at m/z 1292.12, while the Cu(II) complex showed a molecular ion peak at m/z 1299.82, which are in good agreement with the calculated molecular masses of the proposed [6‐Fe(II)Cl2] and [6‐Cu(II)Cl2] complexes, respectively. These results provide additional support for the proposed molecular compositions of the synthesized complexes (Figures S12 and S13).

  After that, the complexes were authenticated through UV−visible as well as IR spectroscopy studies. The UV–vis spectrum of compound 6 and its Fe(II)/Cu(II) complexes were measured in DMSO at room temperature (Figure S14).

  In Figure 1a, it's clear that the ligand 6 displays three UV–visible absorption bands in the UV–vis region, the first band appeared at ∼275 nm (band I) corresponding to π–π* transitions, which may be due to the aromatic portion in the molecule. The second and third absorption band at ∼325 nm (band II) and ∼360 nm (band III), respectively, pertains to n–π* transition which may arise because of azomethine group (C═N) or conjugation present in the molecule. Upon complexation with Fe(II), the second and third band showed some blue shift about ∼2–7 nm but the third band unveiled a perceptible red shift to 425 nm along with the solution color change from yellow to red–brown (Figure 1b). This feature is the result of the establishment of a charge‐transfer bands (CT bands) at 425 nm in spectral window. Furthermore, in the case of Cu(II) complex band III shows a distinguishable bathochromic shift to 390 nm but bands I and II are relocated to a small extent, that is, 280 and 330 nm, respectively, along with color of a solution change from yellow to light green (Figure 1b). The noticeable shifts in the absorption band positions validate the coordination of probe 6 to Fe(II)/Cu(II) ions. The bands that appear in the visible region, that is, 390–425 nm in both cases were described as a ligand‐to‐metal charge transfer (LMCT) from a non‐bonding electron of heteroatom (O, N) in ligand to the eg unoccupied d‐orbital of Fe(II)/Cu(II) [37, 38]. The absorption maxima (λ max) for the analytes 6 and its complexes of Fe(II)/Cu(II) in DMSO solvent were converted into their corresponding transition energies, ET 1 (π–π*), ET 2 (n–π*) values in kcal mol−1, using the relationship E T = 28,590/λ nm, [39, 40] with ± 0.1 kcal mol−1 precision (1 kcal mol−1 = 4.184 kJ mol−1), cf. Table 1. The energies E T 1 and E T 2 correspond to (π–π*) and (n–π*) transitions, respectively.

  Further, with the aim to estimate the binding stoichiometry of both complexes, Job's method of continual variation was employed (Figure 1c,d). The maximum absorbance values at 422 and 392 nm were obtained at ∼0.31 and ∼0.33 mol fraction of Fe(II) and Cu(II) ions, respectively, which alludes 2:1 stoichiometric binding ratio betwixt ligand 6 and metal ions, Fe(II)/Cu(II) in the complexes. In addition to this an akin binding was also verified by IR studies (Figures 2 and S15), where the peak shifts in ν(C═N)py [6: 1598.22 cm−1 and 6−Fe(II): 1589 cm−1; 6−Cu(II): 1587 cm−1] and ν(C─O─C) [6: 1074 cm−1 and 6−Fe(II): 1060 cm−1; 6−Cu(II): 1062 cm−1] were observed. The shift of these stretching bands on complexation toward lower wave number indicates coordination occurs through the N (pyridine) and O (C─O─C) of the ligand 6 to the metal (II) centre. In‐addition, new band seen in the prepared complexes around 420–462 cm−1 and 580–582 cm−1 indicated the formation of M─N and M─O bonds, respectively. The Fe(II) and Cu(II) complexes were not characterized by 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopy because their paramagnetic nature results in significant line broadening and paramagnetic chemical shifts, making conventional NMR spectra difficult to obtain and interpret. Therefore, structural characterization was based on FT‐IR, UV–vis spectroscopy, elemental analysis, ESI‐MS, Job's plot, and DFT calculations.


### Theoretical Structural Optimization
  In theoretical investigations, the ligand 6 and its two possible 2:1 complexes of compound 6 with Fe(II)/Cu(II) ions were modeled and geometry‐optimized using the density functional theory (DFT) calculations (Figure 3a–d). Both complexes are assumed to be hexa‐coordinated. The ligand 6 behaves as a bidentate N,O‐donor ligand, coordinating through one pyridine nitrogen atom and one ether oxygen atom. Two ligand molecules together with two chloride ions complete the six‐coordinate environment around the metal center. The optimization of these two models exhibits that the Fe(II) complex has a trigonal prismatic geometry (D3h, symmetry; d6 configuration) in which two pyridine nitrogen (N18, N58), two oxygen (O40, O80) of ligand 6 and two chlorine atoms (Cl82, Cl83) are arranged at the vertices of triangular prism with Fe(II) at the center (Figure 3b). Whereas, the 6b‐Cu(II) complex has Jahn–Teller distortion in the octahedral geometries in which two pyridine nitrogen (N18, N58), two oxygen (O40, O80) of C─O─C in ligand 6 are occupied at the equatorial position, and two axial chlorine atoms (Cl82, Cl83) of metal salt on axial sites. The two axial bonds get elongated. This distortion (elongation) appears owing to high‐spin configuration of d9 (Figure 3c). In Fe (II) complex, the Fe─O bond distance lies in the range 1.614–2.431Å, and Fe─N bond distance is 1.732 Å while in Cu(II) complex, the Cu─O bond distance appears to be 1.943 Å, and Cu─N bond distance exhibits in the range 1.787–2.277 Å. The optimized metal–ligand bond lengths are comparable to those reported for related Fe(II) and Cu(II) coordination complexes obtained from DFT calculations and crystallographic studies [41].

  In both complexes M─O bond length is slightly larger than M─N bond due to +R effect of O atom (C─O─C), that is; participated in conjugation.

  An analysis of the contour plots of significant frontier orbitals displays that, in the case of ligand 6 in the ground state, the electron density exists mostly on HOMO and LUMO orbitals at the aryl ring, oxygen atom of C─O─C group, and nitrogen atoms of the imine group (C═N) with HOMO and LUMO energy gap of 3.11 eV. The pyridine moiety and oxygen of the C─O─C group contribute mostly to the HOMO orbitals in the case of 6‐Fe(II), whereas the phenyl ring, metal center, and pyridine nitrogen contribute to the LUMO orbitals, which have an energy gap of 1.11 eV. In the case of 6‐Cu(II), HOMO orbitals arise from the contribution of the pyridine moiety, imine moiety and whereas, LUMO orbitals emerge from pyridine nitrogen and metal center with a gap of energy between HOMO and LUMO of 2.12 eV (Figure 3d). The calculated HOMO–LUMO gap of the Fe(II) complex is comparable to values reported for related Fe(II) coordination complexes investigated using DFT methods, indicating that the present results are consistent with previous theoretical studies (40). From the energy level diagram, it can be perceived that the addition of the Fe(II)/Cu(II) ions leads to the stabilization of the HOMO and LUMO of receptor 6. From the computed results, HOMO and LUMO energy levels of 6 decrease after the incorporation of these ions relative to that of the free ligand 6. Furthermore, when comparing Fe(II)/Cu(II) complexes to the parent receptor 6, the energy gap between HOMO and LUMO is reduced by 1.99 and 1.68 eV, respectively. This could be the cause of the absorption spectrum's redshift anomaly.


### DNA Binding Studies

#### Metal Ion‐Induced Modulation of DNA Binding
  It has been well accepted that the prime goal of antitumor drugs is DNA [42]. The interaction of synthesized molecules and their metal ion complexes with DNA generates electronic perturbations in these complexes. Spectroscopic investigations can identify these perturbations and offer valuable insights into the occurrence of these complexes' interaction with DNA. Moreover, UV–vis along with fluorescence spectroscopy estimated the interaction of 6 and its metal complexes 6‐M(II)Cl2 (M = Fe, Cu) with CT‐DNA.


#### UV−vis Analysis
  UV–vis absorption spectroscopy is widely used to detect the interaction between designed molecules or metal ion complexes and DNA. The changes in the position of absorption peak upon gradual inclusion of DNA to a solution of synthesized ligand or its metal complexes give crucial information about the mode of interaction between molecule and DNA. The observed variations in the absorbance magnitude or movement in peak position may be associated with the strength of the interaction. In general, it is anticipated that when a molecule inserts itself between DNA base pairs, it will induce a hypochromic change with a notable redshift [43]. Contrarily, the hyperchromic effect is consistent with minor or major groove binding and unwinding of the DNA double helix [44]. Molecules containing aromatic ring and electron donor atoms (N, O) can interact with DNA double helix. Moreover, metal ions can modulate the binding interaction [44].

  UV–vis absorption titration studies have been carried out (cf. Figures S16–S18) in order to investigate the change in binding mode and binding strength (K b), for interaction of the synthesized probe 6 with CT‐DNA, by comparing with bare ligand and after complex formation with metal ions Fe(II)/Cu(II) (cf. Figures S19–S21).

  The UV–vis absorption spectra of compound 6 in the absence as well as presence of CT‐DNA are displayed in Figure 4a. In the absence of CT DNA, compound 6 exhibits three absorption bands in the range 277, 325, and 362 nm, and with incremental addition of CT‐DNA resulted hyperchromic effect on all three absorption bands: band I (277 nm), band II (325 nm), and band III (362 nm), which indicates groove binding interactions (Figure 4a). Purine N(3) and pyrimidine O(2) are the main H‐bond acceptor groups found at the floor of the groove walls of the DNA minor groove. On the other hand, in the case of complex 6‐Fe(II) band 1(275 nm) exhibits a hyperchromic effect but band II (322 nm) and band III (422 nm) show a hypochromic effect, however all three bands exhibit slight red shift ∼3 nm (λ = 278 nm), ∼3 nm (λ = 325 nm), and ∼4 nm (λ = 426 nm), respectively (Figure 4b). Similarly, in the case of complex 6‐Cu(II), upon the subsequent addition of CT‐DNA, band I (280 nm), band II (326 nm), and band III (388 nm) exhibited a hypochromic effect with a slight red shift ∼3 nm (λ = 283 nm), ∼4 nm (λ = 330 nm), and ∼10 nm (λ = 398 nm), respectively (Figure 4c). Therefore, the aforementioned observations basically suggest that the Fe(II) and Cu(II) complexes interact with CT‐DNA through a mixed groove‐binding/intercalative mode (or groove binding with partial intercalative character). It is noteworthy that the Fe(II) complex exhibits different spectral responses for the three absorption bands. While band I displays a hyperchromic effect, bands II and III show hypochromism accompanied by slight bathochromic shifts. Such behavior can be attributed to the different nature of the electronic transitions involved. The ligand‐centered transition is more sensitive to local environmental and conformational changes upon DNA binding, whereas the metal‐influenced transitions are more strongly affected by π–π stacking interactions with DNA base pairs. Therefore, the simultaneous occurrence of hyperchromic and hypochromic effects reflects the involvement of multiple electronic transitions and is consistent with a mixed groove‐binding/intercalative interaction rather than a single binding mode.

  The K b value was calculated from the ratio of the slope to the intercept in the plot of [DNA]/(ε a−ε f) versus [DNA] shown in the insets of Figure 4a–c and Figures S17–S19. The K b values were found to 1.9 × 105, be 1.65 × 106 and 0.41 × 106 for 6 and its 6‐Fe(II)/Cu(II) complexes, respectively, specifying strong binding of the 6‐Fe(II)/Cu(II) complexes to DNA, as compared to parent ligand  6  cf Table 2.


#### Competitive Binding With EtBr (EB, 3,8‐Diamino‐5‐ethyl‐6‐phenylphenanthridinium bromide)
  A competitive binding test using the traditional DNA intercalator EtBr is used to mark the DNA molecule and exploit its interaction with ligand molecules. Because the solvent molecule quenches the fluorescence of the free EtBr, the fluorescence intensity of EtBr in a buffer solution is low. However, the emission intensity of EtBr increases substantially in the presence of DNA after a 15‐min incubation period owing to its intercalation within the DNA base pairs [45]. The binding of ligand 6 and its complexes 6‐Fe(II)/Cu(II) to DNA via a akin binding method as that of EtBr leads to reduction in the fluorescence intensity of the DNA‐EtBr complex due to competition for the same binding site. According to the literature, a classical intercalator can quench the fluorescence intensity to about more than 50% of the initial [46, 47].

  It is clear from Figure 5a–d that the incremental addition of 6 to the solution of the DNA–EtBr complex results in an insignificant quenching (∼27%) of the fluorescence intensity, effectively ruling out classical intercalative binding and indirectly supporting the groove‐binding interaction of ligand 6 with DNA. In contrast, the 6‐Fe(II) and 6‐Cu(II) complexes caused significant quenching (∼46.8% and 49.2%, respectively) of the fluorescence intensity, suggesting a mixed groove‐binding/intercalative interaction between the complexes and CT‐DNA. Although the observed quenching efficiencies are slightly below those generally reported for classical intercalators, the combined evidence from UV–vis absorption titration (hypochromism with slight bathochromic shifts), EtBr displacement, and molecular docking indicates that metal coordination enhances the DNA‐binding affinity and introduces a partial intercalative component in addition to groove binding. Such mixed binding behavior has also been reported for structurally related transition‐metal complexes in the literature [46, 47]. The findings are consistent with the absorption investigations. Furthermore, to provide a more detailed explanation of the fluorescence quenching phenomenon, the Stern–Volmer equation (Equation 1) was employed, and the corresponding Stern–Volmer plots for the DNA‐bound EtBr system with 6 and its Fe(II)/Cu(II) complexes are shown in the insets of Figure 5(a–d) and Figures S22–S24. The Ksv value for the 6–DNA system was found to be 4.30 × 103 M−1, which is considerably lower than those generally reported for classical DNA intercalators [48, 49], supporting a predominantly groove‐binding interaction of the free ligand. In contrast, the higher Ksv values obtained for the Fe(II) and Cu(II) complexes (Ksv1.86 × 105 M−1and 2.70×106 M−1, respectively), indicate enhanced interaction with CT‐DNA following metal coordination. It should be noted that the Stern–Volmer constant alone cannot unequivocally establish the quenching mechanism or DNA‐binding mode. Therefore, the Ksv values were interpreted together with the UV–vis absorption titration and molecular docking results, which collectively support a mixed groove‐binding/intercalative interaction. These observations are in good agreement with the UV–vis absorption titration and molecular docking results, and collectively suggest that the Fe(II) and Cu(II) complexes interact with CT‐DNA through a mixed groove‐binding/intercalative interaction [50].


#### Theoretical Validation of DNA Binding Interaction
  Our experimental investigation specifies that compound 6 binds to DNA molecules via two different possible modes of interaction, that is, groove binding and electrostatic but metal complexes 6‐Fe(II) Cl2  and 6‐Cu(II) Cl2  show mixed groove‐binding/intercalative binding interaction. Hence, theoretical analysis has been carried out, to verify this change in the mode of interactions. In the theoretical approach, the molecular docking studies are highly recommended to determine the binding abilities of ligands and their metal (II) complexes with DNA double helix. It is an extremely powerful computational technique to predict the appropriate orientation of a designed molecule in the course of interaction to form a stable drug‐DNA complex and also, to recognize the principal binding site on DNA [51]. But the blind molecular docking is unable to demonstrate the intercalative mode of binding, even when powerful DNA intercalators are present, without preformed intercalating sites in the nucleic acid [52, 53]. Such type of problems arise because docking studies are performed with fully flexible ligands, the host macromolecules are treated as rigid. Due to the need for the displacement of nucleic acid bases to accommodate the ligand, molecular docking studies using a rigid DNA structure are unable to produce intercalative binding, even with commonly used and strong intercalators. Therefore, the prevailing method for examining the intercalation mode of interaction through docking studies is to utilize a DNA molecule that already possesses an established intercalating site [53]. To fulfill this objective, we have selected a DNA molecule from the PDB bank with the PDB ID; 1I7V, which has an intercalating ligand. Before doing the docking studies, the intercalating ligand was removed from the PDB file. In molecular docking, the optimized geometry of ligand 6 and metal complexes 6‐Fe(II)/Cu(II) were docked into DNA fragments (Figure 6). The DNA structure and molecule were regarded hard and flexible, respectively, in order to attain the optimal overall posture and binding energy.

  The most stable conformation of DNA interaction of designed ligand 6 is depicted in Figure 6, and results reveal that the ligand 6 interacted with DNA through groove binding and attached in the minor groove region. In free compound 6, the binding is stabilized by hydrogen‐bonding interactions. Five hydrogen‐bonding interactions were noticed between DT19, DA6, DA17, and DA18 with the OH group of the D‐glucamine part and N of triazole moiety. Two electrostatic interactions were spotted between DG16 and DC15 with the nitrogen of triazole and silatrane fragment with average distance of 5.2 Ǻ. The binding energy of 6 was found to be −7.4 kcal/mol.

  In contrast of free ligand 6, the Fe(II) and Cu(II) complexes can be accommodated within a pre‐existing intercalation site of DNA (Figure 7a,b) with a free energy of −9.4 kcal/mol and −9.8 kcal/mol, respectively. Which is consistent with a mixed groove‐binding/intercalative interaction observed experimentally. These complexes fix between the two strands and intercalate between DNA base pairs through a π–π interaction.

  A detailed examination of the docked structures suggests that the silatrane, D‐glucamine, and triazole moieties of both metal complexes are accommodated within the preexisting intercalation cavity of the DNA model and are stabilized through a combination of π–π stacking, hydrogen‐bonding, van der Waals, and electrostatic interactions (Figure 7a,b). The docking calculations indicate that the proposed binding orientations are energetically favorable within the adopted docking protocol and that metal coordination enhances the interaction of the complexes with DNA compared to the free ligand. The accommodation of the relatively bulky metal complexes within the preexisting cavity is accompanied by local structural adjustment of the neighboring DNA base pairs, facilitating favorable non‐covalent interactions. However, these geometric observations should be regarded as qualitative structural features of the docking model rather than definitive evidence of ligand‐induced DNA intercalation. Overall, the docking results are in good agreement with the UV–vis absorption, fluorescence displacement, binding constant, and DFT studies, collectively supporting enhanced DNA interaction after metal complexation and a mixed groove‐binding/intercalative interaction for the Fe(II) and Cu(II) complexes. Although molecular docking alone cannot establish ligand‐induced DNA intercalation, the docking results are consistent with the UV–vis absorption, fluorescence displacement, binding constant, and DFT studies, collectively supporting enhanced DNA interaction after metal complexation. It should be noted that the docking calculations were performed using a DNA structure containing a preexisting intercalation cavity because rigid‐receptor docking algorithms cannot adequately reproduce the conformational changes required for ligand‐induced DNA intercalation. Therefore, the docking results provide a qualitative description of the binding orientation and should be regarded as supportive of the experimental findings rather than definitive proof of the binding mechanism. Likewise, the predicted AutoDock binding energies are interpreted as relative indicators of binding affinity rather than absolute binding free energies. More rigorous computational approaches, such as molecular dynamics simulations coupled with MM/PBSA or MM/GBSA free‐energy calculations, could provide additional insight into the DNA‐binding mechanism and will be considered in future studies.


#### Normal Mode Analysis of the Docked DNA Complexes
  Based on the docking results, the most stable 6‐DNA, 6‐Fe(II)‐DNA, and 6‐Cu(II)‐DNA docked complexes were further subjected to normal mode analysis (NMA) using the iMODS server to evaluate their collective flexibility and dynamic behavior. NMA is a computational approach based on elastic network models that provides information regarding the intrinsic motions, deformability, and flexibility of macromolecular complexes. The results of the NMA analyses are presented in Figure 8 and Figures S25–S27. The NMA results provided information regarding the relative flexibility and collective motions of the docked complexes. The 6‐DNA and 6‐Fe(II)‐DNA/Cu(II)‐DNA complex structures yielded an average deformability index of 0.8, as illustrated in Figure 8. The deformability and B‐factor give the mobility profiles of the docked structures [54]. The deformability plots of the complexes demonstrated the degree of the capability of the respective molecule to deform shown by the peaks (Figure 8a). The deformability plot of the polypeptide chain indicated modifications in all residues represented by the peak points, referred to as hinges, where the ligand, its metal complexes, and DNA interacted. Main chain deformability denotes a molecule's capacity to alter the conformation of each amino acid residue within a polypeptide chain, a trait that elucidates the molecule's functionality in relation to its flexibility and stability. The B‐factor graphs compare the NMA and the PDB fields of the complexes, which are presented in Figure 9b‐c. The deformability and B‐factors of the 6‐DNA and 6‐Fe(II)‐DNA/Cu(II)‐DNA complexes illustrate the peaks corresponding to the regions in the DNA residues with deformability, where the highest peaks represent the regions of high deformability, which means that nucleotides residues in the regions are going to interact most. The deformability and B‐factor analyses provide insight into the relative flexibility and mobility of the docked complexes. The observed differences indicate variations in the dynamic behavior of the ligand‐DNA and metal complex‐DNA assemblies; however, these results should be interpreted as indicators of structural flexibility rather than direct measures of binding affinity.

  Further, the computed eigenvalues of the docked complexes that characterize the motion stiffness and the movement of the amino acid residues in docked complexes are predicted to have comparatively the least required energy to deform its structure based on the lowest eigenvalue [47].

  The calculated eigenvalues for the 6–DNA, 6‐Fe(II)‐DNA, and 6‐Cu(II)‐DNA docked complexes were 9.31 × 10− 4, 4.59 × 10− 5, and 6.12 × 10− 5, respectively. Lower eigenvalues indicate a lower energetic requirement for collective motion and therefore suggest greater structural flexibility of the corresponding docked complex. The covariance and elastic network analyses further provide information regarding correlated motions and relative stiffness within the docked structures (Figures S25–S27). These analyses were used only to assess the intrinsic dynamic behavior of the docked complexes and not as direct measures of binding affinity or binding mode. However, the associated variance is inversely related to the eigenvalue, with the individual variance indicated by blue‐colored bars and the cumulative variance indicated by cyan‐colored bars (Figures S25d–S27d), while the coupling between pairs of residues is illustrated by the co‐variance map (Figures S25e–S27e), where red color indicated the correlated motion between a pair of residues, white color indicated uncorrelated motion, and blue indicated anti‐correlated motion. The elastic network model depicted by the elastic map (Figures S25f–S27f) expresses the link between the atoms denoted by the “dot”, and the color gradient of the dot is directly connected to their stiffness, thus, darker'spot' designates stiffer springs. Overall, the docking and NMA results are consistent with the experimental observations and provide complementary information regarding the structural flexibility and interaction behavior of the ligand and its metal complexes with DNA.


## Conclusion
  In summary, a novel D‐glucamine‐based Schiff base containing a triazolyl silatrane moiety (6) and its Fe(II) and Cu(II) complexes were successfully designed and synthesized in good yields using readily available starting materials. The synthesized compounds were comprehensively characterized by FT‐IR, UV–vis, 1H NMR, 13C NMR, ESI‐MS, elemental analysis, and DFT calculations, which collectively support the proposed structures. Spectroscopic DNA‐binding studies, including UV–vis absorption titration and ethidium bromide (EtBr) displacement assays, demonstrated that the free ligand predominantly interacts with CT‐DNA through groove binding, whereas Fe(II) and Cu(II) complexation significantly modifies the interaction toward a mixed groove‐binding/intercalative mode, accompanied by enhanced DNA‐binding affinity. Molecular docking studies further supported the experimentally observed binding behavior and provided structural insight into the possible binding orientations of the metal complexes within DNA. Overall, this study demonstrates that metal‐ion complexation is an effective strategy for modulating the DNA‐binding properties of triazolyl silatrane derivatives and provides a useful platform for the rational design of new silicon‐based coordination compounds with tunable DNA recognition. Although biological evaluation was beyond the scope of the present work, the promising DNA‐binding characteristics of these complexes warrant further investigations, including cytotoxicity, DNA cleavage, and other biological activity studies, to explore their potential medicinal applications.
