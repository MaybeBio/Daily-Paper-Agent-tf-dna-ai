# An Integrated Analysis of Manganese Metabolism‐Related Genes and Their Association With Biomarkers, Immune Infiltration, and Clinical Subtypes in Alzheimer's Disease


## ABSTRACT
  Alzheimer's disease (AD), a neurodegenerative condition marked by amyloid‐beta plaques and tau protein neurofibrillary tangles, progresses against a backdrop of essential biological processes. Manganese, an indispensable trace element for vital functions including energy metabolism and antioxidant defense, is integral to neurological health. Its precise metabolic role throughout the course of AD pathogenesis is not fully elucidated. Four essential manganese metabolism‐related genes were identified as diagnostic markers through a multi‐omic framework. This approach integrated Weighted Gene Co‐Expression Network Analysis with machine learning ensembles (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator/Random Forest/Extreme Gradient Boosting), followed by rigorous testing in an independent dataset. Beyond identification, we utilized CIBERSORT and ssGSEA to characterize immune infiltration and leveraged GSEA/GO/KEGG for pathway elucidation. Finally, AD patients were stratified into molecular subgroups based on these hub genes, and their underlying regulatory networks involving miRNAs and transcription factors were reconstructed. An integrated bioinformatics framework identified 12 differentially expressed genes related to manganese metabolism in AD. Machine‐learning–based feature selection further pinpointed four key diagnostic biomarkers (TSPO, PTBP1, GLO1, and ACACB) with high discriminative power (AUC: 0.831–0.905). Immune infiltration analysis revealed substantial immune remodeling in AD, including an elevation of naïve B cells. Moreover, AD samples were classified into two molecular subtypes displaying distinct immune and metabolic characteristics, and regulatory miRNA/TF interaction networks were constructed for the core genes. Our results shed new light on the molecular mechanisms underlying AD and support future efforts toward early diagnosis and personalized therapeutic interventions based on molecular subtypes.


## Introduction
  Alzheimer's Disease (AD) is a progressive, irreversible neurodegenerative disorder and the most common cause of dementia in older adults [1]. It is characterized by the gradual worsening of memory, thinking abilities, and behavioral disturbances, eventually becoming severe enough to interfere with daily life and independence [2]. With the accelerating aging of the global population, AD poses increasingly serious public health, social, and economic challenges [3]. The hallmarks of AD are neuritic plaques and neurofibrillary tangles [4]. Amyloid plaques are formed by the abnormal accumulation of beta‐amyloid peptides outside neurons [5]. Neurofibrillary tangles are formed by the aggregation of hyperphosphorylated tau protein inside neurons [6]. Under normal conditions, the tau protein is responsible for maintaining the microtubule structure within neuronal axons. When it becomes abnormally phosphorylated, tau detaches from microtubules and aggregates into tangles, leading to the disintegration of microtubules. This disrupts the intracellular transport system for nutrients and materials within neurons, ultimately resulting in neuronal death [7]. These two pathological processes work synergistically, leading to synaptic dysfunction, neuronal loss, and brain atrophy. The primary clinical symptom of AD is impairment in recent memory. Patients gradually lose their independence and require assistance with daily activities. In severe cases, patients lose the ability to interact with their environment and require round‐the‐clock care [8]. Death is typically caused by complications such as pneumonia or infection [9]. Although there is currently no cure, in‐depth research into its complex pathological mechanisms is crucial for developing early diagnostic tools and effective disease‐modifying therapies.

  Manganese is an essential trace element crucial for living organisms, playing key roles in energy metabolism, antioxidant defense, and neurological function [10, 11]. Its core physiological function is serving as a cofactor for mitochondrial manganese superoxide dismutase (MnSOD), which efficiently scavenges reactive oxygen species and protects cells from oxidative stress damage [12]. Manganese homeostasis is tightly regulated, with relatively low intestinal absorption in humans (less than 10%), and the primary elimination pathway being excretion via bile secreted by the liver. Liver function is central to maintaining manganese homeostasis [13]. Imbalances in manganese metabolism can lead to two consequences: either manganese overload (causing direct neurotoxicity and pathological protein aggregation) or manganese dysregulation (leading to decreased MnSOD activity and oxidative stress). Both may contribute to the onset and progression of AD through different molecular mechanisms [14, 15]. Therefore, maintaining strict homeostatic balance of manganese is crucial for neurological health. Manganese‐related molecular signatures represent an underexplored reservoir of biomarkers with great potential for decoding the pathological heterogeneity of AD and revealing microenvironmental interactions such as oxidative stress and neuroinflammation. Elucidating the association between manganese metabolism and AD will significantly advance the understanding of the complex pathogenesis of AD and provide molecular targets for developing new interventions and therapeutic approaches for neurodegenerative diseases.

  This study employs integrated multi‐omics profiling to decipher manganese metabolism‐related molecular signatures, thereby revealing their potential as diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets. By elucidating the interactions between manganese‐associated pathways and hallmark AD features, it aims to advance precision medicine strategies for molecular subtypes of AD.


## Materials and Methods

### Data Collection
  Download microarray data from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) (GSE132903, GSE122063). Among them, GSE132903 was used as the training set (AD: 97, Control: 98), while GSE122063 (AD: 56, Control: 44) was used as an independent validation set. A total of 2069 genes related to manganese metabolism were obtained from the Genecards website (https://www.genecards.org/Search/Keyword?queryString=Manganese%20metabolism%20related&startPage=82).


### Differential Expression and WGCNA Identify DEMMRGs
  The “WGCNA” R package was employed to perform weighted gene co‐expression network analysis. Genes were first ranked according to expression variance, and the top 30% most variable genes were retained for subsequent hierarchical clustering. A soft‐thresholding power of β = 20 was chosen to establish a scale‐free network architecture, as it achieved a scale‐free topology fit index of R 2 = 0.85. Genes exhibiting similar expression profiles were subsequently clustered into co‐expression modules. Module–trait associations were assessed by calculating Pearson correlation coefficients between module eigengenes and clinical traits, and genes within the module showing the strongest correlation were defined as hub genes. For differential expression analysis, the “limma” package was applied to compare AD and control samples. Genes meeting the criteria of an adjusted p‐value < 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change (|logFC|) > 0.585 were considered differentially expressed genes (DEGs). To further investigate the involvement of AD‐associated manganese metabolism genes, single‐sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) and Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) were performed on both the training dataset (GSE132903) and the validation dataset (GSE122063), enabling comparison of manganese metabolism–related gene (MMRG) activity between AD and normal groups. Ultimately, differentially expressed manganese metabolism–related genes (DEMMRGs) were identified by intersecting DEGs, genes from the WGCNA blue module, and predefined MMRGs.


### Machine Learning Diagnostic Biomarkers
  To enhance the robustness of diagnostic biomarker selection, an integrative feature‐screening framework based on multiple machine learning approaches was implemented. Specifically, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression was first applied with α set to 1, and the optimal penalty parameter (λ) was identified using 10‐fold cross‐validation, retaining variables with non‐zero regression coefficients. In parallel, a Random Forest (RF) model comprising 3000 decision trees was constructed, and feature importance was quantified according to the Gini impurity metric. Additionally, Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) was employed to iteratively train classification and regression trees (CART) within a gradient boosting scheme, with hyperparameters optimized via fivefold cross‐validation and candidate genes ranked based on their importance scores. Genes that were repeatedly identified across all three algorithms were ultimately considered reliable diagnostic biomarker candidates.


### Validation of Diagnostic‐Related Feature Genes
  Using the previously identified diagnostic genes, a predictive diagnostic model was established. A nomogram was developed with the “rms” R package, and its performance was assessed through calibration plots to examine the agreement between predicted and observed outcomes. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was subsequently applied to quantify the clinical utility of the model. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were conducted for each selected feature gene, with validation performed in both the training cohort and an independent validation cohort. Furthermore, the expression patterns of these genes were compared between control and patient samples across the training dataset and the external dataset to evaluate group‐specific differences.


### Immune Infiltration Analysis
  To elucidate the involvement of immune components in AD progression, immune cell composition in AD and control samples was quantified using the CIBERSORT algorithm, enabling a comparative assessment of immune infiltration patterns between the two groups. In parallel, ssGSEA was applied to evaluate differences in immune‐related pathways and immune cell signatures between AD patients and normal individuals. Subsequently, association analyses were conducted to determine the relationships between the identified feature genes, immune cell populations, and immune‐associated functional pathways.


### Gene Set Enrichment Analysis and Pathway Analysis
  The “c5.go.v2024.1.Hs.symbol” and “c2.cp.kegg_legacy.v2024.1.Hs.symbols.gmt” subsets were downloaded from MsigDB (https://www.gsea‐msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp). Gene set enrichment analysis of the selected feature genes was conducted using the GSEA framework, while Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway annotations were carried out with the “clusterProfiler” R package.


### Subtype Identification of Diagnostic Genes
  To delineate the molecular characteristics unique to each subtype, the training set's AD samples were stratified into distinct subgroups via consensus clustering in R (using the “ConsensusClusterPlus” package), guided by the previously identified signature genes. Following this stratification, we quantified the expression variance of these genes across subtypes and performed differential expression analysis (criteria: adjusted p‐value < 0.05, |logFC| > 0.585) to further define the subtype‐specific profiles.


### Construction of ceRNA and TF Regulatory Networks
  To systematically explore the upstream transcriptional regulators of the feature genes, we utilized the ENCODE database through the NetworkAnalyst platform (https://www.networkanalyst.ca/). Subsequently, miRNA‐gene interactions documented in miRTarBase (https://mirtarbase.cuhk.edu.cn/) were retrieved to pinpoint regulatory miRNAs, enabling the construction of an integrated mRNA‐miRNA regulatory network. The final network was rendered graphically using Cytoscape software (version 3.10).


### Statistical Analysis
  All statistical computations and data visualizations were conducted in R (v4.4.0) utilizing pertinent packages. Group comparisons for two‐sample analyses employed the Wilcoxon test. Correlational analyses between variables were assessed using the Pearson method, with data primarily visualized via the “ggplot2” package. A p‐value threshold of < 0.05 was applied to define statistical significance.


## Results

### Weighted Gene Co‐Expression Network Analysis
  To identify pivotal gene co‐expression modules, we applied the “WGCNA” package to analyze samples from both normal and AD groups, using a soft thresholding power of 20 for network construction (Figure 1A,B). This analysis yielded 10 distinct, color‐coded modules, each representing a cluster of highly interconnected genes. The blue module demonstrated a strong positive correlation with AD (r = 0.44, p < 0.001) and a corresponding negative correlation with controls (r = −0.44, p < 0.001) (Figure 1C). Its 1029 constituent genes were deemed key module genes. Further analysis of these core genes revealed a significant positive relationship between module membership and gene significance (r = 0.44, p = 5.8e‐50) (Figure 1D, Table S1).


### Screening of Diagnostic Biomarkers
  The intersection of MMRGs (2069) and key module genes (1029) yielded 78 manganese metabolism‐related AD diagnostic genes (Figure 2A). GO and KEGG functional enrichment analysis of the 78 candidate genes revealed their primary involvement in biological processes and KEGG pathways related to fundamental metabolism (such as energy production, glucose and carbon metabolism) and cellular fate regulation (such as apoptosis and prion diseases) (Figure 2B, Table S2). Building on this, the candidate genes showed strong and significant intercorrelations (Figure 2C). In parallel, GSVA revealed elevated pathway activity scores in AD patients relative to controls within the training set (Figure 2D), a finding that was corroborated in an independent validation cohort (Figure 2E). To refine the gene list for AD relevance, differential expression analysis on the training set identified 481 genes (Table S3). Intersecting these differentially expressed genes with the initial candidate pool yielded 12 key genes, designated as differentially expressed module marker genes (DEMMRGs, Figure 2F).


### Screening of Diagnostic Biomarkers Using Machine Learning (ML)
  To prioritize key variables from the 12 candidate DEMMRGs, we employed three distinct machine learning algorithms: RF, LASSO regression, and XGBoost. Based on non‐zero coefficients at the optimal lambda (lambda.min) in LASSO regression, 10 genes were selected (Figure 3A,B). Subsequently, the Random Forest algorithm, applying a Mean Decrease Gini threshold > 1, identified another set of 10 genes (Figure 3C,D). Meanwhile, XGBoost feature importance ranking yielded five genes (Figure 3E). Finally, the intersection of these algorithm‐specific lists revealed five consensus feature genes, which were defined as robust diagnostic biomarkers for subsequent modeling and validation (Figure 3F).


### Validation of Diagnostic Biomarkers
  Four genes (TSPO, PTBP1, GLO1, ACACB) were established as diagnostic biomarkers in this study. A nomogram was constructed to visualize their combined contribution to the prediction model (Figure 4A). Decision curve analysis indicated the model's clinical utility, with predicted benefits closely matching actual outcomes (Figure 4B,C). The model showed strong discriminative ability, achieving an AUC of 0.831 in the primary analysis, with each gene also demonstrating high individual predictive accuracy (Figure 4D). This diagnostic performance was further validated in an independent dataset (GSE122063), where the model attained an AUC of 0.905 (Figure 4E). Finally, we compared the expression levels of these four feature genes between AD patients and normal controls within the training set. Three feature genes (TSPO, PTBP1, ACACB) showed significantly elevated expression in AD patients, while one feature gene (GLO1) exhibited significantly reduced expression (Figure 4F). The same trends were observed in the validation set (GSE122063) (Figure 4G).


### Immune Infiltration Analysis
  The overall immune cell composition showed a comparable landscape between the control and AD groups (Figure 5A). Further deconvolution via CIBERSORT revealed a marked elevation of naïve B cells in AD, whereas the control group was characterized by higher abundances of activated dendritic cells and plasma cells (Figure 5B). Subsequent ssGSEA confirmed significant upregulation in the AD group across multiple immune‐related categories, including B cells, immune checkpoints, and NK cell activity (Figure 5C,D). Notably, the expression levels of PTBP1, TSPO, and ACACB were inversely correlated with activated dendritic cells and follicular helper T cells, but positively correlated with M0 macrophages and resting mast cells. GLO1 demonstrated opposite correlation patterns (Figure 5E–H).


### Single Gene Enrichment Analysis
  The GSEA results of the four signature genes—PTBP1, GLO1, TSPO, and ACACB—revealed two main cellular functions. PTBP1 and TSPO are primarily associated with high metabolic activity and synthesis (Figure 6A,C). These two genes are mainly linked to pathways such as ribosome, RNA metabolism, and oxidative phosphorylation. They generally indicate a state of high proliferation, elevated activity, and increased energy demand in cells. GLO1 and ACACB are primarily involved in lipid and fatty acid metabolism (Figure 6B,D). These two genes are associated with pathways such as fatty acid elongation, degradation, and cholesterol homeostasis. Their functions are centered around cellular lipid balance and the utilization and storage of energy resources.


### AD Subtype Analysis Based on Diagnostic Signature Genes
  Based on the expression of four diagnostic signature genes, clustering analysis was performed using the R package “ConsensusClusterPlus”, which identified two AD‐related disease subtypes. Cluster 1 contained 56 samples, and Cluster 2 contained 41 samples (Figure 7A–C). The two subtypes can be clearly distinguished by principal component analysis (Figure 7D). Three signature genes (PTBP1, TSPO, and ACACB) were significantly upregulated in Cluster 1, while GLO1 was significantly downregulated in Cluster 1 (Figure 7E). ssGSEA analysis revealed that Cluster 1 had higher scores in B cells, checkpoint, mast cells, and NK cells compared to Cluster 2, while Cluster 2 had higher scores in CD8+ T cells, macrophages, and Treg (Figure 7F). The CIBERSORT algorithm indicated that Cluster 1 had a higher degree of immune cell infiltration in resting mast cells and naive CD4+ T cells than Cluster 2, while Cluster 2 showed a higher degree of immune cell infiltration in neutrophils (Figure 7G). Differential analysis between Cluster 1 and Cluster 2 subgroups (|logFC| > 1, p.adj < 0.05) identified 77 upregulated genes and 232 downregulated genes (Figure 7H).


### Subtype Gene Enrichment Analysis
  The upregulated DEGs primarily involve pathways such as cell membrane transport, amino acid metabolism, skeletal development, as well as viral infection and lipid/insulin signaling (Figure 8A,B), exhibiting complex metabolic, defense, and developmental characteristics. In contrast, the downregulated DEGs display high specificity, overwhelmingly enriching in pathways related to synaptic signal transmission, neurotransmitter metabolism, insulin secretion, and neurodegenerative diseases (Figure 8C,D). GSEA revealed that cluster 1 was enriched in pathways such as allograft rejection, glutathione metabolism, and primary immunodeficiency (Figure 8 E); while cluster 2 was enriched in pathways including oxidative phosphorylation, SNARE interactions in vesicular transport, and vasopressin‐regulated water reabsorption (Figure 8F).


### Construction of a Signature Gene‐Associated Regulatory Network
  The target genes (CLO1, PTBP1, and ACACB) are subject to complex microRNA‐mediated regulation. Certain microRNAs, such as hsa‐miR‐6734‐3p and hsa‐miR‐6756‐3p, exhibit extensive interactions with these target genes (Figure 9A). In the transcription factor (TF) network, multiple transcription factors, including ELK1, ZBTB11, and TAF7, were identified as key regulators interacting with several diagnostic genes, suggesting their central role in transcriptional regulation (Figure 9B).


## Discussion
  This study conducted a systematic and in‐depth investigation into the role of manganese metabolism in the complex pathological process of AD. We successfully identified core manganese metabolism‐related genes closely associated with AD phenotypic traits from large‐scale gene expression data and constructed a molecular model with reliable diagnostic efficacy based on these findings. This achievement provides new potential biomarkers for the early identification of AD. Furthermore, our analysis comprehensively characterized the immune microenvironment in AD patients, revealing significant connections between manganese metabolism dysregulation and the infiltration of specific immune cell populations. Overall, this research not only supports the notion that disrupted manganese metabolism is a key component in AD pathogenesis but also establishes a solid molecular foundation for understanding the disease's complexity, enabling precise diagnosis, and developing subtype‐specific therapeutic strategies.

  These 4 diagnostic genes (TSPO, PTBP1, GLO1, ACACB) also play important roles in the development and progression of AD. TSPO (Translocator Protein) is a protein primarily located on the outer mitochondrial membrane. In the central nervous system, TSPO is mainly expressed in glial cells. Its expression level is significantly elevated in AD patients and is positively correlated with inflammatory responses, suggesting it may play a pro‐inflammatory role in disease progression [16, 17]. In this study, TSPO expression was increased in AD compared to normal tissue, consistent with previous research. Furthermore, studies have shown that TSPO locks microglia into a pro‐inflammatory phagocytic phenotype by amplifying the mitochondrial ROS‐mPTP‐NLRP3 signaling axis, thereby directly accelerating Aβ/tau‐related synaptic degeneration and exacerbating AD progression [18]. PTBP1 (polypyrimidine tract‐binding protein 1) is an RNA‐binding protein that influences neuronal development, synaptic function, and cell fate determination [19]. Significantly highly expressed in the brains of AD patients and model mice, PTBP1 drives decreased microglial phagocytic function and an imbalance in the 3R/4R‐tau ratio by promoting the retention of CD33 exon 2 and enhancing the inclusion of tau exon 10 in alternative splicing events [20]. This directly couples the two major pathological axes of Aβ deposition and neurofibrillary tangles, making PTBP1 a key node linking RNA splicing dysregulation to AD progression [21]. In this study, PTBP1 gene expression was also significantly elevated in AD patients. GLO1 (glyoxalase 1) is the rate‐limiting enzyme in the methylglyoxal detoxification system, thereby protecting cells from damage [22]. By clearing methylglyoxal‐AGEs and blocking the RAGE‐NOX‐2 oxidative‐inflammatory cascade, GLO1 plays a disease‐inhibiting, protective role in AD [23]. In this study, the expression level of GLO1 was significantly lower in AD compared to normal tissue, further supporting this conclusion. ACACB (Acetyl‐CoA carboxylase beta) is a rate‐limiting enzyme involved in mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation, regulating the process by which long‐chain fatty acids enter mitochondria for beta‐oxidation. Studies have identified ACACB as one of the key upregulated genes in AD, demonstrating potential diagnostic and prognostic value [24]. The four diagnostic genes play critical and diverse roles in the pathogenesis of AD. By regulating core pathological pathways such as inflammatory responses, RNA splicing, oxidative stress detoxification, and fatty acid metabolism, they collectively reflect and drive the progression of AD.

  Notably, these four core genes may be involved in key pathological processes underlying Mn neurotoxicity, supporting their potential as shared biomarkers between Mn exposure cohorts and AD. Previous studies have shown that in genetic models of manganese accumulation, TSPO, a well‐established marker of neuroinflammation, is markedly upregulated in regions with activated microglia, suggesting a central role for TSPO in Mn‐induced neurotoxicity [25, 26]. Manganese exposure can trigger severe oxidative stress and disrupt glucose metabolism, leading to the accumulation of methylglyoxal (MG) [27], the key enzyme responsible for MG detoxification plays a critical role in this process [28], and alterations in its expression are closely associated with Mn‐induced oxidative damage. In addition, Mn exposure–induced disruption of intracellular Mn2+ homeostasis may impair enzymatic activity, thereby suppressing fatty acid oxidation and exacerbating energy metabolic dysfunction and ROS production [29, 30]. Manganese exposure leads to abnormal mitochondrial transcriptome processing and disrupts RNA splicing mechanisms in the brain [31]. PTBP1 may be involved in manganese neurotoxicity by regulating neuronal splicing programs [19]. Collectively, these genes may function as shared molecular nodes linking manganese exposure to AD pathology, reflecting convergent pathways between Mn homeostasis dysregulation and neurodegenerative processes.

  Our study revealed significant differences in immune cell subset infiltration and functional activity between the AD group and the control group. Specifically, the AD group exhibited increased infiltration of naive B cells, while the control group showed higher levels of activated dendritic cells and plasma cell infiltration. This shift in the immune profile suggests the potential existence of a distinct immune dysregulation mechanism in AD: the abnormal accumulation of naive B cells may reflect an immature or dysregulated state of the immune system [32]. Naive B cells are antigen‐inexperienced precursors that initiate adaptive humoral immunity: they differentiate into antibody‐secreting cells or memory B cells after first antigen encounter and simultaneously present antigen‐derived peptides to CD4+ T cells, thereby licensing germinal‐center reactions and establishing long‐lived immunological memory [33, 34]. Activated dendritic cells migrate into the brain via the CCL2‐CCR2 axis, promote Th17 polarization through the CD80/86‐IL‐1β axis to amplify neuroinflammation, while simultaneously facilitating Aβ clearance by transporting it to lymphatic vessels [35, 36]. The scarcity of activated dendritic cells in the AD group corresponds with diminished clearance capacity, highlighting a critical impairment in the brain's immune‐mediated clearance mechanisms.

  This study systematically reveals the complex regulatory network of manganese metabolism‐related genes in AD pathology through multi‐level enrichment analysis, with its functional characteristics highlighting the central role of metabolic homeostasis imbalance. Enrichment analysis of candidate genes shows co‐enrichment in the “energy metabolism” and “cell fate regulation” pathways. Manganese metabolism disorders may drive AD progression: mitochondrial energy metabolism impairment directly exacerbates neuronal oxidative damage and aggravates the progression of AD [37, 38]. Decreased MnSOD activity weakens mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation capacity, reduces ATP synthesis, and simultaneously accumulates superoxide anions, directly intensifying ROS burst and cellular oxidative damage [39]. Particularly noteworthy is the significant enrichment of the prion disease pathway, which may reveal the regulatory effect of manganese ions on PrP protein conformation—studies have confirmed that Mn2+ can promote β‐sheet structure formation by replacing Cu2+ binding sites in PrP, thereby accelerating the generation of prion‐like Aβ oligomers and significantly impacting neurodegenerative diseases such as AD [40].

  Subtype‐specific enrichment analysis further revealed the molecular basis of disease heterogeneity. The activation of the “viral infection” pathway may be associated with the aberrant reactivation of human endogenous retroviruses (HERV) [41]. Recent studies have found that upregulated HERV‐K expression in patient brains can induce inflammatory transformation of glial cells through the TLR3/NF‐κB pathway [42]. The enrichment of the “synaptic vesicle‐mediated transport” pathway is closely related to impaired SNARE complex function [43]. Manganese provides a novel molecular explanation for neurotoxicity by synergistically disrupting SNARE‐mediated synaptic vesicle anchoring and fusion [43].

  Although this study provides a relatively systematic analysis of the diagnostic value of MMRGs in AD and the remodeling of the immune microenvironment, it still has several limitations. All findings in this study, including the expression differences of the four core diagnostic genes (TSPO, PTBP1, GLO1, ACACB) and their roles in the diagnostic model, are based on bioinformatic analyses of public databases. There is a lack of experimental validation using in vitro cell models (such as AD patient‐derived iPSC neurons or glial cells) or in vivo animal models (e.g., AD mouse models), which prevents direct demonstration of the causal relationships and molecular mechanisms of these genes in AD pathogenesis. Although miRNA‐mRNA and TF‐mRNA regulatory networks were constructed, and key regulators (such as ELK1, ZBTB11, and specific miRNAs) were identified, these regulatory relationships are solely based on predictive databases and have not been confirmed through functional experiments (such as gene knockdown/overexpression, dual‐luciferase reporter assays) to verify their actual regulatory roles in AD cells and tissues.

  The diagnostic performance of the constructed model has been validated across multiple datasets. However, models based on single‐gene expression levels may be affected by batch effects and data normalization methods, limiting their cross‐dataset generalizability. In contrast, the relative ranking of gene pairs focuses on the ordinal relationships within each gene pair, which can effectively mitigate batch effects and cross‐dataset heterogeneity challenges [44]. In future work, we will further explore expression ranking features based on manganese metabolism‐related gene pairs to construct a more robust diagnostic model. In addition, the associations between the diagnostic genes identified in this study and AD are derived from observational data and cannot establish causality. Mendelian randomization (MR) can effectively reduce confounding and reverse causation bias, serving as a reliable method for causal inference [45]. In future work, we will perform MR analyses using AD‐related GWAS data on these four genes and manganese metabolism‐related traits to validate their causal associations with AD and to clarify the role of manganese dyshomeostasis in AD pathology.

  Furthermore, while we stratified AD into two molecular subtypes (Cluster 1 and Cluster 2) with distinct immune and metabolic landscapes, the current study lacks a high‐resolution analysis of cellular heterogeneity within these subgroups. Bulk tissue analysis may mask cell‐type‐specific contributions to manganese dyshomeostasis. Future directions should leverage scRNA‐seq and spatial transcriptomics to delineate the cellular distribution and functional states of these hub genes across different subtypes [46]. Such single‐cell resolution will be instrumental in decoding how manganese metabolism‐related genes drive disease heterogeneity and will facilitate the development of subtype‐specific, personalized therapeutic interventions. Although this study did not classify patients into high‐ or low‐risk groups, future applications for clinical risk stratification should adopt a fixed cutoff or continuous variable modeling to avoid standardization issues arising from data‐driven cutoffs [47]. Accordingly, we will determine a fixed risk score or continue using a continuous variable framework in subsequent studies to ensure standardized performance across populations.


## Conclusion
  In summary, this study provides a new molecular basis for understanding the complex pathology of AD and lays the foundation for the future development of early diagnostic tools and subtype‐specific precision therapeutic strategies.
