# Heart failure may promote breast cancer progression through a Col10a1-associated Tgf-β/Smad-EMT pathway


## Background
  Heart failure (HF) is clinically associated with aggravated breast cancer (BC) progression; however, the integrative genomic mechanisms underlying this cardio-oncological crosstalk remain poorly understood.


## Methods
  We conducted integrative bioinformatics analyses combining weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) and machine learning algorithms to screen key hub genes involved in HF and BC progression. The candidate gene Col10a1 was further validated using myocardial infarction (MI)-induced HF xenograft mouse models, paired clinical plasma and tumor specimens, and comprehensive in vitro functional assays.


## Results
  COL10A1 expression was higher in breast cancer patients with CVD than in BC-only patients. Given the limited sample size, these findings were considered exploratory. Mechanistically, elevated Col10a1 was associated with activation of the transforming growth factor‑β (Tgf-β)/Smad signaling pathway and induction of epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT). In vivo, the HF microenvironment markedly accelerated BC tumor growth and was accompanied by enhanced Col10a1 expression and Tgf-β signaling activation. In vitro, cardiomyocyte-derived Col10a1 significantly promoted the proliferation, migration, and invasion of BC cells, whereas Col10a1 knockdown significantly attenuated these malignant phenotypes.


## Conclusions
  Our study identifies a potential regulatory link between HF and BC and provides preliminary mechanistic insights into the role of Col10a1 in BC progression under cardiac injury conditions. Col10a1 may serve as a candidate mediator in the interaction between cardiac injury and BC progression.

## Introduction
  Breast cancer (BC) and cardiovascular disease (CVD) are common conditions among women and impose substantial burdens on healthcare systems and economies worldwide [1, 2]. BC and CVD share several pathogenic mechanisms and risk factors [3]. Previous studies have extensively investigated the cardiotoxic effects of cancer therapies and strategies for their prevention or management. In recent years, however, increasing attention has focused on reverse cardio-oncology, which examines how CVD may affect tumor development. Nevertheless, the associations of CVD and heart failure (HF) with cancer incidence and progression remain controversial, and current epidemiological evidence does not establish a consistent association between HF and breast cancer progression [4, 5].

  Although CVD is generally more prevalent in men, particularly among younger and middle-aged adults, it remains a major health burden among women. The relationship between BC, the most common malignancy in women, and CVD is therefore particularly important to investigate [6, 7]. Previous studies have identified several mechanisms linking CVD and BC, including those mediated by cardiac-derived factors such as SerpinA3, SerpinA1, and periostin [8–9]. These findings suggest that secreted proteins may contribute to the interaction between HF and BC progression. Previous studies have also linked Col10a1 to breast cancer progression and phenotypes related to epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT). Thus, the novelty of the present study lies in evaluating Col10a1 in the context of HF-associated BC progression rather than proposing an entirely unknown oncogenic role for Col10a1 [10]. Increasing evidence indicates that extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling may represent an important biological link between cardiovascular disorders and cancer progression. ECM-associated proteins regulate tissue fibrosis and mechanical properties and can also influence tumor cell behavior, immune microenvironment remodeling, and disease progression [11, 12]. Emerging evidence on ECM biomarkers in cardio-oncology suggests that molecular alterations shared by cardiovascular injury and tumor development may provide additional insights into disease mechanisms and therapeutic strategies [13, 14].

  Immune dysfunction and inflammation are also known to play critical roles in the development of CVD and can influence tumor initiation and progression. Trained immunity, a specialized form of innate immune memory, has been implicated in various diseases, including neurodegenerative disorders, CVD, and cancer [15–18]. Previous work has suggested that HF can accelerate BC progression by enhancing trained immunity [19]. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying HF-associated BC progression remain incompletely understood. Clarifying these mechanisms may improve our understanding of this pathological relationship and identify potential predictive biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

  In this study, we integrated bioinformatics analyses, murine models, and clinical specimens to investigate how HF may influence BC progression. Our findings provide exploratory evidence that a Col10a1-associated pathway may contribute to HF-associated BC progression.


## Materials and methods

### Data sources and processing
  The publicly available gene expression datasets for BC and control samples were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under accession numbers GSE21422 and GSE50428; collectively, they comprised 10 control and 26 BC samples. The HF gene expression dataset GSE116250, also obtained from GEO, included 15 control and 50 HF samples. Peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) transcriptomic data from patients with HF were obtained from GSE21125, which comprised 9 control and 36 HF samples. After the data were downloaded, batch effects between the BC datasets were corrected using the “sva” package in R to generate an integrated BC expression dataset. The PBMC dataset was included as a complementary peripheral blood transcriptomic resource to prioritize HF-associated candidates whose transcripts were also detectable in PBMCs; it was not used to infer tissue origin.


### Secreted protein dataset
  We retrieved a list of 3,970 secreted protein-coding genes from the Human Protein Atlas (https://www.proteinatlas.org/) under the category “SPOCTOPUS predicted secreted proteins” (https://www.proteinatlas.org/search/protein_class%3ASPOCTOPUS+predicted+secreted+ proteins).


### Differential gene expression (DEG) analysis
  For the mergedintegrated BC dataset, the HF dataset, and the HF PBMC bulk RNA-seq data, we dataset, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified DEGs using the “limma” package in R. DEGDifferential expression analysis was usedserved as an initial screening step to identify genes associated with disease status in each dataset. To ensure comparability among To apply a consistent screening framework across independent GEO datasets generated fromon different platforms, a consistent DEGcommon filtering criterion (|log₂FClog2FC| ≥ 1 and P ≤ 0.05) was appliedused after dataset-specific normalization. This unified threshold strategy minimizes potential bias caused by variable cutoff selection and facilitates the identification of robust transcriptional alterations across cohorts. Data visualization, including volcanoapproach standardized the cutoff applied across datasets but did not eliminate heterogeneity related to platform or tissue source. Volcano plots and hierarchical clustering heatmaps, was performed were generated using the “ggplot2” and “pheatmap” packages in R.


### Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) and identification of key modules
  WGCNA was performed separately for GSE21422 and GSE50428 to examine gene coexpression patterns and identify modules associated with disease status. This complementary analysis was intended to detect coordinately regulated gene sets that might not be prioritized by single-gene differential expression analysis. The median absolute deviation (MAD) was calculated for each gene in each BC dataset, and genes with MAD = 0 were excluded. The “goodSamplesGenes” function in the “WGCNA” package was used to remove poor-quality genes or samples. A scale-free coexpression network was then constructed using the one-step method. The soft-thresholding power was set to β = 5 based on the scale-free topology criterion; this value provided an acceptable topology fit (R² = XX) while retaining sufficient mean connectivity. Module eigengenes (MEs) were calculated and correlated with disease status. The modules showing the strongest positive and negative correlations were selected for downstream analysis. Within each selected module, genes were prioritized according to module membership and gene significance. Genes shared by the trait-associated modules from the two datasets were then considered key genes.


### Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network construction
  To examine potential interactions between HF-associated candidate secreted proteins and proteins encoded by BC-related key genes, a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using the STRING database (https://www.string-db.org) with a medium-confidence score cutoff (> 0.4). This analysis was used to prioritize biologically connected candidates rather than relying solely on overlap among expression datasets. The network was visualized in Cytoscape (version 3.10.2). High-scoring subnetworks were identified using the Molecular Complex Detection (MCODE) plugin, and the two highest-scoring modules were selected for further analysis.


### Machine learning
  To prioritize HF-associated genes encoding secreted proteins that might influence BC, we applied two machine-learning algorithms. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was used for feature shrinkage and variable selection, whereas random forest (RF) analysis was used to rank variables by importance. LASSO regression was implemented using the “glmnet” package, and the RF model was constructed using the “randomForest” package. Genes with a MeanDecreaseGini value > 2 were considered important. Genes selected by both LASSO and RF were defined as candidate hub genes. Because these analyses were exploratory, the resulting genes were treated as candidates requiring independent validation.


### Gene enrichment analysis
  To characterize the biological functions and pathways associated with the candidate gene sets, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed using the Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/). Terms with P ≤ 0.05 were considered nominally significant. Results were visualized using bubble plots and circular plots.


### Patient sample collection
  Samples were collected from Taihe Hospital in Shiyan, Hubei Province, China, between January 2023 and August 2024. These included included blood, tumor tissue, and adjacent non-tumor tissue from patients with invasive breast cancer, with (n = 5) or without (n = 5) a prior history of CVD, as well as blood samples from healthy controls. Tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissues were not collected from healthy participants. All procedures adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki, and ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Taihe Hospital (Approval No. 2024XM019). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians. Because of the limited sample size and the absence of matched cardiac tissue, the human data were used primarily for exploratory clinical validation and cannot establish causality or exclude confounding by shared risk factors. Peripheral blood and tissue specimens were collected and processed according to standard institutional clinical and laboratory procedures.


### Animal models
  Eight- to ten-week-old female C57BL/6 mice weighing 21–26 g were housed in a specific-pathogen-free facility under controlled temperature and humidity conditions, with a 12-h light/dark cycle and free access to food and water. Only female mice were used in this study to minimize potential sex-related variability. After 1–2 weeks of acclimatization, mice were randomly assigned to the sham group and heart failure group. The exact number of animals included in each experiment is provided in the corresponding figure legends. Animals that died after LAD ligation or met predefined humane endpoints were recorded and excluded from subsequent analyses as indicated. Predefined humane endpoints included: (1) body weight loss > 20% within 72 h; (2) severe dyspnea, cyanosis, or inability to feed or drink spontaneously; (3) persistent severe pain or distress unresponsive to analgesic intervention; (4) moribund state. After accounting for all predefined exclusions and mortality following LAD ligation, a total of 38 mice were included in the final experimental analyses, and the mice were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Huazhong Agricultural University. Tumor caliper measurements and histological quantitative analysis were performed by two independent investigators who were fully blinded to group assignment. All samples were labeled with unique numeric identifiers before detection, and group allocation was decoded only after all quantitative analyses and statistical calculations were completed. All animal procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Huazhong Agricultural University (HZAUMO-2024-0248) and were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.


### Heart failure mouse model
  Heart failure was induced by permanent ligation of the left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery. Briefly, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, placed in the supine position, and subjected to thoracotomy to expose the heart. The LAD coronary artery was permanently ligated using 7 − 0 silk sutures. Successful ligation was confirmed by immediate blanching of the anterior wall of the left ventricle. Sham-operated mice underwent the same procedure without LAD ligation. After surgery, the mice were monitored until recovery and maintained under SPF conditions. Four weeks after LAD ligation, cardiac function was assessed by echocardiography to verify the HF phenotype. Animals that died during surgery or failed to meet the predefined criteria for successful HF induction were excluded from the final analysis.


### Echocardiography
  Transthoracic echocardiography was performed 4 weeks after LAD ligation using a Vevo 3100 system (Fujifilm VisualSonics) while the mice were under 2% isoflurane anesthesia. Mice were placed on a 37 °C heated platform, and hair was removed from the chest. Left ventricular (LV) structure and function were evaluated in parasternal long- and short-axis views. Parameters included LV ejection fraction (EF), fractional shortening (FS), wall thickness, and corrected mass. Baseline echocardiography was not performed, which limits interpretation of the magnitude of functional change after LAD ligation.


### Masson’s trichrome staining
  Mouse heart tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime, P0099) overnight at 4 °C, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin blocks were sectioned at 5 μm thickness using a rotary microtome (Leica RM2235), and sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through graded ethanol solutions. Masson’s trichrome staining was performed using a commercial kit (Servicebio, G1006) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, sections were stained with Weigert’s iron hematoxylin to label nuclei (black), followed by Biebrich scarlet-acid fuchsin solution to stain cytoplasm and muscle fibers (red), and then differentiated in phosphomolybdic-phosphotungstic acid and counterstained with aniline blue to visualize collagen fibers (blue). After dehydration and clearing, slides were mounted with neutral resin (Servicebio, G8590). Stained images were captured using a light microscope and analyzed with ImageJ software to assess collagen deposition.


### Immunofluorescence staining of tissue sections
  Mouse heart and tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4 °C, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness using a rotary microtome. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating the sections in citrate buffer (10 mM, pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 20 min. After cooling, the sections were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 (Beyotime, ST795) for 15 min and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma-Aldrich, A8022) for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies against Col10a1 (Abcam, EPR13044) and Ki-67 (Cell Signaling Technology, #9449) were applied overnight at 4 °C. After washing, Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Beyotime, C1002), and the slides were mounted using antifade mounting medium (Beyotime, P0126). Fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope and analyzed with ImageJ software.


### Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
  Blood-derived samples and tumor specimens were collected, and Col10a1 concentrations were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions. The mouse ELISA kit was obtained from BMASSAY (Antibodies online, ABIN1127423), and the human kit was obtained from CUSABIO (CSB-EL005715HU). All ELISA measurements were performed in triplicate.


### Western blotting
  Proteins were extracted using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors, quantified with a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Beyotime, P0010), separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked and incubated with primary antibodies overnight. After washing, horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied, and signals were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (Beyotime, P0018S). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ. Primary antibodies targeted Col10a1 (abcam, EPR13044), E-cadherin (abcam, EP700Y), Vimentin (abcam, EPR3776), N-cadherin (abcam, EPR1791-4), Tgf-β (CST, #3709), p-Smad2 (CST, #7348), and β-actin (Servicebio, GB15003-100).


### Cell culture
  E0771 mouse breast cancer cells were obtained from SUNNCELL Biotech (Wuhan, China). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Cultures were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination.


### Tumor inoculation
  Four weeks after surgery, mice in the sham group and mice with echocardiographically confirmed HF were orthotopically inoculated with E0771 breast cancer cells. E0771 cells were harvested during the logarithmic growth phase, resuspended in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and injected into the mammary fat pad at a dose of 4 × 105 cells per mouse. Tumor growth was monitored using caliper measurements, and tumor volume was calculated as V = (L × W2)/2, where L represents the longest tumor diameter and W represents the shortest tumor diameter. Ten days after tumor inoculation, the mice were euthanized, and tumor tissue, blood, heart tissue, and other indicated samples were collected for subsequent histological, molecular, and biochemical analyses. Outcome assessment and data analysis were performed by investigators blinded to group allocation when possible.


### Wound healing assay
  E0771 cells were seeded in 12-well plates (1.5 × 10⁵ cells/well), grown to confluence, and scratched with a 200 µL pipette tip. After washing with PBS, serum-free medium was added. Images were acquired at 0 and 24 h, and wound closure was quantified using ImageJ.


### Cell transfection
  For the conditioned-medium experiments, primary cardiomyocytes were transfected with small interfering RNA targeting Col10a1 (si-Col10a1) or negative-control siRNA (si-NC) using Lipofectamine™ 3000 (Invitrogen, L3000015) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates and transfected at approximately 70–80% confluence with 50 nM siRNA in Opti-MEM™ reduced-serum medium (Gibco, 31985062). After 6 h, the transfection medium was replaced with complete culture medium. Forty-eight hours after transfection, conditioned medium was collected for subsequent E0771 functional assays, and knockdown efficiency was assessed by western blotting. The siRNA sequences were synthesized by GeneCreat (Wuhan, China).


### Transwell invasion assay
  For migration and invasion assays, E0771 cells (1 × 10⁴ for migration, 2 × 10⁴ for invasion) were seeded in Transwell inserts without and with Matrigel (1:8 dilution), respectively. After 24 h, cells that had migrated or invaded through the membrane to its lower surface were fixed, stained with crystal violet, and counted in five randomly selected microscopic fields.


### Statistical analysis
  Statistical analyses of the experimental data were performed using GraphPad Prism(version 9.0.2; GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Where feasible, exact P values have been provided rather than only reporting threshold-based significance. For comparisons involving more than two groups, appropriate multiple-comparison procedures have been applied (e.g., one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparison test, as appropriate). For two-group comparisons, assumptions underlying parametric testing were evaluated using (normality and variance assessments). Where these assumptions were not satisfied, appropriate nonparametric tests were used. All reported n values represent independent biological replicates, technical replicates were averaged per biological sample and were not treated as independent biological observations for statistical testing. All quantitative data in bar graphs and line graphs are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), unless otherwise specified in individual figure legends. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical tests were pre-specified and two-sided.


## Results

### Construction of weighted gene co-expression networks and identification of key modules
  After batch-effect correction, GSE21422 and GSE50428 were merged (Fig. 1A). The normalized, integrated BC dataset was used for downstream analyses. Using thresholds of P ≤ 0.05 and |log2FC| ≥ 1, 3,840 DEGs were identified, including 478 upregulated and 3,362 downregulated genes. These genes were visualized in volcano plots and hierarchical clustering heatmaps (Fig. 1B and C, Supplementary Table 1).

  To assess robustness, WGCNA was performed independently in GSE21422 and GSE50428 to identify BC-associated gene modules. A soft-thresholding power of β = 5 was selected based on the scale-free topology criterion. In total, 19 and 10 modules were identified in GSE21422 and GSE50428, respectively (Fig. 1D), and module eigengene clustering is shown in Fig. 1E and F. In GSE21422, the grey60 module showed the strongest positive correlation with BC (r = 0.62, P = 5e-03), whereas the turquoise module showed the strongest negative correlation (r = − 0.90, P = 2e–07) (Supplementary Table 3). In GSE50428, the blue module showed the strongest positive correlation (r = 0.61, P = 2e–04), whereas the turquoise module showed the strongest negative correlation (r = − 0.94, P = 1e–14) (Fig. 1G) (Supplementary Table 2). A total of 124 genes shared by the BC DEG set and the trait-associated modules were selected as BC-related key genes (Fig. 1H, Supplementary Table 4).


### Machine learning identifies key genes
  Because secreted factors may contribute to HF-associated tumor phenotypes, we reanalyzed GSE116250 and GSE21125, representing cardiac and PBMC transcriptomes, respectively. DEGs were visualized using volcano plots and heatmaps (Fig. 2A and D, Supplementary Table 5). Intersecting the upregulated genes with a curated Human Protein Atlas list of 3,946 genes encoding secreted proteins yielded 365 and 46 genes encoding candidate secreted proteins in the cardiac and PBMC HF datasets, respectively. Eleven candidate genes were shared between the two datasets (Fig. 2E).

  A STRING-based PPI network was constructed using proteins encoded by the BC-related key genes and the HF-associated genes encoding candidate secreted proteins, with a confidence score > 0.4. Genes corresponding to the network proteins were enriched for terms related to extracellular matrix organization, endoplasmic reticulum components, and cytokine–cytokine receptor signaling (Fig. 2F). GO (BP, CC, MF) and KEGG enrichment analyses further supported these functional associations (Fig. 2G).

  We next identified 10 genes shared by the BC-related key-gene set and the HF-associated secreted-protein gene set (Fig. 2H, Supplementary Table 6). Given the relatively small sample size, LASSO and RF analyses were used for exploratory candidate-gene prioritization rather than to develop a clinically validated prediction model. LASSO regression reduced the list to 3 genes (Fig. 2I and J, Supplementary Table 7), whereas RF analysis selected genes with MeanDecreaseGini > 2 (Fig. 2K, Supplementary Table 7). Two genes, Col10a1 and Chrdl1, were selected by both methods (Fig. 2L). RT-qPCR detected higher Col10a1 mRNA abundance in tumor tissues from patients with BC and comorbid HF/CVD, whereas Chrdl1 did not show a comparable pattern (Fig. 2M). Because mRNA abundance does not necessarily reflect protein abundance, this finding was interpreted as preliminary screening evidence rather than definitive protein-level validation.


### Association Between Col10a1 and Tgf-β Pathway Activation in HF-associated BC
  Circulating Col10a1 levels were significantly higher in patients with CVD, whereas no significant elevation was observed in patients with BC alone (Fig. 3A). In tumor tissues, Col10a1 levels were higher in the HF + BC group than in the BC-only group (Fig. 3B). This pattern is consistent with, but does not establish, a contribution of CVD/HF to increased circulating Col10a1.

  External validation using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and GEO datasets identified an association between COL10A1 expression and Tgf-β pathway activity (P = 5.03 × 10⁻⁸⁰, R = 0.53; Supplementary Fig. 1). In patient samples, Tgf-β pathway activity was significantly higher in the HF + BC group than in the BC-only group (Fig. 3C). Western blotting showed an EMT-consistent pattern of marker expression (increased N-cadherin, Vimentin; decreased E-cadherin) in HF + BC tumor tissues (Fig. 3D).


### Mouse Model Supports HF-associated Tumor Growth Acceleration
  An MI-induced HF model was established in mice (Fig. 3E). Echocardiography showed reduced EF and FS (Fig. 3F), Masson’s trichrome staining showed myocardial fibrosis (Fig. 3G), and elevated circulating B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) levels supported the HF phenotype (Fig. 3H). After orthotopic E0771 tumor inoculation, MI + BC mice developed significantly larger tumors than sham + BC mice (Fig. 3I). Ki-67 immunofluorescence staining indicated greater tumor-cell proliferation in MI + BC tumors (Fig. 3J). Col10a1 levels were significantly higher in cardiac and tumor tissues and in the circulation of MI + BC mice (Fig. 3K and M). These mice also showed greater Tgf-β pathway activation and EMT-associated marker changes consistent with EMT (Fig. 3N and O).


### In Vitro Validation of Cardiac-Derived Col10a1 Function
  To explore potential mechanisms, E0771 cells were treated with conditioned medium from primary cardiomyocytes subjected to hypoxia/reoxygenation. Compared with conditioned medium from normoxia-treated cardiomyocytes, conditioned medium from hypoxia/reoxygenation-treated cardiomyocytes increased E0771 cell viability (Fig. 4A), migration, and invasion (Fig. 4B and C) and was associated with Tgf-β pathway activation and EMT-related changes(Fig. 4D and E). These findings should be interpreted cautiously because the conditioned medium was not comprehensively characterized, and factors other than Col10a1 may have contributed to the observed effects.

  Silencing Col10a1 in cardiomyocytes before conditioned-medium collection attenuated the effects of the conditioned medium on E0771 cells. Knockdown was assessed by RT-qPCR and functional assays (Fig. 4F and I) showed that conditioned medium from hypoxia/reoxygenation-treated cardiomyocytes no longer significantly promoted E0771 cell proliferation or migration. These results suggest that cardiomyocyte-derived Col10a1 may contribute to Tgf-βpathway activation and EMT in breast cancer cells under the modeled cardiac-injury conditions.


## Discussion
  In this study, we investigated potential mechanisms through which CVD, particularly MI-induced HF, may influence BC progression. By integrating public transcriptomic datasets, a mouse model, and clinical specimens and applying WGCNA and exploratory machine-learning analyses, we identified evidence for a Col10a1-associated pathway that may contribute to HF-related BC progression. This combined approach provided convergent, although exploratory, evidence from bioinformatics, murine experiments, and limited clinical validation. Across the clinical and experimental systems examined, conditions associated with HF or CVD were linked to altered Col10a1 abundance, Tgf-β/Smad pathway activation, and EMT-related changes. However, these findings do not establish Col10a1 as the sole or dominant mediator of this interaction. The main contribution of the present study is therefore to position Col10a1 within a potential HF-associated tumor-promoting pathway rather than to claim a newly discovered oncogenic function of Col10a1.

  Previous studies of the relationship between CVD and BC have focused mainly on chemotherapy-induced cardiotoxicity and the effects of tumors on cardiac function, helping to establish the interdisciplinary field of cardio-oncology [20]. More recently, attention has shifted toward reverse cardio-oncology, particularly the possibility that CVD, especially MI or HF, may promote tumor progression through mechanisms such as immune reprogramming and cardiac-derived exosomes [21, 22]. However, the molecular pathways underlying these processes remain poorly defined. Previous reports have suggested that periostin, a secreted extracellular matrix protein associated with the Tgf-β pathway, may act as a chemoattractant and enhance tumor growth in the setting of CVD [21]. Our integrative analysis did not highlight Periostin but instead identified Col10a1 as a candidate factor for further study. The experimental model represents MI-induced cardiac injury and dysfunction and does not encompass all forms of clinical HF or CVD. The findings should therefore not be generalized directly to all cardiovascular disease subtypes.

  Col10a1 is a secreted short-chain collagen and a component of the extracellular matrix. It is synthesized in the endoplasmic reticulum before secretion [23]. Cellular component enrichment analysis of HF-related BC genes included terms related to the endoplasmic reticulum. This finding is compatible with secretory protein processing but does not specifically implicate Col10a1. Previous studies have associated elevated Col10a1 expression with poor prognosis in several cancers, including BC, pancreatic cancer, and prostate cancer [13, 24, 25]. However, the mechanism through which Col10a1 promotes BC progression remains incompletely defined. Outside oncology, Col10a1 has been associated with aortic valve disease and chondrocyte development [26]. Mechanistically, Col10a1 can be regulated by the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and can modulate valvular interstitial cell differentiation, further supporting a role in tissue remodeling and fibrosis [27, 28]. Given its role in extracellular matrix composition and fibroblast and chondrocyte function, Col10a1 may act as a candidate mediator of tumor microenvironment remodeling and cancer progression, which requires dedicated mechanistic verification. Although increasing evidence supports an interaction between cardiovascular dysfunction and cancer progression, epidemiological studies have not consistently shown a positive association between HF and breast cancer outcomes. Differences in cohort composition, cardiovascular risk profiles, cancer treatment exposure, follow-up duration, and statistical adjustment may contribute to the conflicting observations. Prospective studies in well-matched clinical populations are therefore needed to clarify the clinical relevance of these cardio-oncology interactions. In this context, our findings are consistent with a possible role for COL10A1-associated extracellular matrix remodeling in linking cardiovascular abnormalities to tumor progression. Further clinical validation is required.

  The Tgf-β/Smad pathway has context-dependent roles in normal and tumor tissues and functions in both epithelial and mesenchymal cells [29, 30]. In healthy tissues, Tgf-β regulates wound healing, immune homeostasis, and cell proliferation. In tumors, however, Tgf-β can promote immune evasion, EMT, and invasion [31–35]. Tgf-β also promotes fibrosis and angiogenesis and stimulates collagen production, potentially creating a feedback loop in which collagen deposition further activates Tgf-β signaling [36–38]. Tgf-β inhibitors have shown antifibrotic effects in experimental studies, but their ability to prevent or treat HF clinically remains uncertain [39]. Studies have also described a context-dependent shift of Tgf-β/Smad signaling from tumor suppression to tumor promotion in gastric and breast cancers. Pathway inhibition can reduce tumor progression in selected models [40–42]. Careful modulation of Tgf-β signaling may therefore merit investigation as a strategy to limit HF-associated cancer progression. Recent studies have implicated Col10a1 in breast cancer progression and EMT-related phenotypes [13, 14]. The present study extends this literature by suggesting a possible role for Col10a1 specifically in HF-associated breast cancer progression. Col10a1 abundance was associated with Tgf-β/Smad pathway activation, suggesting that this pathway may contribute to the observed effects. However, the direct molecular link between Col10a1 and Tgf-β/Smad activation was not defined, and receptor-level or intermediary mechanisms remain unknown. The present data also do not establish the heart as the exclusive or predominant source of circulating Col10a1.

  No therapy has been established specifically to prevent HF-associated acceleration of tumor progression. Our findings suggest that Tgf-β pathway inhibitors warrant further investigation in this context. Pathway enrichment analysis of HF-associated BC genes highlighted cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, suggesting that cytokine signaling may also contribute to HF-associated tumor progression. Cytokines released by immune and stromal cells can facilitate tumor immune escape, and Tgf-β/Smad signaling can impair immune surveillance [43]. According to The Human Protein Atlas, Col10a1 is most highly expressed in the hTEC/SVTERT24-B cell line, a lymphocyte-derived model (https://www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000123500-Col10a1/subcellular), and the Tgf-β/Smad pathway is also known to disrupt immune surveillance [34]. These observations are consistent with the hypothesis that MI may accelerate BC partly through immune reprogramming and raise the possibility that Col10a1–Tgf-β signaling contributes to this process. Further mechanistic studies are required.

  This study has several limitations. First, the human validation cohort was relatively small, and potential confounders, including age, body mass index, diabetes, other metabolic or cardiovascular risk factors, cardiovascular medications, cancer subtype or stage, and anticancer treatment, may affect extracellular matrix-related gene expression. Larger prospective cohorts with matched clinical characteristics and multivariable adjustment are needed to validate the clinical significance of COL10A1. Second, the MI-induced model does not represent all etiologies of HF, and baseline echocardiography was unavailable. Third, the bioinformatics and machine-learning analyses were exploratory and may be sensitive to platform heterogeneity, the use of nominal P value thresholds, and limited sample size. Fourth, the conditioned medium was not comprehensively characterized, and the experiments did not directly trace the cardiac origin or systemic trafficking of Col10a1 or establish the receptor-level mechanism linking Col10a1 to Tgf-β/Smad activation. Source-tracing, neutralization, and rescue experiments, as well as independent clinical validation, are needed.


## Conclusion
  In summary, our findings suggest that MI-induced HF may promote BC progression through a Col10a1-associated Tgf-β/Smad–EMT pathway. In MI + BC mice, Col10a1 transcript and protein levels were increased in cardiac tissue, and protein levels were increased in the circulation and tumor tissue. Separately, Col10a1 mRNA was not elevated in the human HF PBMC dataset. Because these observations were obtained from different datasets and experimental systems, they do not establish the predominant source of circulating Col10a1 or its direct delivery to tumors. The conditioned-medium experiments further support a contributory role for cardiomyocyte-derived Col10a1 in Tgf-β pathway activation and EMT-related changes. Col10a1 may therefore represent a candidate mediator linking cardiac injury to BC progression, and serves as a potential research target that requires rigorous validation through source-tracing studies, mechanistic rescue experiments, and large-scale prospective clinical cohorts.
