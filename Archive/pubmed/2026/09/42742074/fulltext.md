# Study on the Antidepressant Effect and Mechanism of Water Extract of Semiliquidambar cathayensis (WESC) Based on the GSK3β/NF‐κB Signaling Pathway


## ABSTRACT
  Water extract of Semiliquidambar cathayensis (WESC) exhibits favorable therapeutic effects against depression, yet its pharmacodynamic material basis and mechanism of action remain unclear. In this study, ultra‐high‐performance liquid chromatography coupled with quadrupole time‐of‐flight mass spectrometry (UPLC‐Q‐TOF‐MS) was employed to analyze the chemical constituents of WESC. Network pharmacology and molecular docking were further applied to screen the antidepressant mechanism basis and potential targets of WESC. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)‐induced mouse and PC12 cell models were established as in vivo depressive animal model and in vitro cellular injury model, respectively. Combined with pathological examination and molecular biological techniques, the antidepressant mechanism of WESC was systematically investigated. Results showed that a total of 14 chemical components were identified from WESC. Network pharmacology and molecular docking analyses indicated that GSK3β was one of the key targets, and the active components in WESC possessed strong binding affinities with GSK3β, Axin1, and NF‐κB. Pathological studies demonstrated that WESC significantly alleviated inflammatory infiltration in the mouse prefrontal cortex and hippocampal neuronal damage. Molecular biological analyses revealed that WESC markedly downregulated the expression of proteins related to the GSK3β/NF‐κB signaling pathway in LPS‐stimulated mice and PC12 cells, thereby attenuating neuroinflammation, promoting neural plasticity, and exerting antidepressant effects.


## Introduction
  Depression is a prevalent global public health disorder affecting approximately 322 million individuals, and it is predicted to rank first in the global disease burden by 2030 [1]. It is clinically characterized by persistent low mood, reduced appetite, anhedonia, despair, sleep disturbances, and even suicidal behaviors in severe cases [2]. Despite its high incidence and severe harm, effective treatments for depression remain limited, largely owing to its complicated and incompletely clarified pathophysiological mechanisms. The etiology of depression arises from the interplay of stress, psychological factors, genetic susceptibility, gender differences, and environmental conditions. Neurobiologically, depression is closely linked to monoamine (serotonin, norepinephrine, and dopamine) [3, 4, 5], HPA axis disorder [5], neuroinflammation [6], impaired neural plasticity [7], and intestinal microbiota dysbiosis [8]. Given the multiple cellular participants and complex signaling cascades underlying depression, conventional single‐target therapies often yield unsatisfactory clinical efficacy. Accordingly, traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), with its inherent multi‐component and multi‐target advantages, has become a promising resource for novel antidepressant exploration.

  In TCM, depression belongs to “Yu Bing” (stagnation disease), which was first defined in Yixue Zhengchuan by Yu Tuan of the Ming Dynasty. This syndrome is mainly triggered by emotional disturbance and liver qi stagnation, manifesting as low mood, irritability, chest tightness, hypochondriac pain, and emotional lability. The basic therapeutic strategies are regulating qi flow, resolving stagnation, and soothing the liver. Contemporary TCM holds that depression generally arises from liver stagnation, kidney insufficiency, and malnutrition of the spirit, so treatments center on liver soothing, kidney tonification, and mental tranquilization [9]. Semiliquidambar cathayensis Chang, an arbor from the Hamamelidaceae family, is a distinctive Zhuang medicinal herb native to Guangxi, China. Recorded in Zhuang medicine, this herb is sweet and warm, acting on the liver, kidney, and spleen meridians with effects of tonifying the kidney and soothing the liver, which matches the TCM therapeutic ideas for depression intervention [10]. However, despite its promising traditional applications, the active pharmacodynamic constituents of S. cathayensis and its precise molecular mechanisms in combating depression remain poorly understood. In particular, how the water extract of S. cathayensis (WESC) coordinates anti‐neuroinflammatory responses and promotes neural plasticity through specific key signaling cascades—such as the GSK3β/NF‐κB pathway—has not yet been systematically investigated.

  To fill these research gaps, this study aimed to identify the bioactive ingredients and antidepressant mechanisms of WESC. First, UPLC‐Q‐TOF‐MS was applied to characterize the chemical composition of WESC, followed by network pharmacology and molecular docking to screen pivotal bioactive components and core therapeutic targets. We further verified the antidepressant effect of WESC using LPS‐stimulated mice in vivo and injured PC12 cells in vitro, and clarified its regulatory effects on neuroinflammation and neuronal plasticity via the GSK3β/NF‐κB signaling pathway. This study first establishes the linkage between the components of S. cathayensis and its multi‐target modulation of neuroinflammation and synaptic plasticity. It offers systematic evidence for the antidepressant capacity of this ethnic medicinal herb, lays a theoretical basis for its further development and utilization, and inspires the development of new antidepressants derived from traditional Chinese herbal medicines.


## Materials and Methods

### Animals and Cells
  Rat adrenal pheochromocytoma PC12 cells (RRID: CVCL_0481) (20240517) were purchased from BIOSPECIES (Guangzhou, China). Male C57BL/six mice (8 weeks, 18–22 g) were obtained from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Hunan, China) with a license number: SCXK (Xiang) 2021‐0002. All animals were housed in the Animal Observation Room of Guangxi Academy of Chinese Medicine, with a housing license number: SYXK (Gui) 2022‐0001. The housing conditions were as follows: temperature of 25 ± 2°C, humidity of 60 ± 5%, and a 24 h light–dark cycle. Animals had ad libitum access to food and water daily, with eight mice per cage. All the animal experiments were granted approval by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Guangxi Academy of Chinese Medicine (Approval No. 2024080501).


### Plant Materials, Reagents, and Major Equipment
  The dried roots of S. cathayensis (No. 20211005) were purchased from Nanning Traditional Chinese Medicine Market (Guangxi, China) and authenticated by Assoc. Res. Yunfeng Huang (Guangxi Academy of Chinese Medicine). To prepare the water extract of S. cathayensis (WESC), 1450 g of raw herb was soaked in 6 L of distilled water for 1 h and extracted at 98°C for 1 h. The extraction process was repeated twice with the residue under identical conditions. Combined filtrates were concentrated to 50 mL, yielding 170.53 g of concentrated extract (with an extract density of 3.4 g·mL− 1, corresponding to a raw herb equivalent concentration of 29 g·mL− 1), and stored at −20°C. Fluoxetine hydrochloride was purchased from Eli Lilly Suzhou Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China), and lipopolysaccharide (LPS, L8880) was purchased from Sigma–Aldrich (USA). Formic acid and LC‐MS‐grade acetonitrile were purchased from Aladdin and Thermo Fisher Scientific, respectively. Key equipment utilized in this study included a Dionex Ultimate 3000 UPLC system coupled with a Thermo Q‐Exactive Plus Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), an Agilent C18 column (250×4.6mm,5.0μm), a VisuTrack behavioral analysis system (Xinruan, Shanghai, China), a LightCycler 480 II qPCR system (Roche), and a Tanon‐4600 chemiluminescence imaging system.


### Identification of Components in WESC by UPLC‐Q‐TOF‐MS
  Fifty milligrams of extract was accurately weighed and dissolved in 30% acetonitrile to yield a 15 mg mL− 1 solution. The solution was subjected to ice‐bath sonication for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 µm membrane and loaded into a 1.5 mL amber vial for UPLC‐MS detection.

  Separation was carried out on an Agilent C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5.0 µm) with acetonitrile as mobile phase A and aqueous solution containing 0.1% formic acid plus 0.1% ammonium formate as mobile phase B under linear gradient elution: 5% A (0 min) → 20% A (10 min) → 30% A (20 min) → 85% A (40 min). The flow rate was set at 1 mL min− 1; column temperature 35°C, autosampler temperature 4°C, injection volume 5 µL, and post‐column split ratio 1:1.

  Mass spectrometry analysis was performed with a heated electrospray ionization (HESI) source in negative ion mode. Key parameters: spray voltage −4.0 kV, capillary temperature 350°C, auxiliary gas temperature 200°C, nebulizing gas pressure 350 kPa, auxiliary gas pressure 70 kPa, ion sweep gas pressure 3.5 kPa. Data were acquired via Full MS and data‐dependent MS2 (dd MS2): scan range 70–1000 m/z; resolution of Full MS 70000 and dd MS2 17500; normalized collision energies of 15, 30, and 45 eV; peak apex trigger 2–6 s; dynamic exclusion 10 s.


### Network Pharmacology Analysis
  Using “depression, major depressive disorder” as keywords, depression‐related targets were collected from databases including HPO (https://hpo.jax.org/app/), OMIM (https://www.omim.org/), DrugBank (https://go.drugbank.com/), GeneCards (http://www.genecards.org/), and TCMIP, and duplicates were removed. The intersection targets of WESC and depression were then obtained via jvenn (https://jvenn.toulouse.inra.fr/app/example.html). The intersection targets were imported into STRING (https://cn.string‐db.org) to construct a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network, which was visualized using Cytoscape (v3.10.0) to calculate Degree values. The top six targets with the highest Degree values were identified as hub nodes to construct the “WESC component–intersection target” network.

  Finally, the intersection targets were submitted to Metascape (https://metascape.org/gp) for GO enrichment analysis (top 15 terms each for BP, MF, and CC) and KEGG pathway analysis (top 25 pathways). Visualization was performed using the MicroBioinformatics platform (http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/).


### Molecular Docking
  Molecular docking was performed between the core targets and the active components of WESC. High‐resolution 3D structures of human targets were retrieved from RCSB PDB (https://www.rcsb.org/) and preprocessed (water/ligand removal, hydrogenation, and charge assignment) using PyMOL and AutoDock to generate PDBQT receptor files. Compound 3D structures were obtained from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) or sketched via Chem3D, converted to PDB format, and processed in AutoDock as semi‐flexible ligands in PDBQT format. Binding sites were predicted using AGFR‐1.2, selecting the pocket with the highest AS score for docking. A binding energy below −5 kcal·mol−1 indicated spontaneous binding. Interaction results and hydrogen‐bonded residues were visualized using PyMOL.


### Grouping of Experimental Animals
  After 7 days of adaptive feeding, 48 mice were randomly divided into six groups, namely the control, LPS, fluoxetine, WESC high‐dose, WESC medium‐dose, and WESC low‐dose groups. Except for the Control and LPS groups, the other mice were given intragastric administration. The Fluoxetine group was administered at a dose of 15 mg·kg− 1·d− 1 daily, while the WESC high‐dose group, WESC medium‐dose group, and WESC low‐dose group were administered at doses of 1.40, 0.70, and 0.35 mg·kg− 1·d− 1, respectively, for 30 consecutive days. The administration dose referred to in the study was that of Jun‐Hui et al. [10]. The Control and LPS groups were given the same volume of distilled water by gavage. Except for the Control group, the other mice received the abovementioned intragastric treatment throughout the 30 day period. For model induction during the final phase of the 30 day period, mice in all groups (except the control group) received daily intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of LPS (1 mg·kg−1·d−1) for 3 consecutive days (Days 23–25). During the subsequent 5 days (Days 26–30), LPS was administered every other day to maintain neuroinflammation, while mice underwent behavioral testing starting 12 h after the respective LPS injection. Oral gavage treatments were maintained continuously across the entire 30 day period.


### Behavioral Tests
  Behavioral tests were conducted 12 h after the last LPS injection, with data recorded via VisuTrack (Xinruan, Shanghai, China). To minimize bias, all trials were performed between 08:00 and 12:00 in a fixed location by the same operator and recorder. All six groups were tested simultaneously using an automated system, with individual mice tested in a randomized sequence. The apparatus was thoroughly cleaned with ethanol between trials to remove olfactory cues. All experimenters and analysts were blinded to group allocations.


### Open Field Test
  Mice were placed in the behavioral observation room for 1 h of adaptation first. Then, each mouse was placed in the center of a white background box with dimensions of 40 × 40 × 40 cm, allowing free exploration. The free exploration behavior of the mice within 5 min was recorded, including the movement trajectory of the mice in the observation box and various indicator data.


### Tail Suspension Test
  The tip of the mouse's tail (2–3 cm from the end) was stuck to the inner wall of a black‐background tail suspension box with medical tape, allowing the mouse to hang upside down. The position change and activity level of the mouse were observed, and the immobility time of the mouse (referring to the time when the mouse stopped struggling and had no activity) was recorded. Each mouse was observed for 5 min.


### Forced Swim Test
  A cylindrical glass tank of 20 × 40 cm was prepared, and 30 cm high pure water was added into the tank, with the water temperature controlled at 25 ± 2°C. Mice were gently placed into the tank to swim freely, and care was taken to avoid water aspiration or stress in mice. Timing was started immediately after the mice entered the water, and the immobility time of the mice in the water was recorded (referring to the time when the mice stopped struggling or showed a floating state, with only slight limb movements to keep their heads floating on the water surface). Each mouse was observed for 5 min.


### ELISA Detection of 5‐HT, MAO, IL‐1β, and NF‐κB Levels in Mouse Serum
  Following the completion of behavioral assessments, mice were humanely euthanized via intraperitoneal administration of pentobarbital sodium (0.5 mg·10 g−1) under deep anesthesia. Blood samples were procured through orbital sinus puncture and subsequent cardiac puncture, then subjected to centrifugation at 4°C, 3000 rpm for 30 min. The obtained supernatant was aliquoted and kept at −20°C for subsequent biochemical analyses. 5‐Hydroxytryptamine (5‐HT) (F2443‐A), Monoamine Oxidase (MAO) (F0046‐MA), Interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β) (F2040‐A), and Nuclear Factor‐κB (NF‐κB) (F30253‐A) concentrations were quantified with high precision in strict compliance with the standard operating protocols (SOPs) specified by the respective ELISA kit manufacturers. All ELISA detection kits were purchased from Shanghai Fankewei Biotechnology Co., Ltd.


### RT‐qPCR
  Detection of GSK3β mRNA expression in mouse tissues and PC12 cells. Total RNA was extracted from cells and brain tissues using Trizol reagent obtained from Vazyme (Nanjing, China). Concentration determination was performed using a NanoDrop 2000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). For the reverse transcription reaction, 2 µg of total RNA was mixed with Hiscript RT Super Mix (DC201‐‍01‐‍AA). After the completion of reverse transcription, amplification was carried out using ChamQ Universal SYBR reagent (Vazyme Biotech) and primers purchased from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) under the condition of 60°C–90°C. After each cycle, the fluorescent signal was detected using a LightCycle 480 II, 384 Real‐Time Fluorescent Quantitative PCR Instrument. The specificity of the products was verified by melting curve analysis. All primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China), and their sequences are listed below: Primer name5'→3'GSK3β‐Mouse‐FTTCCTTTGGAATCTGCCATCGSK3β‐Mouse‐RGATCAACTCTGGTGCCCTGTGAPDH‐Mouse‐FGGTTGTCTCCTGCGACTTCAGAPDH‐Mouse‐RTGGTCCAGGGTTTCTTACTCCJohn Wiley & Sons, Ltd.


### Western Blot Analysis
  Following behavioral testing, the mice were anesthetized and subsequently sacrificed. Brain tissue samples were stored in a −80°C refrigerator for later use. For mouse cerebral cortex tissues, homogenization was performed using RIPA lysis buffer purchased from Beyotime. Cell samples did not require homogenization; RIPA buffer was directly added to the cell samples for lysis. The samples were sonicated in an ice bath for 3 min, followed by incubation on ice for 2 min; this cycle was repeated three times. Afterward, the samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min (4°C), and the supernatant was collected. The protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein quantification kit provided by Beyotime (Shanghai, China). After equivalent amounts of protein were separated by SDS‐PAGE gel electrophoresis, they were transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membrane was blocked with a 5% nonfat milk solution (BD‐Media). Subsequently, The membranes were incubated at 4°C overnight with the following primary antibodies respectively: GSK3β antibody (1:1000), phospho‐GSK3β antibody (p‐GSK3β, 1:1000), NF‐κB antibody (1:1000), phospho‐NF‐κB antibody (p‐NF‐κB, 1:1000), NLRP3 (1:2000), IL‐‍1β (1:1000), PSD95 antibody (1:2000), and SNAP25 antibody (1:2000). After incubation, the membranes were washed three times with TBST (5 min each time), then incubated with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. The membranes were washed three times again with TBST and then reacted with Ultra Signal ECL chemiluminescent substrate. Finally, visualization and analysis were performed using a Tanon‐4600 system. The sources of antibodies used in this study are as follows: antibodies against GSK3β (#12456), phospho‐GSK3β (p‐GSK3β, #5558), Axin1 (#2087T), and NF‐κB (#3033), as well as phospho‐NF‐κB (p‐NF‐κB, #3033), were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (USA). Antibodies against β‐catenin (#17565‐1‐AP), PSD95 (#20665‐1‐AP), SNAP25 (#14903‐1‐AP), NLRP3 (#30109‐1‐AP), anti‐Rabbit IgG (#SA00001‐2), and anti‐Mouse IgG (#SA00001‐1) were all purchased from Proteintech (Wuhan, China).


### HE Staining
  Following behavioral testing, three mice were randomly selected from each group, anesthetized, and transcardially perfused with normal saline until the lungs turned white, followed by perfusion with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for tissue fixation. Brain tissues were harvested, post‐fixed in PFA for 48 h, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 5 µm‐thick slices for storage at room temperature. For HE staining, sections were baked at 70°C for 5 h, deparaffinized in xylene, and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. The sections were then stained with hematoxylin (1 h) and eosin (3 min) solutions (Solarbio, Nanjing, China). Finally, the slides were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted with neutral balsam.


### Nissl Staining
  After the brain tissue sections were prepared following the aforementioned treatment, the Nissl staining procedure was performed as follows: Paraffin sections were baked for 5 h, deparaffinized with xylene, and then subjected to dehydration with gradient ethanol. Afterward, the sections were submerged in Nissl staining solution (LEAGENE, 1118A24) for 1 h. followed by mounting with neutral balsam.


### Immunofluorescence Staining
  After the above treatment, the paraffin sections were dewaxed with xylene and then hydrated with gradient ethanol. The immunofluorescence staining procedure was as follows: Permeabilized with 0.03% Triton X‐100 for 30 min; Heat‐induced antigen retrieval in sodium citrate buffer for 5 min (high temperature and high pressure); The samples were blocked with rapid blocking solution at room temperature for 1 h, and then incubated at 4°C for 12 h with anti‐Iba‐1 antibody (1:100), the anti‐Iba‐1 (#10904‐1‐AP) was purchased from Proteintech (Wuhan, China), After washing with PBS, incubated with Coralite 488‐conjugated Goat Anti‐Rabbit IgG (H+L) (1:200) for 2 h at room temperature. Washed three times with PBS (5 min per wash). Stained with DAPI for 20 min.


### Cell Culture and Treatment
  Rat adrenal pheochromocytoma PC12 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (containing 10% fetal bovine serum and penicillin‐streptomycin) at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. The cells were first pretreated with WESC (2 mg/mL) for 24 h, and then subsequently co‐cultured with LPS (2 µg/mL) for 12 h.


### Cytotoxicity Assay (CCK‐8)
  PC12 cells (1 × 104 cells) were seeded into a 96‐well plate and cultured for 24 h. The old medium was discarded, and 100 µL of fresh medium containing WESC (0, 0.25, 0.50, 1.00, 1.50, 2.00, 2.50, 3.00, 3.50, and 4.00 mg·mL−1) was added to each well. Each concentration group had 5 replicate wells. After 36 h of incubation, the medium was discarded, 100 µL of medium containing CCK‐8 reagent (CCK‐8: RPMI 1640 medium = 1:9) was added to each well, followed by incubation for 1 h in the incubator. The optical density (OD) value at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.


### siRNA Transfection and Cell Grouping
  PC12 cells were divided into 8 groups: Control group; NC group; LPS group; siGSK3β group; siGSK3β+WESC group; NC+WESC group; iGSK3β+WESC+LPS; NC+ WESC+LPS group. PC12 cells (1 ‍× 105 cells) were seeded into six‐well plates and cultured until reaching 60% confluency for transfection. Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher, USA) and Opti‐MEM low‐serum medium were used for transfection. Except for the Control and LPS groups, cells were first transfected with siGSK3β or siNC for 8 h, then the medium was replaced with regular medium containing fetal bovine serum for further culture. The transfection efficiency of siRNA was evaluated by RT‐qPCR and Western blot. Both siGSK3β and siNC were provided by RIBOBIO (Guangzhou, China). The sequence of siGSK3β was 5'‐GCTAGGACAACCAATATTT‐3', and the sequence of siNC can be found on the official website of RIBOBIO.


### Statistical Analysis
  Data analysis and calculations were performed using SPSS 27.0, with results presented as mean ± SD. A one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's test was used to analyze the statistical significance of various factors. p values of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The resulting graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism version 9.0.


## Results

### Compounds Identified in Aqueous Extract of WESC via UPLC‐Q‐Orbitrap‐MS
  Through UPLC‐Q‐Orbitrap‐MS analysis, a total of 14 compounds were identified in WESC, involving various components such as organic acids and tricarboxylic acid cycle intermediates, phenolic acids, phenols, glycosides, lichenic acids, isocoumarins, anthraquinone glycosides, and hydroxy fatty acids. All identification results were confirmed by high mass accuracy (< 5 ppm) and determination of characteristic fragmentation pathways. The extracted ion chromatogram is shown in Figure 1, the structures of the identified compounds are shown in Figure 2, and Table 1 summarizes the corresponding chromatographic and mass spectrometric data, including retention time, molecular formula, m/z, and mass error.


### Mechanism of WESC in Treating Depression Predicted by Network Pharmacology
  Based on the chemical structures of the 14 identified compounds, the targets predicted by Swiss Target Prediction and the chemical components of WESC are shown in Figure 3A. A total of 264 targets were predicted for WESC, and 545 targets were collected for depression. There were 47 intersection targets between the two groups. Among them, Compound 4 had no predicted targets, and the predicted targets of Compound 9 had no intersection with depression‐related targets. After excluding these two compounds, the remaining 12 chemical components were selected for subsequent analysis. The network of WESC components and intersection targets was visualized using Cytoscape software, and the degree values were calculated, as shown in Figure 3B. The top six targets with the highest Degree values were ALB, APP, SLC6A4, MAOB, COMT, and GSK3β, respectively. The “WESC components‐intersection targets” network was visualized using Cytoscape software, and the degree value was calculated to identify core targets. The top six core targets with the highest degree values were ALB, APP, SLC6A4, MAOB, COMT, and GSK3β, which are closely related to the occurrence and development of depression.

  GO analysis results (Figure 3C) showed that the key antidepressant targets of WESC are mainly involved in biological processes such as catecholamine and dopamine catabolism, glutamate secretion, and synaptic transmission; associated with cellular components including microglia, dopamine, and glutamate; and related to molecular functions such as G‐coupled protein receptors, neurotransmitters, and microglial differentiation. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis results (Figure 3D) indicated that the antidepressant effect of WESC may be mediated through the Wnt signaling pathway. Collectively, the network pharmacology prediction suggests that WESC may exert its antidepressant effect by regulating multiple core targets, participating in multiple biological processes and molecular functions, and intervening in the Wnt signaling pathway.


### Verification Results of Molecular Docking Between Potential Active Components in WESC and GSK3β, NF‐κB, Axin1 Proteins
  To further verify the targets of the chemical components of S. cathayensis, the potential active components in WESC, including Malic acid (WESC‐1), 2‐Oxoglutaric acid (WESC‐2), Gentisic acid (WESC‐3), 4‐Hydroxy‐9,10‐dioxo‐9,10‐dihydro‐1‐anthracenyl beta‐glucopyranoside (WESC‐13), and 9,12,13‐Trihydroxy‐15‐octadecenoic acid (WESC‐14), were docked with GSK3β, NF‐κB, and Axin1 proteins, respectively. The molecular binding energies are shown in Table 2. When the binding energy was < −5.0 kcal·mol− 1, the component was considered to have a good binding ability with the target. The docking mode is shown in Figure 4.


### Effects of WESC on Locomotor Trajectory and Total Distance Traveled in Mice During the Open Field Test
  As shown in Figure 5, compared with the Control group, mice in the LPS group exhibited obvious anxiety‐like and depression‐like behaviors, including a smaller activity range, decreased time spent in the central zone, and a significantly reduced total distance traveled. Mice in both the low‐ and high‐dose WESC groups showed significantly increased locomotor distance, enlarged activity range, and prolonged time spent in the central zone.


### Effects of WESC on Tail Suspension Test and Forced Swimming Test in Mice
  As shown in Figure 6, compared with the control group, the LPS‐treated mice exhibited significantly prolonged immobility time in both the tail suspension test (TST) and forced swimming test. WESC treatment ameliorated LPS‐induced behavioral abnormalities and markedly reduced the immobility time of mice in these two behavioral tests.


### Effects of WESC on Prefrontal Cortex in LPS‐Treated Mice
  Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining results are shown in Figure 7. Compared with the control group, the prefrontal cortex of LPS‐treated mice exhibited inflammatory infiltration, neuronal loss, pyknosis, and necrosis, characterized by increased microglia, shrunken cell nuclei, and hyperchromatism. Treatment with fluoxetine hydrochloride and WESC significantly ameliorated these pathological changes, restoring normal neuronal structure, reducing neuronal loss, and decreasing the number of microglia.


### Effects of WESC on Iba‐1 Protein Expression in the Prefrontal Cortex
  Results of further Iba‐1 immunofluorescence staining are presented in Figure 8. Compared with the control group, LPS‐treated mice showed more activated microglia, characterized by enlarged cell soma, shortened protrusion branches, and decreased branch numbers. After treatment with fluoxetine hydrochloride and WESC, the soma of microglia returned to normal size, with elongated protrusions and increased branches, exhibiting a typical ramified microglial morphology.


### Effects of WESC on LPS‐Induced Hippocampal Injury in Mice
  Nissl staining results of the coronal sections in the CA3 and DG regions of the mouse hippocampus are shown in Figure 9. In the control group, pyramidal cells were arranged neatly and compactly with distinct stratification, abundant cytoplasm, and clear Nissl bodies. Compared with the control group, LPS induction caused pathological lesions in the hippocampal CA3 and DG regions of mice: in the CA3 region, hippocampal neurons showed soma atrophy, degeneration, and necrosis; in the DG region, neurons exhibited atrophy, pyknosis, and hyperchromatism. In mice pretreated with WESC and fluoxetine hydrochloride, neurons in the hippocampal CA3 and DG regions were arranged orderly with intact structures.


### Effects of WESC on Serum Levels of NF‐κB, IL‐1β, MAO, and 5‐HT in Mice
  As shown in Figure 10, compared with the control group, serum levels of IL‐1β, NF‐κB, and MAO were significantly increased, while serum 5‐HT levels were markedly decreased in LPS‐treated mice. Pretreatment with low‐, medium‐, and high‐dose WESC significantly reduced serum levels of IL‐1β and NF‐κB. In addition, pretreatment with low‐ and high‐dose WESC significantly decreased serum MAO levels in mice.


### Effects of WESC on GSK3β mRNA Expression Levels
  As shown in Figure 11, compared with the control group, the mRNA expression level of GSK3β in brain tissue was significantly upregulated in LPS‐treated mice. Pretreatment with WESC and fluoxetine hydrochloride significantly downregulated the mRNA expression of GSK3β in a dose‐dependent manner.


### Effects of WESC on the Expression of GSK3β/NF‐κB Pathway‐Related Proteins, PSD95 and SNAP25 in the Cortex of LPS‐Treated Mice
  As shown in Figure 12, compared with the Control group, the protein expression levels of PSD95, SNAP25, and β‐catenin in brain tissue were significantly downregulated in LPS‐treated mice, whereas the expression levels of Axin1, p‐GSK3β, and p‐NF‐κB were significantly upregulated. Compared with the LPS group, high‐, medium‐, and low‐dose WESC significantly upregulated β‐catenin expression; high‐dose WESC markedly increased SNAP25 expression; high‐ and medium‐dose WESC significantly elevated PSD95 expression; and high‐, medium‐ and low‐dose WESC as well as fluoxetine hydrochloride significantly decreased the expression of Axin1, p‐GSK3β, and p‐NF‐κB.


### Effects of WESC on Cell Viability of PC12 Cells
  As shown in Figure 13, compared with the control group, the cell viability dropped below 80% when the concentration of WESC reached 2.50 mg·mL− 1, indicating that WESC exhibited cytotoxicity in PC12 cells. Accordingly, 2.00 and 1.50 mg·mL− 1 were selected as the high‐ and low‐doses of WESC for subsequent experiments.


### Effects of WESC on the Expression of GSK3β, NF‐κB, and NLRP3 Proteins in PC12 Cells
  As shown in Figure 14, compared with the control group, the expression levels of GSK3β, NF‐κB, and NLRP3 were significantly upregulated in LPS‐induced PC12 cells. Pretreatment with both high‐ and low‐dose WESC significantly downregulated the protein expression of GSK3β, NF‐κB, and NLRP3 in PC12 cells.


### Effects of GSK3β Knockdown on the Expression of NF‐κB and NLRP3 Proteins
  As shown in Figure 15, compared with the NC group, the expression levels of GSK3β, NF‐κB, and NLRP3 were significantly increased in the LPS group. The expression levels of GSK3β, NF‐κB, and NLRP3 were markedly decreased in the siGSK3β group and the siGSK3β+WESC group. In the NC+WESC group, only NLRP3 expression was significantly downregulated, while there were no significant differences in GSK3β and NF‐κB expression. In addition, the expression levels of GSK3β, NF‐κB, and NLRP3 were significantly reduced in the siGSK3β+WESC+LPS group, and only NLRP3 was significantly decreased in the NC+WESC+LPS group. These results indicated that both WESC treatment alone and GSK3β silencing could inhibit the activation of the NF‐κB/NLRP3 pathway, and the two exhibited a synergistic effect. Compared with the LPS group, the expression levels of GSK3β, NF‐κB, and NLRP3 were significantly decreased in the siGSK3β, siGSK3β+WESC, NC+WESC, siGSK3β+LPS, and NC+WESC+LPS groups, suggesting that WESC alone and GSK3β silencing both blocked LPS‐induced activation of the NF‐κB/NLRP3 pathway in PC12 cells in a synergistic manner.


## Discussion
  LPS is a widely used endotoxin that reliably induces acute central neuroinflammation and depression‐like behaviors in rodents [22]. By closely mimicking the inflammatory pathogenesis of clinical depression, this classical model provides a sensitive in vivo platform for screening antidepressants and elucidating their mechanisms [23]. In this study, three classic behavioral tests—the open field test (OFT), forced swim test (FST), and TST—were employed to systematically evaluate the effects of S. cathayensis (WESC) on depression‐like behaviors in LPS‐induced mice. Behavioral findings demonstrated that WESC significantly alleviated LPS‐induced depression‐like behaviors in the FST, TST, and OFT. Specifically, in the OFT, WESC markedly increased spontaneous locomotor activity and exploratory intent in mice; in the FST and TST, despair‐like behaviors were significantly mitigated, as evidenced by a marked reduction in immobility time. These results suggest that WESC exerts significant antidepressant effects.

  The chemical constituents of WESC were analyzed using ultra‐high‐performance liquid chromatography coupled with quadrupole time‐of‐flight mass spectrometry (UPLC‐Q‐Orbitrap‐MS), leading to the identification of 14 components. These spanned various chemical classes, including organic acids and tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle intermediates, phenolic acids, phenolic aldehydes, glycosides, lichen acids, isocoumarins, anthraquinone glycosides, and hydroxy fatty acids. Previous studies have demonstrated that compounds from various sources—such as malic acid [24], 2‐oxoglutaric acid [25], polydatin [26], azelaic acid [27], and 4‐hydroxy‐9,10‐dioxo‐9,10‐dihydroanthracen‐1‐yl β‐D‐glucopyranoside [28]—exhibit antidepressant activity. This implies that the antidepressant effects of WESC may be mediated via multi‐component, multi‐target, and multi‐pathway synergistic regulation.

  Network pharmacology was utilized to screen the action targets of WESC. The findings identified GSK3β as a key target. Molecular docking analysis revealed that malic acid and 2‐oxoglutaric acid exhibit favorable binding affinities toward GSK3β and NF‐κB, with binding energies lower than −5 kcal·mol− 1, suggesting that these two compounds may serve as the bioactive constituents of WESC. By mediating pathways such as Wnt, NF‐κB, and BDNF/TrkB, GSK3β is involved in maintaining neuronal survival, synaptic structural stability, and normal neurotransmission [29, 30, 31, 32, 33]. It also regulates the balance of central neuroinflammation to prevent excessive inflammatory activation from causing neuronal damage. Hyperactivation of GSK3β significantly triggers central neuroinflammation and amplifies the inflammatory response, thereby exacerbating neuronal injury [34, 35]; thus, it acts as a core driver in the pathogenesis of depression and represents a critical target for antidepressant drug development.

  Nuclear factor‐κB (NF‐κB) serves as a pivotal transcriptional regulator of central neuroinflammation. Upon pathological activation, NF‐κB translocates into the nucleus to induce microglial M1 polarization and trigger the massive transcription of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, including IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α [36]. This sustained inflammatory cascade compromises synaptic plasticity, disrupts blood‐brain barrier integrity, and exacerbates neuronal apoptosis, thereby driving the neuropathological progression of neurodegenerative and psychiatric disorders such as depression [37, 38].

  Western blot and RT‐qPCR analyses revealed that LPS challenge significantly upregulated the expression levels of GSK3β mRNA, p‐GSK3β, p‐NF‐κB, and Axin1 proteins in the mouse cortex. Axin1, serving as the scaffold framework of the Wnt destruction complex, enhances GSK3β activation, which subsequently promotes β‐catenin degradation and triggers the phosphorylation of NF‐κB. Pretreatment with WESC effectively inhibited the upregulation of Axin1 and blocked the phosphorylation‐induced activation of GSK3β and NF‐κB [5, 39]. Consequently, this preserved intracellular β‐catenin levels and elevated the expression of key synaptic plasticity proteins, PSD95 and SNAP25. Furthermore, WESC downregulated serum levels of IL‐1β, NF‐κB, and MAO in mice while restoring 5‐HT content. The inhibition of MAO prevented the degradation of monoamine neurotransmitters and acted synergistically with NF‐κB suppression, thereby alleviating neuroinflammation and restoring neurotransmitter homeostasis. Nevertheless, the regulation of serum inflammatory cytokines and monoamine neurotransmitters by WESC does not directly reflect its modulation of central neuroinflammation and synaptic plasticity. Further, Iba‐1 immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that WESC restored cortical microglia from an activated state with enlarged cell bodies back to a typical ramified resting morphology. Concurrently, HE and Nissl staining confirmed that WESC markedly attenuated LPS‐induced neuronal loss, pyknosis, and necrosis in the prefrontal cortex as well as the CA3/DG regions of the hippocampus [40, 41]. These findings suggest that WESC exerts its antidepressant effects potentially by modulating synaptic plasticity and neuroinflammation through the GSK3β/NF‐κB pathway.

  To further validate the direct signaling cross‐talk within this pathway, in vitro experiments in LPS‐stimulated PC12 cells demonstrated that WESC dose‐dependently inhibited the expression of GSK3β, NF‐κB, and the downstream NLRP3 inflammasome [42, 43]. Crucially, knocking down the GSK3β gene via siRNA—whether used alone or in combination with WESC—effectively blocked LPS‐induced NF‐κB/NLRP3 activation. This confirms that GSK3β is an indispensable upstream driver in NF‐κB‐mediated neuroinflammation.

  Nonetheless, this study possesses several limitations. First, network pharmacology and molecular docking analyses rely on computational predictions rather than direct physical binding validation; subsequent biochemical assays remain necessary for confirmation. Second, although gene knockdown experiments were conducted in vitro, direct pharmacological activation or inhibition experiments have not yet been performed in vivo. To address these limitations, future work will prioritize the screening and isolation of the major bioactive constituents, along with in‐depth evaluation of their binding affinities. Furthermore, the absolute causal role of the GSK3β/NF‐κB pathway, as well as the in vivo efficacy of these specific bioactive compounds, warrants further verification using pathway‐targeted pharmacological agents in animal models.


## Conclusions
  WESC may alleviate neuroinflammation and ameliorate neuronal injury via the GSK3β/NF‐κB pathway, thereby exerting antidepressant effects.
