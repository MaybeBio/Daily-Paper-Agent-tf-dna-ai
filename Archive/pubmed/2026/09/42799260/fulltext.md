# Integrative Transcriptomic and Genetic Analyses Identify CDC20 as a Potential Biomarker Associated with Cell Cycle and Immune-Inflammatory Processes in Psoriasis


## Objective
  To identify psoriasis-associated candidate genes and regulatory features via integrated transcriptomic, immune and genetic analyses, and validate them in an imiquimod-induced psoriasis-like murine model.


## Methods
  Public GEO transcriptomic datasets were integrated after batch-effect correction. We performed differential expression, WGCNA, machine-learning-based feature selection and diagnostic modelling, CIBERSORT immune infiltration estimation, transcription-factor regulation, drug enrichment, colocalization and SMR analyses. In-vivo histological and Western blot validation was conducted in mice.


## Results
  We obtained 767 candidate genes enriched in cell-cycle and immune-inflammatory pathways. The Lasso-XGBoost model (AUC = 0.923) yielded 11 candidate genes that well separate psoriasis patients from healthy controls. Drug enrichment identified Lucanthone. SMR revealed consistent expression-genetic directionality for LYN and IL1RN. Notably, the 11 machine-learning-derived signature genes mainly mark psoriasis disease activity and differ from the SMR-identified genetically supported causal genes LYN and IL1RN. Increased CDC20, CCNB1 and CDK1 protein levels were confirmed in mouse lesional skin.


## Conclusion
  This study suggests that CDC20 may serve as a potential biomarker for cell cycle and immune-inflammatory processes in psoriasis, providing a basis for further mechanistic and translational studies.


## Introduction
  Psoriasis is a common chronic, relapsing, immune-mediated inflammatory skin disease characterized by excessive proliferation and abnormal differentiation of epidermal keratinocytes, along with dermal immune cell infiltration, substantially impairing physical health and quality of life.1,2 Zou et al, based on Global Burden of Disease (GBD) data, reported an increasing burden of psoriasis in China, highlighting its growing public health significance.3 In addition, Rendon and Schäkel4 emphasized that psoriasis imposes a substantial disease burden through its chronic and recurrent course and its adverse effects on patients’ physical and psychological well-being and quality of life. It is widely accepted that its pathogenesis involves genetic susceptibility, immune dysregulation, and environmental factors.5

  Despite substantial progress, several limitations remain in current psoriasis research. Zhou et al6 applied LASSO and SVM-RFE combined with immune infiltration analysis to identify UGGT1, CCNE1, MMP9, and ARHGEF28 as potential diagnostic biomarkers for psoriasis and further validated their expression using RT-qPCR and immunohistochemistry; however, their study mainly focused on biomarker identification and immune-cell associations, without integrating genetic causal inference or systematic transcriptional regulatory analyses. Deng et al7 employed a tree-based machine learning approach to characterize gene regulatory networks in psoriasis and identified key regulators and interferon signaling associated with psoriatic inflammation; nevertheless, genetic causal inference was not incorporated to further evaluate the causal relevance of the identified regulators. Strange et al8 conducted a genome-wide association study and identified several novel psoriasis susceptibility loci involving immune-related genes, including IL28RA, REL, IFIH1, ERAP1, TRAF3IP2, NFKBIA, and TYK2; however, genetic association alone cannot fully establish the functional or causal roles of the implicated genes. More recently, Si et al9 integrated proteome-wide Mendelian randomization, transcriptomic analyses, colocalization, and other multi-omics approaches to prioritize potential therapeutic targets for psoriasis; however, their study primarily focused on therapeutic target discovery rather than the development of an integrated diagnostic framework incorporating transcriptomic feature selection, machine learning, immune infiltration, and transcriptional regulation. Collectively, these studies highlight the need for an integrative framework combining transcriptomic analysis, robust machine learning, immune and regulatory network characterization, genetic causal inference, and therapeutic target exploration.

  Recent advances in high-throughput sequencing and bioinformatics have established a framework for systematic interrogation of psoriasis at the transcriptomic level. Integrative strategies that combine multi-omics, network-based analyses and machine learning enable the dissection of disease mechanisms across layers of expression, regulation, immunity and genetics, thereby enhancing the robustness of gene prioritisation and providing a more rigorous basis for precision diagnosis and targeted intervention.5,10

  Building on this framework, we combined differential expression analysis with weighted gene co-expression network analysis and applied a Lasso–XGBoost ensemble to improve the robustness of gene prioritisation. Immune infiltration profiling, transcriptional regulatory network construction and drug enrichment analyses were integrated to delineate the molecular landscape of psoriasis within the context of the immune microenvironment and its regulatory architecture. Colocalization and SMR analyses were then undertaken to support the genetic causality of candidate genes. Through this multi-layered strategy, we aimed to identify key genes and therapeutic targets with diagnostic and translational relevance, thereby providing a framework for precision diagnosis and personalised treatment in psoriasis. Experimentally, an imiquimod-induced murine model of psoriasis was used to assess histopathological changes by H&E staining and to validate the protein expression levels of CDC20, CCNB1, and CDK1 by Western blotting.


## Materials and Methods
  Psoriasis gene expression datasets were retrieved from the GEO database. After normalisation and correction for batch effects, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was then applied to construct co-expression networks and to define disease-associated core modules, which were intersected with DEGs to yield candidate genes of interest.

  Twelve feature selection algorithms were subsequently implemented, generating 120 integrated machine learning models for gene prioritisation and diagnostic modelling. Immune cell infiltration analysis was undertaken to characterise the immune microenvironment, alongside the construction of transcription factor regulatory and drug–gene interaction networks. Colocalization and SMR analyses were further employed to delineate the genomic context of key genes and to support their causal association with psoriasis phenotypes. ### Data Sources and Preprocessing
  Transcriptomic datasets for psoriasis were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo). GSE13355, GSE6710 and GSE14905 were used as the training set, while GSE30999, GSE106087 and GSE166388 served as independent validation cohorts. Batch effects were corrected using surrogate variable analysis (SVA) in combination with the ComBat algorithm, and principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to assess normalisation and batch correction, ensuring comparability across datasets.

  For colocalization analysis, eQTL data for core genes were retrieved from the GWAS database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/), and psoriasis GWAS summary statistics were obtained from the FinnGen consortium (https://www.finngen.fi/en/access_results).


### Identification of Differentially Expressed Genes and Construction of the Co-Expression Network
  Differential expression analysis was performed on the training set using the limma package, with thresholds set at |log2FC| > 0.585 (1.5-fold change) and adjusted P < 0.05 to define DEGs between psoriasis and normal tissues. Results were visualised using volcano plots and heatmaps generated with the ggplot2 package.

  Gene co-expression networks were constructed using the WGCNA package, followed by phenotype association analysis. Genes with low variability (standard deviation ≤ 0.5) were excluded. A soft-thresholding power was selected to achieve a scale-free topology fit index of R2 ≥ 0.8. The minimum module size was set to 60 genes, and modules with a correlation coefficient ≥ 0.75 were merged.


### Functional and Pathway Enrichment Analysis
  Intersecting genes derived from DEGs and WGCNA core modules were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) annotation and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis using the clusterProfiler package within the Bioconductor framework. Significantly enriched terms were defined at P < 0.05 and visualised using bubble plots, bar charts and pathway network diagrams.

  The intersecting genes were then uploaded to the STRING database (restricted to Homo sapiens) with a confidence score ≥ 0.4 to obtain protein–protein interaction (PPI) data. The resulting PPI network was imported into Cytoscape (version 3.10.4) for visualisation, and the top 20 ranked genes were selected as key candidates.


### Machine Learning Model Construction and Key Gene Selection
  Based on the training transcriptomic data, 12 classical algorithms, including Lasso, XGBoost and random forest (RF), were incorporated and combined to generate 120 classification models. Ten-fold cross-validation was implemented for Lasso via cv.glmnet within the training set to select the optimal λ (lambda.min). For XGBoost, five-fold internal cross-validation was applied to determine the optimal number of boosting rounds (nround), with fixed hyper-parameters: max.depth = 2, eta = 1. For random forest, ntree = 1000 and nodesize = 5 were used. For GBM, ten-fold cross-validation was performed, with fixed hyper-parameters: interaction.depth = 3, n.minobsinnode = 10, shrinkage = 0.001. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the curve (AUC), accuracy and F1 score, and the optimal model was selected accordingly.

  Feature genes derived from the optimal model were ranked according to statistical significance (P values), and the top 11 genes were identified as key candidates. Their classification performance was further validated in independent external datasets, with discriminatory efficacy in both the training and validation cohorts assessed by AUC.


### Immune Cell Infiltration and Correlation Analysis of Key Genes
  Immune cell infiltration was estimated using the CIBERSORT deconvolution algorithm. Visualisation and statistical analyses were performed using the reshape2, ggpubr and corrplot packages. Stacked bar plots were generated to display the relative proportions of immune cells in control and disease groups, while intergroup differences were assessed using boxplots and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Spearman correlation analysis based on the disease cohort was used to construct an immune cell interaction heatmap, thereby delineating co-infiltration patterns.

  To further characterise the relationship between key genes and the immune microenvironment, Spearman correlation analysis was conducted by integrating gene expression profiles with immune infiltration data. Data processing and statistical testing were performed using limma, reshape2, ggpubr and ggExtra, with correlation coefficients and P values calculated accordingly. The results were visualised using lollipop plots, in which node size represented correlation strength and node colour denoted statistical significance, providing a systematic view of the association between key genes and immune cell infiltration.


### Regulatory Network Construction and Drug Enrichment Analysis
  Human transcription factor–target gene regulatory interactions were obtained from the TRRUST database. Core genes identified from the optimal model were used as input to construct a transcription factor–gene regulatory network using Cytoscape (https://cytoscape.org/).

  Drug–gene interaction data were retrieved from the DSigDB database, and drug enrichment analysis was performed on the core gene set using the clusterProfiler package in R. Significantly enriched candidate drugs were identified using thresholds of P < 0.05 and adjusted P < 0.05. Enrichment results were visualised using bar plots and bubble plots generated with ggplot2, illustrating both statistical significance and gene enrichment patterns.


### Genetic Colocalization Analysis
  Genetic colocalization analysis between eQTL signals of core genes and psoriasis GWAS data was performed using the coloc package to assess the sharing of causal variants. Prior to analysis, SNP matching, quality control and locus filtering were conducted for both eQTL and GWAS datasets, with a ±100 kb window around the target gene defined as the region of interest. Posterior probabilities (PP) were estimated using the coloc.abf algorithm, with PP.H4 > 0.5 indicating significant colocalization between eQTL and disease-associated signals.

  Colocalization results were further visualised using the locuscomparer package, illustrating association strength and local linkage disequilibrium structure.


### Mendelian Randomization (SMR) Analysis
  Psoriasis GWAS summary statistics were obtained from the FinnGen R12 release. Data quality control and harmonisation were performed in R, extracting key information including SNPs, alleles, effect sizes, standard errors and P values. SMR results were filtered using thresholds of PSMR < 0.05 for significance and PHEIDI > 0.05 to exclude horizontal pleiotropy, thereby identifying robust causal genes.

  Results were visualised using the CMplot package, with Manhattan and circular plots displaying genome-wide association signals as −log10(P), and significant genes highlighted to illustrate the distribution of association signals across the genome.


### Integrated Candidate Gene Selection and Visualisation
  Differentially expressed genes, core genes derived from the optimal model, and SMR-identified causal genes were integrated, and their overlap was visualised using the ggvenn package in R. Gene sets were stratified into upregulated and downregulated groups according to logFC direction and the sign of the SMR effect estimate (bSMR). Venn diagrams were used to delineate the overlap between differential expression and causal association, and robust candidate genes were defined as those showing concordant expression patterns and causal effects.

  Further visualisation of candidate genes was performed to characterise their genetic architecture. Gene-centric plots were generated using SMRLocusPlot to illustrate colocalization patterns between eQTL and GWAS signals, while SMREffectPlot was used to depict the magnitude and direction of causal effects, enabling systematic interpretation of the genetic regulatory mechanisms underlying candidate genes.


### Establishment of the Psoriasis-Like Murine Model
  Animal experiments were conducted using male BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old, 30–35 g), obtained from Jiangsu Qinglongshan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). Psoriasis-like skin inflammation was induced by topical application of 62.5 mg of 5% imiquimod (IMQ) cream (Tianfang Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China), as previously described.11 Mice were randomly assigned to two groups (n = 3 mice per group): a control group (vehicle, petrolatum) and a psoriasis-like model group (IMQ). The intervention period lasted 1 week.

  Body weight, affected skin area and Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) scores were recorded every other day. A modified PASI scoring system was used to evaluate disease severity, with erythema, induration and scaling each graded on a scale from 0 (none) to 4 (most severe).12

  At the end of the intervention, mice were humanely euthanized. Mice were deeply anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 20% urethane (1.5 g/kg), followed by immediate cervical dislocation to ensure rapid, complete, and irreversible euthanasia. Death was confirmed by the permanent cessation of spontaneous respiration and heartbeat. Lesional skin samples were then carefully collected for subsequent histological and molecular analyses, including hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and Western blot (WB) analysis. All animal experimental procedures were approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Qinghai University (Approval No. FYXC-2024-13). All experiments were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and the national regulations for the care and use of laboratory animals in China. Every effort was made throughout the study to minimize animal suffering and reduce the number of animals used.


### Histological Analysis
  Skin samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm thickness. Sections were stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to assess histopathological changes, and epidermal thickness was measured accordingly.


### Western Blot Analysis
  Total protein was extracted from murine skin tissues, and protein concentration was determined using the BCA assay. Proteins were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes, followed by blocking with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CDC20 (UpingBio, YP-Ab-16676), CCNB1 (UpingBio, YP-Ab-16722), CDK1 (UpingBio, YP-Ab-16696) and β-actin (Proteintech, Cat. No. 66009-1-Ig), followed by incubation with appropriate secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL), and relative protein expression levels were quantified using ImageJ, normalised to β-actin. Western blot analyses were performed using independent skin samples from three mice per group, with each mouse serving as an independent biological replicate (n = 3 biological replicates per group).

  To evaluate the discriminatory performance of each key gene, ROC curve analysis was performed. All candidate genes exhibited relatively high AUC values in distinguishing psoriasis patients from healthy controls within the current dataset, with AUCs exceeding 0.88. ## Discussion
  In the present study, we applied an integrated analytical framework combining differential expression analysis, weighted gene co-expression network analysis, machine learning, immune infiltration profiling, transcriptional regulatory analysis, drug enrichment, colocalization, and summary data-based Mendelian randomization to characterize the molecular features of psoriasis. Rather than supporting a single-gene model, our findings converge on a broader cell cycle–immune inflammation signature, in which proliferative keratinocyte programmes and inflammatory immune components appear to be closely coupled. This interpretation is consistent with the established pathological features of psoriasis, including epidermal hyperplasia, abnormal differentiation, and sustained immune activation.

  A total of 950 differentially expressed genes were identified, and integration with the psoriasis-associated MEbrown module yielded 767 candidate genes. Functional enrichment analysis showed that these genes were mainly involved in cell cycle regulation, cytokine activity, chemokine-related functions, and immune-inflammatory pathways. Protein–protein interaction analysis further highlighted hub genes such as CCNA2, CCNB1, CDC20, and CDK1, suggesting that aberrant activation of cell cycle-related machinery may be a prominent transcriptional feature of psoriatic lesions. These findings support the view that excessive keratinocyte proliferation in psoriasis is not an isolated event, but part of a broader pathogenic programme linked to inflammatory signalling and tissue remodelling.

  Among the prioritised genes, the Lasso–XGBoost model identified 11 candidate markers with strong discriminatory performance across datasets, with CDC20 showing the highest AUC value. However, these genes may be better interpreted as components of a proliferative disease-associated expression pattern rather than as standalone disease-specific drivers.13–15 In this context, CDC20, CCNB1, and CDK1 likely reflect enhanced mitotic activity and dysregulated epidermal turnover, both of which are central to psoriatic lesion development.16,17 The experimental validation performed in the imiquimod-induced mouse model further supported this interpretation, as the upregulation of these proteins was accompanied by typical histopathological changes, including epidermal hyperplasia and inflammatory infiltration. Together, these results suggest that cell cycle-related genes may serve as useful candidates for biomarker prioritization, while their precise mechanistic roles require further investigation.

  Immune infiltration analysis provided an additional layer of evidence for the inflammatory context of psoriasis. Compared with controls, psoriatic lesions showed increased infiltration of several pro-inflammatory immune cell populations, including activated CD4 memory T cells, follicular helper T cells, γδ T cells, M0/M1 macrophages, activated dendritic cells, and neutrophils,18,19 whereas resting dendritic cells and resting mast cells were relatively reduced.20,21 These findings are consistent with the recognised contribution of both innate and adaptive immune responses to psoriasis pathogenesis. Notably, the prioritised genes showed strong positive correlations with activated dendritic cells and strong negative correlations with resting mast cells, suggesting that the transcriptional programme represented by these genes may be linked to immune activation status within lesional skin. This pattern supports a model in which abnormal keratinocyte proliferation and immune-cell-mediated inflammation are biologically intertwined rather than independent processes.22,23

  The transcription factor regulatory network further reinforced this interpretation. Several of the key genes were linked to transcription factors known to participate in cell proliferation, inflammatory signalling, and stress responses.24,25 From a systems perspective, this suggests that the observed transcriptional changes in psoriasis may arise from coordinated upstream regulatory programmes rather than isolated gene-level abnormalities. Such an interpretation is more consistent with the complex and relapsing nature of psoriasis, in which epidermal, immune, and genetic factors interact dynamically.22,26 Accordingly, the prioritised genes should be viewed as nodes within a broader regulatory network that reflects the disease state.

  Drug enrichment analysis identified several compounds potentially connected to the core gene set, with Lucanthone emerging as the top-ranked candidate in silico. Given its known associations with DNA damage response, proliferation control, and inflammatory signalling in other biological contexts, Lucanthone may represent a hypothesis-generating candidate for future evaluation in psoriasis.27–29 However, this result should be interpreted cautiously. In the absence of direct pharmacological validation in psoriasis-related experimental systems, the current findings do not support therapeutic conclusions, but rather provide a starting point for future drug repositioning studies.

  The genetic analyses added another dimension to the interpretation of the results. Colocalization analysis indicated that the prioritised genes were associated with psoriasis-relevant eQTL–GWAS signals, while SMR analysis identified several genes, including LYN and IL1RN, with consistent directionality between gene expression and genetically predicted effects. Importantly, these genes were not identical to the top-ranked transcriptomic markers highlighted by the machine learning model. This distinction suggests that genes with strong discriminatory performance in transcriptomic datasets are not necessarily those with the strongest genetically supported associations.30–32 Therefore, the present work highlights two complementary but non-equivalent layers of prioritization: one based on disease-state expression patterns and another based on genetic regulatory support. This distinction may be important for future studies aiming to separate biomarkers of disease activity from genes more directly implicated in inherited susceptibility or regulatory architecture.33–35

  Compared with studies relying on a single analytical strategy, the present work used a multi-step integrative design to connect transcriptomic alterations with immune characteristics and genetic signals. This approach improves robustness at the level of candidate prioritization and provides a broader view of psoriasis-associated molecular features. At the same time, the findings should be interpreted within the constraints of the study design.

  The analyses were based primarily on public datasets, which may retain residual inter-cohort heterogeneity despite batch-effect correction. Machine-learning models also carry intrinsic over-fitting risks even with cross-validation. The short-term imiquimod-induced murine model only enables histological and protein-level expression assessment and cannot recapitulate the chronic nature of human psoriasis; the observed upregulation of cell-cycle-related proteins including CDC20 represents merely correlative evidence and does not establish their direct functional contribution to disease pathogenesis. Similarly, drug-enrichment outputs remain purely in-silico exploratory predictions without supporting wet-lab experimental validation, and genetic analyses provide only supportive evidence for candidate prioritization rather than definitive mechanistic proof. Moreover, the absence of a large independent human validation cohort limits the immediate translational applicability of the identified candidates. In addition, samples from other inflammatory skin diseases were not included in this study; therefore, the disease specificity of these genes cannot be confirmed.


## Conclusion
  In summary, this study identifies a psoriasis-associated cell cycle–immune inflammation signature and several candidate genes. CDC20, CCNB1, and CDK1 may serve as candidate disease-activity biomarkers, whereas the SMR-supported LYN and IL1RN may reflect genetic susceptibility to psoriasis. CDC20 should be considered a candidate hub biomarker rather than a causal driver. These findings provide potential biomarkers and directions for further mechanistic and therapeutic studies in psoriasis.
