# DeepPNI: a language- and graph-based model for mutation-driven protein–nucleic acid binding energetics


## Abstract
  Protein–nucleic acid interactions (PNIs) are central to fundamental biological processes, and mutations can disrupt these interactions by altering local structural features and binding free energy. Here, we present DeepPNI, a deep learning regression model that integrates sequence- and structure-based features to estimate mutation-induced changes in binding free energy in protein–nucleic acid complexes. The model was developed using a comprehensive dataset of 1754 mutations spanning protein–DNA and protein–RNA complexes, representing one of the largest curated datasets for PNI binding free energy prediction. Structural features were encoded using an edge-aware relational graph convolutional network, while sequence features were represented using the Evolutionary Scale Modeling 2 protein language model. Despite the increased dataset size and heterogeneity, DeepPNI achieved an overall Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.76 in five-fold cross-validation. Consistent performance was observed across protein–DNA and protein–RNA subsets, datasets grouped by experimental temperature, and external blind test datasets, suggesting robustness against dataset heterogeneity. DeepPNI is freely available as a web server at https://research.iitbhilai.ac.in/molinfo/deeppni.


## Introduction
  Cellular processes are maintained through communication among biomolecules. Protein–nucleic acid interactions (PNIs) regulate key biological functions, including DNA replication, repair, recombination, gene expression, and translation [1, 2]. The affinity between proteins and nucleic acids in a complex is governed by intermolecular forces, physicochemical properties, and structural features, which can be disrupted by missense mutations in proteins [3, 4]. Such mutations are associated with several pathogenic conditions [5–13]. Alterations in DNA binding protein, such as the p53 tumour suppressor can impair sequence-specific DNA recognition and can be associated with disease, such as cancer development [14, 15]. Similarly, mutations affecting RNA-binding proteins, such as FUS protein and TDP-43, have been implicated in neurodegenerative disorders, including amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and frontotemporal dementia [16, 17].

  The rapid growth of genomic data demands high-throughput analysis of mutations [18]. Experimental methods are conventionally used to assess mutation-induced changes in binding free energy [19–23]; however, these approaches are costly and not readily scalable for large-scale screening [24]. Recent high-throughput experimental techniques partially address this challenge, but their applicability remains restricted [25–28]. In this context, computational approaches provide an effective alternative for screening large mutation datasets, offering a practical balance between efficiency and predictive accuracy. Subsequent in vitro evaluation can then be focused on a smaller set of pre-screened mutations predicted to be potentially harmful. Thus, in silico mutation analysis is advantageous for estimating the pathogenic potential of emerging mutations.

  Several methods have been proposed to predict the effects of mutations on PNIs. DeePNAP [29] and MutPNI [30] are sequence-based approaches, with MutPNI additionally using FoldX-derived energy terms [31]. Structure-based methods include SAMPDI-3D [32], which uses gradient boosting, and PremPDI [33], which applies multiple linear regression on physics-based energy features. mCSM-NA [34] and mmCSM-NA [35] both use graph-based structural signatures with Gaussian process and extremely randomized trees algorithms, respectively, with mmCSM-NA further incorporating protein dynamics and interaction networks. Despite decent accuracy, existing tools are limited by relatively small datasets, and most of these do not fully exploit recent advances in natural language processing and graph neural networks. In a nutshell, these approaches often rely on relatively small and separate protein–DNA and protein–RNA datasets, and many are primarily based on pre-2020 machine learning methods.

  We introduce DeepPNI, a deep learning tool designed to estimate changes in binding free energy upon amino acid mutation (ΔΔG) in protein–nucleic acid complexes. The model was trained on a comprehensive dataset of 1754 mutations across protein–DNA and protein–RNA complexes compiled from the NABE database [36], representing one of the largest curated datasets for PNI binding free energy prediction. Structural features of the complexes were encoded using a 3D atomic relational graph convolutional network (RGCN) [37], which was integrated with sequence representations derived from the Evolutionary Scale Modeling 2 (ESM-2) protein language model [38]. DeepPNI was evaluated using complex-based five-fold cross-validation (five-fold CV), external validation, and multiple dataset splits, including protein–DNA and protein–RNA subsets as well as temperature-stratified datasets. The model stands out for its accuracy and robustness, providing a careful solution for pathogenicity assessment through thermodynamically guided analysis of protein–nucleic acid complex stability. With predictive and high-throughput screening features, DeepPNI offers a trustworthy platform for studying mutation effects and supporting the development of therapeutic strategies.


## Materials and methods

### Overview of the DeepPNI approach
  Predicting the binding free energy of protein–nucleic acid complexes is challenging because it depends on both sequence composition and 3D-conformations. Binding free energy reflects the stability of the complex, and even minor mutations can substantially alter its function [39, 40]. Considering only structural features would neglect sequence information, and considering only sequence features would omit the structural context. This limitation can be addressed by combining both sequence and structural information from the complexes.

  This study aims to quantitatively predict how single amino acid mutations at protein–nucleic acid interfaces affect their binding free energy. The effect of a mutation is measured by the binding free energy change (ΔΔG), defined as the difference in binding free energy between the mutant and the wild-type complexes:

  A positive ΔΔG indicates a destabilizing mutation, whereas a negative value represents a stabilizing mutation. Figure 1 shows the overall model architecture used in this study. The ΔΔG estimation was formulated as a supervised regression problem, where each mutation was represented by a combination of structural and sequence features. Specifically, a local atomic graph centered on the mutation site was encoded using a graph convolutional network to capture spatial and physicochemical information, while an ESM-based protein language model embedding was used to describe the sequence context. The concatenated representation was then used by DeepPNI to predict the experimentally measured ΔΔG.


### Preparation of training and validation datasets
  Experimentally measured changes in binding free energy upon amino acid mutation were compiled from the NABE database [36]. This database contains over 2500 mutations across >400 protein–DNA and protein–RNA complexes. Duplicate entries were averaged, resulting in 1951 unique mutations categorized into 1328 protein–DNA, 620 protein–RNA, and 3 protein–DNA–RNA mutations (Supplementary Fig. S1 and Table 1).

  The datasets were refined using Z-scores, computed as the difference between a specific data point and the dataset mean, divided by the dataset standard deviation. A Z-score threshold of 1.5 was used to remove extreme values from the raw data [41, 42], resulting in refined datasets of 1754, 1197, and 558 mutations (Fig. 2A) in protein–nucleic acid (PN), protein–DNA (PD), and protein–RNA (PR) complexes, respectively. DeepPNI was developed using the comprehensive PN dataset of 1754 data points (Table 1), which represents one of the largest curated datasets for PNI binding free energy prediction. The PD and PR datasets were used to assess model generalizability.

  In addition, three datasets were constructed based on experimental temperature. The 1951 mutations were grouped into 747, 757, and 447 mutations evaluated at 298 K, above 298 K, and below 298 K, respectively (Supplementary Table S1). Outliers were removed using a Z-score threshold of 1.5, resulting in refined datasets of 665 mutations for 298 K, 697 mutations above 298 K, and 398 mutations below 298 K.

  External validation was performed using blind datasets compiled from the ProNAB database [43], which contains experimental data on mutation effects in PNIs. Given the high level of heterogeneity among mutation dataset, the data were segregated based on the experimental technique employed used for analysis, including ITC, FB, FP, GS, as detailed in Supplementary Table S2 and Supplementary Section S1. Additionally, two existing benchmark datasets, MPR311 [30] and PremPDI [33], were used for validation and further assessment of DeepPNI generalizability.


### Generation of sequence-based features
  The core framework of DeepPNI relies on both sequence- and structure-level information from protein–nucleic acid complexes. Protein sequence contexts surrounding each mutation were extracted from the corresponding Protein Data Bank (PDB) structure [44]. The wild-type residue and its positional index were parsed and validated against the structural chain to ensure consistency between sequence annotation and 3D coordinates. This generated a continuous amino acid sequence for each chain, where missing residues were represented by the padding character ‘X’. A sequence window of ±200 residues centered on the mutation site was then used to capture the local mutation environment.

  Each extracted sequence was embedded using the ESM-2 protein language model [38], generating 1280-dimension representation. Mean pooling was applied across the sequence length to obtain fixed-length numerical representations of the local mutation context. These embeddings encode higher order structural and evolutionary features learned from large-scale protein corpora, offering sequence-level information for integration with structural graph representations in ΔΔG regression modelling.

To define the local environment for each mutation, atoms within 10 Å of the geometric center of the wild-type residue undergoing mutation (hereafter referred to as ‘target residue’) were included. The 10 Å cutoff was selected to capture the local interface environment around the mutated residue, including direct contacts with the nucleic acid partner and nearby protein residues that may contribute to mutation-induced changes in binding free energy. This radius covers both immediate interaction partners and second-shell structural context while avoiding inclusion of distant atoms that may add noise and increase graph size.   This dual representation was processed through a multilayer perceptron (MLP) architecture comprising three hidden layers with dimensions of 512, 256, 64 (Fig. 1B). At each hidden layer, batch normalization was applied to stabilize gradients and accelerate convergence, followed by a ReLU activation function and dropout with a probability of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $p = 0.3$\end{document} to prevent overfitting. The final layer produced a scalar output corresponding to the predicted ΔΔG in kcal/mol. No activation function was applied to the output layer, allowing predictions across the full real-number range.

### Implementation
  The model was implemented in PyTorch 2.0 + using PyTorch Geometric for graph operation. Training was performed on an NVIDIA RTX A5000 GPU with CUDA 11.6, supported by an Intel Xeon Gold 6226R CPU and 251 GB system RAM. Reproducibility was ensured by fixing the random seed (SEED = 42) across all stochastic operations.


### DeepPNI web server
  To make DeepPNI publicly accessible, we developed a web server available at https://research.iitbhilai.ac.in/molinfo/deeppni. The server was built using HTML5, CSS3, and jQuery 3.6.0 for the frontend, with a Flask-based Python backend serving the REST API. DeepPNI accepts a CSV file with three columns: mutation, PDB_ID, and chain. The mutation should be provided in the format W596M, where W is the wild-type residue, 596 is the residue position, and M is the mutant residue. The server accepts CSV-formatted files up to 5 KB. A sample CSV file is available in the ‘Tutorial’ section to guide new users.

  Each job runs in the background through two stages: graph construction and ESM-2 embedding extraction, followed by ΔΔG prediction using DeepPNI model. Predicted ΔΔG values are displayed in the results table and can be downloaded as a CSV file. Each result entry also includes an interactive 3D-structure viewer option powered by NGL viewer [47]. This allows users to visualize mutations with stabilizing or destabilizing effects in the 3D protein–nucleic acid complexes. By default, proteins and nucleic acids are shown in sage green and navy blue, respectively. Stabilizing and destabilizing mutation sites are coloured as blue and red, respectively. The stepwise procedure for using this server is explained in Supplementary Section S2 and Supplementary Figs S2–S4.


### Statistical analyses
  Dataset outliers were identified and removed using a Z-score threshold of 1.5, calculated as the deviation of each ΔΔG value from the dataset mean divided by the dataset standard deviation. Model performance was assessed using five-fold CV, in which the dataset was randomly partitioned into folds with 80% of the data used for training and 20% held out for testing in each iteration. The reported cross-validation metrics represent the mean ± standard deviation across folds. Predictive performance was quantified using the PCC to assess linear correlation between experimental and predicted ΔΔG values, MAE, and RMSE.


## Results and discussion

### Five-fold CV performance of DeepPNI
  DeepPNI was developed using the comprehensive PN dataset of 1754 mutations, with 80% of the data used for training and 20% for testing. Accordingly, 1404 mutations were used for training and 350 mutations used to evaluate model performance. In five-fold CV of the training set, DeepPNI achieved an average PCC of 0.76 ± 0.03, MAE of 0.52 ± 0.03, and RMSE of 0.65 ± 0.03 (Supplementary Table S4). Figure 2B shows a strong correlation between the experimental and average predicted ΔΔG values for the test sets, with most data points lying close to the diagonal line. The low PCC standard deviation of 0.03 across the five folds indicates consistent model performance in estimating ΔΔG. Notably, one of the five folds showed the highest performance, with a PCC of 0.80, MAE of 0.54, and RMSE of 0.66.

  These results show that DeepPNI achieves high predictive performance while being trained on one of the largest and most diverse curated datasets for PNI binding free energy prediction. This is important because previous ΔΔG prediction methods were developed using smaller mutation datasets or separate datasets for protein–DNA and protein–RNA complexes, which can limit their applicability across different PNI systems [29, 30, 32–34]. In contrast, DeepPNI was trained on a unified PN dataset containing both protein–DNA and protein–RNA complexes, allowing the model to learn broader structure-sequence patterns associated with mutation-induced binding free energy changes. To the best of our knowledge, DeepPNI achieves one of the highest reported PCC using five-fold or higher cross-validation among available PNI ΔΔG prediction methods, despite being trained and evaluated on a larger and more heterogeneous dataset. This supports the accuracy and generalizability of the model for predicting mutation effects across diverse protein–nucleic acid complexes.


### Cross-validation performance on protein–DNA and protein–RNA datasets
  To assess model performance on individual protein–DNA and protein–RNA datasets, we performed five-fold CV separately on the PD and PR datasets (Table 1). The PD dataset comprised 1197 mutations, whereas the PR dataset comprised 558 mutations. Since these two datasets were unevenly represented in the full PN dataset, separate evaluation was necessary to determine whether model performance was affected by dataset distribution. An 8:2 training-to-test ratio was used consistently.

  DeepPNI achieved an average PCC of 0.75 for both the PD and PR datasets (Supplementary Table S4 and Fig. 2E). The corresponding MAE values were 0.53 and 0.50, and the RMSE values were 0.67 and 0.64 for the PD and PR datasets, respectively. Figure 2C and d show the distribution of experimental and average predicted ΔΔG values, with most data points lying close to the diagonal. Notably, despite the PR dataset containing approximately half the number of mutations in the PD dataset, DeepPNI maintained comparable predictive accuracy for both datasets. This indicates that the model is not biased toward the more represented protein–DNA class.

  The PCC values obtained for the separate PD and PR datasets were also comparable to that achieved for the full PN dataset (0.76), further supporting the generalizability of DeepPNI across different PNI systems. One of the five runs showed the highest PCC values of 0.79 and 0.85 for the PD and PR datasets, respectively (Supplementary Table S4 and Fig. 2F), comparable to or higher than the performance observed for the full PN dataset. These findings indicate that DeepPNI retains high predictive accuracy across both protein–DNA and protein–RNA complexes, with minimal dependence on dataset size or class distribution.


### Performance across temperature-based mutation groups
  Temperature can influence experimentally measured binding free energy changes upon mutation [48, 49]. Since the experimental temperatures in our dataset ranged from 4 K to 363 K, we evaluated whether DeepPNI could maintain predictive accuracy across temperature-dependent datasets. The dataset was divided into three groups: mutations measured at 298 K, above 298 K (>298 K), and below 298 K (<298 K), containing 665, 697, and 398 data points, respectively.

  Five-fold CV was performed separately on each temperature-dependent dataset. DeepPNI achieved high average PCC values of 0.77 ± 0.06, 0.82 ± 0.06, and 0.77 ± 0.04 for 298 K, >298 K, and <298 K datasets, respectively (Fig. 2E). The corresponding PCC, MAE, and RMSE values are summarized in Fig. 2F and Supplementary Table S4. The consistently high PCC values across all three groups indicate that DeepPNI maintains predictive accuracy over a broad experimental temperature range. Although the highest PCC was obtained for the >298 K dataset, the 298 K dataset showed lower MAE and RMSE than both the >298 K and <298 K datasets. These results suggest that DeepPNI maintains stable performance across temperature-dependent dataset groups and can robustly predict ΔΔG across variable experimental conditions.


### Feature contribution within the graph representation
  As described above, structural information from 3D protein–nucleic acid complexes was encoded using an edge-aware RGCN comprising three node types and six edge types. To assess the relative contribution of each node type in the RGCN component, we computed the L2 norm of the learned embedding vectors from the final RGCN layer on PN dataset (Supplementary Section S3). The L2 norm shows a measure of the magnitude of the learned representation, with larger values indicating stronger contribution to the graph-level embedding.

  The nodes belonging to nucleic acid atoms, wild-type target residue atoms, and other protein residue atoms showed L2 norm values of 0.82, 0.72, and 0.31, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S5A). These results indicate that the graph representation is primarily influenced by the target residue undergoing mutation and the interacting nucleic acid atoms. In particular, the high contribution from nucleic acid atoms suggests that the model captures the interaction environment between the mutated residue and its nucleic acid binding partner, which is directly relevant for ΔΔG prediction.

  Similarly, the contribution of the six edge types was evaluated by averaging the learned edge weights across the four RGCN layers. The average edge weights were comparable across all edge types, ranging from 0.50 to 0.53 (Supplementary Fig. S5B). The highest edge weight was observed for interactions between the target residue and nucleic acid atoms (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${{e}_{wn}} = 0.53$\end{document}). This observation is consistent with the node-level analysis and suggests that the interaction between the mutation site and nucleic acid partner is an important structural component in the graph representation used for ΔΔG estimation.

  DeepPNI provides a data-driven method for estimating the physically meaningful quantity ΔΔG and prioritizing mutations that may alter protein–nucleic acid binding stability. While it is not intended to replace detailed mechanistic energy-decomposition analyses, the edge-aware RGCN component provides a route to examine model-level feature contributions from the learned graph representation. The L2 norm analysis of node-type embeddings showed that nucleic acid atoms and target residue atoms contributed more strongly to the final graph representation than other protein atoms. In addition, the learned edge weights indicated that interactions between the target residue and nucleic acid atoms had the highest average contribution among the six edge classes. These observations are consistent with the expected biological role of the mutation site and its local nucleic acid-binding environment in determining mutation-induced changes in binding free energy.

  To further quantify the contribution of individual edge types, we performed an edge ablation study in which each of the six edge types was individually excluded from the graph representation and the model was retrained using five-fold CV on the PN dataset (Supplementary Fig. S5C and Supplementary Table S5). Removal of any single edge type consistently reduced predictive performance, confirming that all six edge types contribute meaningfully to ΔΔG prediction. The largest performance decrease was observed upon removal of the target residue–nucleic acid edge type (ewn), where the PCC decreased from 0.76 ± 0.03 for the full model to 0.67 ± 0.02. This result is consistent with the highest learned average weight for this edge type and the high contribution of nucleic acid atoms in the node-level analysis. By contrast, the smallest decrease was observed upon removal of the target residue–other protein edge type (ewo), where the PCC was 0.71 ± 0.03, suggesting that indirect protein-side contacts contribute less strongly to ΔΔG prediction than direct mutation-site interactions with the nucleic acid partner. These analyses provide interpretable evidence that DeepPNI relies on biologically relevant structural features from the protein–nucleic acid interface and that the direct structural connection between the mutation site and its nucleic acid-binding partner is the most important feature group for accurate ΔΔG prediction.

  These analyses represent model-level interpretation rather than mutation-specific mechanistic attribution. DeepPNI does not currently provide residue-by-residue, atom-level, or interaction-level explanations for individual predictions. Future developments will include mutation-level explainability approaches, such as atom/residue attribution, perturbation-based feature importance, or gradient-based analyses, to identify the specific local interactions that drive individual ΔΔG predictions.


### Ablation study based on five-fold CV
  DeepPNI was built by integrating RGCN-derived structural embeddings with ESM-2-based sequence embeddings. To evaluate the impact of each feature type, an ablation study was performed on the PN dataset using five-fold CV. As shown in Fig. 3A and Supplementary Table S6, the RGCN-only and ESM-2-only models achieved average PCC values of 0.52 and 0.73, respectively, indicating that both structural and sequence information contribute to ΔΔG prediction. The combined model (RGCN + ESM-2) achieved the highest PCC of 0.76. The combined model also improved the error metrics, reducing MAE from 0.61 and 0.53 for the RGCN-only and ESM-2-only models, respectively, to 0.51. Similarly, the corresponding RMSE decreased from 0.75 and 0.67 to 0.64 in the combined architecture.

  Although the improvement over the ESM-2 model was modest, the combined representation provides a more complete description of the mutation environment by incorporating both local structural interactions and protein sequence context. This complementary feature design is useful for heterogeneous protein–nucleic acid complexes, where either structural or sequence information alone may be insufficient. Thus, integrating RGCN and ESM-2 embeddings enables robust ΔΔG prediction while maintaining manageable computational cost.


### Evaluation using protein structures-based train-test splits
  To further assess model generalizability and avoid possible data leakage from shared structures, we evaluated DeepPNI using a more stringent structure-based splitting strategy. The PN dataset contained 1754 mutations from 439 PDB structures, with an average of approximately four mutations per structure. To avoid such overlap, the dataset was divided based on PDB structures rather than individual mutations. In each fold, mutations from 351 structures were assigned to the training set (80%), while mutations from the remaining 88 structures were assigned to the test set (20%). This ensured that no PDB structure appeared in both training and test sets (details in Supplementary Section S4).

  Under this stringent setting, DeepPNI achieved an average PCC of 0.47 ± 0.05, MAE of 0.66 ± 0.06, and RMSE of 0.83 ± 0.05 across five folds (Supplementary Table S7). The highest PCC was 0.54 in one fold, with an MAE of 0.64 and RMSE of 0.82 (Supplementary Fig. S6). To place this result in context, we also evaluated the existing method DeePNAP using the same structure-based split on the PN dataset. DeePNAP showed lower performance, with a PCC of 0.29 ± 0.06, MAE of 0.91 ± 0.05, and RMSE of 1.33 ± 0.04 (Supplementary Table S7). This comparison indicates that structure-based splitting is a challenging generalization setting for ΔΔG prediction models, but DeepPNI retains better predictive performance than the baseline method under the same nonoverlapping structure-based evaluation. The number of mutations in each training and test set varied because the split was performed at the structure level rather than the mutation level. Across the five folds, the training sets contained 1365–1436 mutations, while the test sets contained 318–389 mutations (Supplementary Table S8). This variation, together with the nonoverlapping structure-based split, likely contributed to the lower PCC values.

  The structure-based split represents a more stringent generalization task than mutation-level cross-validation. In mutation-level cross-validation, the model is tested on unseen mutations, but related structural environments from the same or similar protein–nucleic acid complexes may still be represented in the training set. In contrast, structure-based splitting removes this shared structural context by ensuring that no PDB structure is common between the training and test sets. Therefore, this analysis evaluates extrapolation to completely unseen structural scaffolds, which is expected to be more difficult for any structure-based ΔΔG prediction model. Although the PCC of DeepPNI decreased under this stringent setting compared with mutation-level cross-validation, its higher performance than DeePNAP under the same split supports the advantage of integrating ESM-2 sequence embeddings with edge-aware RGCN structural representations. Thus, the structure-based split should be viewed as a conservative estimate of performance under the most challenging generalization scenario, whereas mutation-level cross-validation and external blind validation reflect the practical utility of DeepPNI for high-throughput mutation prioritization across structurally characterized protein–nucleic acid complexes, as further discussed below.


### External validation on blind mutation datasets
  To evaluate the robustness of DeepPNI, we tested the model on external blind datasets with known ΔΔG values that were not used during model training. The external datasets were compiled from the ProNAB database [43] and grouped according to the experimental method used to determine ΔΔG (Supplementary Section S1). Four groups with sufficient data coverage of over 70 mutations per group were selected: isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC; 210 mutations), gel shift (GS; 181 mutations), fluorescence polarization (FP; 95 mutations), and filter binding (FB; 70 mutations).

  DeepPNI achieved PCC values ranging from 0.58 to 0.81 across the four blind datasets, with MAE and RMSE values ranging from 0.44 to 0.79 and from 0.58 to 1.01, respectively (Supplementary Table S9, and Fig. 3B and C). The highest PCC was observed for the GS dataset (PCC = 0.81), followed by the FP dataset (PCC = 0.78), ITC dataset (PCC = 0.62), and FB dataset (PCC = 0.58). These results suggest that DeepPNI can capture ΔΔG trends across independent datasets generated using different experimental techniques.

  The external validation supports the generalizability of DeepPNI beyond the training dataset. The consistently positive correlations across blind datasets indicate that the model retains predictive ability when applied to unseen protein–nucleic acid mutation data.


### DeepPNI performance in comparison with existing methods
  Benchmarking protein–nucleic acid ΔΔG prediction methods is challenging because available tools differ in training datasets, input requirements, feature-generation procedures, and validation protocols. Therefore, to provide a complete and balanced evaluation with respect to existing methods, we considered the comparisons in three categories (Supplementary Table S10). First, DeepPNI was directly compared with DeePNAP on the PN dataset because both methods can be evaluated using sequence- and structure-derived inputs available for the full dataset. Second, DeepPNI was compared with DeePNAP and MutPNI on the MPR311 benchmark, where previously reported MutPNI results are available. Third, the PremPDI dataset was used only as a legacy benchmark comparison, because a substantial fraction of its mutations overlap with the PN dataset used for DeepPNI training. For each comparison, consistently the same experimental conditions were maintained for fair evaluations.

  For comparison with existing methods, we used both the PN dataset and the previously compiled MPR311 dataset, which contains 311 amino acid mutations in protein–nucleic acid complexes [30]. The PN dataset serves as a larger and more diverse benchmark, whereas MPR311 represents a smaller established benchmark, approximately one-fifth the size of the PN dataset.

  The performance of DeepPNI was compared with two recent baseline methods, DeePNAP and MutPNI. MutPNI was not evaluated on the full PN dataset because it requires FoldX-derived energy-profile features for each mutation. Generating these features for all 1754 mutations would require additional structure preparation and FoldX calculations for each mutation, making a full PN-scale comparison computationally demanding and less directly comparable to methods that do not depend on external energy calculations. Therefore, for the PN dataset, we compared DeepPNI with DeePNAP (Supplementary Table S10). For the smaller MPR311 dataset, previously reported MutPNI results from 10-fold cross-validation were available; therefore, DeepPNI and DeePNAP were also evaluated on this dataset using 10-fold cross-validation for a fair comparison.

  In five-fold CV on the PN dataset, DeePNAP obtained a PCC of 0.52 (Supplementary Table S11 and Fig. 4A). In comparison, DeepPNI showed a substantially higher average PCC of 0.76, with lower average MAE and RMSE values of 0.51 and 0.64, respectively. On the MPR311 dataset, DeepPNI again showed the best performance (Supplementary Table S11 and Fig. 4B), with a PCC of 0.79 and MAE and RMSE values of 0.59 and 0.75, respectively. In comparison, MutPNI and DeePNAP achieved PCC values of 0.75 and 0.59, MAE values of 0.97 and 0.82, and RMSE values of 1.30 and 1.15, respectively. These results indicate that DeepPNI improves both correlation and error metrics compared with the tested baseline methods on the evaluated datasets.

  To further compare DeepPNI with existing tools, we evaluated its performance on the external PremPDI dataset comprising 219 mutations [33]. Because 193 of the 219 PremPDI mutations were already present in the PN dataset used for DeepPNI training, the PremPDI comparison should be interpreted as a legacy benchmark comparison rather than an independent external validation. Therefore, the high PCC of 0.90 observed between experimental and predicted ΔΔG values should be interpreted with caution, as it likely reflects partial overlap between the PremPDI and training datasets (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Table S12). Nevertheless, this comparison was included to maintain consistency with previous benchmarking studies. DeePNAP previously used the PremPDI dataset for comparison with four methods, including mmCSM-NA, mCSM-NA, PremPDI, and SAMPDI-3D, reporting PCC values ranging from 0.51 to 0.83 (Supplementary Table S12). Among those methods, SAMPDI-3D showed the highest PCC of 0.83, whereas DeePNAP showed the lowest PCC of 0.51. DeepPNI showed a higher PCC of 0.90 on the same dataset, although the overlap with the PN training dataset should be considered when interpreting this outcome.

  These results indicate that DeepPNI shows favourable performance across the evaluated benchmarks, while avoiding dependence on third-party energy calculations such as FoldX. However, because existing methods differ in input requirements, training data, and validation protocols, direct performance comparisons should be interpreted with these differences in mind. The main strength of DeepPNI lies in combining high predictive accuracy with a large curated training dataset and a computationally efficient prediction workflow. DeepPNI can predict ΔΔG for new mutations within a short runtime. In our timing analysis, predictions for up to 100 mutations were completed within one minute (Fig. 4D). The same hardware architecture described in the ‘Materials and methods’ section has been deployed for the publicly available web server. This makes DeepPNI suitable for high-throughput screening of mutation effects in protein–nucleic acid complexes.

  A representative case analysis covering stabilizing, destabilizing, and neutral mutations, as well as protein–DNA–RNA hybrid complexes is provided in Supplementary Section S5 and Supplementary Fig. S7. This analysis further supports the ability of DeepPNI to capture mutation effects across broad ΔΔG ranges and shows that the model generally captures the direction of stabilizing and destabilizing effects.


### Analysis of overfitting and model capacity relative to dataset size
  From a general deep learning perspective, 1754 experimentally measured mutations is a modest dataset size. However, in the specific context of protein–nucleic acid ΔΔG prediction, it represents one of the largest curated experimental datasets currently available. DeepPNI was therefore designed to balance model capacity with the available data size. To reduce the risk of overfitting, DeepPNI does not train a large protein language model from scratch; instead, it uses fixed ESM-2 embeddings as pretrained sequence representations and trains only the RGCN and downstream regression layers. In addition, the RGCN uses basis decomposition for relation-specific transformations, and the model training includes dropout, L2 regularization, batch normalization, gradient clipping, and validation-based checkpoint selection. These design choices help control model complexity and reduce overfitting risk.

  To further evaluate whether model capacity was appropriate relative to the available training data, we performed three complementary analyses (Supplementary Fig. S8). First, the training and validation loss curves over 250 epochs showed parallel decreasing behaviour without marked divergence during training, and the final model was selected at the early stopping point (approximately epoch 120) based on the lowest validation loss (Supplementary Fig. S8A). This training behaviour supports that overfitting was effectively controlled during model optimization. Second, we examined the relative generalization gap, defined as [(validation loss – training loss)/training loss], across increasing fractions of the PN training data from 20% to 100%. The relative generalization gap decreased consistently from 1.77 to 0.40 as the amount of training data increased (Supplementary Fig. S8B), indicating progressive improvement in generalization with additional data. Third, we evaluated relative generalization gap across different MLP architectures with trainable parameter counts ranging from 3.94E + 05 to 1.97E + 06. The parameter count reflects model size and capacity. Using the PN dataset, the lowest relative generalization gap was observed for 8.74E + 05 trainable parameters (Supplementary Fig. S8C), which were used in DeepPNI. In contrast, architectures with both lower and higher parameter counts showed larger generalization gaps. These results indicate that the full PN dataset of 1754 mutations and the selected DeepPNI architecture provide an appropriate balance between model capacity and the available data size.

  The modest performance under structure-based splitting reflects the challenges of extrapolating to completely unseen structural scaffolds, which is a more demanding task than mutation-level prediction. At the same time, the consistent performance across mutation-level cross-validation, protein–DNA and protein–RNA subsets, temperature-based datasets, external blind datasets, and baseline comparisons support the practical utility of DeepPNI for high-throughput mutation prioritization within the currently available structural and experimental data landscape.

  Thus, DeepPNI should be interpreted as a computational prioritization tool rather than a replacement for experimental measurement of binding free energy. The model is designed to rapidly estimate mutation-induced ΔΔG values for single amino acid mutations in structurally characterized protein–DNA and protein–RNA complexes. These predictions can help identify candidate mutations that are likely to alter protein–nucleic acid binding stability and can therefore guide the selection of mutations for downstream experimental validation.


## Conclusions
  We developed DeepPNI, a deep learning-based regression model for predicting the effects of missense mutations on protein–DNA and protein–RNA stability. The model combines ESM-2-based protein sequence embeddings with 3D structural features represented using an edge-aware RGCN. A major strength of DeepPNI is its development on a large curated experimental dataset of 1754 mutations, representing one of the largest datasets currently available for PNI binding free energy prediction. DeepPNI showed consistent predictive performance, with PCC values around 0.75 on the full PN dataset and across multiple dataset splits based on complex type and experimental temperature. Notably, the model also showed good performance on external blind datasets, with PCC values up to 0.81, supporting its generalizability to previously unseen mutation data. A more stringent structure-based split, in which no PDB structure was shared between training and test sets, further provided a conservative assessment of DeepPNI generalization to unseen structural scaffolds. In comparisons with existing methods, DeepPNI showed favourable performance across the evaluated benchmarks while avoiding dependence on third-party energy calculations. DeepPNI also provides a computationally efficient prediction workflow, with prediction of up to 100 mutations completed within one minute on the deployed hardware. Its availability as a public web server makes the model directly accessible for high-throughput assessment of mutation effects in protein–nucleic acid complexes. In brief, these results support DeepPNI as a robust and practical tool for prioritizing mutations that may alter protein–nucleic acid binding stability and contribute to disease-associated functional changes.
