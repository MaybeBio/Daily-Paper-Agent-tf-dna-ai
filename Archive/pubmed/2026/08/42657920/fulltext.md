# Apigenin Alleviates Pyroptosis in Lung Adenocarcinoma Cells by Modulating TNF‐α/NF‐κB Signaling and AIM2 Inflammasome Activation


## Background
  Pyroptosis‐related signaling contributes to lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) progression and prognosis. Apigenin (AP) has anti‐inflammatory and cytoprotective properties, but its effects on pyroptosis‐associated signaling in injured LUAD cells remain unclear.


## Methods
  A549 cells were treated with carbonyl cyanide m‐chlorophenyl hydrazone (CCCP) and ferrous sulfate (FeSO4) to establish a cellular injury model. Cell morphology, Cell Counting Kit‐8 signals, and pyroptosis‐associated proteins were assessed after AP treatment. Network pharmacology, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, and analyses of The Cancer Genome Atlas and Gene Expression Omnibus datasets were used to prioritize candidate targets, characterize pyroptosis‐related genes, and develop a prognostic signature.


## Results
  CCCP/FeSO4 reduced CCK‐8 signals and induced morphological injury, whereas AP partially reversed these changes. AP also reduced alterations in GSDMD‐N, caspase‐1, IL‐1β, TNF‐α/NF‐κB pathway proteins, AIM2, and ASC. TNF was prioritized as a candidate target, and computational analyses predicted a potentially stable AP–TNF interaction without demonstrating direct binding. Bioinformatic analyses identified distinct pyroptosis‐related LUAD subgroups and generated a prognostic signature that stratified survival in training and external validation cohorts. AIM2 was additionally prioritized as a LUAD‐ and stress‐associated candidate.


## Conclusion
  AP attenuated CCCP/FeSO4‐induced injury and pyroptosis‐associated molecular changes in A549 cells, potentially through TNF‐α/NF‐κB and AIM2‐related signaling. Further mechanistic validation is required.


## Introduction
  Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most common histological subtype of lung cancer and remains a major cause of cancer‐related mortality worldwide [1]. Although targeted therapies and immunotherapies have substantially improved clinical outcomes for selected patients, acquired resistance, disease progression, and treatment‐related adverse events continue to limit durable therapeutic benefits, particularly in advanced LUAD [2, 3]. In recent years, transcriptomic profiling, single‐cell sequencing, multi‐omics integration, and prognostic modeling have been increasingly applied to resolve the molecular heterogeneity of lung cancer, identify disease‐associated subtypes, and prioritize candidate regulators [4, 5]. These approaches provide a valuable framework for target discovery and patient stratification, but their predictions require validation in biologically relevant experimental settings.

  Natural products represent an important source of bioactive molecules for cancer‐related research and drug discovery [6]. Among them, flavonoids have attracted considerable attention because of their broad anti‐inflammatory, antioxidant, and signaling‐regulatory activities [7]. Apigenin (AP) is a naturally occurring flavone widely present in fruits, vegetables, and medicinal plants and has exhibited diverse biological activities in preclinical models [8]. Recent studies have applied network pharmacology, bioinformatics, molecular docking, and experimental epigenetic analyses to elucidate the molecular actions of natural products in LUAD and other cancers, providing a basis for candidate‐target prioritization and subsequent experimental evaluation [9, 10]. Previous studies have shown that AP can modulate oxidative stress, nuclear factor‐κB (NF‐κB) signaling, and pyroptosis‐related pathways [11, 12]. However, whether AP regulates pyroptotic signaling in chemically injured LUAD cells and the molecular basis underlying this effect remains unclear.

  Pyroptosis is a lytic and pro‐inflammatory form of regulated cell death characterized by inflammasome‐associated signaling, activation of inflammatory caspases, gasdermin cleavage, membrane pore formation, and the release of inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin‐1β and interleukin‐18 [13, 14]. The role of pyroptosis in cancer is highly context dependent. On the one hand, inducing pyroptotic death in tumor cells may directly suppress tumor growth and enhance antitumor immune responses. On the other hand, persistent pyroptosis‐associated inflammation may promote tumor‐microenvironment remodeling and disease progression [15, 16]. Increasing evidence indicates that the expression patterns of pyroptosis‐related molecules are associated with molecular subtypes, tumor progression, and clinical outcomes in LUAD [17]. Meanwhile, the progression of non‐small cell lung cancer is governed by multilayered and interconnected regulatory networks, as illustrated by the NSUN2/FOXM1 positive‐feedback loop [18]. Therefore, pyroptosis‐associated molecular changes induced by chemical stress should be evaluated together with broader cellular stress and cell‐death responses.

  Based on these observations, we hypothesized that AP attenuates pyroptosis‐associated injury in LUAD cells by regulating the tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α)/NF‐κB/absent in melanoma 2 (AIM2) axis. To test this hypothesis, network pharmacology was first used to identify candidate targets shared between AP and pyroptosis, followed by molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to evaluate the potential interactions between AP and prioritized proteins. In parallel, transcriptomic and clinical data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) cohorts were analyzed to characterize pyroptosis‐related genes (PRGs), construct and externally validate a PRG‐based prognostic signature, and prioritize AIM2 for cellular evaluation. Finally, carbonyl cyanide m‐chlorophenyl hydrazone (CCCP) and ferrous sulfate (FeSO4) were used to induce cellular injury in A549 cells, and the effects of AP on cellular injury, pyroptosis‐associated proteins, TNF‐α/NF‐κB signaling, AIM2, and apoptosis‐associated speck‐like protein containing a caspase recruitment domain (ASC) were evaluated. By integrating computational predictions, clinical cohort analyses, and cellular experiments, this study aimed to define the potential molecular basis through which AP regulates pyroptotic signaling in LUAD cells.


## Methods

### Reagents
  AP, CCCP, and FeSO4 were purchased from Aladdin Reagent Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). AP and CCCP were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare 100 and 50 mM stock solutions, respectively, aliquoted, and stored at −20°C protected from light. FeSO4 was freshly dissolved in sterile deionized water to prepare a 100 mM stock solution before each experiment. The final concentration of DMSO in all culture systems was maintained below 0.1% (v/v).

  RPMI‐1640 medium, high‐glucose Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM), and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were purchased from Gibco, and penicillin–streptomycin was obtained from Procell. CCK‐8 reagent, RIPA lysis buffer, BCA Protein Assay Kit, protease and phosphatase inhibitors, and enhanced chemiluminescence reagents were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology.


### Cell Culture and Treatment
  Human LUAD A549 cells and human bronchial epithelial BEAS‐2B cells were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. A549 cells were cultured in RPMI‐1640 medium, whereas BEAS‐2B cells were maintained in high‐glucose DMEM. Both media were supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2.

  To screen the optimal cell injury conditions, A549 cells were treated with 0, 20, or 40 μM CCCP in combination with 100, 200, or 300 μM FeSO4 for 24 h. For morphological observation, the groups included untreated control cells, cells treated with 20 μM CCCP plus 100 μM FeSO4, and cells treated with 40 μM CCCP plus 100 μM FeSO4. Based on the morphological changes and CCK‐8 results, 20 μM CCCP combined with 100 μM FeSO4 for 24 h was selected for subsequent experiments.

  For AP intervention, cells were pretreated with 25 or 50 μM AP for 2 h and then exposed to CCCP and FeSO4 for an additional 24 h in the continued presence of AP. The experimental groups included the control group, vehicle‐control group, CCCP/FeSO4 group, CCCP/FeSO4 plus 25 μM AP group, and CCCP/FeSO4 plus 50 μM AP group. A concentration of 50 μM AP was used for morphological observation and the time‐dependent cell viability assay, whereas 25 and 50 μM AP were used for Western blot analysis. For comparison of AIM2 expression, A549 cells were treated with 20 or 40 μM CCCP in combination with 100 μM FeSO4 for 24 h.


### Cell Morphology Observation
  A549 cells were seeded in culture plates and treated as described above. After treatment, cellular morphology was observed using an inverted phase‐contrast microscope. Images were acquired under identical magnification and exposure settings, and changes in cell density, swelling, rounding, and adhesion were recorded.


### Cell Viability Assay
  A549 cells were seeded into 96‐well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well and treated as described above after attachment. Subsequently, 10 μL of CCK‐8 reagent was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37°C for 2 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Wells containing culture medium without cells were used as blanks. Cell viability was calculated as follows: Cell viability%=Atreatment−AblankAcontrol−Ablank×100%.

  Five replicate wells were included for each treatment condition. In the concentration‐screening experiment, the results were expressed as normalized cell viability. In the time‐dependent experiment, absorbance at 450 nm was measured at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h, and blank‐corrected absorbance values were used to generate the growth curves.


### Western Blot Analysis
  Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with 1% protease inhibitor and 1% phosphatase inhibitor. Cell lysates were centrifuged at 12 000g for 15 min at 4°C, and protein concentrations were determined using the BCA method. Protein samples, 30 μg per lane, were separated by 10% or 12% SDS‐PAGE and transferred onto 0.22 μm PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk for 1 h, whereas membranes used for phosphorylated proteins were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin.

  The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. Antibodies against AIM2, caspase‐1, GSDMD, GSDMD‐N, IL‐1β, TNF‐α, ASC, GAPDH, β‐tubulin, and β‐actin were purchased from Bioworld Technology, whereas antibodies against P65, p‐P65, IκBα, and p‐IκBα were obtained from Startbio. Primary antibodies against target proteins were diluted 1:1000, and loading‐control antibodies were diluted 1:5000. After washing, the membranes were incubated with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies diluted 1:5000 for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence and quantified using ImageJ. Target proteins were normalized to the corresponding loading controls, whereas p‐P65 and p‐IκBα were normalized to P65 and IκBα, respectively.


### Network Pharmacology Analysis and Molecular Docking
  Potential targets of AP were retrieved from the TCMSP, TargetNet, DrugBank, and SwissTargetPrediction databases, whereas PRGs were obtained from the GeneCards database. The overlapping targets were identified and visualized using a Venn diagram.

  The overlapping targets were imported into the STRING database to construct a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network [19], which was visualized using Cytoscape version 3.10.0 [20]. Nodes with a degree greater than twice the median and a betweenness centrality greater than twice the median were considered core targets. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed using the DAVID database [21].

  The three‐dimensional structures of candidate proteins were obtained from the RCSB PDB database, and the structure of AP was downloaded from the PubChem database. Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina [22], and the binding conformations and key interaction sites were visualized using PyMOL.


### MD Simulations
  MD simulations of AP–target protein complexes were performed using GROMACS 2023.2 with the CHARMM36 force field [23]. The receptor structures were preprocessed using Swiss‐PdbViewer. Each complex was placed in a cubic box, solvated using the TIP3P water model, and neutralized by adding counterions.

  After energy minimization using the steepest descent algorithm, the system was sequentially equilibrated under the NVT and NPT ensembles, followed by a 100 ns production simulation at 300 K and 1 bar. RMSD, RMSF, radius of gyration, solvent‐accessible surface area, hydrogen bonds, and the free‐energy landscape were analyzed using GROMACS. The results were visualized using QtGrace and VMD.


### Bioinformatics Analysis
  RNA sequencing data and corresponding clinical information for LUAD were obtained from the TCGA database, and the GSE13213 dataset from the GEO database was used as an external validation cohort. A total of 72 predefined PRGs were included in the analysis.

  Differentially expressed PRGs between LUAD and normal lung tissues were identified using the limma package in R [24], with an adjusted p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Consensus clustering was performed using the ConsensusClusterPlus package with 1000 resampling iterations. The optimal number of clusters was determined according to the cumulative distribution function curves and clustering stability.

  Prognosis‐related PRGs were screened using univariate Cox regression analysis, followed by least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression to construct the prognostic model. Patients were divided into high‐ and low‐risk groups according to the median risk score. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, principal component analysis, and time‐dependent receiver operating characteristic curve analysis were used to evaluate model performance. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were further performed to assess the independent prognostic value of the risk score.


### Statistical Analysis
  Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 6 and R software. Quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Comparisons between two groups were performed using a two‐sided Student's t‐test. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one‐way analysis of variance followed by Tukey's post hoc test. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.


## Results

### AP Attenuates CCCP/FeSO4‐Induced Cellular Injury and Pyroptosis‐Associated Signaling in A549 Cells
  To establish a reproducible cellular injury model, A549 cells were treated with different concentrations of CCCP and FeSO4 for 24 h. CCCP treatment induced marked morphological injury, including cell rounding, reduced cell density, and impaired attachment, with more pronounced changes observed at 40 μM CCCP (Figure 1A). Consistently, CCK‐8 analysis showed that CCCP markedly reduced cell viability, whereas increasing the FeSO4 concentration from 100 to 300 μM produced only limited additional changes under the same CCCP concentration (Figure 1B). Based on these results, 20 μM CCCP combined with 100 μM FeSO4 was selected for subsequent experiments because this condition induced substantial but incomplete cellular injury.

  Under the selected injury condition, AP treatment partially preserved cell morphology and reduced CCCP/FeSO4‐induced cell loss after 24 h (Figure 1C). Time‐dependent CCK‐8 analysis further showed that AP increased the absorbance signal compared with the CCCP/FeSO4 group, indicating partial recovery of cell viability and proliferative activity (Figure 1D). Western blot analysis showed that CCCP/FeSO4 treatment increased the levels of pyroptosis‐associated proteins, including GSDMD‐N, caspase‐1, and IL‐1β, whereas AP attenuated these alterations in a concentration‐dependent manner (Figure 1E). CCCP/FeSO4 treatment also increased TNF‐α expression and the phosphorylation levels of IκBα and P65, while AP reduced these changes without markedly altering total IκBα or P65 expression (Figure 1F). These results indicate that AP attenuates CCCP/FeSO4‐induced cellular injury in A549 cells, accompanied by suppression of pyroptosis‐associated proteins and TNF‐α/NF‐κB signaling.


### Network Pharmacology and Computational Analyses Prioritize TNF as a Candidate AP‐Related Target
  To explore the potential molecular basis underlying the regulation of pyroptosis‐associated signaling by AP, network pharmacology analysis was first performed. A total of 47 overlapping targets were identified between the predicted targets of AP and PRGs (Figure 2A). PPI network analysis further highlighted several hub targets, including EGFR, NFKBIA, AKT1, BCL2, IFNG, JUN, HSP90AA1, TNF, CASP3, and TP53 (Figure 2B). Molecular docking predicted potential interactions between AP and multiple hub proteins (Figure 2C). TNF was selected for further MD analysis based on its central position within the interaction network, its close association with inflammatory and NF‐κB signaling, and its relevance to the subsequent cellular findings. Other hub targets, including EGFR and AKT1, were not excluded, and their predicted docking conformations are presented in Figure S1.

  The docking model revealed a plausible interaction conformation between AP and TNF (Figure 2D). During the 100 ns MD simulation, the AP–TNF complex exhibited relatively stable root‐mean‐square deviation values, persistent hydrogen‐bond interactions, limited fluctuations in the radius of gyration, and a concentrated low‐energy conformational region (Figure 2E–H). Additional trajectory analyses are provided in Figure S2. Collectively, the network topology, molecular docking, and dynamic stability analyses support TNF as a key candidate target through which AP may regulate pyroptosis‐associated signaling, although direct binding and target specificity require further validation using surface plasmon resonance or microscale thermophoresis.


### Integrated PRG Analysis Identifies AIM2 as a Candidate Linking LUAD Transcriptional Features With Cellular Stress Responses
  To characterize the expression profile of PRGs in LUAD, predefined PRGs were analyzed in the TCGA‐LUAD cohort, and 12 genes were identified as differentially expressed between LUAD and normal lung tissues (Figure 3A). PPI analysis showed that AIM2, CASP5, NLRC4, and MEFV occupied central positions within the network, with AIM2 displaying relatively high network centrality (Figure 3B). Co‐expression analysis further revealed close associations between AIM2 and CASP5, NLRC4, IL1B, and other PRGs (Figure 3C). Based on its differential expression pattern, network position, and established relevance to inflammasome‐associated signaling, AIM2 was prioritized for subsequent cellular validation.

  Western blot analysis showed that AIM2 protein abundance was higher in A549 cells than in BEAS‐2B cells and was further increased after treatment with 20 or 40 μM CCCP in combination with 100 μM FeSO4FeSO4 (Figure 3D,E). Under CCCP/FeSO4‐induced injury conditions, AIM2 and ASC protein levels were both increased, whereas AP treatment reduced their expression, with a more pronounced effect at the higher AP concentration (Figure 3F). These findings identify AIM2 as a candidate linking LUAD‐associated PRG expression patterns with CCCP/FeSO4‐induced cellular stress and support an inhibitory effect of AP on AIM2/ASC‐associated signaling. However, because AIM2–ASC complex formation and ASC speck assembly were not directly assessed, the present evidence does not establish direct inhibition of functional AIM2 inflammasome assembly.


### PRG Expression Patterns Define Two Molecular Subtypes in LUAD
  To evaluate the relationship between PRG expression patterns and molecular heterogeneity in LUAD, consensus clustering analysis was performed using PRG expression profiles from the TCGA‐LUAD cohort. Based on the cumulative distribution function curves, clustering consistency, and sample distribution, k = 2 produced the most stable classification, dividing patients into Cluster 1 and Cluster 2 (Figure 4A). The heatmap showed distinct PRG expression patterns and differences in the distribution of selected clinicopathological characteristics between the two subtypes (Figure 4B).

  Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed a significant difference in overall survival between the two PRG‐related subtypes, with one cluster showing a less favorable prognosis (Figure 4C). These findings indicate that PRG expression patterns are associated with molecular heterogeneity and clinical outcomes in LUAD and provide a basis for subsequent construction of a pyroptosis‐related prognostic model.


### Construction and Internal Evaluation of a PRG‐Based Prognostic Signature in the TCGA‐LUAD Cohort
  To construct a pyroptosis‐related prognostic signature, survival‐associated PRGs were first screened using univariate Cox regression analysis (Figure 5A). The candidate genes were subsequently subjected to LASSO Cox regression, and the penalty parameter was selected by cross‐validation to generate the prognostic signature (Figure 5B,C). A risk score was calculated for each patient using the expression levels and corresponding regression coefficients of the selected genes, and patients in the TCGA‐LUAD cohort were divided into high‐ and low‐risk groups according to the median risk score.

  The distributions of risk scores and survival status showed that mortality events became more frequent as the risk score increased (Figure 5D,E). Principal component analysis showed separation between the two risk groups based on the expression profiles of the signature genes (Figure 5F). Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated that patients in the high‐risk group had significantly poorer overall survival than those in the low‐risk group (Figure 5G). The areas under the time‐dependent receiver operating characteristic curves were 0.716, 0.720, and 0.705 for 1‐, 3‐, and 5‐year overall survival, respectively (Figure 5H). These findings indicate that the PRG‐based signature stratified overall survival and showed moderate discriminative performance within the TCGA‐LUAD cohort.


### External Validation and Independent Prognostic Assessment of the PRG‐Based Signature
  To assess the external performance of the PRG‐based prognostic signature, the risk score formula derived from the TCGA‐LUAD cohort was applied to the GSE13213 cohort. Patients were divided into high‐ and low‐risk groups according to the median risk score. The distributions of risk scores and survival status showed that mortality events were more frequent among patients with higher risk scores (Figure 6A,B). Principal component analysis showed partial separation between the two risk groups based on the expression profiles of the signature genes (Figure 6C).

  Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated that patients in the high‐risk group had significantly poorer overall survival than those in the low‐risk group (Figure 6D). The areas under the time‐dependent receiver operating characteristic curves were 0.794, 0.572, and 0.635 for 1‐, 3‐, and 5‐year overall survival, respectively, indicating that the discriminative performance of the signature varied across follow‐up periods in the external cohort (Figure 6E). Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses further showed that the risk score remained independently associated with overall survival after adjustment for the available clinicopathological variables (Figure 6F,G). These findings support the prognostic relevance of the PRG‐based risk score, while indicating that its longer‐term predictive performance requires further validation in additional independent cohorts.


### Functional Enrichment Analysis Highlights Inflammatory and Pyroptosis‐Related Pathways Associated With AP‐Related Targets and Prognostic PRGs
  To characterize the biological functions associated with survival‐related PRGs, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed. The significantly enriched GO terms were mainly related to cell proliferation and differentiation, pyroptosis, immune activation, inflammatory regulation, extracellular components, cytolytic granules, receptor complexes, cytokine activity, lipid binding, and kinase‐related functions (Figure 7A–D). KEGG analysis further highlighted several inflammation‐ and cancer‐related pathways, including NF‐κB signaling.

  Overlap analysis showed that two pathways were shared between the 98 pathways enriched for AP–pyroptosis overlapping targets and the five pathways associated with survival‐related PRGs (Figure 7E). These enrichment results prioritize inflammatory and pyroptosis‐related pathways, particularly NF‐κB signaling, as candidate biological processes potentially linking AP‐related targets with the prognostic PRG landscape in LUAD. However, pathway enrichment reflects statistical association rather than direct mechanistic validation.


### Proposed Working Model of AP‐Associated Regulation of Pyroptosis‐Related Signaling in CCCP/FeSO4‐Treated A549 Cells
  Based on the integrated computational, bioinformatic, and cellular findings, a working model was proposed to summarize the potential mechanism by which AP modulates pyroptosis‐associated signaling in CCCP/FeSO4‐treated A549 cells (Figure 8). In this model, CCCP/FeSO4‐induced cellular stress is associated with mitochondrial damage and DAMP release, which may contribute to AIM2/ASC‐associated signaling and caspase‐1‐related downstream molecular changes. In parallel, TNF‐α/NF‐κB signaling may provide an upstream inflammatory context linked to the expression of pyroptosis‐associated mediators, including IL‐1β and GSDMD‐related changes. AP treatment was associated with reduced TNF‐α/NF‐κB signaling, lower AIM2/ASC protein abundance, and attenuation of pyroptosis‐associated molecular alterations. This schematic model summarizes the potential relationships among these pathways but does not establish direct molecular causality or functional AIM2 inflammasome assembly.


## Discussion
  This study showed that AP attenuated CCCP/FeSO4‐induced injury in A549 cells, as evidenced by improved cellular morphology and CCK‐8 signals, together with reduced levels of pyroptosis‐associated proteins, including GSDMD‐N, caspase‐1, and IL‐1β, and weakened TNF‐α/NF‐κB‐related signaling. Cleavage of GSDMD by inflammatory caspases generates the pore‐forming GSDMD‐N fragment, which represents a central execution event in pyroptosis. Accordingly, the changes in GSDMD‐N observed in this study provide direct biochemical evidence for the involvement of pyroptosis‐associated signaling in the injury process [25]. Previous studies have also shown that AP can attenuate pyroptotic responses in different cellular models by modulating oxidative stress, NF‐κB signaling, and inflammasome‐related pathways [11, 12]. However, CCCP primarily disrupts mitochondrial membrane potential, whereas FeSO4 promotes iron‐dependent oxidative stress. Therefore, apoptosis, ferroptosis, and nonspecific oxidative injury may coexist with pyroptosis‐associated changes in this model. Given the well‐established antioxidant activity of AP, part of its protective effect may also arise from the attenuation of mitochondrial and oxidative stress rather than the selective inhibition of a single form of cell death. The present findings support the involvement of pyroptosis‐associated molecular changes in this injury model but do not establish pyroptosis as the exclusive mode of cell death. Future studies incorporating pathway‐specific inhibitors, lipid peroxidation assays, apoptosis markers, membrane‐permeability measurements, and cleaved caspase‐1 detection will help distinguish the contributions of overlapping cell‐death programs.

  Network pharmacology analysis identified several hub targets, including EGFR, AKT1, NFKBIA, BCL2, TP53, CASP3, and TNF. TNF was prioritized for further investigation based on its network centrality, its close association with inflammatory and NF‐κB signaling, and its consistency with the subsequent cellular findings. NF‐κB signaling not only regulates inflammatory responses but also promotes the transcription of inflammasome components and pro‐IL‐1β, thereby providing a priming signal for subsequent pyroptosis‐associated responses [26, 27]. Molecular docking and MD simulations suggested that AP and TNF may form a relatively stable interaction model, while Western blot analysis showed that AP reduced TNF‐α expression and the phosphorylation of IκBα and P65. Together, these findings support TNF as an important candidate target involved in AP‐mediated regulation of inflammatory signaling. Nevertheless, computational modeling cannot substitute for direct binding evidence. Surface plasmon resonance, microscale thermophoresis, cellular thermal shift assays, or target‐knockdown and rescue experiments are still required to verify binding affinity and target specificity. Other hub targets, including EGFR and AKT1, were not experimentally excluded and may also contribute to the multi‐target pharmacological effects of AP.

  Integrated analysis of differentially expressed PRGs, PPI network topology, co‐expression relationships, and cellular experiments further prioritized AIM2 as a candidate molecule linking LUAD transcriptional features with CCCP/FeSO4‐induced cellular stress. AIM2 protein abundance was higher in A549 cells than in BEAS‐2B cells and was further increased after CCCP/FeSO4 exposure, whereas AP reduced AIM2 and ASC protein expression, supporting an inhibitory effect on AIM2/ASC‐associated signaling. AIM2 is canonically activated by cytosolic double‐stranded DNA and forms an inflammasome complex through recruitment of ASC and caspase‐1 [26]. Previous evidence has also shown that AIM2 can promote non‐small cell lung cancer cell growth through inflammasome‐dependent and inflammasome‐independent mechanisms, suggesting a potential role in lung cancer‐associated inflammation and tumor biology [28]. Mitochondrial damage and the release of damage‐associated molecular patterns induced by CCCP/FeSO4 provide a biologically plausible link between cellular stress and AIM2‐related responses. However, changes in AIM2 and ASC expression cannot be considered equivalent to functional inflammasome assembly. AIM2–ASC co‐immunoprecipitation, ASC speck imaging, ASC oligomerization assays, or caspase‐1 cleavage analysis will be required for further confirmation. Canonical inflammasome models, including cytosolic dsDNA transfection for AIM2, LPS plus nigericin for NLRP3, flagellin transfection for NLRC4, and cytosolic LPS for non‐canonical inflammasome activation, should also be used to clarify the selectivity of AP toward distinct inflammasome pathways.

  Bioinformatic analyses further demonstrated that PRG expression patterns were associated with molecular heterogeneity and clinical outcomes in LUAD, consistent with the context‐dependent role of pyroptosis in tumor development and progression [13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. The PRG‐based signature stratified patient survival in both the TCGA and GSE13213 cohorts, although variation in the time‐dependent AUC values in the external cohort indicates that its discriminative performance requires further validation in larger independent cohorts. Experimental validation was also limited to A549 cells, whereas gasdermin expression and epigenetic silencing vary among tumor cell types. Validation in additional LUAD cell lines, primary cells, organoids, immune coculture systems, and in vivo models is therefore necessary. Importantly, the current study demonstrates a cytoprotective effect of AP in chemically injured A549 cells rather than antitumor efficacy or immune activation. In addition, the clinical translation of AP is constrained by poor aqueous solubility, limited oral bioavailability, extensive first‐pass metabolism, and rapid systemic clearance [29]. These limitations may be addressed through nanoparticle‐based delivery, phospholipid complexes, prodrug design, structural modification, or local pulmonary administration. Overall, AP attenuated CCCP/FeSO4‐induced injury and pyroptosis‐associated molecular changes in A549 cells, accompanied by reduced TNF‐α/NF‐κB signaling and lower AIM2/ASC protein levels. However, direct target engagement, functional inflammasome assembly, pathway specificity, antitumor activity, and in vivo relevance remain to be established.
