# miRSiC: a regulatory-aware machine learning framework for microRNA expression inference across bulk and single-cell transcriptomes


## Abstract
  MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are key post-transcriptional regulators embedded in gene regulatory networks between upstream transcription factors (TFs) and downstream target genes (TGs), yet most computational approaches infer miRNA expression using unstructured transcriptomic features without explicitly modeling their regulatory architecture. In this study, we develop miRSiC, an interpretable machine learning framework that integrates TFs and experimentally validated TGs for regulatory-aware miRNA expression inference. miRSiC formulates prediction as miRNA-specific regression tasks and evaluates three regulatory configurations (TF-only, TG-only, and combined TF–TG) using Light Gradient Boosting Machine. Applied to The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) breast cancer cohort, the integrated model achieves superior performance (mean Spearman correlation = 0.5462 across 326 miRNAs), outperforming single-layer models and demonstrating the effectiveness of incorporating both upstream and downstream regulatory signals. Feature importance analysis and regulatory network reconstruction show that selected features are enriched in biologically coherent TF–miRNA–target circuits. Prediction performance varies across breast cancer subtypes, reflecting differences in regulatory patterns and sample size. Cross-platform evaluation further reveals that models trained on bulk transcriptomes do not generalize to single-cell data due to distributional shifts and sparsity; however, domain-specific retraining partially restores performance. Together, miRSiC provides an interpretable and biologically grounded framework for miRNA expression inference, highlighting the importance of modeling regulatory context and adopting domain-aware strategies across bulk and single-cell transcriptomic data.


## Introduction
  MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are ~21–23-nucleotide noncoding RNAs that post-transcriptionally repress gene expression [1–3] and are themselves regulated by transcription factors (TFs), which form multilayered feedback and feedforward circuits that govern development, homeostasis, and tumor progression [4–6]. Despite extensive bulk-profiling efforts that have generated comprehensive miRNA atlases [7–9], quantifying miRNA expression at single-cell or context-specific resolution remains technically challenging [10, 11]. Conventional bulk RNA-seq captures mature miRNAs only through dedicated small-RNA or capture-based protocols, whereas single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) generally fails to recover nonpolyadenylated species because of poly(A)-primed library preparation [10, 11]. As a result, existing atlases provide broad tissue-level coverage but lack the resolution to delineate cell-type-specific or condition-dependent regulation [12–14], underscoring the need for computational approaches to infer miRNA expression from transcriptomic data.

  Computational efforts to infer miRNA behavior from transcriptomic data generally fall into two categories. Activity-based approaches, such as miREACT [15], estimate miRNA activity through enrichment of seed-motif targets and inverse expression correlations, analogous to TF activity inference in SCENIC [16]. While biologically motivated, these approaches are sensitive to database noise and rely on predicted interactions. In contrast, expression-based frameworks such as miRSCAPE [17] employ machine learning to directly predict miRNA expression from transcriptomic profiles, achieving high accuracy but offering limited biological interpretability. Extensions to single-cell data often assume that regulatory relationships derived from bulk datasets remain valid across cellular contexts [18–20], an assumption that rarely holds in heterogeneous tumors [18–21]. Consequently, there remains a need for interpretable, biologically informed, and generalizable models to infer miRNA expression (Supplementary Table S1). Recent studies in multi-omics integration have shown that biologically informed deep learning and graph-based modeling can improve interpretability and predictive utility in complex omics settings, including subtype-aware multi-omics learning [22] and context-specific transcriptomic imputation [23], supporting the value of structured modeling strategies for regulatory inference tasks.

  Despite their biological importance, miRNA expression profiling remains challenging, particularly in single-cell settings, due to low RNA abundance, lack of poly(A) tails, and technical noise [10, 24]. Moreover, large-scale paired mRNA–miRNA datasets are limited, restricting direct modeling of regulatory relationships [9, 10, 24]. To address these limitations, we developed miRSiC, an interpretable machine learning framework that infers miRNA expression by integrating both upstream TFs and experimentally validated downstream target genes (TGs). Unlike existing approaches that rely on seed-motif enrichment or treat transcripts as independent features, miRSiC explicitly models the bidirectional regulatory structure surrounding each miRNA. This design increases biological interpretability by aligning predictive features with known regulatory mechanisms and enables robust evaluation across both bulk and single-cell transcriptomic data. The miRSiC framework constructs miRNA-specific models using paired mRNA–miRNA profiles, incorporates regulatory context through TF and TG integration, and supports cross-domain analysis, including transfer from bulk to single-cell data and domain-specific retraining. Finally, reconstructed TF–miRNA–target networks provide mechanistic insight into the inferred expression patterns.

### Computational workflow of miRSiC
  The miRSiC framework integrates bulk and single-cell transcriptomic data to construct and evaluate biologically informed predictive models (Fig. 1). First, paired mRNA and miRNA expression profiles from the TCGA-BRCA cohort were processed through standardized normalization and filtering procedures. After quality control, expression-based filtering, and matching of mRNA and miRNA profiles, 1078 primary tumor samples were retained as the bulk modeling cohort. These samples were divided into a training set (N = 862) and a held-out test set (N = 216) before any supervised feature selection, model optimization, or performance evaluation. No adjacent normal tissue samples were included in model training or held-out testing. PAM50 molecular subtype annotations, including Basal-like, HER2-enriched, Luminal A, Luminal B, and Normal-like tumor subtypes, were used for subtype-aware performance assessment.

  TFs were annotated using the Human TF Database [25], resulting in 1424 candidate TF features prior to forward selection. Experimentally validated miRNA TGs were obtained from miRTarBase [26], yielding heterogeneous regulatory footprints across miRNAs. These upstream and downstream components defined the biologically informed feature space \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${X}_{TF}\ and\ {X}_{TG}$\end{document} used in the predictive formulation. Based on this formulation, three LightGBM-based regression models were constructed for each miRNA: a TG-only model, a TF-only model, and a combined TF–TG model. These complementary feature sets correspond to the three model formulations, enabling systematic evaluation of upstream, downstream, and integrated regulatory effects.

  To assess robustness across biological contexts, the trained models were evaluated on bulk RNA-seq data stratified by PAM50 subtypes and further extended to single-cell RNA-seq data from GSE151334 following appropriate normalization and imputation. Finally, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and miRNAs (DEmiRs) were integrated using the miRGTF-net framework to reconstruct multilayer TF–miRNA–TG regulatory networks, enabling biological interpretation of model outputs.


### Data acquisition and preprocessing
  Paired mRNA and miRNA expression data from the TCGA-BRCA cohort were downloaded from the Genomic Data Commons. To ensure a disease-specific modeling signal, adjacent normal tissue samples were strictly excluded from all downstream feature selection, training, and testing phases. After matching mRNA and miRNA expression profiles and applying expression-based filtering, the final bulk modeling cohort consisted of 1078 primary tumor samples (barcodes ending in “01”). To partition the bulk data into independent modeling sets, we applied a sequential split to the matched expression matrix. No random shuffling or seed initialization was performed prior to the split. The first 862 tumor samples were allocated to the bulk training set, and the remaining 216 tumor samples were reserved exclusively as the held-out test set. Because the native ordering of samples in the raw GDC download files carries no systematic biological or clinical bias, this sequential assignment effectively represents an unbiased 80/20 partition without replacement. The detailed PAM50 molecular subtype composition of the training and test partitions is provided in Supplementary Table S2.

  The held-out test set was reserved exclusively for final model evaluation and was not used during differential-miRNA selection, TF ranking, top-TF selection, or hyperparameter optimization. Tumor samples classified as Normal-like by PAM50 were retained as a molecular subtype and should not be interpreted as adjacent normal tissue. mRNA abundance was derived from RNA-seq and normalized using fragments per kilobase per million mapped reads–upper quartile (FPKM-UQ), whereas miRNA expression was normalized as reads per million (RPM). Genes and miRNAs expressed in at least 80% of samples were retained to ensure stable modeling input. All analyses were restricted to human miRNAs and genes. miRNA identifiers followed human-specific annotations (hsa-miR-*), ensuring consistency across TF annotations and experimentally validated miRNA–target interactions derived from miRTarBase. TFs were annotated using the Human TF Database, and experimentally validated miRNA–TG interactions were obtained from miRTarBase to provide biologically supported regulatory links. Only TFs and validated TGs present in the filtered expression matrix were retained for downstream modeling.

  For single-cell analyses, raw counts from GSE151334 (MCF7, HEK293T, and fibroblast cells) were obtained from GEO. To harmonize these sparse profiles with the bulk training distribution and mitigate technical dropout effects, raw counts were imputed using SAVER [27], converted to FPKM, normalized via FPKM-UQ, and finally log-transformed with a pseudocount of 1. To evaluate domain-specific modeling within the single-cell space, a total of 633 cells across the three cell types were divided into a training set of 507 cells and a testing set of 126 cells. This split was structured using cell-type stratified partitioning to maintain uniform cellular representation across the two subsets (Supplementary Table S3). The exact cell indices used in this split were identically mapped across all single-cell benchmarking experiments to guarantee direct comparability.

### Baseline methods and benchmarking configurations
  To rigorously evaluate the predictive performance and cross-domain robustness of miRSiC, we benchmarked our framework against two state-of-the-art miRNA inference algorithms representing distinct methodological paradigms: miRSCAPE (a machine learning–based approach) and miREACT (a statistical motif–based approach). To ensure a fair and direct quantitative comparison, both baseline methods were executed using the identical data partitions applied to miRSiC, including the 862 TCGA-BRCA bulk training samples, the 216 bulk held-out test samples, and the MCF7 single-cell profiles.


#### Execution of miRSCAPE
  miRSCAPE utilizes an extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) architecture to infer miRNA expression. Following the original protocol, we implemented the framework using the provided R scripts. The input bulk mRNA matrices were log-transformed and scaled using the native bulkTransform function. We trained independent XGBoost regressors for each miRNA from scratch on our 862 TCGA-BRCA bulk training samples using the default hyperparameters (nrounds = 50). For bulk evaluation, the trained models were directly applied to the 216 held-out samples. For single-cell evaluation, the MCF7 scRNA-seq raw counts were processed via Seurat, and the bulk-trained XGBoost weights were applied to the scaled single-cell expression matrix without additional domain-adaptation or quantile normalization steps.


#### Execution of miREACT
  Unlike machine learning frameworks, miREACT employs a training-free, statistical model based on 3′ untranslated region (UTR) motif enrichment. The algorithm computes motif background probabilities and utilizes an exponential decay statistical model to infer motif activity. We executed run_miReact() with standard parameters (species = “hs,” seq.type = “utr3,” alpha = 1e-10). Within the pipeline, median gene expression was calculated across the input cohort to establish fold-change ranks. Motif activity scores were subsequently computed and inversely mapped to represent miRNA activity. Notably, due to its reliance on static predefined motif databases and rigid paralogue deduplication rules (which inherently collapse distinct locus-specific variants such as -5p and -3p suffixes), miREACT could only map and generate predictions for 303 out of the 326 evaluated miRNAs.


#### Single-cell retraining and evaluation alignment
  Finally, to rigorously evaluate in-domain learning capacity on single-cell data, we further subjected miRSCAPE to the identical single-cell retraining protocol used for miRSiC. We trained new, independent XGBoost models for each miRNA using the 507 single-cells and evaluated their performance on the held-out 126 test cells. miREACT, being a training-free statistical framework, was evaluated directly on the 126 test cells to compute motif activities based on single-cell median gene expression fold-changes. Crucially, to ensure a strictly fair, apples-to-apples comparison, the single-cell evaluation metrics across all three methods were restricted to the exact same 58 miRNAs successfully modeled by miRSiC (defined as being expressed in at least 5% of cells with retained regulatory features).

### Regulatory network reconstruction
  TF–miRNA–TG regulatory networks were reconstructed using the miRGTF-net framework [30], which integrates curated regulatory databases with tissue-specific expression correlations. Regulatory interactions were obtained from miRTarBase [26] (miRNA–TGs), TransmiR [31] (TF–miRNA), and TRRUST [32] and HTRIdb [33] (TF–gene). miRNA nomenclature was standardized using miRBase [34].

  The framework integrates these regulatory interactions with transcriptomic and miRNA expression profiles to generate multilayer regulatory circuits comprising upstream TF regulators, miRNA intermediates, and downstream TGs. Paired miRNA and mRNA expression profiles from TCGA breast cancer samples were used to compute Spearman correlation coefficients, enabling incorporation of context-specific co-expression information into the reconstructed networks. DEGs and DEmiRs were first identified from the TCGA-BRCA cohort and used as input features for network assembly. These differential-expression results were used to subsequently prioritize regulatory components included in downstream network analyses and to support regulatory-network interpretation and visualization and were not used for model training, feature selection, hyperparameter tuning, or held-out test evaluation. PAM50 Normal-like samples were treated as tumor samples assigned to the Normal-like molecular subtype and were not used as the normal reference group.

  Regulatory network reconstruction integrated four evidence layers: curated regulatory interactions, expression-correlation evidence, differential expression evidence, and model-derived feature-importance evidence. Curated regulatory edges were obtained from miRTarBase for miRNA–target interactions, TransmiR for TF–miRNA interactions, and TRRUST/HTRIdb for TF–gene interactions. Expression evidence was incorporated by filtering candidate interactions using TCGA-BRCA Spearman correlations. Differential expression evidence was used to prioritize tumor-associated DEGs and DEmiRs, whereas LightGBM gain-based feature importance was used to identify TFs and TGs that contributed strongly to miRNA-specific prediction models. Thus, network edges should be interpreted as curated and expression-supported relationships, while model importance provides predictive prioritization rather than causal evidence.


## Results

### Optimizing miRNA prediction by forward selection of transcription factors
  To evaluate the contribution of TFs to miRNA expression prediction, we examined model performance using progressively refined TF subsets derived from the TCGA-BRCA dataset. A representative panel of 30 DEmiRs, comprising the 15 most up-regulated and 15 most down-regulated species, was selected to characterize the effects of TF-based feature reduction while maintaining computational feasibility (Fig. 2A). A baseline LightGBM model incorporating all 1424 annotated TFs achieved a mean Spearman correlation of 0.6346 across these DEmiRs. Gain-based feature importance analysis identified a subset of TFs that consistently contributed strongly to prediction across multiple miRNAs, although individual miRNAs retained distinct regulatory importance profiles (Fig. 2B and C). Representative importance rankings for the top five up- and down-regulated DEmiRs are shown in Fig. 2B and C, while full results for all 30 DEmiRs are provided in Supplementary Fig. S1.

  Models trained on the top 200, 600, and 900 TFs achieved comparable or modestly improved predictive performance relative to the full-TF baseline, with mean Spearman correlations of 0.6350, 0.6380, and 0.6414, respectively (Fig. 2D). Notably, predictive performance began to plateau beyond ~200 TFs, suggesting that a relatively compact subset of TFs captures most of the informative regulatory signal required for miRNA expression inference. In contrast to the relatively small differences in mean performance, feature refinement substantially reduced prediction variability across DEmiRs. Among the evaluated configurations, the 600-TF model exhibited the lowest variance and standard deviation, indicating the most stable predictive behavior (Fig. 2E; Supplementary Fig. S2).

  Taken together, these results indicate that forward TF selection primarily improves model robustness rather than maximizing predictive accuracy. Although mean Spearman correlations remained comparable across models using 200–900 TFs, the 600-TF subset achieved the best balance between predictive stability and feature compactness. This balance motivated its selection as the upstream regulatory feature set for downstream miRSiC analyses.


### Integrating upstream transcription factors and downstream targets improves bulk miRNA prediction
  To optimize miRNA expression prediction in breast cancer, we evaluated three biologically informed feature configurations, each representing a distinct regulatory perspective: (i) the TF-only model, which captures upstream transcriptional regulation as a proxy for miRNA activation; (ii) the Target-only model, reflecting downstream repression signatures via experimentally validated TGs; and (iii) the Combined model, which integrates both regulatory layers by jointly modeling the prioritized top 600 TFs alongside miRNA-specific validated targets. Individual LightGBM regressors were constructed for each miRNA using corresponding mRNA expression profiles, with predictive performance assessed using Spearman correlation coefficients.

  We first investigated whether upstream transcriptional signals alone harbor sufficient information to recapitulate miRNA expression patterns. Using a representative panel of 30 DEmiRs (15 up-regulated and 15 down-regulated), the TF-only models demonstrated clear monotonic relationships between predicted and observed miRNA levels (Fig. 3A and B). Both elevated and suppressed miRNA expression patterns were partially captured using TF features alone, indicating that transcriptional programs encode essential regulatory signals and establishing a robust baseline for downstream integrative modeling. To further illustrate the effects of incorporating downstream targets, corresponding scatter plots for the combined TF–Target model are provided in Supplementary Figs S3 and S4. Integration of both regulatory layers results in visibly tighter clustering around the identity line for several miRNAs, particularly among highly dysregulated species, supporting the incremental gain observed in global performance comparisons.

  A systematic comparison across the entire transcriptome (326 expressed miRNAs) revealed that the combined TF–Target framework yielded superior performance over single-layer models (Fig. 3C). Specifically, the integrated model achieved a mean Spearman correlation of 0.5462, surpassing the TF-only (0.5371) and Target-only (0.4914) configurations. The full performance distributions for each feature configuration are shown in Supplementary Figs S5, S6 and S7. These histograms illustrate that the combined model not only shifts the central tendency toward higher correlation values but also expands the right tail of the distribution, indicating improved prediction for a subset of highly predictable miRNAs. Notably, the number of miRNAs achieving high predictive accuracy (Spearman ρ ≥ 0.7) increased from 34 in the TF-only configuration to 38 in the combined model. Pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests further demonstrated significant differences in predictive performance among the three feature configurations. The Combined model significantly outperformed both the TF-only model (P = 8.85 × 10−5) and the Target-only model (P = 1.10 × 10−44), whereas the TF-only model also outperformed the Target-only model (P = 4.27 × 10−31). The top 50 most predictable miRNAs ranked by test-set Spearman correlation are summarized in Supplementary Table S5. Several well-established breast cancer-associated miRNAs, including hsa-miR-205, hsa-miR-210, and members of the let-7 family, were among the highest-ranking entries, suggesting that strong predictive performance aligns with biologically relevant regulatory signatures. The biological relevance of these highly predictable miRNAs is supported by multiple lines of breast cancer literature. hsa-miR-205 has been implicated in epithelial identity, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) suppression, and therapy response, including regulation of ZEB1/SIP1-related EMT programs, radiosensitivity through ZEB1/Ubc13, and p53-associated tumor-suppressive signaling in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) [35–37]. hsa-miR-210 represents a hypoxia-associated breast cancer miRNA and has been linked to HIF-dependent hypoxic adaptation, tumor proliferation and invasion, poor clinical outcome, and treatment-related prognosis [38–40]. Members of the let-7 family are broadly connected to tumor-suppressive regulatory programs, including LIN28-linked stemness control, suppression of cancer-promoting stromal activation, and inhibition of HK2-mediated glycolysis and metastasis [41–43]. hsa-miR-150 further illustrates the context-dependent nature of miRNA regulation in breast cancer: it has been reported to promote malignant phenotypes through P2X7- and SRCIN1-related mechanisms in some settings, but to suppress TNBC metastasis through HMGA2 in others [44–46]. Together, the enrichment of these biologically characterized miRNAs among the best-predicted candidates suggests that miRSiC preferentially captures miRNAs embedded in strong and transcriptionally visible regulatory programs, rather than simply identifying statistically predictable but biologically uninformative signals.

  To further quantify the practical contribution of target-gene features beyond the TF-only model, we performed a paired per-miRNA gain/loss analysis on the held-out test set. For each miRNA, the gain from adding target-gene features was defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\Delta \rho ={\rho}_{(Combined)}-{\rho}_{\left( TF- only\right)}$\end{document}. Across 326 miRNAs, the combined TF–TG model showed a modest but statistically significant improvement over the TF-only model (mean Spearman ρ: 0.5462 versus 0.5371; mean Δρ = 0.0091; median Δρ = 0.0066; paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P = 8.95 × 10−5; Supplementary Fig. S8A–C and Supplementary Table S6). Overall, 191 of 326 miRNAs (58.6%) showed nominal improvement (Δρ > 0), whereas 135 miRNAs (41.4%) showed decreased performance after adding target-gene features. To identify miRNAs with more pronounced benefit, we defined the top 30 miRNAs ranked by Δρ as the meaningful-gain subset, corresponding to a Δρ cutoff of ~0.0495. This subset represented 30 of 326 miRNAs (9.2%), among which 18 miRNAs showed bootstrap-supported robust improvement. These results indicate that target-gene features provide selective rather than uniform benefit, with the strongest gains concentrated in a subset of miRNAs.

  We next examined whether the gain from target-gene features was associated with miRNA-level properties. Continuous association analysis showed weak but statistically detectable positive associations between Δρ and miRNA mean abundance (Spearman r = 0.125, P = .024) and between Δρ and the number of validated TGs retained in the expression matrix (Spearman r = 0.113, P = .042), whereas miRNA expression variance was not significantly associated with Δρ (Spearman r = 0.086, P = .121). Categorical comparison across loss, nominal-gain, and meaningful-gain groups further showed significant differences in miRNA abundance (Kruskal–Wallis P = .0026), while miRNA variance and validated target count did not differ significantly across groups (Supplementary Fig. S8D and Supplementary Table S7). These findings suggest that the incremental benefit of target-gene features is modestly associated with miRNA abundance and regulatory feature coverage, although these factors alone do not fully explain the heterogeneous gain across miRNAs.

  To determine whether the observed target-gene contribution reflected miRNA-specific regulatory information rather than increased feature dimensionality alone, we performed negative-control analyses by replacing validated TGs with random expressed genes, expression-matched random genes, or TGs from permuted miRNA–target mappings while preserving feature-set size. These analyses were restricted to miRNAs with at least one experimentally validated TG retained in the transcriptomic feature space. Accordingly, 279 of the 326 evaluated miRNAs were included in the full negative-control analysis, whereas 47 miRNAs were excluded because they lacked retained validated TGs and therefore could not undergo size-matched target-gene substitution.

  Among the 279 miRNAs eligible for negative-control analysis, the true TF–TG model showed only modest differences from the control feature sets, with small median advantages over expression-matched random genes (median Δρ = 0.0046, Wilcoxon P = 9.45 × 10−3) and permuted target mappings (median Δρ = 0.0050, Wilcoxon P = 1.25 × 10−3), while the difference from random expressed genes did not reach statistical significance (median Δρ = 0.0032, Wilcoxon P = .078; Supplementary Fig. S9A and Supplementary Table S8). However, within the meaningful-gain subset, 24 of the 30 miRNAs had retained validated TGs and were therefore eligible for subset-specific negative-control analysis. In this subset, true validated TGs consistently outperformed all three negative controls, with median Δρ values of 0.0486 versus random expressed genes, 0.0428 versus expression-matched random genes, and 0.0375 versus permuted target mappings (all Wilcoxon P < 5 × 10−5; Supplementary Fig. S9B and Supplementary Table S8). These results suggest that the regulatory-aware target-gene layer provides miRNA-specific biological signal primarily for the subset of miRNAs that meaningfully benefit from target-gene features, rather than uniformly across the entire miRNA repertoire.

  To further elucidate the factors driving prediction heterogeneity, we examined the relationship between model accuracy and the regulatory footprint of individual miRNAs. While most miRNAs were associated with fewer than 500 validated targets, a subset exhibited extensive repertoires exceeding 2000 genes (Fig. 3D). We observed a moderate positive association between the TG count and predictive performance (Pearson r = 0.34; Fig. 3E), suggesting that downstream target expression provides additional predictive information that complements TF signals, particularly for miRNAs with widespread regulatory influence. Collectively, these results demonstrate that while upstream TFs provide a necessary regulatory scaffold, explicit integration of downstream target-gene expression patterns enhances predictive fidelity and robustness, supporting the use of the combined miRSiC framework for miRNA expression inference in breast cancer. These results should be interpreted as evidence that validated target-gene expression provides complementary predictive information, rather than as evidence that target expression directly measures miRNA abundance.


### Comparative assessment of machine learning and deep learning approaches for miRNA expression prediction
  Having established that integrating TFs and downstream targets improves miRNA expression prediction, we next asked whether the observed performance gains arise primarily from the biologically informed feature design or from the specific choice of machine learning algorithm. To address this question, we systematically compared our approach against representative machine learning frameworks (LightGBM, XGBoost) and deep learning–based graph neural networks (GCNs, hybrid Lasso + GCN, and Heterogeneous Graph Attention Network, HeteroGAT). The comparisons were conducted using the exact same feature configuration, evaluated by the mean miRNA-wise Spearman correlation across 326 expressed miRNAs.

  Among the evaluated methods, LightGBM achieved the highest overall performance, with a mean Spearman correlation of 0.546. In addition to its superior mean performance, LightGBM exhibited comparable or lower variance relative to alternative methods (Supplementary Table S9).

  XGBoost, another tree-based ensemble method, achieved a lower mean correlation of 0.437. For the deep learning models, graph-based modeling using GCN yielded a mean correlation of 0.404. The hybrid Lasso + GCN method achieved a mean correlation of 0.459, reflecting partial gains from combining feature selection with deep graph propagation.

  To further investigate whether dynamically weighting regulatory edges could improve predictions, we implemented a Methodological Sensitivity Analysis using deep HeteroGAT. Despite rigorous training optimization (200 epochs with adaptive learning rate scheduling), HeteroGAT severely underperformed in predicting individual miRNA expression trends across samples, yielding a mean miRNA-wise Spearman correlation near zero (−0.024), even though it captured moderate within-sample ranking (mean sample-wise correlation = 0.345).

  This performance drop highlights a fundamental challenge in applying deep graph neural networks to omics inference: biological regulatory networks often contain inherent noise and false-positive edges that can mislead deep attention mechanisms, causing oversmoothing. In contrast, LightGBM’s tree-based splitting criterion functions as a dynamic and robust feature selector, effectively filtering uninformative regulatory signals.

  Overall, these results indicate that LightGBM provides the most effective balance between predictive accuracy and robustness. While deep learning–based graph approaches show theoretical potential for modeling regulatory interactions, their performance in this highly sparse, tabular setting did not surpass that of gradient boosting methods.


### Comparative performance evaluation across breast cancer subtypes
  We evaluated the generalizability of miRNA expression prediction across breast cancer molecular subtypes to assess whether model performance varied under distinct biological and data-structural contexts. To provide a reference for subtype-specific heterogeneity in the prediction task itself, we first examined the expression patterns of the 15 most up-regulated and 15 most down-regulated miRNAs identified based on log2 fold change between tumor and normal samples. As shown in Fig. 4A, clear subtype-dependent expression patterns were observed, with basal-like tumors exhibiting the most distinct miRNA profiles, while Luminal A (LumA) and Luminal B (LumB) displayed greater similarity. Several miRNAs showed pronounced subtype-associated differences, including elevated hsa-miR-210 expression in HER2-enriched tumors and reduced expression of hsa-miR-375 and hsa-miR-184 in basal-like tumors, highlighting substantial heterogeneity in the underlying expression landscape across subtypes.

  Model performance in the training set was uniformly high across all subtypes, with Spearman correlations exceeding 0.94 (Fig. 4B), reflecting effective in-sample fitting under each molecular context. In contrast, test-set performance revealed greater variability across subtypes (Fig. 4C), with mean Spearman correlations ranging from 0.3771 in HER2-enriched tumors to 0.4935 in LumA. Among the evaluated subtypes, LumA exhibited the most consistent predictive performance, whereas HER2-enriched and normal samples showed broader dispersion and occasional extreme outliers, indicating reduced generalization stability. Pairwise comparisons of subtype-specific performance distributions further revealed statistically significant differences between several subtype pairs, particularly involving HER2-enriched tumors (Supplementary Table S10), supporting the presence of subtype-dependent variation in predictive behavior.

  To further assess whether data availability contributed to subtype-specific variability in prediction performance, we examined the lowest-performing miRNA, hsa-miR-6806, as a representative case. As summarized in Supplementary Table S11, predictive performance increased with the number of available samples, ranging from a Spearman correlation of −0.821 in the normal group (7 samples) to 0.459 in LumB (51 samples). Representative regression plots for hsa-miR-6806 across subtypes are shown in Supplementary Fig. S10. Consistent with the tabulated results, model fit is substantially stronger in LumB compared to the normal group, visually reinforcing the impact of cohort size and expression variability on predictive stability. Across subtypes, sample size was positively associated with predictive performance (Spearman ρ = 0.71; Fig. 4D), suggesting that cohort size contributes to observed differences in model generalization.

  At the miRNA level, predictive performance was more consistent in LumA and LumB subtypes, where most miRNAs exhibited moderate to high correlations. In contrast, HER2-enriched and normal subtypes displayed greater dispersion in performance, with the normal group showing the widest correlation range (−0.64 to 0.93). Together, these results indicate that while the proposed modeling framework generalizes reliably in well-represented molecular subtypes, performance variability in underrepresented groups reflects both expression heterogeneity (Fig. 4A) and limited sample size, underscoring the importance of subtype-aware evaluation when interpreting miRNA prediction performance.


### Cross-cancer transferability of BRCA-trained miRSiC models
  To assess whether the regulatory patterns learned from breast cancer remained informative in other tumor contexts, we applied all 326 BRCA-trained combined TF–TG models to TCGA-LIHC, TCGA-LUAD, and TCGA-OV without retraining. Across the evaluated miRNAs, the median Spearman correlations between predicted and measured expression were 0.222 in LIHC, 0.251 in LUAD, and 0.210 in OV, with corresponding mean correlations of 0.233, 0.256, and 0.222, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S13A and Supplementary Table S12).

  Although overall performance was lower than that observed in the held-out TCGA-BRCA test set, a substantial subset of miRNA models retained moderate or high predictive performance across cancer types. In total, 104 models in LIHC, 128 models in LUAD, and 94 models in OV achieved Spearman correlations of at least 0.30. Among these, 28, 24, and 15 models, respectively, achieved correlations of at least 0.50. The highest-performing models included hsa-miR-150 in LIHC (ρ = 0.782), hsa-miR-205 in LUAD (ρ =0.868), and hsa-miR-205 in OV (ρ =0.887; Supplementary Fig. S13B and Supplementary Table S12).

  Several miRNAs showed consistently strong transferability across more than one cancer type. For example, hsa-miR-150, hsa-miR-483, hsa-miR-155, hsa-miR-196b, and hsa-miR-10a were among the higher-ranking predictors in multiple cohorts. Nevertheless, considerable variation was observed across individual miRNAs and tumor types, indicating that cross-cancer transferability was selective rather than universal. Collectively, these results demonstrate that a subset of the regulatory signals captured by miRSiC is conserved across distinct cancer contexts while also emphasizing the influence of tumor-specific regulatory architecture on prediction performance.


### Construction and analysis of transcription factor-miRNA–target gene networks
  To examine whether the regulatory signals captured by miRSiC are consistent with known transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulatory relationships, we analyzed TF–miRNA–TG regulatory networks reconstructed from the TCGA breast cancer cohort. Network reconstruction revealed regulatory connections linking upstream TFs with differentially expressed miRNAs and their downstream TGs, providing a systems-level view of regulatory circuits associated with miRNA expression patterns predicted by miRSiC.

  From the TCGA-BRCA cohort, 16,293 protein-coding genes were retained for downstream analysis. Differential expression analysis identified 4271 DEGs, including 1538 up-regulated and 2733 down-regulated genes (Fig. 5A). For miRNA data, 326 highly expressed miRNAs were retained, among which 104 DEmiRs were identified (45 up-regulated and 59 down-regulated; Fig. 5B). Network analyses focused on the 30 most extreme DEmiRs (15 up-regulated and 15 down-regulated), which were also used in earlier modeling evaluations. The resulting regulatory networks were further filtered using confidence criteria including expression correlations above the median (top 50%), interaction scores within the top 10%, and a minimum incoming regulatory score of 0.1. Nodes were colored by differential expression status (red, up-regulated; blue, down-regulated), with TFs represented as circles, miRNAs as triangles, and TGs as squares [47].

  To illustrate how model-informative features align with established regulatory architectures, we examined several representative miRNA-centered subnetworks. miR-21-5p, a well-established oncomiR, was linked to upstream regulators including TGFB1 and BMPR1B, as well as the tumor suppressor TGFBR3, consistent with previously reported regulatory relationships in breast cancer (Fig. 5C) [48–51]. Similarly, the miR-200a subnetwork highlighted its association with EZH2 and downstream repression of EMT-related genes such as ZEB1, ZEB2, and EGFR (Fig. 5D), reflecting known roles in epithelial–mesenchymal transition regulation [35, 52, 53]. In addition, the miR-200c network suggested a potential regulatory axis involving FOXM1 and ERG (Fig. 5E), consistent with reported associations in proliferative and oncogenic signaling contexts [54, 55].

  Collectively, these network analyses demonstrate that TFs and TGs selected by the miRSiC predictive models are embedded within biologically coherent regulatory circuits. This consistency supports the interpretability of the predictive models and reinforces that miRNA expression inference is grounded in established transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulatory architectures rather than purely statistical associations. In this analysis, differential expression was defined using the tumor-versus-adjacent-normal comparison, whereas the miRSiC predictive models were trained and evaluated only on primary tumor samples. These subnetworks should therefore be interpreted as model-supported regulatory hypotheses that integrate curated interactions, expression correlation, differential expression, and feature-importance evidence, rather than as experimentally validated causal regulatory mechanisms.


### Application and insights from single-cell data in breast cancer research
  While the network analysis above supports the biological plausibility of the regulatory signals captured by miRSiC, it remains unclear whether these regulatory relationships generalize across different transcriptomic modalities. To evaluate cross-platform generalizability, we applied the miRSiC framework to paired single-cell miRNA–mRNA expression data from GEO dataset GSE151334, comprising 278 fibroblasts, 260 HEK293T cells, and 95 MCF7 breast cancer cells. To harmonize dynamic ranges between bulk and single-cell transcriptomes, expression values were transformed using log2(FPKM-UQ + 1). As shown in Fig. 6A, single-cell data exhibited broader and partially bimodal expression distributions consistent with cellular heterogeneity and dropout-driven sparsity, whereas bulk RNA-seq data displayed narrower unimodal distributions. This discrepancy highlights a pronounced domain shift between bulk and single-cell transcriptomic landscapes.

  To evaluate cross-platform generalizability, we applied the miRSiC framework to paired single-cell miRNA–mRNA expression data from GEO dataset GSE151334. We first systematically benchmarked the transferability of the bulk-trained miRSiC model to 95 MCF7 single-cells under five distinct preprocessing strategies (Fig. 6B). These configurations included: (1) nonimputed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\log}_2\left(\mathrm{FPKM}-\mathrm{UQ}+1\right)$\end{document}; (2) imputed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\log}_2\left(\mathrm{FPKM}-\mathrm{UQ}+1\right)$\end{document}; (3) imputed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\log}_2\left(\mathrm{raw}\ \mathrm{count}+1\right)$\end{document}; (4) standard library size normalization (LogNormalize, Scanpy); and (5) analytical Pearson residuals (Scanpy).

  Across all five strategies, predictive performance remained consistently low, with median Spearman correlations ranging from 0.001 to 0.064. Notably, even advanced single-cell-specific protocols such as LogNormalize (Strategy 4) and Pearson residuals (Strategy 5) failed to trigger a substantial performance breakthrough compared to the baseline, yielding median ρ values of only 0.055 and 0.064, respectively. In addition, comparison of the predicted and observed miRNA expression distributions revealed a systematic rightward shift in predicted values across the evaluated preprocessing pipelines, indicating overestimation of low-expression miRNAs that persisted despite imputation (Supplementary Fig. S11). These findings indicate that the cross-platform transfer bottleneck is fundamentally driven by the inherent technical limitations of single-cell data, including extreme sparsity and zero-inflation, rather than the choice of normalization methodology. Given this performance ceiling, adhering to the FPKM-UQ-aligned normalization framework remains a robust decision, as it preserves direct metric alignment with the bulk training distribution while maintaining a comparable performance baseline.

  To determine whether domain-specific training could mitigate these limitations, we trained a single-cell-specific model using paired mRNA–miRNA expression profiles from 507 cells and evaluated performance on a held-out test set of 126 cells. Among 58 miRNAs expressed in at least 5% of cells, predictive performance improved significantly relative to the bulk-trained model (Fig. 6C). As shown in Fig. 6C, the distribution of Spearman correlations was right-skewed (mean ρ =0.1927; maximum ρ =0.7815), indicating that regulatory relationships remain partially recoverable when the model is optimized within the single-cell domain. In contrast, the bulk-trained model applied to the same 45 overlapping miRNAs resulted in near-zero predictive performance (mean ρ =0.0294; Fig. 6D), confirming that bulk-derived regulatory weights do not directly generalize to sparse single-cell environments.

  Feature utilization varied substantially across miRNAs, as reflected by the number of nonzero importance TFs and TGs contributing to each model (Fig. 6E). MiRNAs achieving higher predictive accuracy were generally associated with broader effective regulatory footprints, whereas poorly predicted miRNAs tended to exhibit sparse or weakly informative feature sets. This heterogeneity in effective regulatory signal density partially explains the uneven performance distribution observed across miRNAs (Fig. 6C). Stratification by cell type revealed comparable median performance across fibroblasts, HEK293T, and MCF7 cells (Fig. 6F), suggesting that miRNA-specific regulatory complexity may contribute more strongly than gross cell-type differences to the observed performance variability. Consistent with the overall bulk-to-single-cell transfer failure, cell-type-specific evaluation of the bulk-trained model showed uniformly weak performance across fibroblasts, HEK293T, and MCF7 cells (Fig. 6G).

  Collectively, these findings demonstrate that bulk-trained miRNA inference models do not generalize effectively to single-cell transcriptomes due to distributional shifts and severe sparsity. However, retraining within the single-cell domain partially restores predictive capability, underscoring the necessity of domain-aware modeling strategies when extending miRNA expression inference frameworks to high-resolution transcriptomic contexts.

## Discussion
  In this study, we developed miRSiC as an interpretable modeling framework for miRNA expression inference that explicitly integrates upstream TFs and downstream validated TGs. Conceptually, our results suggest that miRNA inference should be viewed not simply as a transcriptome-to-expression regression task but as the reconstruction of a biologically structured regulatory state. In this setting, TFs represent upstream regulatory inputs, whereas TGs reflect the downstream transcriptional footprint associated with miRNA activity. By integrating these two regulatory layers, miRSiC provides a structured representation of miRNA-centered regulatory states beyond single-layer approaches.

  A key finding of this study is that upstream transcriptional signals alone provide a strong but incomplete basis for miRNA expression prediction. While the combined model yielded only modest improvement in mean test-set correlation over the TF-only models (mean Spearman ρ = 0.5462 versus 0.5371), gains were concentrated among highly predictable miRNAs (ρ ≥ 0.70) increasing from 34 to 38 (Supplementary Figs S5–S7). This suggests that downstream target information does not uniformly improve prediction across all miRNAs but instead preferentially benefits a subset of highly predictable miRNAs. This pattern may explain why the overall performance gain appears modest despite meaningful improvements in high-confidence predictions. Consistent with this interpretation, miRNAs with broader validated target repertoires tended to be more predictable, indicating that extensive downstream repression may generate stronger transcriptomic signals that can be recovered from mRNA data. The presence of well-characterized breast cancer–related miRNAs, including hsa-miR-205, hsa-miR-210, let-7 family members, and hsa-miR-150, among the highly predictable candidates further supports this interpretation. These miRNAs are associated with EMT regulation, hypoxia, stromal activation, metabolism, treatment response, and subtype-dependent tumor progression, suggesting that miRSiC may preferentially recover miRNAs embedded in strong and biologically coherent transcriptional programs. Importantly, miRSiC should not be interpreted as uniformly accurate for all miRNAs. Predictive performance varied substantially across miRNAs, indicating that the framework is most reliable for miRNAs with strong, stable, and transcriptomically visible regulatory footprints. In contrast, miRNAs with low abundance, limited validated target annotations, weak downstream transcriptional effects, or highly context-dependent regulation may be difficult to infer from mRNA expression alone. Therefore, miRSiC predictions should be interpreted at the individual-miRNA level, and per-miRNA performance metrics should be considered when prioritizing inferred miRNAs for downstream biological analysis.

  Our comparative analyses further indicate that biologically informed feature construction can contribute more to predictive performance than increasing model complexity alone. By explicitly distinguishing upstream regulators from downstream target-gene consequences, miRSiC provides a structured representation of miRNA-centered regulatory context. Under the same regulatory feature setting, LightGBM outperformed XGBoost, GCN, and Lasso + GCN, suggesting that performance gains were driven primarily by regulatory feature design rather than architectural complexity. This interpretation was further supported by network analysis, in which model-prioritized features formed biologically coherent TF–miRNA–target circuits. These findings suggest that future miRNA inference frameworks should prioritize biologically structured feature representation, miRNA-specific modeling, and interpretability while recognizing that more complex architectures may be beneficial only when paired with appropriate regulatory context, consistent with our previous work on multi-omics graph learning and context-aware transcriptomic modeling [22, 23].

  Recent ncRNA interaction-prediction studies provide complementary support for this interpretation. ZeroStem integrates biological prior knowledge, graph Transformer-based macro-topological representations, and meta-path-guided state-space modeling for zero-shot circRNA–miRNA interaction prediction [56]. GraCMI combines multi-hop graph structural modeling with denoised molecular attribute features to improve cancer-related circRNA–miRNA interaction (CMI) prediction [57], while EGATCMI fuses Transformer-derived sequence features, sequence similarity, graph attention–based structural information, and LightGBM classification for CMI prediction [58]. Although these frameworks address circRNA–miRNA interaction prediction rather than miRNA expression inference, they reinforce the broader principle that ncRNA regulatory modeling benefits from biologically structured, multi-source feature integration.

  Comparable principles have also been demonstrated across other biomedical prediction tasks. Transformer-based sequence encoding combined with imbalance-aware multi-label learning has enabled the simultaneous identification of antimicrobial peptides and their functional activities, illustrating the importance of aligning model architecture and learning objectives with the structure of the prediction task [59]. Similarly, KinasePhos 3.0 integrated experimentally verified phosphorylation-site annotations with machine-learning prediction and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)-based feature attribution, demonstrating how curated biological evidence can be incorporated to improve both predictive utility and model interpretability [60]. Together, these studies support the broader view that effective biomedical prediction depends not only on model complexity but also on task-specific representation, biologically informed feature construction, and interpretable learning strategies.

  Our findings indicate that miRNA inference is fundamentally governed by biological and technical context. The importance of domain-specific feature representation has also been observed in other high-dimensional biomedical data modalities. For example, peak binning of MALDI-TOF mass spectra was used to mitigate peak-shifting effects and transform heterogeneous spectral signals into structured features for large-scale machine-learning classification of methicillin-resistant and methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus [61]. Although this application differs from transcriptomic inference, it similarly demonstrates that preprocessing and feature representation must be adapted to the technical characteristics of the underlying measurement platform. Prediction was more stable in well-represented breast cancer subtypes, whereas smaller or more heterogeneous subtypes showed greater variability. Similarly, bulk-trained models fail to generalize to sparse single-cell transcriptomes, even after imputation, whereas single-cell retraining partially restored performance. While these results suggest that miRNA regulatory signals are conserved across modalities, robust inference across heterogeneous cohorts and platforms requires modality-appropriate learning through subtype-aware benchmarking and domain-aware modeling.

  Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, reliance on curated TF and miRNA–target annotations may limit the discovery of novel or condition-specific interactions. Curated miRNA–target databases are not complete and are biased toward well-studied miRNAs, cancer-associated miRNAs, and experimentally validated interactions. Consequently, the observed association between target repertoire size and predictability may partly reflect annotation bias rather than purely biological regulatory breadth. Second, gain-based feature importance reflects predictive relevance rather than causal regulation. Target-gene expression is not a direct readout of miRNA abundance. It may reflect miRNA-associated regulatory effects but is also influenced by transcriptional regulation, copy-number variation, tumor composition, tumor state, and other post-transcriptional mechanisms. Accordingly, target-gene features in miRSiC should be interpreted as indirect regulatory footprints and predictive covariates rather than causal measurements of miRNA activity. Third, the primary model was developed using TCGA-BRCA samples with matched mRNA and miRNA expression profiles, which reduced the final modelling cohort relative to the total number of available TCGA-BRCA samples. Although cross-cancer evaluation in TCGA-LIHC, TCGA-LUAD, and TCGA-OV supported partial transferability of the BRCA-trained models, all bulk cohorts were derived from the TCGA framework. Further validation in fully independent non-TCGA cohorts, across distinct sequencing platforms, and in prospectively collected datasets remains necessary to establish broader generalizability. Fourth, transfer to single-cell data also remains constrained by sparsity and modality mismatch. Direct transfer from bulk to single-cell data is constrained by zero inflation, dropout, reduced dynamic range, and cell-to-cell heterogeneity, which can disrupt the co-expression relationships learned from population-averaged bulk transcriptomes. Therefore, single-cell predictions should be interpreted cautiously, particularly for lowly expressed or sparsely detected miRNAs. Fifth, although the reconstructed TF–miRNA–target subnetworks were biologically coherent, they should not be interpreted as causal regulatory maps. Gain-based feature importance reflects predictive relevance, and the network edges combine curated interaction databases with expression-based filtering rather than perturbation-based evidence. Future experimental validation, such as TF perturbation, miRNA mimic/inhibitor assays, or target-gene reporter assays, will be required to confirm causal regulatory relationships. Future work should focus on improving miRSiC in four directions: expanding validated regulatory annotations, incorporating perturbation or longitudinal datasets to strengthen causal interpretation, developing domain-adaptation strategies for cross-platform transfer, and extending the framework to additional cancer types and noncancer transcriptomic contexts. The continued development of comprehensive molecular resources may facilitate this expansion. For example, dbPTM 2025 integrates large-scale experimentally validated post-translational modification annotations with cancer proteomic profiles, kinase activity information, functional annotations, and regulatory networks, illustrating how curated molecular evidence and disease-specific omics data can be organized to support context-aware biological interpretation [62]. Analogous integration of expanded TF–miRNA and miRNA–target evidence may further improve the biological coverage and interpretability of future miRSiC models. In single-cell applications, future improvements may require feature harmonization, transfer learning, and paired single-cell miRNA/mRNA datasets to reduce the impact of zero inflation and modality mismatch.

  For practical application to new datasets without matched miRNA expression, miRSiC should be used according to the similarity between the target dataset and the training domain. For breast cancer bulk transcriptomic datasets with compatible gene features and normalization, users may apply the supplied TCGA-BRCA-trained models for exploratory inference, with predictions interpreted primarily as relative miRNA expression rankings. When paired mRNA–miRNA profiles are available, training a cohort-specific miRSiC model is recommended because performance can be affected by tumor subtype composition, platform-specific expression distributions, and cohort-specific regulatory programs. For datasets from different cancer types, distinct sequencing platforms, or sparse single-cell settings, transfer learning, feature harmonization, or domain-specific retraining should be considered. In particular, direct application of bulk-trained models to single-cell data should be avoided unless appropriate domain adaptation or paired single-cell retraining data are available.

  Overall, miRSiC provides an interpretable and biologically grounded framework for miRNA expression inference that links predictive modeling with regulatory network structure. With further validation and domain adaptation, it may provide a foundation for explainable miRNA inference across bulk and single-cell transcriptomic data.
