# Post-Translational Modification–Driven Metabolic Reprogramming Shapes Melanoma Progression and Immune Microenvironment


## Background
  Post-translational modifications (PTMs) critically regulate protein function, yet their intratumoral heterogeneity and clinical relevance in melanoma remain poorly characterized. Elucidating PTM-driven programs may uncover novel mechanisms of tumor progression and therapeutic vulnerability.


## Methods
  We implemented an integrative multi-scale framework combining single-cell RNA sequencing (GSE215120), bulk transcriptomic cohorts (TCGA-SKCM and GSE19234), and spatial transcriptomics (VISDS000459). Twenty curated PTM functional programs were quantified using AUCell and ssGSEA. PTM-defined melanoma subpopulations were interrogated for transcription factor activity, cell-cell communication, copy number variation, and metabolic reprogramming. A PTM-based prognostic signature was constructed using LASSO-Cox regression, validated across independent cohorts, and interpreted using SHAP and LIME. Spatial transcriptomics was used to resolve the tissue localization of prognostic genes. Drug sensitivity was predicted with oncoPredict and evaluated by molecular docking.


## Results
  PTM-based stratification identified melanoma subtypes with distinct proliferative, metabolic, and immune states. Melanoma-high cells exhibited elevated PTM-associated transcriptional enrichment, increased CNV burden, enhanced glycolysis/PPP/TCA metabolism, and dominant VEGF, MIF, GALECTIN, CXCL, and PDGF signaling, whereas Melanoma-low cells were enriched in antigen presentation and IFN-γ-related pathways. A 15-gene PTM risk score robustly stratified overall survival in TCGA-SKCM (p = 4.52×10−12) and GSE19234 (p = 0.027), with predictive performance comparable to clinical stage and superior to age and sex. High-risk tumors showed increased tumor mutation burden, reduced immune and stromal infiltration, lower immunophenoscores, widespread immune checkpoint activation, and suppressed cytolytic activity. SHAP and LIME highlighted FOXM1, FOXK1, TTYH2, SLC25A15, and FCGR2A as key contributors. Drug modeling suggested heightened sensitivity of high-risk tumors to kinase and cell-cycle inhibitors, supported by favorable TTYH2-drug docking.


## Conclusion
  This PTM-centered integrative framework delineates metabolic and immune remodeling in melanoma, establishes an interpretable prognostic model, and identifies candidate therapeutic vulnerabilities for precision oncology.


## Introduction
  Melanoma is a highly aggressive skin cancer with strong metastatic potential and therapeutic resistance.1 While mutations in BRAF, NRAS, and NF1 are well-established drivers, they do not fully account for the tumor’s heterogeneity and variable clinical outcomes.2,3 The tumor microenvironment (TME)—comprising immune cells, fibroblasts, and stromal elements—interacts with melanoma cells to modulate progression and immune evasion.4 Although targeted therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors have improved outcomes, resistance remains common.

  Recent advances in single-cell RNA sequencing have revealed that melanoma cells exist along a dynamic phenotypic continuum, including melanocytic, neural crest-like, and mesenchymal-like states.4 This plasticity contributes to disease progression and therapy resistance. However, the upstream regulatory mechanisms underlying this heterogeneity remain poorly defined.

  PTMs represent a crucial regulatory layer influencing protein function, localization, and turnover. Modifications such as phosphorylation, ubiquitination, acetylation, and methylation affect signaling, gene expression, metabolism, and epigenetic reprogramming.5 Aberrant PTM-associated transcriptional activity has been implicated in sustaining oncogenic signaling, DNA repair, immune evasion, and metabolic rewiring in melanoma.6 Yet, comprehensive single-cell analyses of PTM-associated transcriptional activity remain scarce.

  In this study, we integrated single-cell transcriptomic data (GSE215120), bulk RNA-seq cohorts (TCGA-SKCM and GSE19234), and the spatial transcriptomic dataset VISDS000459 to characterize PTM-associated transcriptional programs in melanoma. Functional stratification was performed using curated gene sets corresponding to 20 PTM types, including lactylation, acetylation, ubiquitination, methylation, phosphorylation, SUMOylation, neddylation, hydroxylation, glycosylation, and citrullination. These PTM-associated signatures were quantified using AUCell and ssGSEA and were used for functional stratification of melanoma cells, followed by analyses of transcription factor activity, copy number variation, metabolic reprogramming, immune features, and clinical prognosis. Spatial transcriptomics was further used to contextualize the tissue localization of selected prognostic genes within the melanoma microenvironment.

## Materials and Methods

### Single-Cell Transcriptomic Analysis and PTM Signature Profiling

#### Preprocessing and Annotation of Single-Cell RNA-Seq Data
  Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data of melanoma (GEO accession GSE215120) were downloaded in January 2025. Raw count matrices were processed in R (v4.3.2) using Seurat (v4.3.0). Cells with fewer than 200 or more than 6,000 detected genes, or with >15% mitochondrial content, were excluded, and genes expressed in fewer than three cells were removed. Data were normalized, log-transformed, and scaled after selecting 2,000 highly variable genes. Doublets were identified and removed with DoubletFinder (v2.0.3). Batch effects were corrected using Harmony (v1.0) with sample ID as the batch variable, followed by principal component analysis (PCA), uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) for visualization, and Louvain clustering (FindClusters) at a resolution of 0.5.

  Cell identities were determined by integrating canonical marker gene expression with SingleR annotation, using the Human Primary Cell Atlas as a reference. Marker gene expression patterns were z-score normalized for visualization. Cell–cell communication was analyzed using CellChat (v1.6.1) with the CellChatDB human ligand–receptor database, producing both unweighted interaction networks and communication probability–weighted networks.


#### Scoring and Cell-Type Profiling of PTM Signatures at the Single-Cell Level
  Gene sets for 20 PTM types—including lactylation, acetylation, ubiquitination, methylation, phosphorylation, sumoylation, neddylation, hydroxylation, glycosylation, and citrullination—were curated from published literature, the CellMarker database, and our group’s previous datasets7–13 (Supplementary Table 1). AUCell (v1.20.1) was used to compute enrichment scores for each PTM signature in individual cells.

  These gene sets were imported using GSEABase (v1.2.x), and single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was performed via GSVA (v1.46.0) to calculate enrichment scores for each cell in the Seurat object. Cell identities were defined by prior clustering and annotation. The resulting PTM-associated enrichment scores were averaged across cells within each annotated cell type to obtain mean enrichment scores. The scores were visualized as a clustered heatmap using the pheatmap package, with both PTM types and cell types hierarchically clustered based on Euclidean distance and complete linkage.

  It should be noted that the AUCell/ssGSEA-derived PTM-associated enrichment scores in this study were used to quantify the relative enrichment of PTM-associated transcriptional programs rather than to directly measure biochemical PTM-associated transcriptional activity or the direction of specific modification events. Genes within each PTM-related gene set may include regulators, effectors, substrates, or pathway-associated components, and some genes may exert opposite regulatory effects on the same PTM process. Therefore, these scores should be interpreted as PTM-related functional enrichment scores at the transcriptomic level, rather than direct indicators of hyper- or hypo-modification.


#### Correlation Analysis of PTM Signatures with Metabolic Pathway Activities
  Metabolic pathway gene sets were obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) and imported using GSEABase (v1.2.x). Single-sample gene set enrichment analysis was performed with GSVA (v1.46.0) to compute pathway enrichment scores for each cell. Cell type identities were binarized (1 = belonging, 0 = not belonging), and Spearman’s rank correlation was calculated between cell types and pathway scores. Multiple testing correction was performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, and correlations were displayed as clustered heatmaps.

  PTM-associated enrichment scores were computed at the single-cell level using AUCell, based on curated PTM-related gene sets. Spearman correlation analysis was performed between PTM-associated enrichment scores and ssGSEA-derived metabolic pathway scores. P-values were adjusted via the Benjamini–Hochberg method, and results were visualized in clustered heatmaps.


#### Clinical Relevance of PTM-Associated Transcriptional Activity
  Single-cell samples were annotated with corresponding patient clinical metadata. Cells were grouped by tumor stage (stage III vs stage IV) or patient age (<60 years vs ≥60 years). PTM-associated enrichment scores were compared between groups using the two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and results were visualized with violin plots.


### Immune Cell Subclustering and PTM-Associated Functional Characterization

#### Identification of Immune Cell Subclusters and Their PTM Characteristics
  The marker gene sets used for defining macrophage, NK cell, and B cell subclusters were derived from previously published literature,14–17 validated findings from our research group, and the CellMarker database to ensure robustness and biological relevance (Supplementary Table 2). Single-cell RNA-seq data were processed using Seurat (v4.3.0). Macrophages, NK cells, and B cells were extracted from the annotated dataset and analyzed independently. The workflow included: log-normalization with NormalizeData (scale factor = 10,000), variable gene selection with FindVariableFeatures (vst method), scaling with ScaleData (optionally regressing total UMI counts and mitochondrial percentage), PCA (top 30 PCs), neighbor graph construction with FindNeighbors (dims = 1–30), clustering with FindClusters (resolution 0.3–0.8), and UMAP embedding with RunUMAP (dims = 1–30). Positive marker genes were identified using FindAllMarkers (Wilcoxon test, adjusted P < 0.05).

  PTM-associated transcriptional activity per cell was quantified using AUCell (v1.20.1) to obtain AUC scores, summarized at the subcluster level, and visualized. Statistical comparisons were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by pairwise Wilcoxon tests with Benjamini–Hochberg correction.


#### Functional Enrichment of Immune Cell Subclusters
  Subcluster-specific upregulated genes were identified using FindMarkers (Wilcoxon test, avg_log2FC > 0, BH-adjusted P < 0.05), with background genes defined as those expressed in ≥10% of cells in the lineage. Gene symbols were converted to ENTREZID using bitr in clusterProfiler (v4.4.4) with org.Hs.eg.db. GO enrichment for BP, CC, and MF categories was performed using enrichGO (pAdjustMethod = “BH”, pvalueCutoff = 0.05, qvalueCutoff = 0.2).

  Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated between AUCell-derived PTM-associated enrichment scores and ssGSEA-derived GO pathway scores (BP, CC, MF) within each lineage, with multiple testing correction by the BH method. Correlation matrices were visualized as clustered heatmaps using Euclidean distance and average or complete linkage.


### PTM-Based Stratification of Melanoma Cells and Multi-Omics Functional Characterization

#### PTM-Based Classification of Melanoma Cells
  For each melanoma cell, activity scores for 20 curated PTM gene sets were calculated using AUCell (v1.20.1). Median AUCell scores for each PTM category across melanoma cells were computed, and cells with all PTM-associated enrichment scores above the respective median were classified as “Melanoma-high”, while those with all scores below the median were classified as “Melanoma-low”. Intermediate cases were labeled as “Melanoma-other”. UMAP was used for dimensionality reduction, and the three subgroups were visualized in a two-dimensional space. Heatmaps were generated to visualize PTM-associated transcriptional activity distributions.


#### Cell–Cell Communication Between Melanoma Subgroups and Other Cell Types
  Cell–cell communication networks were inferred using CellChat (v1.6.1) with the CellChatDB human ligand–receptor database. Overexpressed ligands and receptors were identified for each cell type, and interaction probabilities were calculated and normalized by the proportion of cells in each group. Both unweighted and probability-weighted networks were constructed to illustrate overall interaction patterns and communication strength distribution. Significant ligand–receptor pairs between melanoma-high and other cell types were identified using CellChat subsetCommunication and filtered based on adjusted P < 0.05. Dot plots were generated to display the expression patterns and interaction strengths for selected pairs across different melanoma groups.


#### Inference of Copy Number Variations in Melanoma Cells
  Large-scale chromosomal CNVs were inferred using inferCNV (v1.12.0), with non-malignant immune and stromal cells serving as reference populations. Parameters included a cutoff of 0.1, denoising enabled, a gene smoothing window of 101 genes, and the hidden HMM disabled. CNV profiles were visualized as heatmaps to depict genome-wide variation patterns and group-specific chromosomal alterations.


#### Comparative Functional and Metabolic Profiling of Melanoma Subgroups
  For each group, group-specific up-regulated markers were identified with Seurat FindMarkers (Wilcoxon rank-sum; BH-adjusted P < 0.05). Genes were mapped from SYMBOL to ENTREZID using clusterProfiler::bitr with org.Hs.eg.db. GO over-representation analysis was then performed separately for each group with clusterProfiler (v4.4.4) enrichGO across BP, CC and MF ontologies (pAdjustMethod=“BH”, pvalueCutoff=0.05, qvalueCutoff=0.2, readable=TRUE). For visualization, the top 10 enriched terms per ontology (ranked by adjusted P) were displayed as bar plots, with bar length indicating gene count and color encoding the adjusted P (or −log10 adjusted P, as specified in the plotting script).

  Metabolic pathway gene sets were obtained from KEGG, Reactome, and Hallmark collections in MSigDB. Single-cell pathway activity scores were computed with GSVA/ssGSEA (v1.46.0). Group-level mean scores for melanoma-high and melanoma-low were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and bar plots display the difference in activity across pathways.

  Signaling pathway network activity for VEGF, MIF, TNF, GALECTIN, CXCL, CCL and PDGF pathways was inferred using CellChat. For each pathway, sender, receiver, mediator, and influencer roles of melanoma-high, melanoma-low, and other cell types were quantified using the netAnalysis_signalingRole function. Heatmaps were generated to display the relative importance scores for each role and cell type in the network.


#### Differential Gene Expression Between Melanoma Subgroups
  Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the two groups were identified using the Seurat (v4.3.0) FindMarkers function with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, setting parameters logfc.threshold = 0, min.pct = 0.1, and only.pos = FALSE. Genes with average log2 fold change (avg_log2FC) > 0 and BH-adjusted P < 0.05 were considered significantly upregulated in the melanoma-high group, while those with avg_log2FC < 0 and BH-adjusted P < 0.05 were considered significantly upregulated in the melanoma-low group. Volcano plots were generated using ggplot2 (v3.4.4) to visualize the distribution of DEGs, with the x-axis representing log2 fold change and the y-axis representing −log10(P-value).


### Construction and Validation of a PTM-Based Prognostic Risk Model

#### Identification of Prognostic Feature Genes Through Integrated Differential Expression and LASSO Analysis
  RNA-seq data for TCGA-SKCM tumor samples and GTEx normal skin tissues were obtained from the UCSC Xena platform. DEGs between TCGA-SKCM and GTEx skin tissues were identified using the limma package (v3.54.0), with thresholds of |log2FC| > 1 and Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) adjusted P < 0.05. These DEGs were then intersected with the DEGs derived from the Melanoma-high versus Melanoma-low comparison described above to obtain a shared candidate gene set. This intersection step was used to retain genes that were both associated with PTM-based melanoma cell stratification and differentially expressed between melanoma and normal skin tissues.

  The shared candidate genes were subsequently subjected to least absolute shrinkage and selection operator Cox regression (LASSO-Cox) analysis using the glmnet package (v4.1). Ten-fold cross-validation was performed to select the optimal penalty parameter, and genes with non-zero coefficients at the selected lambda value were retained as prognostic feature genes for subsequent risk model construction.

  The prognostic performance of the model was systematically evaluated in both the TCGA-SKCM and GSE19234 cohorts. Specifically, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was conducted to compare overall survival between the high- and low-risk groups, with statistical significance assessed using the Log rank test. In addition, time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to further assess the discriminative power of the model. Survival analyses and visualizations were carried out using the survival (v3.5) and survminer (v0.4.9) packages.


#### Evaluation of Predictive Accuracy with ROC and C-Index Analyses
  ROC curve analyses were used to evaluate the predictive accuracy of the risk score and clinical variables for overall survival (OS). In the TCGA-SKCM cohort, multi-variable ROC curves were generated for the risk score and clinical factors including age, gender, and stage, while time-dependent ROC curves at 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS were generated for both cohorts using the timeROC (v0.4) package. The corresponding area under the curve (AUC) values were calculated. Additionally, time-dependent concordance index (C-index) curves were generated for both cohorts using the survcomp (v1.46.0) package to compare predictive performance over a 3-year period.


#### Multivariate Cox Analysis and Independent Prognostic Evaluation
  Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed to assess whether the risk score was an independent prognostic factor after adjusting for clinical variables (age, gender, stage). Hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated, and results were visualized as forest plots.


#### Development and Calibration of a Prognostic Nomogram
  A prognostic nomogram was constructed using the rms (v6.7) package, incorporating the risk score and significant clinical variables from the multivariate Cox regression analysis to predict 1-, 2-, and 3-year OS in the TCGA-SKCM cohort. Calibration curves were generated to assess agreement between predicted and observed survival probabilities, and the concordance index (C-index) with 95% CI was reported.


#### Visualization of Risk Score Distribution and Associated Clinical Characteristics
  For each patient in both cohorts, risk scores were plotted in ascending order to visualize their distribution, with survival status (alive or dead) annotated. Heatmaps of normalized expression values for the prognostic model’s feature genes were generated using the pheatmap (v1.0.12) package, with patients ordered according to their risk scores and annotated by risk group and survival status.


### Multi-Omics and Immunological Insights into Prognostic Risk Stratification

#### Comparison of PTM Activities Between Risk Groups
  To investigate whether prognostic risk stratification is associated with alterations in PTM-associated transcriptional activity, single-sample PTM-related transcriptional enrichment scores were calculated for each patient based on curated PTM-related gene sets using the AUCell algorithm. Patients were classified into high-risk and low-risk groups according to the median prognostic risk score. Differences in mean PTM-related transcriptional enrichment scores between the two groups were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and results were visualized as boxplots to highlight potential associations between PTM regulation and prognostic risk.


#### Correlation Analysis of Risk Score with Tumor Mutation Burden
  Somatic mutation data from the TCGA-SKCM cohort were used to calculate tumor mutation burden (TMB) for each patient, defined as the total number of somatic mutations per megabase of the coding region. Patients were divided into high- and low-risk groups according to the median prognostic risk score. Intergroup differences in TMB were evaluated using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and the results were presented as boxplots to assess the relationship between genomic instability and prognostic risk stratification.

  To further explore the association between genomic alterations and prognostic risk, Spearman correlation analysis was performed between the continuous risk scores and TMB values across all patients. The correlation coefficient and corresponding P value were calculated, and the relationship was visualized using scatter plots with linear regression trend lines. This analysis aimed to determine whether mutation burden was linearly associated with the prognostic risk score.


#### Tumor Microenvironment Scoring and Risk Group Comparison
  The ESTIMATE algorithm was applied to the transcriptomic profiles of TCGA-SKCM patients to derive StromalScore, ImmuneScore, and ESTIMATEScore, reflecting stromal content, immune cell infiltration, and their combined contribution to the tumor microenvironment, respectively. These scores were compared between high- and low-risk groups using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Results were visualized using violin plots, providing insight into the relationship between prognostic risk and the tumor immune microenvironment landscape.


#### Immunophenoscore Analysis in Different Immune Checkpoint Contexts
  To assess the potential responsiveness of different risk groups to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy, immunophenoscores (IPS) were retrieved from The Cancer Immunome Atlas (TCIA). IPS values were compared between high- and low-risk groups under four immune checkpoint expression conditions: CTLA-4–/PD-1–, CTLA-4+/PD-1–, CTLA-4–/PD-1+, and CTLA-4+/PD-1+. Statistical differences were evaluated using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and results were presented as boxplots. This analysis aimed to evaluate whether risk group stratification could predict variation in immune checkpoint inhibitor sensitivity.


#### Immune Cell Infiltration Analysis and Risk Group Comparison
  Relative proportions of 22 immune cell types were estimated using the CIBERSORT algorithm with the LM22 signature matrix. Correlation between prognostic risk scores and immune cell infiltration was examined by Spearman correlation analysis. Additionally, group-wise differences in immune cell fractions were tested using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and violin plots were applied for visualization.


#### Expression of Immune-Related Signatures and Immune Checkpoint Genes
  Immune-related functional signatures were quantified, including cytolytic activity (CYT) score and interferon-γ (IFNG) expression. CYT scores were computed as the geometric mean of GZMA and PRF1 expression levels. Expression levels of immune checkpoint molecules, including CD27, CD40, CTLA4, PDCD1, LAG3, TIGIT, and others, were extracted from normalized transcriptomic data. Comparisons between high- and low-risk groups were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and violin plots were used to illustrate differences.


#### Metabolic Pathway Activity Analysis
  Metabolic pathway activities were estimated using the GSVA method with curated metabolism-related gene sets from Hallmark, KEGG, Reactome, and GO databases. Single-sample pathway activity scores were calculated for each patient. Group-wise comparisons between high- and low-risk cohorts were conducted using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Results were visualized using barplots and heatmaps to display pathway activity distributions and clustering patterns.


### Model Interpretability and Cross-Platform Validation of Prognostic Genes

#### SHAP-Based Global Interpretation of Prognostic Feature Genes
  To quantify the contribution of each feature gene to the prognostic risk score, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis was conducted using the “shap” Python package. Mean absolute SHAP values were computed to rank genes by their overall influence on model predictions. ROC curves derived from SHAP-informed predictions were used to assess the model’s discrimination ability via the AUC. Summary plots visualized the distribution, magnitude, and direction of each gene’s effect across all patients, while scatterplots of selected genes illustrated the relationship between expression levels and SHAP-derived contribution scores. This global interpretation step provided a comprehensive overview of how the model integrates gene expression features to produce risk classifications.


#### Local Model Interpretability by LIME Analysis
  Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations (LIME) analysis was performed using the “lime” R package to evaluate the local decision-making process of the prognostic model. For each selected patient, the model’s predictions were perturbed by modifying gene expression inputs, and an interpretable surrogate model was fitted to approximate the original model’s behavior. The resulting heatmaps indicated the magnitude and direction of each gene’s influence on the individual prediction, enabling a patient-specific interpretability assessment that complements SHAP’s global insights.


#### Single-Cell Validation of Feature Gene Expression Prevalence
  scRNA-seq data for melanoma samples were obtained from the GSE215120 dataset. After quality control, normalization, and cell type annotation using Seurat (v4.3.0), the proportion of cells with detectable expression (expression value > 0) was calculated for each feature gene. These percentages were visualized as bar plots to assess the breadth of gene expression within the tumor microenvironment and across cell types. This single-cell validation determined whether the prognostic feature genes are broadly or selectively expressed in melanoma tissues.


#### Bulk RNA-Seq Validation of Feature Gene Expression Patterns
  Bulk RNA-seq data from the GSE19234 melanoma cohort were used to validate the differential expression patterns of the prognostic feature genes. Normalized expression values were compared between tumor and normal samples using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and violin plots were generated to display expression distributions and median values. This external bulk RNA-seq validation step confirmed the reproducibility of the identified gene signatures across independent datasets and sequencing platforms.


#### Spatial Transcriptomic Contextualization of Prognostic Feature Gene Expression
  Spatial transcriptomic data from the melanoma dataset VISDS000459 were accessed from the China National Center for Bioinformation (CNCB) CROST database. Precomputed cell type annotations for major immune and stromal cell populations were mapped to the histological tissue section to define the spatial architecture of the tumor microenvironment. The expression patterns of selected prognostic feature genes were then examined by log-normalizing their expression values and overlaying them as color-scaled maps on tissue images. This spatial analysis was used to provide spatial contextualization of selected prognostic genes in a representative melanoma tissue section, rather than population-level validation of spatial expression patterns.


### Drug Sensitivity Prediction and Molecular Docking Analysis for Therapeutic Prioritization
  To identify potential therapeutic agents associated with the prognostic risk model, drug sensitivity prediction was performed based on RNA-seq expression profiles from the TCGA-SKCM cohort. The oncoPredict (v0.2) R package was used to integrate transcriptomic data with the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database, generating predicted half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values for a panel of small-molecule compounds. Drug sensitivity was compared between the high- and low-risk groups after log2 transformation of IC50 values, and differences were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with a significance threshold of P < 0.001. The distribution patterns of significantly different compounds were visualized using boxplots.

  From the significantly different compounds, the top 10 drugs predicted to have higher sensitivity (lower IC50 values) in the high-risk group were selected for further evaluation. The top five prognostic feature genes identified by SHAP analysis were then subjected to molecular docking with these candidate drugs. Three-dimensional protein structures were obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB), and ligand structures for candidate drugs were retrieved from the PubChem database. Protein and ligand preparation was performed using AutoDockTools (v1.5.7), including addition of polar hydrogens, removal of water molecules, and assignment of Gasteiger charges. Docking simulations were conducted using AutoDock Vina (v1.2.0), and binding affinities (kcal/mol) were calculated. Binding energy results were displayed as a heatmap for each drug–gene pair, and detailed structural binding modes were visualized, highlighting binding pockets, hydrogen bonds, and hydrophobic interactions.

Major cell populations included melanoma cells, T cells, NK cells, endothelial cells, B cells, fibroblasts, macrophages, and keratinocytes, which displayed clear spatial segregation in the low-dimensional UMAP embedding (Figure 2B). Canonical marker genes for each cell type exhibited specific and non-overlapping expression patterns, supporting accurate cell-type identification (Figure 2C). #### Melanoma Cells Act as Central Hubs in Tumor–Stroma–Immune Communication Networks
  Cell–cell communication network analysis demonstrated extensive interactions among the major cell types. In the unweighted network, melanoma cells were connected to nearly all stromal and immune compartments, indicating a broad interaction spectrum. In the communication probability–weighted network, melanoma cells showed the strongest interactions with fibroblasts and multiple immune cell subsets, including macrophages, NK cells, and B cells, suggesting a central role for these interactions in shaping the tumor microenvironment (Figure 2D and E) (Supplementary Figure 1).


#### Cell Type–Specific PTM Signatures Highlight Functional Diversity Across Populations
  PTM profiling revealed cell type–specific modification signatures (Figure 2F). Lactylation, acetylation, and ubiquitination were most enriched in melanoma cells, fibroblasts, and macrophages, whereas keratinocytes and endothelial cells exhibited higher levels of glycosylation and citrullination. Additional patterns were observed for phosphorylation, sumoylation, and methylation, which showed moderate enrichment in immune cell subsets such as T cells and NK cells.


#### PTM Activities Align with Cell Type–Specific Metabolic Programs
  Metabolic pathway enrichment analysis revealed marked differences in metabolic activity among cell types (Figure 2G). Melanoma cells were enriched in glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, alanine/aspartate/glutamate metabolism, purine and pyrimidine metabolism, serine/glycine/one-carbon metabolism, and fatty acid biosynthesis. Immune cells such as T cells and NK cells were predominantly enriched in oxidative phosphorylation, fatty acid degradation, and amino acid catabolism, whereas fibroblasts displayed enrichment in extracellular matrix-related metabolic pathways.

  To further explore the relationship between PTM-associated transcriptional programs and metabolic states, we performed single-cell-level Spearman correlation analysis between AUCell-derived PTM-associated enrichment scores and ssGSEA-derived metabolic pathway scores across individual cells. This analysis was not based on bulk patient samples or on group-wise comparisons between Melanoma-high and Melanoma-low cells. The results demonstrated strong positive correlations between lactylation-associated enrichment and glycolysis, acetylation-associated enrichment and glycolysis, phosphorylation-associated enrichment and amino acid/nucleotide metabolism, as well as ubiquitination-associated enrichment and oxidative phosphorylation. Additional associations were observed between neddylation-associated enrichment and fatty acid metabolism, and between methylation-associated enrichment and purine biosynthesis (Figure 2H).


#### PTM Enrichment Associates with Tumor Stage and Patient Age
  Clinical stratification analyses revealed that multiple PTM types—including phosphorylation, lactylation, and ubiquitination—were significantly elevated in stage IV tumors compared with stage III tumors (Figure 2I). Age-stratified comparisons showed that acetylation, methylation, and glycosylation were more abundant in patients aged ≥60 years, while certain PTMs such as hydroxylation and sumoylation exhibited minimal differences between age groups (Figure 2J). These findings suggest that specific PTM patterns may be linked to tumor progression and age-related biological changes in melanoma.

#### PTM Activities Differ Across Immune Subtypes
  Heatmap and violin plot analyses revealed marked differences in PTM-associated transcriptional activity among macrophage subtypes (Figure 3D). Among macrophage subtypes, M1 macrophages displayed the highest overall PTM-associated transcriptional activity. In NK cells (Figure 3E), Tissue-resident NK cells had significantly higher cytotoxicity-related PTM-associated transcriptional activity than CD56dim cells. In B cells (Figure 3F), plasma cells showed elevated PTM-associated transcriptional enrichment across multiple modification types compared with naïve B cells, consistent with their high metabolic and secretory demands. Across macrophage, NK cell, and B cell subpopulations, PTM activities were predominantly elevated in lactylation, acetylation, and ubiquitination.


#### Functional Enrichment Shows Tissue-Resident NK Cells and Plasma B Cells are Specialized for Immune Effector Functions
  For macrophage subclusters (Figure 3G) (Supplementary Table 3), enrichment was observed in immune effector processes, cytokine-mediated signaling, leukocyte activation, and antigen processing and presentation. In particular, M1 macrophages showed the strongest enrichment in pathways linked to phagocytosis, ribosomal structure and function, and chemokine activity. For NK cell subtypes (Figure 3H) (Supplementary Table 4), Tissue-resident NK cells were predominantly enriched in pathways related to leukocyte-mediated cytotoxicity, immune receptor signaling, regulation of leukocyte-mediated immunity, cytokine binding, and C–C chemokine binding. For B cell subtypes (Figure 3I) (Supplementary Table 5), Plasma B cells were significantly enriched in ribosome- and translation-related biological processes (eg, cytoplasmic translation, ribosome biogenesis, rRNA processing), structural and functional components of ribosomes, and molecular functions such as structural constituent of ribosome, rRNA binding, antigen binding, and NADH dehydrogenase activity.


#### PTM–Pathway Correlations Reveal Lactylation/Acetylation Linked to Immune Activation in Macrophages and Antibody Secretion in Plasma Cells
  For macrophages (Figure 3J), PTM-associated transcriptional activity—including lactylation, acetylation, and ubiquitination—showed strong positive correlations with immune activation pathways such as regulation of leukocyte-mediated immunity, cytokine signaling (eg, TNF and interferon-related pathways), and antigen processing and presentation via MHC class II. Inflammatory macrophage states demonstrated the most pronounced associations, suggesting that PTMs may enhance macrophage-mediated immune surveillance and antigen presentation functions in the melanoma microenvironment.

  For NK cells (Figure 3K), PTM-associated transcriptional activity exhibited strong correlations with cytotoxic effector functions, particularly pathways related to cytolytic granule organization, NK cell–mediated cytotoxicity, immune synapse assembly, and interferon-γ signaling. CD56dim NK cells displayed the most robust associations, reflecting their effector phenotype, while tissue-resident NK cells showed more moderate correlations, consistent with their homeostatic and regulatory bias.

  For B cells (Figure 3L), plasma cells demonstrated the strongest PTM–function correlations, particularly with pathways related to antibody secretion, immunoglobulin complex assembly, ER protein folding, and unfolded protein response. These findings suggest that PTM-associated transcriptional activity supports high-level protein synthesis and secretion demands in plasma cells. By contrast, naïve B cells exhibited relatively weak correlations across immune pathways, indicating a lower reliance on PTMs during their resting and antigen-priming state.

#### Melanoma-High Cells Serve as Central Hubs in Stromal–Immune Communication Networks
  Cell–cell communication mapping revealed that melanoma-high cells established strong and extensive interactions with multiple immune and stromal cell types, with the most notable communication occurring with macrophages and fibroblasts, followed by endothelial cells and keratinocytes. These interactions were prominent in the probability-weighted network, indicating both high frequency and strong communication intensity (Figure 4C) (Supplementary Figure 2).


#### Ligand–Receptor Interactions in Melanoma-High Cells Enrich for MIF, TNF, COL1A1–ITGB1, and FN1–ITGA5 Signaling
  Ligand–receptor pair analysis showed that melanoma-high cells actively participated in a range of signaling pathways, engaging in frequent interactions with immune and stromal components. Notable examples included MIF–CD74, TNF–TNFRSF1B, COL1A1–ITGB1, and FN1–ITGA5, which were significantly enriched compared to melanoma-low cells. In contrast, melanoma-low cells showed markedly lower involvement in these key interactions (Figure 4D).


#### Melanoma-High Cells Display Extensive Chromosomal Alterations with Higher CNV Burden
  CNV analysis demonstrated widespread chromosomal gains and losses in melanoma-high cells, particularly involving chromosomes 1, 7, and 8, whereas melanoma-low cells displayed relatively stable genomic profiles. CNV burden scores were significantly higher in the melanoma-high group (Figure 4E and F).


#### Functional Enrichment Highlights Proliferative Programs in Melanoma-High versus Immune Activation in Melanoma-Low Cells
  Melanoma-high cells were significantly enriched in cell cycle–related biological processes, including mitotic nuclear division, chromosome segregation, DNA replication initiation, and spindle organization, highlighting their proliferative phenotype. At the cellular component level, enrichment was observed in chromosomal regions, centromeric chromatin, and kinetochore structures, while at the molecular function level, terms such as ATP binding, DNA helicase activity, and microtubule motor activity were predominant, underscoring enhanced DNA synthesis and mitotic machinery.

  In contrast, melanoma-low cells exhibited enrichment in immune and inflammatory processes, including regulation of leukocyte activation, T cell–mediated cytotoxicity, antigen processing and presentation, cytokine production, and interferon-γ–mediated signaling pathways. These were accompanied by cellular component terms related to the MHC class I and II protein complexes and molecular function categories involving antigen binding and receptor activity. Collectively, these results suggest that melanoma-high cells are primarily driven by proliferative and genomic maintenance programs, whereas melanoma-low cells are characterized by heightened immune activation and cytotoxic functions. (Figure 4G) (Supplementary Table 6).


#### Metabolic Pathway Analysis Shows Anabolic and Energy-Producing Programs Dominate in Melanoma-High Cells
  Melanoma-high cells exhibited significantly elevated activity in triglyceride metabolism, glucose metabolism, and glycolysis compared with the melanoma-low group (Figure 4H).


#### Melanoma-High Cells Show Enhanced Stromal–Immune Communication Through VEGF, MIF, GALECTIN, CXCL, CCL, and PDGF Signaling
  Signaling pathway network analysis showed that Melanoma-high cells exhibited higher communication activity in multiple stromal–immune signaling pathways, including VEGF, MIF, GALECTIN, CXCL, CCL, and PDGF signaling (Figure 4I and J; Supplementary Figure 3). In these pathways, Melanoma-high cells were identified as major senders, receivers, or influencers. Among them, melanoma-high cells and macrophages showed prominent involvement in GALECTIN and CXCL signaling, while endothelial cells and fibroblasts contributed strongly to PDGF-associated interactions. These findings indicate that Melanoma-high cells are associated with enhanced communication with stromal and immune compartments.


#### Differential Gene Expression Between Melanoma-High and Melanoma-Low Groups
  Differential expression analysis between the Melanoma-high and Melanoma-low groups identified a total of 378 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (adjusted p < 0.05, |log2FC| > 0.25). Among these, 299 genes were upregulated and 79 genes were downregulated in the Melanoma-high group compared with the Melanoma-low group (Supplementary Table 7). The volcano plot (Figure 4K) illustrates the overall distribution of these DEGs.


### Prognostic Risk Model Stratifies Patients by Survival Outcomes and Clinical Relevance

#### Identification of Prognostic Feature Genes Using LASSO Regression
  LASSO regression identified 15 prognostic feature genes (MRPS2, SLC27A4, SEMA6A, KIT, CIB2, TRIM32, TTYH2, FOXM1, SRD5A3, PTK6, ABCC2, FCGR2A, FOXK1, SLC25A15, and ZNF25), which were subsequently incorporated into the PTM-related prognostic risk score (Supplementary Table 8).


#### High-Risk Group Exhibits Significantly Worse Overall Survival

#### Prognostic Performance of the Risk Score is Comparable to Clinical Stage and Superior to Age and Gender
  ROC curve analysis demonstrated that in the TCGA-SKCM cohort, the risk score (AUC = 0.617) had comparable predictive accuracy to clinical stage (AUC = 0.685), and both outperformed gender (AUC = 0.463) and age (AUC = 0.450) (Figure 5C). In the external GSE19234 cohort, the risk score also demonstrated favorable predictive performance (AUC = 0.617), which was comparable to clinical stage (AUC = 0.686) and superior to gender (AUC = 0.463) and age (AUC = 0.450), further supporting the robustness of the prognostic model (Figure 5D). Time-dependent ROC curves confirmed the predictive ability of the risk score at 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS in TCGA-SKCM (AUCs = 0.691, 0.666, 0.700, Figure 5E) and at 1- and 3-year OS in GSE19234 (AUCs = 0.617, 0.841, Figure 5F).


#### Risk Model Shows Stable Prognostic Accuracy and Remains Comparable to Clinical Stage by C-Index Analysis
  C-index analysis confirmed consistent predictive performance of the risk score across three years in both TCGA-SKCM (C-index = 0.571, Figure 5G) and GSE19234 (C-index ≈ 0.62, Figure 5H). Comparative analyses showed that the risk score achieved higher predictive power than age and gender, and was comparable to stage (Figure 5I and J).


#### Independent Prognostic Value of the Risk Score and Construction of an Accurate Survival Prediction Nomogram
  Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses (Figure 5K) demonstrated that the risk score was an independent predictor of overall survival (HR > 1, p < 0.01). Based on this, a prognostic nomogram integrating risk score and clinical variables was developed for the TCGA-SKCM cohort (Figure 5L). Calibration curves showed strong agreement between predicted and observed survival probabilities, supporting the model’s accuracy for individualized survival prediction.


#### High-Risk Patients Exhibit Distinct Gene Expression Patterns Linked to Poor Prognosis
  Risk score distribution plots showed that higher risk scores were associated with greater mortality in both the TCGA-SKCM and GSE19234 cohorts (Figure 5M and N). Heatmaps revealed distinct gene expression patterns in high-risk patients compared with low-risk patients, consistent with adverse survival outcomes.

#### High-Risk Tumors Exhibit an Immunosuppressive Microenvironment
  ESTIMATE analysis revealed that StromalScore, ImmuneScore, and the combined ESTIMATEScore were all significantly lower in the high-risk group (Figure 6D) (Supplementary Table 9), indicating reduced stromal content and immune infiltration. Immunophenoscore (IPS) comparison under four immune checkpoint contexts—CTLA-4–/PD-1–, CTLA-4+/PD-1–, CTLA-4–/PD-1+, and CTLA-4+/PD-1+—showed consistently higher IPS values in the low-risk group (Figure 6E), implying greater potential responsiveness to immune checkpoint blockade therapy.


#### Risk Score Inversely Correlates with Immune Activation Signatures
  Correlation analysis demonstrated that the risk score was negatively associated with a broad spectrum of immune-related functional features, including cytotoxicity, antigen presentation, interferon signaling, and T-cell activation, as indicated by multiple immune signature scores (Figure 6F) (Supplementary Table 10). The correlation map further revealed distinct clusters of immune parameters that were inversely correlated with risk score, highlighting the immunosuppressive phenotype of high-risk tumors.


#### Differential Relative Immune Cell Composition Between Risk Groups
  CIBERSORT-based immune cell composition analysis revealed differences in the relative proportions of immune cell subsets between risk groups. The high-risk group showed a higher relative proportion of CIBERSORT-inferred M1-like macrophages, whereas the low-risk group showed higher relative proportions of M2-like macrophages, resting dendritic cells, and follicular helper T cells (Figure 6G). These results reflect relative immune cell composition rather than absolute immune infiltration levels.


#### Low-Risk Tumors Show Enhanced Cytotoxicity and IFN-γ Signaling
  Cytolytic activity scores were significantly elevated in the low-risk group (Figure 6H), consistent with increased effector immune cell infiltration. Similarly, IFNG expression levels were markedly higher in low-risk tumors (Figure 6I), supporting the presence of a more inflammatory and immunologically active tumor microenvironment.


#### Broad Immune Checkpoint Activation in the High-Risk Group
  In the high-risk group, the expression of multiple co-inhibitory receptors—including PDCD1 (PD-1), CTLA4, LAG3, TIGIT, and HAVCR2 (TIM-3)—was significantly upregulated compared with the low-risk group. Additional inhibitory checkpoint molecules such as BTLA, CD96, CD276 (B7-H3), and VSIR (VISTA) also exhibited elevated expression in the high-risk subgroup. These findings indicate that high-risk tumors are characterized by a markedly immunosuppressed phenotype mediated through broad checkpoint activation. By contrast, the low-risk group displayed relatively lower expression of most immune checkpoint genes, consistent with a less exhausted and more immunologically active tumor microenvironment. Notably, TNFRSF14 did not show significant differences between groups. (Figure 6J).


#### Distinct Metabolic Pathway Preferences Between High- and Low-Risk Groups
  Metabolic pathway analysis demonstrated that multiple anabolic and energy-producing pathways were significantly enriched in the high-risk group, including serine metabolism, glutamate and glutamine metabolism, purine metabolism, pentose phosphate pathway, alanine/aspartate/glutamate metabolism, glycolysis, fatty acid metabolism, and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. Only triglyceride metabolism did not show a significant difference between groups (Figure 6K).


#### Distinct Metabolic Reprogramming Patterns Between Risk Groups
  High-risk tumors demonstrated distinct PTM enrichment patterns across multiple metabolic pathways. Glycolytic processes and the pentose phosphate pathway were strongly associated with elevated lactylation and acetylation, while the tricarboxylic acid cycle showed additional coupling with ubiquitination. Fatty acid metabolism was predominantly linked to ubiquitination and NEDD8 modification, indicating PTM-driven regulation of lipid utilization. In contrast, amino acid–related pathways such as serine, glutamate/glutamine, and alanine/aspartate/glutamate metabolism, together with purine metabolism, were more strongly correlated with methylation and phosphorylation. Collectively, these findings highlight a coordinated pattern in which carbohydrate metabolism aligns with lactylation and acetylation, amino acid and nucleotide metabolism align with methylation and phosphorylation, and lipid metabolism is regulated primarily through ubiquitination and NEDD8 modification in the high-risk subgroup (Figure 6L).

## Discussion
  In this study, we systematically characterized the landscape of PTMs in melanoma by integrating single-cell, multi-omics, and bulk transcriptomic data. Single-cell transcriptomic analysis revealed marked heterogeneity of PTM-associated transcriptional activity across distinct cell types and subpopulations, with PTM-related signatures closely linked to metabolic reprogramming and immune regulation. Stratification based on PTM-associated transcriptional activity demonstrated that melanoma cells with elevated PTM levels exhibited enhanced proliferative potential, expanded cell–cell communication networks, and increased chromosomal copy number alterations, whereas cells with lower PTM-associated transcriptional activity were enriched for immune activation pathways and displayed features of a more inflamed tumor microenvironment. By constructing and validating a prognostic risk model across TCGA and GEO cohorts, we further showed that high-risk patients were characterized by elevated PTM-associated transcriptional enrichment, greater genomic instability, and an immunosuppressive tumor milieu, while low-risk patients exhibited an “immune-hot” phenotype accompanied by favorable metabolic adaptations. This risk model demonstrated robust prognostic predictive power across multiple independent cohorts and was further supported by explainable machine learning analyses (SHAP and LIME), single-cell and spatial validation, as well as drug sensitivity and molecular docking analyses. Collectively, these findings delineate a comprehensive PTM-driven molecular and immune landscape in melanoma and establish a clinically applicable risk score system, thereby providing novel insights into tumor progression and potential precision therapeutic strategies.


### Lactylation, Acetylation, and Ubiquitination Form a Tumor–Stroma PTM Hub
  In the melanoma microenvironment, lactylation, acetylation, and ubiquitination were most prominently enriched in melanoma cells, fibroblasts, and macrophages, underscoring their central roles within the PTM regulatory network. Rather than acting as isolated molecular events, these PTM-associated signatures appeared to define a tumor-stroma regulatory hub involving malignant cells, fibroblasts, and macrophages. This pattern suggests that PTM-related transcriptional programs may coordinate tumor cell plasticity, stromal remodeling, and immune regulation within the melanoma microenvironment.

  Acetylation was broadly observed in these cell types, not only modulating transcription factor activity but also promoting chromatin relaxation, thereby enhancing melanoma cell proliferation and migration. In fibroblasts, acetylation supported the activation of extracellular matrix remodeling genes, facilitating tumor invasion.18 In macrophages, histone acetylation promoted transcription of inflammatory genes, but under chronic stimulation, it could also drive maladaptive inflammatory responses that paradoxically support tumor progression.19 These observations highlight acetylation as a key node linking transcriptional plasticity with microenvironmental remodeling.

  Ubiquitination, by regulating the degradation and localization of proteins such as the tumor suppressor PTEN, profoundly influenced signaling stability and cell fate decisions.20 Aberrant ubiquitination not only destabilized tumor suppressors but also fine-tuned receptor signaling in fibroblasts and macrophages, skewing them toward tumor-promoting phenotypes. Crosstalk between ubiquitination and other PTMs further enhanced signaling flexibility, reinforcing cellular adaptation to environmental stress.

  Taken together, the enrichment of lactylation-, acetylation-, and ubiquitination-associated signatures in melanoma cells, fibroblasts, and macrophages suggests the presence of a PTM-centered functional hub that coordinates tumor progression, matrix remodeling, and immune regulation. Compared with previous studies that mainly focused on individual PTMs, our findings highlight an integrated tumor-stroma PTM network that may jointly shape melanoma aggressiveness.6


### Tight Coupling of PTMs with Metabolic Reprogramming Sustains Melanoma Aggressiveness
  Based on our research results, melanoma cells exhibit pronounced activation across multiple metabolic pathways, including glycolysis, purine/pyrimidine metabolism, the TCA cycle, oxidative phosphorylation, and lipid metabolism. This extensive metabolic reprogramming not only satisfies the energy and biosynthetic substrate demands required for rapid tumor proliferation, but also establishes a tight molecular coupling with various PTMs.21

  Enhanced glycolysis leads to lactate accumulation, which drives histone lactylation and consequently promotes transcriptional activation of tumor-associated genes.22 In parallel, glycolytic and mitochondrial metabolites, including acetyl-CoA and other acyl-CoA derivatives, can serve as substrates for acetylation and related acylation reactions, while nucleotide and amino acid metabolism may influence phosphorylation and methylation-dependent regulatory programs.23,24 Thus, the metabolic alterations observed in melanoma-high cells may provide both energetic support and biochemical substrates for PTM-associated transcriptional remodeling.

  Meanwhile, the TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation not only provide energy, but also generate various acyl-CoA derivatives, which act as substrates for novel PTMs such as succinylation, glutarylation, and lactylation, enabling efficient transmission of metabolic signals to epigenetic regulation. Activation of lipid metabolic pathways contributes membrane precursors and signaling molecules to tumor progression; the regulation of these pathways is closely related to protein palmitoylation and ubiquitination, influencing the stability and localization of membrane receptors and metabolic enzymes.23–25

  Furthermore, amino acid metabolism—such as serine, cysteine, and methionine pathways—supplies crucial substrates for one-carbon metabolism and glutathione synthesis, directly determining methylation levels and thereby modulating gene expression and redox homeostasis.

  In summary, melanoma cells build a coordinated network between metabolism and PTMs to integrate energy supply, signal transduction, and epigenetic control. Compared with prior research on metabolic reprogramming alone, our data provide mechanistic evidence that PTMs act as essential molecular relays, linking metabolic flux directly to epigenetic remodeling and thereby sustaining melanoma aggressiveness under immune and hypoxic pressures.


### Immune Subsets Display Distinct PTM Signatures Aligned with Functional Specialization
  Beyond tumor and stromal compartments, our analysis revealed that immune cells also exhibit pronounced PTM heterogeneity. In particular, single-cell profiling identified M1 macrophages, tissue-resident NK cells, and plasma B cells as subsets with markedly elevated PTM-associated transcriptional enrichment, each showing distinct modification patterns that align with their specialized functions. These findings highlight the pivotal role of PTMs in shaping immune cell differentiation, effector activity, and adaptive responses within the melanoma microenvironment.

  In macrophages, the M1 phenotype exhibited markedly higher PTM-associated transcriptional activity compared to other subsets, with enrichment in immune activation, cytokine secretion, NF-κB signaling, and oxidative stress regulation. However, in our stratified analysis, M1 macrophages were predominantly enriched in the high-risk group, indicating that although these cells display pro-inflammatory signatures, their accumulation may not translate into effective antitumor responses. Instead, this enrichment likely reflects a dysfunctional or exhausted inflammatory state within an immunosuppressive microenvironment, consistent with the poor prognosis of high-risk melanoma. Previous studies support this view, showing that histone acetylation promotes transcription of inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, while histone methylation regulates NF-κB–dependent gene expression. Histone-modifying enzymes, including acetyltransferases, deacetylases, methyltransferases, and demethylases, dynamically control promoter accessibility of inflammatory genes and thereby determine macrophage responsiveness and polarization. Dysregulation of these epigenetic mechanisms has been linked to inflammatory diseases, underscoring their role as central regulators of macrophage function and immune homeostasis.26 Similarly, ubiquitination and phosphorylation enhance TLR4/NF-κB and MAPK/JAK-STAT signaling, thereby amplifying immune responses. Consistent with our results, pharmacological inhibition of HDACs has been shown to favor M1 polarization and augment antitumor immunity, whereas modulation of E3 ligases fine-tunes NF-κB activity. Moreover, macrophage polarization is increasingly recognized as a product of metabolic–epigenetic interplay, where metabolites such as acetyl-CoA and SAM serve as cofactors for histone-modifying enzymes. Through this mechanism, histone acetylation and methylation directly link metabolic states to NF-κB–driven transcriptional programs, thereby sustaining M1 activation and antitumor responses.27 Collectively, our findings reinforce the emerging concept that PTMs function as key regulatory switches that connect environmental cues to macrophage phenotype and effector function.

  For NK cells, our analysis revealed that TR-NK cells exhibit significantly elevated PTM-associated transcriptional enrichment compared to circulating CD56dim NK cells, particularly in acetylation, methylation, ubiquitination, and phosphorylation. These modifications regulate essential processes such as STAT5/ERK phosphorylation–driven cytokine secretion, ubiquitination-mediated receptor turnover, and chromatin remodeling of effector genes. Similar to growth hormone signaling, where receptor downregulation and post-receptor mechanisms determine pathway responsiveness, PTMs in NK cells fine-tune both receptor availability and downstream transcriptional programs, thereby balancing activation and refractoriness in response to repeated stimulation.28 Intriguingly, correlation analysis uncovered a divergence: while TR-NK cells were enriched in cytotoxic granule exocytosis and IFN-γ production pathways, several immune receptor activity and cytokine signaling pathways showed negative associations with PTM-associated transcriptional activity. This indicates that PTMs in TR-NK cells are not limited to enhancing cytotoxicity, but also serve as regulatory mechanisms to restrain excessive activation and preserve immune balance in tissues. Such findings are consistent with previous reports that TR-NK cells specialize in long-term persistence and tissue homeostasis, with tissue-specific clonal expansions suggesting roles in localized immune memory rather than immediate cytotoxicity.29 Mechanistically, persistent TGF-β signaling, intricately regulated by ubiquitination and SUMOylation, is essential for sustaining tissue residency, while concomitant histone modifications reprogram transcriptional networks to promote cellular adaptation and survival.30 These results suggest that PTM regulation in NK cells is highly context-dependent, functioning to enhance cytotoxicity in circulating NK cells but maintaining immune tolerance in TR-NK cells. This dual paradigm underscores the importance of subset-specific strategies when considering NK cell–based immunotherapies.

  In B-cell subclusters, plasma cells displayed a distinct PTM profile characterized by elevated acetylation, methylation, and glycosylation, consistent with their role as professional antibody-secreting cells. GO enrichment analysis revealed that plasma cell–specific pathways involved immunoglobulin production, ER protein folding, unfolded protein response (UPR), and glycosylation metabolism, all of which showed strong positive correlations with PTM-associated transcriptional activity. This highlights the essential role of PTMs in sustaining the extraordinary biosynthetic demands of plasma cells. In contrast, pathways linked to mitochondrial protein complexes and oxidative metabolism showed negative correlations with PTMs, suggesting a functional trade-off in which plasma cells prioritize secretory capacity over mitochondrial activity. HDAC inhibitors promote plasma cell differentiation by enhancing histone acetylation, thereby facilitating chromatin accessibility and transcription of key regulators such as XBP1 and IRF4. Concurrently, DNA methylation remodeling governs the B-to-plasma cell transition by reprogramming lineage-specific gene expression. Together, these epigenetic modifications integrate to sustain antibody-secreting function, highlighting their potential as therapeutic targets for modulating humoral immunity in both autoimmune disorders and cancer.31 Moreover, N-glycosylation of antibodies regulates both structural stability and effector functions by ensuring proper folding and maintaining conformational fidelity. These glycan modifications critically influence antigen binding, Fc receptor interactions, and complement activation, thereby modulating immune recognition, effector activity, and inflammatory responses.32 Aberrant glycosylation patterns can impair antibody-mediated cytotoxicity or promote pro-inflammatory signaling, linking altered glycosylation to autoimmune disorders and tumor immune evasion. Consequently, targeting antibody glycosylation has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy to enhance antibody efficacy in cancer immunotherapy and control dysregulated immune responses. Abnormal PTM profiles in plasma cells have been implicated in multiple myeloma and autoimmune disorders, where they contribute to dysregulated antibody production and immune tolerance breakdown. Beyond their canonical roles in regulating protein stability and transcription, aberrant acetylation, methylation, ubiquitination, and glycosylation in plasma cells have been shown to reshape secretory programs, alter antigen recognition, and disrupt cellular stress responses such as the unfolded protein response. These PTM-driven perturbations link metabolic reprogramming with maladaptive immune activation, thereby promoting disease progression. Understanding how specific PTM networks control plasma cell survival and antibody quality may thus uncover novel therapeutic targets for both malignant and autoimmune pathologies.33

  Together, these results underscore that PTMs shape immune specialization in a context-dependent manner. Importantly, these findings suggest that M1-like macrophage signatures in melanoma should be interpreted in a context-dependent manner rather than as direct evidence of effective antitumor immunity.


### Melanoma-High Subgroups Exploit PTM Plasticity and Genomic Instability
  Beyond immune regulation, we further dissected the molecular and functional differences between melanoma-high and melanoma-low subgroups, and the results indicated that melanoma-high cells represent a more malignant phenotype. First, classification based on PTM features showed that melanoma-high exhibited significantly elevated activity across multiple PTM types, particularly acetylation, methylation, and ubiquitination. This broadly enhanced PTM regulation suggests stronger transcriptional and protein regulatory plasticity, consistent with a highly aggressive tumor phenotype. Such plasticity enables melanoma-high cells to dynamically reprogram gene expression and protein function in response to environmental cues, thereby sustaining rapid proliferation and survival under stress. Similar to cancer cell plasticity driven by epithelial-mesenchymal transition or stem-like transitions, PTM-mediated epigenetic remodeling provides a flexible mechanism through which tumor cells can adapt to diverse microenvironmental pressures, evade immune surveillance, and develop resistance to therapy. Thus, elevated PTM-associated transcriptional enrichment not only reflects an intrinsic feature of tumor aggressiveness but also represents a critical driver of tumor heterogeneity and adaptability.34

  Further supporting the malignant potential of melanoma-high are its cell–cell communication features. Network analysis revealed that melanoma-high had markedly stronger interactions with macrophages, B cells, and NK cells compared with melanoma-low. Ligand–receptor analysis further identified several key immune regulatory pathways, including MIF–CD74, ICAM–ITGB2, and HLA-I–CD8A. These interactions have been implicated in impaired T-cell effector function and reduced cytotoxic CD8+ T-cell activity, processes that facilitate immune suppression and tumor immune evasion. Consistent with recent findings in hyper-progressive disease and sepsis, disruption or dysregulation of these pathways may critically weaken antitumor immunity and contribute to poor clinical outcomes.35,36 This indicates that melanoma-high cells not only possess intrinsic proliferative advantages but also actively reshape the tumor immune microenvironment to achieve immune escape. In addition, CNV analysis revealed that melanoma-high harbored widespread chromosomal copy number variations involving multiple amplifications and deletions. Genomic instability is a hallmark of tumor progression, frequently associated with elevated mutational burden, clonal evolution, and therapeutic resistance. In melanoma-high cells, widespread chromosomal alterations may not only fuel intratumoral heterogeneity but also undermine the predictive value of conventional biomarkers, thereby contributing to immune escape and treatment resistance. Similar to observations in other immunotherapy-responsive cancers, such as clear-cell renal-cell carcinoma, where high immune infiltration or mutational burden fail to consistently predict clinical outcomes, genomic instability in melanoma-high likely represents a key driver of its aggressive behavior and therapeutic refractoriness.37

  Beyond malignant potential, functional analyses also revealed marked differences between melanoma-high and melanoma-low. GO enrichment analysis showed that melanoma-high was specifically enriched in pathways related to protein synthesis, unfolded protein response (UPR), and antigen processing and presentation, reflecting its strong biosynthetic demands and immune regulatory capacity. In contrast, melanoma-low was more enriched in differentiation-related pathways such as keratinization and extracellular matrix organization, suggesting a relatively differentiated state with lower proliferative and adaptive pressure Metabolic pathway analysis further demonstrated that melanoma-high displayed higher activity in amino acid metabolism (glutamate, serine, aspartate) and lipid metabolism (triglyceride and fatty acid metabolism), providing not only energy support for rapid growth but also essential precursors for biosynthesis and redox balance. Such metabolic reprogramming resembles the well-established cancer paradigm in which deregulated amino acid utilization and lipid metabolism enable tumor cells to thrive under nutrient-limiting or stressed conditions. In particular, enhanced glutamine and serine metabolism contributes to nucleotide biosynthesis and one-carbon metabolism, while altered fatty acid synthesis and oxidation support membrane production and signaling modulation. These findings suggest that melanoma-high cells exploit metabolic plasticity as a key adaptive strategy, linking nutrient metabolism with proliferative capacity, survival, and therapy resistance.38,39 In contrast, melanoma-low exhibited relatively lower metabolic activity, consistent with its weaker biosynthetic requirements.

  Cell–cell communication pathway analysis also revealed distinct roles within the tumor microenvironment. The results showed that melanoma-high dominated the VEGF and MIF signaling networks, primarily functioning as a sender and influencer, suggesting that it promotes tumor progression through angiogenesis and macrophage regulation. Collectively, these findings indicate that melanoma-high integrates advantages in epigenetic remodeling, metabolic plasticity, and immune communication regulation, thereby constructing a microenvironment conducive to tumor progression and immune escape.

  Current research on melanoma heterogeneity has mainly focused on transcriptomic features and immune checkpoint expression, while the role of PTMs in tumor stratification remains insufficiently explored. Although previous studies have reported that alterations in PTMs such as acetylation and ubiquitination are associated with melanoma progression and drug resistance, few have systematically integrated PTM-associated transcriptional activity, CNV characteristics, immune communication, and metabolic reprogramming at the single-cell level. Considering that tumor evolution is profoundly influenced by both intrinsic genetic alterations and extrinsic microenvironmental factors, a comprehensive framework that incorporates PTM dynamics with genomic instability and TME heterogeneity is essential. Such integrative analyses may uncover how PTMs not only serve as molecular switches governing protein stability and signaling but also function as critical regulators linking chromatin remodeling, immune evasion, and metabolic adaptation. This systems-level perspective could provide novel insights into melanoma heterogeneity and highlight PTMs as actionable targets within the tumor microenvironment.40

  By systematically integrating PTM-associated transcriptional activity with genomic instability, metabolic rewiring, and immune communication, our study reveals that melanoma-high cells exploit PTM plasticity as a unifying mechanism of aggressiveness. This systems-level perspective advances beyond prior transcriptomic stratifications, highlighting PTMs as central regulators of tumor adaptability.


### Context-Dependent Macrophage Polarization and Immune Evasion in Melanoma
  In addition to these intrinsic tumor features, our immune infiltration analysis revealed an apparent discrepancy in macrophage polarization between the high- and low-risk melanoma groups. Specifically, the high-risk group showed a higher proportion of M1-like macrophages, whereas the low-risk group exhibited higher proportions of M2-like macrophages.41

  At first glance, this pattern appears to contrast with the classical M1-antitumor/M2-protumor paradigm. However, recent single-cell and functional studies have shown that tumor-associated macrophages are not adequately described by a rigid M1/M2 dichotomy. Instead, they occupy heterogeneous, spatially organized, and microenvironment-dependent states.42

  However, recent single-cell and functional studies suggest that tumor-associated macrophages are not adequately described by a rigid M1/M2 dichotomy. Similarly, recent work on TREM2-positive macrophages showed that specific tumor-associated macrophage states can impair NK-cell abundance and function, supporting the concept that myeloid-rich tumors may still display ineffective antitumor immunity.43 Consistently, PD-L1-positive macrophages have been shown to suppress CD8+ T-cell responses, further suggesting that macrophage-associated inflammation can coexist with checkpoint-mediated immune dysfunction.44

  In our study, the high-risk group showed increased M1-like macrophage proportions together with reduced stromal and immune scores, lower cytolytic activity and IFNG expression, broad upregulation of immune checkpoint molecules, and poorer survival. Therefore, the enrichment of M1-like macrophages should not be interpreted as evidence of effective antitumor immunity. Rather, it may reflect a chronic inflammatory but functionally constrained macrophage state shaped by the immunosuppressive melanoma microenvironment. Conversely, in our dataset, the higher proportion of M2-like macrophages in the low-risk group may reflect a less inflammatory immune context that accompanies lower overall risk, rather than a uniformly protumor state. These findings support a context-dependent interpretation of macrophage polarization in melanoma. They also suggest that transcriptome-inferred macrophage labels should be interpreted together with cytolytic activity, checkpoint expression, and clinical outcome when evaluating immune function.

  Consistent with this interpretation, our analysis of immune checkpoint gene expression further underscores the immunological divergence between high- and low-risk melanoma. The high-risk group exhibited significantly elevated expression of multiple checkpoint molecules, including CD274 (PD-L1), CD276 (B7-H3), CTLA4, LAG3, and PDCD1 (PD-1), while the low-risk group displayed comparatively lower levels of these inhibitory receptors and ligands. Notably, PD-L1 expression has been widely recognized as a key determinant of response to checkpoint blockade, particularly in lung cancer and melanoma, where PD-L1–positive tumors often correlate with higher immune infiltration but variable therapeutic outcomes.45 Similarly, B7-H3 (CD276), a novel inhibitory immune checkpoint, has been reported to be aberrantly upregulated across diverse cancers and is strongly associated with tumor immune evasion and poor prognosis, making it an emerging target for next-generation immunotherapies.46 The co-expression of classical checkpoints (PD-1/PD-L1, CTLA4) with emerging molecules (B7-H3, LAG3) in the high-risk group underscores the complexity of immune suppression in melanoma and suggests that combinatorial strategies targeting both established and novel checkpoints may be required to restore effective antitumor immunity.

  Interestingly, the co-upregulation of PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA4 in the high-risk group reflects classical immune evasion strategies, while increased expression of emerging checkpoints such as B7-H3 (CD276), LAG3, and TNFRSF members suggests the involvement of non-redundant inhibitory pathways. These molecules have been increasingly recognized as mediators of T-cell and NK-cell dysfunction in advanced melanoma, and their expression has been associated with resistance to conventional immune checkpoint blockade (ICB). This aligns with our finding that the high-risk group, despite harboring more pro-inflammatory M1-like macrophages, may remain immunologically suppressed through checkpoint-mediated regulation.

  By contrast, the low-risk group demonstrated relatively lower expression of these inhibitory checkpoints, implying a less exhausted immune microenvironment. Although M2-like macrophages were relatively enriched in the low-risk group, this finding should be interpreted cautiously and in the context of the overall lower-risk phenotype. M2-like macrophages may participate in immune regulation, tissue remodeling, and cytokine-mediated signaling, but their presence in this dataset did not coincide with the aggressive checkpoint-high and survival-poor phenotype observed in the high-risk group.47

  Collectively, these results indicate that high- and low-risk melanomas may adopt distinct immune regulatory patterns. High-risk tumors are characterized by maladaptive inflammatory macrophage signatures and checkpoint-mediated immune dysfunction, whereas low-risk tumors show relatively lower checkpoint activation despite the presence of M2-like macrophage signatures. Thus, rather than following the classical M1-antitumor/M2-protumor paradigm, our data support a more context-dependent model of macrophage polarization in melanoma progression.


### Glycolysis–PTM Coupling Links Metabolic Reprogramming to Immune Suppression
  In our metabolic pathway analysis, glycolysis was significantly enriched in the high-risk melanoma group, suggesting that these cells depend heavily on aerobic glycolysis to sustain rapid proliferation and survival, a hallmark feature consistent with the Warburg effect. Enhanced glycolysis not only supplies ATP and biosynthetic precursors for nucleotide, amino acid, and lipid synthesis, but also enables tumor cells to adapt to hypoxic niches. Meanwhile, lactate accumulation can acidify the tumor microenvironment, suppress cytotoxic T and NK cell activity, and promote regulatory immune populations. This metabolic reprogramming highlights the reciprocal crosstalk between cancer and immune cells, where altered nutrient utilization by tumor cells drives immune suppression and fosters tumor progression.48,49 Notably, correlation analysis revealed that glycolysis was tightly linked with multiple PTMs, including acetylation, methylation, phosphorylation, and ubiquitination. Mechanistically, glycolytic intermediates such as acetyl-CoA and S-adenosylmethionine provide essential cofactors and substrates for histone acetylation and methylation, directly linking metabolic flux to epigenetic regulation.50 Through these modifications, fluctuations in metabolite availability reshape chromatin accessibility and transcriptional programs, thereby promoting the expression of tumor-driving genes and facilitating cancer progression.51 In parallel, phosphorylation regulates the activity of key glycolytic enzymes such as PKM2, while ubiquitination determines the stability of HIF-1α, a central driver of glycolytic gene expression.

  These findings suggest that glycolysis and PTM regulation form a feed-forward loop in high-risk melanoma cells, whereby metabolic reprogramming enhances epigenetic plasticity and, in turn, PTMs sustain glycolytic capacity. Together, this metabolic–epigenetic interaction may contribute to immune suppression and tumor progression in high-risk melanoma.


### Identification of PTM-Associated Feature Genes with Biomarker and Therapeutic Potential
  To further elucidate the molecular drivers underlying this loop, we identified FCGR2A, TTYH2, SLC25A15, FOXM1, and FOXK1 as the top five feature genes through SHAP-based machine learning analysis, and further validated their expression patterns, cellular localization, and potential therapeutic implications. These genes not only distinguished high- and low-risk melanoma groups but also exhibited strong associations with immune infiltration, metabolic reprogramming, and PTMs.

  FCGR2A encodes a low-affinity Fc gamma receptor (FcγR) that mediates antibody-dependent phagocytosis and immune complex clearance. As a transmembrane glycoprotein expressed on various immune cells, FCGR2A plays a pivotal role in linking innate and adaptive immunity by recognizing the Fc portion of IgG. Genetic polymorphisms of FCGR2A have been reported to influence susceptibility to autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus and lupus nephritis, as well as to affect responses to antibody-based therapies. Owing to its critical role in immune regulation and disease pathogenesis, FCGR2A has emerged as a potential biomarker for immune dysfunction, infection, and cancer diagnosis.52 Our spatial transcriptomics analysis revealed that FCGR2A is highly expressed in macrophage-enriched regions within the tumor microenvironment, consistent with its role in shaping macrophage polarization and antibody-mediated immunity. Previous studies have linked FCGR2A polymorphisms to clinical responses to immunotherapy and antibody-based treatments, as these genetic variations can alter receptor affinity, expression, and downstream effector functions such as antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity and phagocytosis. Such findings suggest that FCGR2A may represent a predictive biomarker of therapeutic sensitivity and could aid in optimizing antibody-based therapeutic strategies.53 Notably, its association with ubiquitination and glycosylation pathways in PTM correlation analysis implies that receptor turnover and antibody interaction could be fine-tuned by PTMs, ultimately affecting immune surveillance.

  TTYH2, a member of the tweety homolog family, has been implicated in ion transport and tumor-related cellular behavior. Recent structural studies showed that TTYH2 and TTYH3 adopt Ca2+-dependent dimeric conformations, providing a structural basis for exploratory analyses of membrane-associated interactions.54 In addition, TTYH2 has been linked to inflammatory myeloid-cell responses, suggesting that this protein family may participate in immune-related cellular processes beyond its canonical annotation as a chloride channel-associated protein. More broadly, chloride channels and related membrane proteins have been associated with tumor progression and multidrug resistance, supporting the potential relevance of ion-channel-associated molecules in cancer biology.55

  In our study, TTYH2 was upregulated in melanoma tissues, associated with the high-risk score, and spatially enriched in tumor cell-dense regions. The compounds prioritized in the docking analysis also have independent relevance in melanoma or cancer therapy through their established targets. For example, JQ1, a BET/BRD4 inhibitor, has been reported to suppress melanoma cell survival, while IGF1R/IR signaling contributes to resistance to BRAF and MEK inhibitors in BRAF-mutant melanoma.56 However, these studies do not establish BMS-754807, KU-55933, NU7441, or JQ1 as validated TTYH2-targeting agents. Therefore, our molecular docking results should be interpreted as exploratory virtual-screening findings rather than evidence of direct pharmacological targeting of TTYH2. The observed binding poses may represent candidate interactions, but non-specific binding cannot be excluded. Further biochemical binding assays and functional experiments will be required to determine whether these compounds directly interact with TTYH2 or exert melanoma-related effects through their canonical targets.

  SLC25A15, encoding the mitochondrial ornithine transporter, is a crucial regulator of the urea cycle and amino acid metabolism. As a member of the SLC25 family, it participates in mitochondrial material transport and energy regulation, thereby linking nitrogen disposal to broader metabolic homeostasis. Dysregulation of SLC25A15 has been associated with altered amino acid metabolism and endoplasmic reticulum stress, processes increasingly recognized as drivers of tumor progression and immune modulation. These findings suggest that SLC25A15 may represent not only a metabolic regulator but also a potential therapeutic target within the cancer–metabolism–immunity axis.57 We observed increased expression of SLC25A15 in tumor tissues, aligning with the enrichment of glutamine/glutamate metabolism in the high-risk group. This finding is consistent with recent reports that metabolic rewiring supports melanoma growth and immune evasion. Oncogenic-driven alterations in glycolysis, lipid, and amino acid metabolism not only sustain rapid proliferation and metastatic potential but also reshape the tumor immune microenvironment by impairing effector immune cell functions. Such dual effects highlight metabolism as a central axis linking intrinsic tumor progression with extrinsic immune suppression in melanoma.25 Interestingly, SLC25A15 expression was positively correlated with acetylation and succinylation pathways, both of which are essential PTMs in mitochondrial function and metabolic regulation. Thus, SLC25A15 may contribute to metabolic adaptation in melanoma via PTM-dependent regulation of mitochondrial homeostasis.

  FOXM1, a transcription factor known to promote cell cycle progression and DNA repair, was markedly upregulated in high-risk melanoma. Consistent with pan-cancer findings, elevated FOXM1 expression has been associated with poor prognosis and adverse clinical outcomes. Beyond its classical roles in proliferation and genomic stability, FOXM1 expression in melanoma also correlated with immune checkpoint gene upregulation and reshaping of the tumor immune microenvironment, suggesting that it may act as both a driver of tumor aggressiveness and a modulator of immune responses.58 Its established role as a driver of tumor proliferation and resistance to therapy is consistent with our findings. FOXM1 has been reported to interact extensively with acetylation and phosphorylation modifications, which regulate its transcriptional activity and protein stability. Beyond these canonical PTM-dependent mechanisms, FOXM1 also participates in shaping the tumor microenvironment, modulating epigenetic programs, and influencing immune cell infiltration, highlighting its multifaceted role in tumor progression and therapeutic resistance.59 This suggests that PTM-mediated regulation of FOXM1 may further reinforce its oncogenic function in melanoma. Our data highlight FOXM1 as a potential therapeutic target, particularly in combination with HDAC inhibitors or kinase inhibitors that interfere with PTM-dependent activation.

  FOXK1, another forkhead family transcription factor, was also elevated in tumor tissues and associated with high-risk scores. Unlike FOXM1, FOXK1 has been less extensively studied in melanoma, though it has been reported to regulate glucose metabolism and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) in other cancers. Emerging evidence suggests that dysregulation of FOXK1 contributes to metabolic reprogramming, enhanced motility, and malignant progression, linking its activity to tumor growth and metastasis. Given these functional associations, FOXK1 holds potential as both a biomarker and a therapeutic target in melanoma, warranting further mechanistic and translational investigation.60 Given the enrichment of glycolysis in the high-risk group, FOXK1 may promote metabolic reprogramming in melanoma, and its activity could be regulated by acetylation and ubiquitination as suggested by our PTM-pathway associations. This positions FOXK1 as a novel candidate for further mechanistic exploration.

  From a translational perspective, our molecular docking analysis suggested that these five feature genes have distinct druggable potentials, with TTYH2 and FOXM1 emerging as particularly promising targets due to their strong binding affinities with multiple small molecules. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the integration of machine learning, single-cell transcriptomics, spatial mapping, and PTM-pathway analysis can uncover key molecular drivers in melanoma. Importantly, our findings highlight the dual relevance of these genes: as biomarkers for risk stratification and as potential therapeutic targets within the PTM-metabolism-immune regulation axis.


### PTM-Driven Plasticity Provides a Unifying Framework for Melanoma Heterogeneity and Therapy Resistance
  Taken together, our study establishes a conceptual framework in which post-translational modifications serve not merely as downstream molecular events but as central regulators that integrate genomic instability, metabolic rewiring, and immune remodeling in melanoma. By dissecting PTM heterogeneity at single-cell resolution and linking it to distinct metabolic programs, immune phenotypes, and genomic features, we provide novel insights into how PTM-driven plasticity fuels tumor aggressiveness and therapeutic resistance. Importantly, the identification of PTM-associated feature genes (FCGR2A, TTYH2, SLC25A15, FOXM1, and FOXK1) bridges mechanistic understanding with translational relevance, offering promising biomarkers for patient stratification and therapeutic intervention.

  Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the PTM-associated scores used in this study were inferred from transcriptomic gene-set enrichment analyses rather than from direct proteomic or biochemical measurements. Therefore, these scores should not be interpreted as direct indicators of protein-level PTM abundance, modification stoichiometry, enzymatic activity, or the direction of specific modification events, such as hypermethylation versus hypomethylation. In addition, genes within a given PTM-related gene set may include regulators, effectors, substrates, or pathway-associated components with different or even opposing biological effects. Thus, the PTM-associated enrichment scores in this study are best interpreted as relative PTM-associated transcriptional enrichment scores. Future proteomic, PTM-omic, and functional experiments will be required to validate the direction, magnitude, and biological consequences of specific PTM events.

  Second, although the single-cell dataset enabled high-resolution characterization of melanoma and microenvironmental heterogeneity, the sample size of the scRNA-seq cohort was limited. Therefore, the identified PTM-associated cell states and melanoma subgroups require further validation in larger single-cell cohorts from independent melanoma populations.

  Third, the spatial transcriptomic analysis was based on a single melanoma sample (VISDS000459). Therefore, this analysis provides illustrative spatial contextualization of selected prognostic gene expression patterns rather than population-level validation of generalizable spatial distributions. Additional independent spatial transcriptomic cohorts will be necessary to confirm the reproducibility and clinical relevance of these spatial patterns.

  Fourth, although the prognostic model was constructed using LASSO-Cox regression with cross-validation and was externally evaluated in the GSE19234 cohort, the possibility of overfitting cannot be completely excluded. In addition, the relatively small sample size of the GSE19234 validation cohort may limit the generalizability of the model. Further validation in larger, multicenter, and clinically annotated melanoma cohorts will be needed to confirm the robustness and clinical utility of the risk signature.

  Fifth, the molecular docking analysis involving TTYH2 should be interpreted with caution. TTYH2 is a chloride channel-associated protein, whereas several candidate compounds analyzed in this study have established pharmacological targets that are not known to be directly related to TTYH2. Therefore, the docking results should be considered exploratory virtual-screening findings rather than evidence of validated pharmacological targeting. Non-specific binding cannot be excluded, and biochemical binding assays and functional experiments are required to determine whether these compounds directly interact with TTYH2 or act through their canonical targets.

  Finally, although the integration of single-cell transcriptomic, bulk transcriptomic, spatial transcriptomic, immune infiltration, drug sensitivity, and molecular docking analyses supports the biological relevance of the PTM-associated signatures, the present study remains primarily computational. Experimental validation using melanoma cell models, patient-derived samples, PTM-specific proteomics, spatial proteomics, multiplex immunofluorescence, and functional assays will be needed to confirm the mechanistic roles and therapeutic implications of the identified PTM-associated programs.


## Conclusion
  This study presents an integrative single-cell and multi-omics framework to characterize PTM-associated transcriptional programs in melanoma. By integrating single-cell transcriptomics, bulk RNA-seq cohorts, spatial transcriptomics, immune infiltration analysis, drug sensitivity prediction, and molecular docking, we showed that PTM-associated transcriptional enrichment is closely linked to melanoma cell heterogeneity, metabolic reprogramming, immune microenvironment remodeling, and patient prognosis. The PTM-derived risk score may provide a complementary tool for prognostic stratification, although its clinical utility requires further validation in larger independent cohorts.

  We further identified five PTM-associated feature genes—FCGR2A, TTYH2, SLC25A15, FOXM1, and FOXK1—that contributed to risk prediction and displayed distinct expression patterns across transcriptomic and spatial contexts. Among them, TTYH2 showed exploratory docking interactions with several candidate compounds; however, these findings should be interpreted as virtual-screening results rather than evidence of a validated druggable target. Future studies incorporating PTM-specific proteomics, spatial validation, biochemical binding assays, and functional experiments will be necessary to confirm the mechanistic and therapeutic relevance of these PTM-associated programs in melanoma.
