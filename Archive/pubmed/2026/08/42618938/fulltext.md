# CHFR drives gastric cancer stemness and hepatic metastasis by suppressing ITCH-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of OCT4


## Background
  Gastric cancer (GC) is one of the most lethal malignancies worldwide, and hepatic metastasis is a major cause of its poor clinical prognosis. The stemness regulator octamer-binding transcription factor 4 (OCT4) drives the migration, invasion and metastasis of GC cells. As a RING-type E3 ubiquitin ligase, Checkpoint with Forkhead-associated and RING finger domains (CHFR) has been identified as a key promoter of metastasis in advanced GC. However, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying CHFR-mediated modulation of GC metastasis remain unclear.


## Results
  This study systematically explored the biological functions and molecular mechanism of CHFR in GC via public data analysis, in vitro cell experiments and in vivo hepatic metastasis models. Functional assays revealed that CHFR overexpression enhanced the stemness and metastatic capacity of GC cells but inhibited cell proliferation. Mechanistically, CHFR stabilized OCT4 protein by blocking itchy E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase (ITCH)-mediated ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of OCT4. Molecular docking predicted a direct interaction between CHFR and ITCH, and site mutation studies showed that their binding interface was essential for CHFR-induced OCT4 stabilization. DynaMut2 and FoldX mutagenesis confirmed Tyr530 (CHFR) and Thr222 (ITCH) as critical interfacial residues, whose alanine substitution weakened complex binding. Biochemical Co-IP ubiquitination assays verified CHFR targets ITCH for proteasomal degradation to suppress ITCH-dependent OCT4 breakdown. OCT4 knockdown rescue experiments further validated OCT4 as the essential downstream mediator of CHFR-induced glycolytic reprogramming, stemness and invasion in GC cells. Importantly, in vivo xenograft assays demonstrated that CHFR overexpression significantly promoted GC liver metastasis, whereas simultaneous overexpression of CHFR and ITCH reversed this metastatic phenotype.


## Conclusion
  CHFR enhances the stemness and metastatic progression of advanced GC cells by antagonizing ITCH-dependent OCT4 degradation, highlighting the CHFR/ITCH/OCT4 axis as a therapeutic target for GC treatment.

## Background
  Gastric cancer (GC) remains a significant global health challenge, ranking as the fifth most common cancer and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide [1, 2]. Despite advancements in surgical techniques and systemic therapies, including immunotherapies and targeted agents, the 5-year survival rate for advanced GC remains below 30% [3, 4], largely due to frequent metastasis, particularly to the liver [5]. Hepatic metastasis occurs in approximately 30–40% of GC patients [6], drastically worsening prognosis and limiting therapeutic efficacy [7]. Even with multimodal approaches combining neoadjuvant chemotherapy and radical resection [8], recurrence rates remain high, underscoring the urgent need to identify novel molecular targets to disrupt metastatic cascades and improve patient outcomes.

  Recent studies have highlighted the role of cancer stem cells (CSCs) in the metastatic potential of GC [9, 10]. CSCs possess stem-like properties, including self-renewal and the ability to differentiate, which contribute to tumor heterogeneity and resilience against conventional therapies [11–13]. Key factors associated with stemness, such as Octamer-binding Transcription factor 4 (OCT4), SRY-box transcription factor 2 (SOX2), and Homeobox protein Nanog (Nanog), have been implicated in the regulation of glycolysis, a metabolic pathway that supports the rapid proliferation of cancer cells [14, 15]. The enhanced glucose uptake and elevated glycolytic flux, which are hallmarks of the “Warburg effect”, serve as pivotal driving forces for stem cells. In this process, critical glycolytic enzymes such as Pyruvate Kinase M2 (PKM2), Hexokinase 2 (HK2), Glucose Transporter Type 4 (GLUT4), and Lactate Dehydrogenase A (LDHA) have emerged as essential regulatory factors, as their overexpression correlates with enhanced clonogenicity, drug resistance, and metastatic potential in stemness-enriched GC cell populations [16, 17]. However, the precise mechanisms governing GC stemness and its association with hepatic metastasis are still poorly understood, highlighting the need for further research in this domain.

  Emerging evidence delineates a paradoxical duality in Checkpoint with Forkhead-associated and RING finger domains (CHFR) functionality during gastric carcinogenesis. CHFR, a RING-type E3 ubiquitin (Ub)-ligase, functions as a tumor suppressor by ubiquitinating key regulatory proteins to control genomic stability and tumor progression [18]. While promoter hypermethylation of CHFR has been historically associated with tumor-suppressive effects in early-stage GC by impairing mitotic checkpoint control [19, 20], recent mechanistic studies reveal its context-dependent oncogenic reprogramming in advanced disease. Notably, our prior work uncovered that CHFR exerts metastasis-promoting functions by activating AKT and ERK via the NRF2-ROS axis [21] and induces epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) through Histone Deacetylase 1 (HDAC1)-dependent chromatin remodeling, further facilitating migratory phenotypes [22]. These findings collectively redefine CHFR as a molecular rheostat that dynamically regulates metastatic competence and cancer stemness maintenance in GC progression.

  Given that post-translational modifications play a crucial role in regulating protein function and cellular processes, the ubiquitination pathway has emerged as a potential mechanism underlying CHFR-mediated GC progression [23]. Ubiquitination, a dynamic and reversible modification, is primarily mediated by E3 Ub-ligase, which determine the substrate specificity of the ubiquitination process [24]. Among these, the E3 Ub ligase itchy E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase (ITCH) has been implicated in various cellular pathways, including those related to cancer development [25, 26]. ITCH targets numerous proteins for ubiquitination and subsequent degradation, thereby modulating key cellular functions such as cell signaling [27], differentiation [28], and survival [29].

  Of particular interest is OCT4, a master pluripotency transcription factor that governs the self-renewal of embryonic stem cells (ESCs) [30]. OCT4 protein stability is tightly regulated by ITCH-mediated Lys63-linked polyubiquitination [31], a key post-translational modification critical to maintaining cellular pluripotency. Prior studies have demonstrated that impaired ubiquitination of OCT4 enhances the stability of the OCT4 protein [32–34]. However, the molecular mechanisms orchestrating OCT4 protein stability in GC cells, particularly those operating post-translational regulation, tumor stemness, and metastatic dissemination, remain poorly characterized. In this study, we investigate the novel mechanistic axis by which CHFR inhibits ITCH-dependent ubiquitination and degradation of OCT4, augmenting OCT4-driven stem-like properties in GC cells and thereby promoting hepatic metastasis. Elucidating these mechanisms deepens our understanding of GC progression and could uncover new therapeutic targets for this aggressive disease.


## Methods

### Cell culture and treatments
  The GC cell lines used in this investigation were MKN-45 (YDT-0408) and AGS (YDT-0054), both obtained from INDIT Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China). AGS cells were grown on Ham’s F-12 K medium (PM150810, Procell, China). MKN-45 cells were grown in RPMI-1640 media (PM150110, Procell, China). The culture medium was supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, s-FBS-x-015, SERANA, China) and an antibiotic solution comprising 1% penicillin and streptomycin (15140-122, Gibco, USA). The cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 environment.


### Cell transfection
  To generate stable GC cell lines overexpressing CHFR and ITCH, lentiviral vectors carrying the target genes (Lenti-CHFR or Lenti-ITCH) and control vector (Lenti-control) were purchased from Youbio (Hunan, China). The plasmids were co-transfected with packaging plasmids into GC cells to produce lentiviral particles. Following 48 h of incubation, the viral supernatant was harvested, filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane, and transduced into GC cells at 60–70% confluence using Lipofectamine 2000 (11668-019, Invitrogen, USA) for 24 h. After replacing the culture medium, stable transfectants were selected by treatment with 2.5 µg/mL puromycin (Yuanye, China) for 7–10 days.

  For transient overexpression studies, AGS cells were transfected with either pcDNA3.1 empty vector, wild-type CHFR (CHFR-WT), or mutant CHFR (CHFR-MUT) constructs obtained from Youbio (Hunan, China). The human CHFR coding sequence was synthesized, digested with appropriate restriction enzymes, and cloned into the expression vector. Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent, followed by 24 h of culture. Transfection efficiency was subsequently assessed by western blot analysis to confirm protein expression.

  For transient endogenous CHFR knockdown, CHFR-specific small interfering RNAs (siR-CHFR) and negative control siRNAs (siR-NC) were synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd (China). MKN-45 and AGS GC cells were transfected with the corresponding siRNAs via Lipofectamine 2000, and western blotting was performed 24 h post-transfection to validate CHFR silencing efficiency. Detailed siRNA sequences are provided as below:

  siR-CHFR Sense: CGCCATGAAATTCAATCATAT

  siR-OCT4 Sense: CCCTCACTTCACTGCACTGTA

### Bioinformatics
  RNA-seq profiles and matched clinical data of GC were downoaded from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). GC samples were stratified into tertiles according to CHFR expression values: the top one-third samples were assigned to teh High-CHFR group, while the bottom one-third samples constituted the Low-CHFR group. The R package ggplot2 was applied to screen differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with cutoffs of |log₂FC| > 1 and p < 0.05, visulized with a volcano plot. Functional enrichment analyses of these DEGs for Gene Ontology (GO) and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways were further performed using the R package clusterProfiler. The survival curves were analyzed based on CHFR expression levels using the Kaplan-Meier Plotter online database (http://kmplot.com).


### Western blotting (WB)
  The total proteins of pretreated MKN-45 cells and AGS cells were extracted, and the concentration was examined using a BCA protein assay kit (G2026, Servicebio, China). Then the samples were separated through sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and then transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were incubated in 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature. After three times washes, the membranes were successively incubated in the primary antibodies at 4℃ overnight and in the corresponding secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. The protein bands were washed again and visualized using an integrated chemiluminescence imager (Clinx, China, ChemiScope S6). Details of the antibodies used are shown in Table 1.

### Transwell assay
  Cell migration and invasion capabilities of GC cells were examined using CytoSelect™ 24-well Cell Invasion assay kits in a transwell system. For invasion assessment, polycarbonate membranes (3422, Corning, USA) were pre-coated with 50% Matrigel (356234, Corning, USA), while uncoated membranes served for migration studies. Cells suspended in serum-free medium were plated in the upper chamber, with complete medium (500 µl) added to the lower compartment as a chemoattractant. Following 24 h incubation at 37 °C, cells were fixed with methanol (10014118, Sinopharm, China) and stained using 2% crystal violet (C110703, Aladdin, China). Quantitative analysis was performed by counting cells in multiple randomly selected microscopic fields after imaging.


### Methyl thiazolyl diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay
  The viability of the cells was evaluated using the MTT assay. Transfected Lenti-CHFR or Lenti-control cells (3 × 103 cells per well) were seeded into 96-well plates. After the cells had adhered to the plate, they were further cultured for 24, 48, and 72 h, respectively. Subsequently, 50 µL of MTT (M2128, Sigma, USA) was introduced to each well. Following a 3 h incubation, DMSO (30072418, HUSHI, Shanghai, China) was introduced into each well to dissolve any formazan crystals present. Relative cell viability was assessed by measuring the absorbance at 570 nm using a microplate reader (51119570, Thermofisher, USA).


### Colony formation assay
  Transfected GC cells (Lenti-CHFR, Lenti-control, pcDNA3.1, CHFR WT, or CHFR MUT) were plated in 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 10³ cells per well and cultured for 14 days to allow colony formation. Following incubation, the resulting colonies were sequentially fixed with methanol and stained with 1% crystal violet solution (C110703, Aladdin, China). Microscopic examination was performed, with colony quantification conducted by imaging and analyzing a minimum of three randomly selected microscopic fields per well.


### Sphere formation assay
  The GC cells were plated into low-adhesion plates (3471, Corning, USA) at a density of 3 × 10³ cells per well. The spheroid culture medium consisted of fibroblast growth factor (FGF, CYT-218, 10 ng/mL, Prospec, IL), epidermal growth factor (EGF, 20 ng/mL, Prospec, IL), B27 supplement (1% v/v, 12587010, Gibco, USA), and insulin (5 µg/mL, CYT-27, Prospec, IL). After a 10-day incubation period, cell spheroid formation was assessed under a microscope, and images were captured.


### Real-time reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR)
  After the appropriate treatment, the cells were collected, and total RNA was extracted. RNA was converted into cDNA using a cDNA synthesis kit (6215B; Takara, Japan). cDNA was diluted with DEPC H2O to a suitable concentration and used for the downstream qPCR assay. The PCR mixture (10 µL per reaction) consisted of 5 µL SYBR (RR420L, Takara, Japan), 4 µL of cDNA template, and 0.5 µL of each primer. The primers used are listed in Table 2. qPCR was performed using an ABI-7500 system (4351106; Thermo Fisher, USA). The mRNA expression of relative genes was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method. The β-actin was used as an endogenous control.

### Cycloheximide (CHX) chase experiments
  To examine the OCT4 protein degradation kinetics in Lenti-CHFR and control AGS cells. CHFR overexpressed cell lines and corresponding control cells were treated with CHX (20 µg/mL, S7418, Selleck, USA) for 0, 1, 2, and 4 h. The cell proteins were collected, and the expression of OCT4 and CHFR was measured using a WB assay.


### Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay
  AGS cells were subjected to five distinct experimental conditions: (1) The AGS cells were treated with MG132 (20 µM 6 h, HY-13295, MCE, USA) for 6 h or transfected with Lenti-CHFR; (2) The Myc-tagged ITCH (ITCH-Myc) or Flag-tagged CHFR (CHFR-Flag) plasmids were co-transfected into AGS cells expressing HA-tagged OCT4 (OCT4-HA); (3) OCT4-HA was co-transfected with either wild-type (WT) CHFR-Flag or mutant (MUT) CHFR-Flag (the tyrosine at the binding site with ITCH was mutated to alanine) into AGS cells; (4) ITCH-Myc was co-transfected with either WT CHFR-Flag or MUT CHFR-Flag into AGS cells; (5) CHFR-Flag was co-transfected with either WT ITCH-Myc or MUT ITCH-Myc (the threonine at the binding site with CHFR was mutated to alanine) into AGS cells. Subsequently, the treated AGS cells were lysed in NP-40 lysis buffer (P0013F, Beyotime, Shanghai) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail for general use (P1045, Beyotime) on ice for 30 min. Cleared lysates were obtained by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the clarified supernatant was collected. For each sample, a 50 µl aliquot of the total lysate was saved as the Input control. For the immunoprecipitation, 500 µg of total protein from the cleared lysate was incubated overnight at 4 °C with 2 µg of the relevant primary antibody (anti-OCT4, #2750, CST; anti-HA Tag, #3724, CST; anti-Flag Tag, #14793, CST; or anti-Myc Tag, #2278, CST) under gentle rotation. To capture the antigen-antibody complexes, 20 µl of pre-washed Protein A/G Magnetic Beads (#88802, Thermo Fisher) were added to each reaction and incubated for an additional 4 h at 4 °C. Subsequently, lysate loading buffer was added, and the mixture was boiled to denature proteins for WB analysis. The eluted proteins and corresponding input lysates were subsequently analyzed by western blotting using the indicated antibodies. To specifically detect poly-ubiquitinated OCT4, cell lysates were immunoprecipitated with an anti-OCT4 antibody, followed by WB with an anti-Ubiquitin (Ub) antibody (#43124, CST).


### Proteomic sequencing
  Following immunoprecipitation of samples with CHFR overexpression, proteomic sequencing was performed. First, quantitative analysis of the raw mass spectrometry data was conducted to obtain the expression intensity of each protein in different samples. Based on the processed quantitative data, differential protein expression between the overexpression group and the control group was calculated using an independent two-sample t-test to determine the p-value for each protein. Subsequently, the log2 fold change (log2FC) of each protein’s expression was computed. Thresholds for identifying differentially expressed proteins were set at p < 0.05 and |log2FC| ≥ 1. KEGG and GO enrichment analyses were then performed on these differentially expressed proteins to explore potential signaling pathways and biological functions. For visualization of differential proteins, a dot-bar plot was constructed to display the top 25 upregulated proteins sorted by log₂(Fold Change); dot color was coded by log₂FC value and dot size represented -log₁₀(P value). Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis was conducted on 181 upregulated proteins. Interaction pairs were predicted via STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) and imported into Cytoscape for circular layout visualization sorted by node degree. Minor isolated small interaction modules were eliminated to retain core interactions. POU5F1 (OCT4) interactors were specially arranged in the inner network layer, with other proteins stained pink. POU5F1 interactors were stratified by STRING experimental interaction scores: >0.5 (high-confidence, orange); 0.1–0.5 (medium-confidence, green); 0-0.1 (low-confidence, bright yellow); residual interactors were pale yellow. A separate scatter plot was further generated to specifically compare the expression differences of two candidate hub proteins.


### Molecular docking analysis
  The structures of for ITCH (pdb_00002p4r) and CHFR (pdb_00002xoc2P4R) were retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/). After removing water and ligands in PyMOL, hydrogen atoms were added to each protein structure using the “h_add” command to optimize the identification of hydrogen-bond interactions. The ZDOCK online server (https://zdock.umassmed.edu/) was used with default settings to dock the ITCH and CHFR structures which were designated as receptor (Chain A) and ligand (Chain B), respectively. The docking conformation with the highest ZDOCK score (indicating the most favorable binding energy) was selected as the predicted complex model for further analysis.

  The top-ranked docking complex was imported into PyMOL for visualization and interaction profiling. The ITCH and CHFR chains were distinctly colored and labeled as Chain A and Chain B, respectively. The binding interface was defined by residues within a 4 Å distance between the two chains. Specific intermolecular interactions were analyzed: hydrogen bonds were identified and plotted (distance ≤ 3.2 Å, donor-hydrogen-acceptor angle ≥ 120°) and polar contacts were detected using the “find_polar_contacts” command. For clarity in the final figures, hydrogen bonds and polar contacts are represented by green and yellow dashed lines, respectively.


### Animal experiments
  18 five-week-old female BALB/c nude mice, acquired from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) were housed in a controlled environment (free of pathogens, with a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle and a temperature maintained at 22℃) for approximately one week to establish subcutaneous xenografts tumor models. The mice were divided into three groups: Lenti-control (n = 6), CHFR overexpressing MKN45 cells (Lenti-CHFR, n = 6), and MKN45 cells overexpressing both CHFR and ITCH (Lenti-CHFR + ITCH, n = 6). Xenograft tumor models were established by subcutaneously injecting with MKN45 cells stably transfected with Lenti-control, Lenti-CHFR or Lenti-CHFR + ITCH (5 × 106/200 µL) into the mice. Once the tumors had grown to about 1500 mm3, all experimental mice were euthanized, and tumor tissues were excised. The weight and volume of the isolated tumor tissues were measured and recorded. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula V = 1/2 × (length × width²). Tumor dimensions were measured with digital calipers to record the longest diameter (length) and the perpendicular shorter diameter (width).

  9 five-week-old female BALB/c nude mice were used to establish liver metastasis models, with the same conditions for purchase and feeding as those for xenograft tumor models. For liver metastasis models, 4 × 10⁶ MKN45 cells in 50 µl were injected using the same experimental groups (n = 3) as described for xenograft tumor models. All experimental mice were euthanized at 4 weeks post-injection, and liver tissues were excised and photographed for documentation.


### Immunohistochemistry (IHC) assay
  The target protein expression in liver tissues was detected by IHC staining. Liver tissue specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (G1101, Servicebio, China) for 7 days prior to processing. The fixed tissues underwent sequential dehydration through graded ethanol (100092683, Sinopharm, China) and xylene (10023418, Sinopharm, China) solutions, followed by paraffin embedding. Subsequently, 5-µm-thick tissue sections were prepared and processed through standard deparaffinization and rehydration steps. Antigen retrieval was performed before overnight incubation with primary antibodies at 4 °C. Following thorough washing, the sections were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies and developed using DAB substrate (P0203, Beyotime, China). All antibodies used in this study are detailed in Table 1. Finally, the stained sections were examined and imaged under an optical microscope (CX31, Olympus Corp, Japan) at 400× magnification for protein expression analysis.


### Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining
  The liver tissues of the mouse model were fixed, dehydrated in ethanol solutions of different concentrations, cleared with xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Section  (5 μm) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, stained with H&E stain (G1003, Servicebio, China), dehydrated again, and mounted with neutral balsam. Images were captured under a light microscope (Nikon, Japan, E100) to assess tissue morphology.


### Statistical analysis
  GraphPad Prism 6.0 (USA, SCR_002798) was used to analyze differences between the two groups using a t-test, and comparisons among multiple groups were evaluated through one-way ANOVA. Data were derived from at least three independent experiments and are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The statistical significance level was set at 0.05; * corresponds to a p-value < 0.05; ** corresponds to a p-value < 0.01. Figures were created using Photoshop CC 2019 (Adobe, USA).


## Results

### CHFR drives GC progression, correlating with poor prognosis and advanced disease stage
  Based on our previous findings identifying CHFR as a critical driver for GC progression [21, 22], this study further investigated its underlying mechanisms. RNA-seq profiles and corresponding clinical data of GC downloaded from TCGA were stratified into High-CHFR (top tertile) and Low-CHFR (bottom tertile) patient subgroups by CHFR expression values, and subsequent DEG screening with cutoffs of |log₂FC| > 1 and p < 0.05 identified 3108 differential genes (2049 upregulated, 1059 downregulated), whose global expression distribution was visualized using a volcano plot (Fig. 1A). Additionally, the GO and KEGG pathway enrichment results indicated that the CHFR-related DEGs were significantly enriched in pathways related to cellular stemness, energy metabolism (Glycolysis/Gluconegenesis), and other signaling cascades (Fig. 1B&C). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed that the low-CHFR group exhibited significantly better prognosis than the high-CHFR group, implying CHFR’s potential oncogenic role (Fig. 1D). Furthermore, the clinical correlation analyses showed that CHFR expression correlated considerably with metastatic (M) stage, tumor stage, and histological grade (p < 0.05) (Table 3), underscoring its importance in disease progression and patient outcomes of GC.

### CHFR overexpression promotes metastasis, inhibits proliferation, and enhances stemness in GC cells
  To deeply investigate the functional role of CHFR in GC, two stable CHFR-overexpressing (Lenti-CHFR) cell lines (MKN-45 and AGS) were established using lentiviral vectors, with control cells infected with empty lentivirus (Lenti-control). WB assay showed a significant upregulation of CHFR protein levels in both Lenti-CHFR cells compared to controls (Fig. 2A), confirming its overexpression efficiency. Transwell migration and invasion assays revealed a notable enhancement in the migratory and invasive capacities of CHFR-overexpressing GC cells compared to their respective controls (Fig. 2B). Interestingly, MTT assays showed that CHFR overexpression markedly inhibited cell proliferation in both MKN-45 and AGS cells (Fig. 2C). Similarly, colony formation assays demonstrated a significant decrease in the number of single-cell-derived colonies in CHFR-overexpressing cells (Fig. 2D). These results indicate that CHFR overexpression enhances metastatic potential while inhibiting cell growth and clonogenic ability in GC, aligning with our earlier findings on CHFR’s role in GC cells (SGC-7901 and AGS) [21]. Additionally, sphere-forming assays indicated enhanced cancer stemness and self-renewal potential, with CHFR-overexpressing GC cells forming larger and more numerous tumor spheroids (Fig. 2E). We further performed complementary CHFR knockdown experiments with siR-CHFR and siR-NC negative control in the same two GC cell lines (MKN-45 and AGS), with all corresponding functional and molecular data summarized in Supplementary Figure S1. First, WB verified the robust silencing efficiency of endogenous CHFR upon siRNA transfection (Fig. S1A). Opposite phenotypic changes were consistently detected after CHFR knockdown: MTT and colony formation assays displayed accelerated cell proliferation and elevated clonogenic capacity (Fig. S1B-C), whereas Transwell and spheroid formation assays presented suppressed migration, invasion and stem cell self-renewal (Fig. S1D-E). These findings highlight a dual role for CHFR in GC: while suppressing cell growth and clonogenicity, it enhances metastatic potential and cancer stem-like features, reinforcing the complex role of CHFR in GC progression.

### CHFR overexpression stabilizes OCT4 protein by inhibiting its ubiquitination in GC cells
  Given the significant enrichment of CHFR-related DEGs in cell stemness and metabolic reprogramming, stemness-related biomarkers and glycolytic enzymes were analyzed. Unexpectedly, RT-qPCR showed no significant differences in mRNA levels of stemness-associated biomarkers, including OCT4, SOX2, and Nanog, between CHFR-overexpressing and control GC cells (Fig. 3A). However, WB assays showed that CHFR overexpression significantly upregulated protein levels of OCT4, SOX2, and Nanog in both GC cell lines (Fig. 3B), indicating post-transcriptional regulation of these stemness factors. WB analyses further revealed that CHFR-overexpressing cells exhibited elevated protein levels of PKM2, HK2, GLUT4, and LDHA compared to controls (Fig. 3C), suggesting that CHFR overexpression enhances glycolysis, a hallmark of cancer stem-like cells [35]. Additionally, qPCR and western blot analyses illustrated that stemness markers and glycolytic effectors were markedly downregulated following CHFR silencing (Fig. S1F-H). OCT4, a core pluripotency transcription factor, regulates the maintenance of ESCs, differentiation, and somatic reprogramming via its protein levels [34, 36]. Reportedly, disrupting OCT4 ubiquitination enhances its protein stability [32], thereby boosting CSCs tumorigenicity [37]. Notably, we found that the protein degradation rate of CHFR and OCT4 was slower in CHFR-overexpressing AGS cells treated with CHX, a protein synthesis inhibitor, than in control cells (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, when cells were treated with MG132, a proteasome inhibitor, and then subjected to Co-IP with an anti-OCT4 antibody, WB analysis of the immune complex revealed an increased protein level of OCT4 and a lower level of OCT4 ubiquitination in CHFR-overexpressing AGS cells compared to control cells (Fig. 3E). To establish the causal link between OCT4 and CHFR-induced glycolytic remodeling and malignant phenotypes, we performed OCT4 knowdown rescue experiments in AGS cells. WB assays first confirmed the efficient silencing of endogenous OCT4 upon siR-OCT4 transfection (Fig. 4A). Subsequent phenotypic detection showed that OCT4 depletion abolished the elevated migratory, invasive and sphere-forming capacities triggered by CHFR overexpression (Fig. 4B-C). Immunoblotting results further illustrated that silencing OCT4 reversed the upregulation of stemness biomarkers (OCT4, SOX2, and Nanog) and glycolysis-associated proteins (PKM2, HK2, LDHA, and GLUT4) induced by CHFR overexpression (Fig. 4D-E). These findings suggest that CHFR overexpression stabilizes OCT4 protein by inhibiting its ubiquitination and subsequent degradation through the proteasome pathway, thereby contributing to the enhanced stemness of GC cells, at least partially.

### CHFR stabilizes OCT4 by interfering with ITCH-mediated ubiquitination to promote cancer stemness
  To investigate the proteomic changes induced by CHFR overexpression, Co-IP samples were subjected to proteomic sequencing. Following quantitative data normalization and differential expression analysis, 257 proteins were identified as significantly altered between the CHFR-overexpressing group and control group, including 181 upregulated and 76 downregulated proteins (Fig. 5A). Functional enrichment analyses via KEGG and GO revealed prominent involvement of these DEPs in glycolysis and cellular stemness-related signaling pathways, highlighting their potential roles in CHFR-mediated cellular processes (Fig. 5B&C). The dot-bar plot (Fig. 5D) illustrated the rank of top upregulated proteins with distinct fold change and significance gradients. From the filtered PPI network (Fig. 5E), SALL4 and ITCH were labeled as proteins that have highly reliable interactions with the target protein POU5F1 (OCT4). The dedicated scatter plot (Fig. 5F) further confirmed that ITCH possessed a larger log₂(Fold Change) value than SALL4, indicating a more robust upregulation after CHFR overexpression. Notably, ITCH was significantly upregulated in the CHFR-overexpressing group, functioning as a critical regulator of OCT4 stability in ESC self-renewal and somatic cell reprogramming [31]. Next, the effects of CHFR on the E3 ubiquitin ligase-mediated OCT4 ubiquitination and degradation were investigated to understand how CHFR stabilizes OCT4 in GC. We first performed a molecular docking analysis. As shown in Fig. 6A, a predicted three-dimensional model positions the CHFR protein (Chain B) in proximity to a specific region of the ITCH protein (Chain A). Detailed visualization of the interface revealed that CHFR (3498 residues) engages ITCH (601 residues) through key intermolecular contacts. Critical residues at the binding interface are shown in stick representation. The interaction is stabilized by six specific hydrogen bonds (green dashed lines) and four additional polar contacts (yellow dashed lines).

  To further dissect the functional contribution of the core interfacial residues Tyr530 of CHFR and Thr222 of ITCH, we performed two sets of computational mutagenesis analyses. DynaMut2 stability prediction demonstrated that Tyr530A and Thr222A only induced mild structural destabilization of individual proteins, with ΔΔGstability values of -0.4 kcal/mol and − 0.61 kcal/mol (both below 1 kcal/mol), which were categorized as weak destabilizing mutations (Fig. S2A&B). Given that alanine is a small hydrophobic residue, alanine substitution primarily eliminates native intermolecular interactions at the binding interface rather than disrupting the global tertiary conformation of each protein. We next utilized FoldX 5.1 combined with AnalyseComplex to quantify changes in CHFR-ITCH complex binding free energy upon single-point alanine substitutions. The wild-type complex displayed a baseline binding free energy (ΔGbind) of -50.55 kcal/mol. For the Tyr530A mutant, ΔGbind was altered to -24.22 kcal/mol with a ΔΔG of + 26.33 kcal/mol, while the Thr222A mutant presented ΔGbind = -35.95 kcal/mol and ΔΔG = + 14.60 kcal/mol (Table S1). The positive ΔΔG values of both mutants indicated markedly weakened binding affinity between CHFR and ITCH, supporting that Tyr530 and Thr222 serve as pivotal residues maintaining the CHFR-ITCH binding interface. Collectively, these computational data suggests a potential physical interaction between CHFR and ITCH.

  To validate whether CHFR directly mediates proteasomal degradation of ITCH via this binding interaction, we performed Co-IP ubiquitination assays with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 in AGS GC cells. Input western blot measurements revealed that stable CHFR overexpression decreased endogenous ITCH protein levels, whereas MG132 treatment partially rescued this ITCH protein loss. Immunoprecipitation targeting ITCH followed by ubiquitin immunoblotting further demonstrated that CHFR overexpression increased the ubiquitination modification of ITCH, and this ubiquitinating effect was further augmented upon MG132-mediated proteasome blockade (Fig. 6B). Importantly, following transfection of AGS cells with ITCH-Myc and either CHFR-WT-Flag or CHFR-MUT-Flag (point mutation at residue Tyr530), Co-IP with Flag antibodies revealed stronger ITCH binding to CHFR-WT than to CHFR-MUT (Fig. 6C). Conversely, Myc-tagged ITCH-WT showed stronger binding to CHFR-Flag compared to an Myc-tagged ITCH-MUT (point mutation at residue Thr222) with impaired CHFR-binding capacity (Fig. 6D). Subsequently, Myc-tagged ITCH (ITCH-Myc) or Flag-tagged CHFR (CHFR-Flag) plasmids were co-transfected into HA-tagged OCT4 (OCT4-HA) AGS cells. Co-IP with HA antibodies followed by WB analysis with Ub antibodies revealed that ITCH expression significantly reduced OCT4 protein levels by enhancing OCT4 ubiquitination in AGS cells, indicating ITCH-mediated OCT4 degradation in GC cells. In AGS cells overexpressing CHFR, the ubiquitination level of OCT4 was significantly reduced compared to the control group, accompanied by an increase in OCT4 protein expression. Importantly, when ITCH and CHFR coexisted within the immune complex, the ubiquitination level of OCT4 was partially reversed, leading to a decrease in OCT4 protein levels (Fig. 6E). To validate the functional relevance of the predicted CHFR-ITCH interaction interface, we generated a CHFR-MUT by introducing point mutation at residue Tyr530. AGS cells were transfected with OCT4-HA and CHFR-WT-Flag or CHFR-MUT-Flag, followed by Co-IP with HA antibodies and WB analysis with Ub antibodies. We observed that CHFR-mediated stabilization of OCT4 was significantly impaired in CHFR-MUT cells compared to CHFR-WT cells, as evidenced by reduced protein expression and increased ubiquitination of OCT4 (Fig. 6F). These findings confirm that CHFR inhibits OCT4 ubiquitination and subsequent degradation through its interaction with ITCH, thereby maintaining OCT4 protein expression.

  To assess functional consequences, AGS cells were transfected with pcDNA3.1 (empty vector), CHFR-WT, or CHFR-MUT, with WB assay confirming the expression efficiency (Fig. 7A). Functional assays revealed that CHFR-WT suppressed proliferation and colony formation, enhanced migration and invasiveness, whereas CHFR-MUT partially restored these capacities in AGS cells (Fig. 7B-D). More importantly, the facilitation effect of CHFR-WT on spheroid size and number was also reversed by CHFR-MUT in AGS cells (Fig. 7E). Taken together, these data reinforce that CHFR stabilizes OCT4 by disrupting ITCH-mediated ubiquitination, driving cancer stemness.

### CHFR stabilizes OCT4 through ITCH-mediated protein degradation to potentiate hepatic metastasis in GC xenograft models
  To assess the roles of CHFR and ITCH in vivo, we established subcutaneous xenografts in nude mice (n = 6 per group) using MKN45 cells transduced with Lenti-control, Lenti-CHFR (CHFR overexpression), or Lenti-CHFR + ITCH (CHFR and ITCH co-overexpression). WB assay confirmed successful overexpression and revealed that ITCH co-expression effectively rescued the CHFR-induced reduction in ITCH protein levels (Fig. 8A). In subcutaneous GC xenograft models, Lenti-CHFR tumors were significantly smaller and lighter than controls, whereas Lenti-CHFR+ITCH tumors exhibited markedly larger volume and greater weight compared to Lenti-CHFR alone (Fig. 8B), demonstrating that CHFR overexpression suppressed GC progression, an effect effectively rescued by ITCH overexpression.

  Hepatic metastasis frequently complicates GC progression, severely compromising therapeutic efficacy and prognostic outcomes [7, 38], which underscores the imperative for mechanistic investigation of this metastatic cascade. WB analysis revealed an inverse correlation between OCT4 and ITCH protein levels in GC xenograft tumor tissues, supporting ITCH’s negative regulation of OCT4 (Fig. 8C). Pathological examination of liver tissues post-euthanasia demonstrated that mice injected with CHFR-overexpressing MKN45 cells alone harbored significantly more metastatic lesions than other groups. Notably, co-overexpression of CHFR and ITCH partially attenuated hepatic metastasis, suggesting that ITCH overexpression alleviates CHFR-mediated suppression (Fig. 8D). IHC staining further confirmed that CHFR overexpression inhibits ITCH-mediated OCT4 protein degradation in liver tissues (Fig. 8E). Moreover, HE staining revealed larger and more numerous metastatic lesions in the CHFR-overexpressing group, consistent with its pro-metastatic role in GC (Fig. 8F). These findings collectively demonstrate that CHFR drives GC progression and hepatic metastasis through OCT4 stabilization via suppression of ITCH-mediated proteasomal degradation.


## Discussion
  GC is a highly heterogeneous malignancy driven by abnormal proliferation and differentiation of gastric mucosal epithelial cells [39, 40]. CSCs have been shown to play key roles in GC initiation, recurrence, metastasis, and treatment resistance [41, 42]. They contribute to tumor heterogeneity and drive distant spread, particularly to the liver-the most common and deadly metastatic site [43]. However, the molecular pathways governing CSCs maintenance and their role in GC metastasis remain poorly understood, limiting the development of targeted therapies.

  CHFR has traditionally been recognized as a mitotic checkpoint regulator and considered a tumor suppressor due to its frequent promoter hypermethylation and reduced expression across multiple cancers [20, 44, 45]. However, our previous studies have revealed a paradoxical role of CHFR in GC, suggesting it may function as a metastasis-promoting factor in specific contexts [21, 22]. This study further dissected the molecular mechanism by which CHFR drives hepatic metastasis in GC. We demonstrated that CHFR acts as a key regulatory factor in GC metastasis by stabilizing OCT4 protein through the inhibition of ITCH-mediated ubiquitination. Consistent with our prior findings, this study further uncovered the molecular mechanism by which CHFR contributes to GC progression, reinforcing its oncogenic potential beyond its classical tumor-suppressive profile.

  Disruption of the Ub-proteasome system (UPS), which regulates protein and cellular homeostasis, is a hallmark of many cancers [46–48]. This often results from the aberrant activity of E3 Ub ligases and deubiquitinating enzymes, which can exert dual roles by promoting the degradation of tumor suppressors or stabilizing oncogenic proteins, depending on their specific substrates [49, 50]. For example, Ub-protein ligase E3C (UBE3C) promotes GC progression by targeting axis inhibition protein 1 (AXIN1) for degradation and activating β-catenin signaling [51], while F-box and WD-repeat domain-containing 2 (FBXW2) facilitate epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) degradation to suppress prostate cancer growth and metastasis [52]. Similarly, the tripartite motif-containing 21 (TRIM21)-mediated ubiquitination of tyrosine aminotransferase (TAT) inhibits liver metastasis in gallbladder cancer [53]. ITCH is a well-characterized E3 ligase that targets various oncoproteins for degradation [54–56]. OCT4, a key regulator of cancer stem cell properties, has been closely linked to poor prognosis, chemoresistance, and metastasis in several cancers [57, 58]. However, its post-translational regulation by ITCH remains unexplored in GC.

  In this study, we investigated the role of CHFR in regulating OCT4 stability and its contribution to GC progression. Mechanistically, our results demonstrated a physical association between CHFR and ITCH, as confirmed by molecular docking and Co-IP assays, thereby hindering its ability to ubiquitinate OCT4. This interference led to OCT4 stabilization, thereby bolstering the stemness properties of GC cells. Functionally, CHFR overexpression promoted tumor spheroid formation and significantly increased OCT4, SOX2, and Nanog expression at protein levels, underscoring its role in promoting stem-like properties. Notably, CHFR overexpression also suppressed GC cell proliferation and colony formation, potentially reflecting a transition toward a less proliferative but more invasive and stem-like phenotype. The CHFR mutant lacking the ITCH-binding domain partially reversed these effects, further substantiating the functional specificity of the CHFR-ITCH-OCT4 axis. In vivo, we innovatively demonstrated that CHFR overexpression promoted hepatic metastasis and OCT4 expression, an effect attenuated by ITCH co-expression, supporting the biological relevance of this pathway in metastatic GC. Together, these findings position CHFR as a key regulator of metastasis and stemness in GC through post-translational stabilization of OCT4 and highlight the CHFR-ITCH-OCT4 axis as a promising therapeutic target.

  Consistent with our mechanistic findings, clinical data analyses from public GC cohorts indicate that elevated CHFR expression correlated significantly with poorer patient survival and increased metastatic progression. These clinical correlations strengthen the hypothesis that CHFR functions as a context-dependent metastasis promoter rather than a classical tumor suppressor, highlighting its potential as a valuable prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target. Furthermore, CHFR-driven OCT4 stabilization may promote metabolic reprogramming toward glycolysis, a hallmark associated with metastatic competency and CSCs traits. This is supported by our previous finding showing that CHFR activates protein kinase B (AKT) and extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) pathways via nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2)-mediated reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby promoting GC metastasis [21].

  Despite these insights, our study has several limitations. First, as the experiments were primarily conducted in preclinical models, using immunodeficient mice precludes the evaluation of CHFR-ITCH-OCT4 signaling in an intact immune microenvironment context. Given the emerging association between CSCs and immune evasion, it remains to be determined whether this pathway influences immune cell infiltration or modulates responses to immunotherapy. Second, while we confirmed the interaction between CHFR and ITCH and demonstrated its effect on OCT4 stabilization, a limitation of this study is the lack of direct structural evidence regarding how CHFR binding might alter ITCH conformation or activity. Future structural biology work (e.g., isothermal titration calorimetry and surface plasmon resonance) will be essential to resolve this mechanism. Finally, the prognostic value of CHFR expression was derived from correlative database analyses, and further validation using clinical specimens will be necessary to establish its clinical relevance. Future investigations utilizing immunocompetent models and patient-derived tissues will be critical to fully elucidate the therapeutic potential of targeting this axis in GC.


## Conclusion
  This study elucidates a novel mechanism by which CHFR promotes GC stemness and hepatic metastasis through post-translational regulation of OCT4. By binding ITCH, CHFR inhibits ITCH-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of OCT4, thereby stabilizing OCT4 protein levels. Our findings redefine CHFR as a context-dependent oncoprotein in advanced GC, and uncover the CHFR/ITCH/OCT4 pathway as a pivotal regulator of cancer stem-like cell-driven metastatic dissemination. Targeting this pathway may offer therapeutic strategies to disrupt the metastatic and improve outcomes in GC patients.
