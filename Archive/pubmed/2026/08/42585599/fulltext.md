# Elucidating the Mechanisms of Trichloroethylene‐Induced Kidney Cancer: Network Toxicology, Molecular Docking, and In Vitro Validation


## ABSTRACT
  Trichloroethylene (TCE), a pervasive environmental contaminant, is epidemiologically linked to kidney, but the precise molecular mechanisms underlying this association remain insufficiently elucidated. To decipher the toxicological network, an integrated approach combining network toxicology, molecular docking, and in vitro validation was employed, resulting in the identification of TP53, ACTB, and CASP3 as pivotal hubs from 599 intersecting targets. Functional enrichment analyses implicated these targets in the dysregulation of cellular proliferation and apoptosis, predominantly mediated by the PI3K‐Akt signaling cascade, while clinical correlation analysis revealed that their significant overexpression in tumor tissues was associated with advanced pathological staging, unfavorable prognosis, and heightened infiltration of immunosuppressive cell populations, including Tr1 cells, macrophages, and exhausted T cells. Furthermore, potential binding interactions between TCE and these proteins were predicted by molecular docking and subsequently validated by in vitro experiments, wherein TCE exposure dose‐dependently upregulated TP53 and ACTB expression while concomitantly reducing cleaved CASP3 levels. Collectively, this study establishes a mechanistic framework demonstrating that TCE promotes kidney carcinogenesis by directly dysregulating TP53, ACTB, and CASP3, offering critical insights into environ‐mental oncogenesis and potential therapeutic interventions.


## Introduction
  The acceleration of industrialization has exacerbated environmental degradation, precipitating a global public health crisis characterized by the pervasive dissemination of chemical contaminants [1, 2]. Of particular concern is the escalating incidence of neoplasms linked to environmental pollutants, a trend that underscores the urgency of addressing carcinogenic exposures [3]. Notably, chronic exposure to organic solvents, specifically trichloroethylene (TCE), has been strongly associated with elevated carcinogenic risk [4, 5].

  TCE constitutes a ubiquitous industrial solvent extensively employed in metal degreasing and chemical synthesis [6]. Despite its industrial utility, prolonged high‐level exposure to TCE has been implicated in the pathogenesis of neurological impairment, hepatotoxicity, and malignancies, thereby posing substantial hazards to occupationally exposed populations [7]. Epidemiological investigations have corroborated a robust correlation between occupational TCE exposure and tumorigenesis, with a notably pronounced association observed for kidney cancer [8].

  Although the etiology of kidney cancer is multifactorial, environmental carcinogens such as TCE are increasingly recognized as critical etiological agents [9, 10]. Upon entry via inhalation or dermal absorption, TCE is metabolized into genotoxic intermediates that facilitate tumorigenesis through the induction of genomic instability, oncogene activation, and the suppression of tumor suppressor expression [11]. Furthermore, interference with intracellular signaling cascades and apoptotic pathways has been postulated to drive neoplastic progression [12, 13]. Consequently, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying TCE‐induced kidney carcinogenesis remain to be fully elucidated.

  Network toxicology, an emerging interdisciplinary paradigm integrating systems biology and bioinformatics, enables the systematic mapping of molecular interaction networks, offering comprehensive toxicity assessments with reduced reliance on animal models [14, 15]. Complementarily, molecular docking facilitates the prediction of optimal ligand‐receptor binding orientations, thereby elucidating binding modes and affinities critical for deciphering toxicological mechanisms [16].

  In this study, network toxicology and molecular docking were synergistically integrated with in vitro experiments to systematically identify and characterize the key toxic targets of TCE in kidney cancer. The primary objective was to unveil the underlying molecular mechanisms and carcinogenic potential of TCE, thereby establishing a scientific foundation for the formulation of targeted prevention and control strategies (Figure 1).


## Methods

### Prediction of Toxicity Targets for TCE
  The standard structure and SMILES notation of TCE were retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Putative TCE targets were identified by querying the ChEMBL (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/chembl/), STITCH (http://stitch.embl.de/), and CTD (https://ctdbase.org/) databases using the keyword “TCE,” with the species restricted to Homo sapiens. For CTD‐derived data, an interaction count exceeding the median value of 1 was applied as a screening threshold. Retrieved target names were standardized via the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/), and the resulting dataset was integrated and deduplicated.


### Prediction of Targets for Kidney Cancer
  Kidney cancer‐associated targets were retrieved from the GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/) and OMIM (https://www.omim.org/) databases. For GeneCards‐derived data, a relevance score above the median value of 2.656 was applied as a cutoff. These datasets were merged with OMIM entries and subjected to deduplication. Venn diagram analysis was performed to identify overlapping targets between TCE‐related and kidney cancer‐related datasets, and intersecting genes were considered candidate common targets implicated in TCE‐induced kidney toxicity.


### Functional Enrichment Analysis
  To elucidate the biological functions of the common targets, Gene Ontology (GO, http://geneontology.org) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG, https://www.genome.jp/kegg) enrichment analyses were conducted. GO analysis utilized gene annotations from the R package “org. Hs. eg. db” as a background, whereas KEGG analysis employed the latest pathway annotations retrieved via the KEGG REST API (https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/rest/keggapi.html). Enrichment analyses were performed using “clusterProfiler” with gene set sizes restricted to 5‐5000. Terms exhibiting p < 0.05 were retained, and the top 10 significantly enriched terms were visualized in descending order of gene count.


### Construction of Protein‐Protein Interaction (PPI) Networks and Identification of Key Targets
  Common targets were submitted to the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) with the species set to Homo sapiens and a minimum confidence score of 0.4. The resulting interaction data were imported into Cytoscape 3.9.0 [17] to construct a PPI network. Node degree and betweenness centrality were calculated using the CytoHubba plugin, and the top 20 ranked nodes were defined as hub targets. These hub targets were subsequently analyzed in the GSCA database (https://guolab.wchscu.cn/GSCA/#/) for prognostic significance and expression levels in kidney clear cell carcinoma (KIRC). Targets significantly associated with patient survival and exhibiting elevated expression in tumor tissues were designated as key targets.


### Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA)‐Related Analyses
  To evaluate the clinical relevance and functional implications of the key targets, GSVA was performed using the GSCA database. In the Expression module, associations between GSVA scores and gene expression, survival outcomes, and clinical stage were assessed. The Immunity module was utilized to examine correlations between GSVA scores and immune cell infiltration levels.


### Prediction of Intergene Interactions and Functional Annotation of Key Targets
  The NetworkAnalyst platform (https://www.networkanalyst.ca/NetworkAnalyst/) was employed to further characterize the biological functions and interacting partners of the key targets. Key targets were uploaded with the organism parameter set to Homo sapiens, and transcription factor‐gene interactions were inferred using the database of experimentally validated binding profiles. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of the predicted interacting genes were performed as described in Section 2.3.


### Molecular Docking
  Molecular docking between TCE and the key target proteins was performed using CB‐Dock2 (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2/php/index.php), which integrates cavity detection, docking, and homology template fitting. The three‐dimensional structure of TCE was obtained from PubChem, and crystal structures of the target proteins were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB; https://www.rcsb.org/). Key targets were submitted to CB‐Dock2 for blind docking with TCE, and binding energies were calculated to identify the optimal docking pose characterized by the lowest energy.


### Validation of Protein Expression of Key Targets
  Tissue‐specific expression patterns of the key targets were validated using the Human Protein Atlas (HPA; https://www.proteinatlas.org/) and immunohistochemistry (IHC).


### Cell Culture and Treatment
  Human kidney proximal tubular epithelial cells (HK‐2; ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) were maintained in DMEM/F‐12 medium (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Lonsera, Suzhou, China) and 1% penicillin‐streptomycin (Gibco) at 37°C under a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Upon reaching 70%–80% confluency, cells were exposed to TCE (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) at concentrations ranging from 0 to 10,000 μM for 24 h. Based on cell viability results, non‐cytotoxic concentrations were chosen for subsequent molecular analyses.


### Cell Viability Assay
  Cytotoxicity was assessed using a CCK‐8 kit (MCE, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). HK‐2 cells were seeded in 96‐well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well, treated with TCE for 24 h, and subsequently incubated with 10 μL CCK‐8 solution for 2 h at 37°C. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a Bio‐Rad microplate reader (Hercules, CA, USA). Cell viability was expressed relative to that of untreated controls (0 μM TCE; n = 3 biological replicates).


### Western Blotting
  Proteins were extracted from TCE‐treated HK‐2 cells using RIPA buffer (Beyotime) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche). Following quantification via BCA assay, equal amounts of protein were separated by 10% SDS‐PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% non‐fat milk and subsequently probed with primary antibodies against TP53, ACTB, and CASP3 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology and Proteintech). After incubation with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies, signals were detected using ECL substrate. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ and normalized to GAPDH.


### Reverse Transcription Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction
  Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and reverse‐transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Kit (Takara). RT‐qPCR was performed using ChamQ SYBR Master Mix (Yeasen Biotechnology) on a LightCycler 480 II system (Roche). Relative expression levels of the target genes were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method with β‐actin as the internal reference.


## Results

### Identification of TCE‐Induced Kidney Cancer Targets
  TCE‐associated targets were retrieved from the ChEMBL, STITCH, and CTD databases, yielding 6, 10, and 889 entries, respectively (Supplementary Table 1). Following integration and deduplication, a total of 896 unique TCE‐related targets were obtained. Concurrently, kidney cancer‐associated targets were extracted from the OMIM and GeneCards databases, identifying 526 and 11,937 entries (Supplementary Table 2), respectively, which were consolidated into 12,290 unique targets. Venn diagram analysis identified an intersection of 599 common targets, which were postulated to represent potential candidates implicated in TCE‐induced kidney carcinogenesis (Figure 2A; Supplementary Table 3).


### Biological Function Annotation and Pathway Enrichment Analysis
  To elucidate the biological significance of the intersecting targets, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed. At a significance threshold of p < 0.05, targets were significantly enriched in 2682 GO terms and 174 KEGG pathways. Prominent GO terms pertained to cellular proliferation and apoptosis, including “apoptotic process” and “cell population proliferation,” as well as responses to chemical stimuli, such as “response to oxygen‐containing compound” (Figure 2B). KEGG pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment in oncogenic signaling cascades, notably “Pathways in cancer” and the “PI3K‐Akt signaling pathway” (Figure 2C).


### Construction of the PPI Network and Screening of Key Targets
  A PPI network was constructed utilizing the STRING database to elucidate functional interactions among the common targets (Figure 3). Topological analysis via Cytoscape identified the top 20 hub targets based on degree and betweenness centrality (Figure 4A,B). Subsequent survival and expression analyses revealed that only TP53, ACTB, and CASP3 exhibited both upregulation in kidney cancer tissues and significant associations with patient prognosis (Figure 4D,E). Consequently, these three genes were designated as key targets for subsequent investigation (Figure 4F–K; Table 1).


### Clinical Significance of Key Targets Analyzed by GSVA
  GSVA was employed to investigate the clinical relevance of the key targets. GSVA scores were found to be significantly elevated in kidney cancer tissues compared to normal controls (Figure 5A). Furthermore, increased expression scores correlated with unfavorable prognostic outcomes and advanced clinical stages (III/IV) (Figure 5B–D), suggesting a potential role for these targets in tumor progression.


### Correlation Between Expression of Key Targets and Immune Cell Infiltration
  A significant positive correlation was observed between the GSVA scores of the key targets and the infiltration levels of multiple immune cell populations, including Tr1 cells, macrophages, iTregs, exhausted T cells, and CD8 + T cells (FDR < 0.05) (Figure 5E). These findings suggest that immune cell infiltration may modulate the expression of these targets, thereby contributing to the tumor microenvironment in TCE‐induced kidney cancer.


### Prediction of Intergenic Interactions and Functional Exploration
  To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms, potential interacting partners of the key targets were predicted. Nineteen genes exhibiting close interactions were identified (Figure 6A). Functional enrichment analysis of these interacting partners revealed associations with the regulation of gene expression and immune system development (Figure 6B). KEGG pathway analysis indicated significant enrichment in pathways such as “apoptosis,” “breast cancer,” and “viral carcinogenesis” (Figure 6C).


### Molecular Docking Validation
  All three key target proteins exhibited potential binding interactions with TCE. The binding energies ranged from −2.5 to −4.0 kcal/mol, indicating weak to moderate binding affinities. Specific binding affinities were as follows: TP53 (PDB: 3D06; −2.5 kcal/mol) (Figure 6D), ACTB (PDB: 7W28; −4.0 kcal/mol) (Figure 6E), and CASP3 (PDB: 2DKO; −2.7 kcal/mol) (Figure 6F).


### Validation of Protein Expression of Key Targets
  Validation using the HPA database and IHC confirmed that the protein expression levels of TP53, ACTB, and CASP3 were significantly upregulated in kidney cancer tissues compared to normal tissues (Figure 7A–C).


### Effects of Low‐Concentration TCE on the Expression of Key Targets
  To validate the regulatory effects of TCE, in vitro experiments were conducted using HK‐2 cells. CCK‐8 assays indicated that TCE concentrations below 100 μM were non‐cytotoxic, whereas concentrations ≥ 1000 μM significantly reduced cell viability in a dose‐dependent manner (Figure 8A). Western blotting revealed that TCE upregulated the protein expression of TP53 and ACTB in a dose‐dependent manner (Figure 8B). Specifically, 100 μM TCE significantly increased protein levels compared to the control. While Pro‐CASP3 levels remained stable, cleaved CASP3 expression was diminished at higher TCE concentrations. RT‐qPCR results corroborated these findings, demonstrating significant upregulation of TP53 and ACTB mRNA expression at 100 μM TCE, whereas CASP3 mRNA levels remained unchanged (Figure 8C).


## Discussion
  TCE, an environmental pollutant and industrial chemical, has emerged as a significant global public health concern due to its widespread utilization and improper disposal, thereby posing serious threats to human health [18]. Occupational exposure to TCE has been established as the most common route of exposure, with its toxicity affecting multiple organs and being particularly associated with various types of cancer [19]. However, the underlying carcinogenic mechanisms remain incompletely elucidated. This contaminant not only contributes to the overall cancer burden but also exerts long‐term impacts on ecosystems and human health due to its resistance to degradation and tendency to bioaccumulate [20]. Moreover, recent epidemiological studies indicate that the burden of kidney cancer associated with occupational TCE exposure increased from 1990 to 2021, with projections suggesting a significant upward trend by 2050, particularly among males and individuals aged 50–79 years [21]. Despite these findings, research on TCE‐induced kidney cancer remains limited, and the potential molecular mechanisms underlying its carcinogenic effects have not been thoroughly explored from a network toxicology perspective. Therefore, there is an urgent need for in‐depth mechanistic investigations to support the development of effective preventive strategies.

  By employing network toxicology, TP53, ACTB, and CASP3 were identified as key targets shared between TCE exposure and kidney cancer. Functional enrichment linked these targets to cell proliferation, apoptosis, and the PI3K‐Akt pathway. GSVA confirmed their significant upregulation in kidney tumors, which was associated with advanced stage, poor prognosis, and increased infiltration of Tr1 cells, macrophages, iTregs, and exhausted T cells. Molecular docking revealed weak to moderate binding affinities between TCE and these proteins, suggesting direct interactions. Furthermore, in vitro experiments demonstrated that TCE dose‐dependently upregulated TP53 and ACTB while suppressing cleaved CASP3, indicating impaired apoptosis.

  Network toxicology identified TP53, ACTB, and CASP3 as key regulatory targets in TCE‐induced kidney cancer due to their established oncogenic roles. TP53, a pivotal tumor suppressor and transcription factor, is characterized by mutations that disrupt its DNA‐binding domain to generate oncogenic mutp53 proteins that drive tumor progression and chemoresistance, particularly in osteosarcoma [22, 23], while serving as early molecular drivers in gastric cardia adenocarcinoma (GCA) [24]. ACTB, a conserved cytoskeletal protein, exhibits dysregulated expression that enhances tumor invasiveness and metastasis [25], with heterogeneous patterns in bladder cancer suggesting potential diagnostic and prognostic utility [26]. Although traditionally recognized as a housekeeping gene, emerging evidence suggests ACTB is dysregulated in various cancers and contributes to cytoskeletal remodeling and tumor invasion. However, we acknowledge that without loss‐of‐function experiments (e.g., knockdown assays), designating it as a definitive oncogenic driver is an overinterpretation. Therefore, ACTB should be considered a potential critical contributor, and future functional validation is necessary. CASP3, a crucial protease in the execution phase of apoptosis, is recognized as a determining factor in tumor cell death and a key therapeutic target [27, 28].

  GSVA enhances pathway‐level interpretation and improves the robustness of research conclusions [29]. In this study, a network toxicology‐based strategy revealed that key targets were upregulated in kidney cancer and correlated with advanced stages. Previous studies have shown that CASP3 inhibits metastasis and increases chemosensitivity in colon cancer [30], mediates pyroptosis via the ROS/JNK pathway in breast cancer [31], and is regulated by miR‐524‐5p in gastric cancer [32]. Similarly, ACTB promotes colorectal cancer progression via cytoskeletal remodeling [33, 34], while its suppression by miR‐450b‐5p in ovarian cancer influences drug resistance [35]. Furthermore, TP53 mutations accelerate breast cancer cell growth [36], whereas its tumor‐suppressive function is epigenetically inhibited in glioblastoma [37]. Although consistent with our data, the specific mechanisms of these targets in TCE‐induced kidney cancer warrant further investigation.

  Immunological analysis revealed positive correlations between the GSVA scores of TP53, ACTB, and CASP3 and the infiltration of multiple immune cell types, particularly macrophages and CD8+T cells, highlighting the immune contexture of TCE‐mediated kidney carcinogenesis. Functionally inactivated TP53 facilitates immune evasion by suppressing the cGAS‐STING pathway and driving stem cells to secrete IL‐34, which polarizes macrophages toward an immunosuppressive M2‐like phenotype [38, 39]. In kidney cancer, single‐cell sequencing links metastatic disease to exhausted CD8+T cells and increased M2 macrophages [40]. Furthermore, DCVC, the primary TCE metabolite, suppresses pro‐inflammatory macrophage functions. CASP3 acts as a switch between apoptosis and pyroptosis [28], while ACTB influences immune infiltration via cytoskeletal remodeling [25]. Collectively, TCE and its metabolites are likely to promote carcinogenesis by inducing TP53 accumulation/dysregulation, driving M2 polarization, and modulating CASP3 and ACTB to establish an immunosuppressive microenvironment.

  GO and KEGG enrichment analyses identified the PI3K‐Akt signaling pathway as a critical nexus in TCE‐induced kidney cancer, involving processes like proliferation and apoptosis. Integrating network toxicology, TP53, ACTB, and CASP3 were identified as key targets co‐regulating this pathway. Specifically, TP53 negatively regulates PI3K‐Akt via PTEN and signalosome formation [41], while Akt phosphorylates MDM2 to degrade p53, forming a feedback loop [42]. TCE exposure may dysregulate TP53 or induce its accumulation, releasing its inhibition on the pathway and promoting proliferation, which is consistent with epidemiological evidence [43]. Additionally, ACTB overexpression enhances tumor invasion via PI3K‐Akt‐mediated cytoskeletal remodeling [25, 44], a finding supported by molecular docking [45]. Furthermore, the PI3K‐Akt pathway inhibits CASP3 by phosphorylating BAD to block apoptosis [46, 47], although paradoxically, caspase‐3 can also promote tumor repopulation [48, 49]. Thus, TCE exposure induces key target dysfunctions, including TP53 dysregulation, ACTB upregulation, and CASP3 suppression, triggering aberrant PI3K‐Akt activation and malignant progression. Furthermore, recent studies highlight the intricate regulatory networks downstream of AKT that balance cell survival and apoptosis. For instance, Song et al. demonstrated that the p‐AKT/BCL‐6/ABCA1 axis critically modulates chemosensitivity and apoptosis, suggesting that TCE‐induced dysregulation of the PI3K‐Akt pathway may similarly engage downstream effectors like BCL‐6 to inhibit CASP3‐mediated apoptosis, thereby promoting carcinogenesis [50]. These findings provide a theoretical foundation for TCE‐induced carcinogenesis and identify the PI3K‐Akt pathway as a potential therapeutic target.

  Molecular docking technology was employed to investigate the potential interactions between TCE and three key targets (TP53, ACTB, and CASP3) identified through network toxicology analysis. This approach provided valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying TCE‐induced kidney cancer. The molecular docking results demonstrated that TCE exhibited weak to moderate binding affinities with all three key target proteins. According to previous studies [13, 51], these findings suggest possible interaction mechanisms between TCE and the target proteins at the molecular level. Upon binding to the active site of TP53, TCE may disrupt its normal conformation and interfere with its interactions with other regulatory molecules. This disturbance could impair TP53‐mediated functions such as cell cycle regulation, DNA repair, and apoptosis induction, potentially contributing to TCE‐induced carcinogenesis. It is important to note that the observed dose‐dependent upregulation of TP53 protein in our in vitro experiments does not necessarily imply functional activation; rather, it frequently reflects the accumulation of mutant or dysfunctional TP53. Wild‐type TP53 possesses a very short half‐life and typically exists at low concentrations within cells. Conversely, conformational shifts or mutations potentially induced by TCE binding stabilize the protein, driving its detectable accumulation alongside abrogation of tumor‐suppressive activity. These changes correspond to inhibited apoptosis, indicated by reduced cleaved CASP3. When TCE binds to a specific domain of ACTB, it may induce conformational changes that affect its interactions with other cytoskeletal proteins. These alterations could compromise cytoskeletal integrity and dynamics, thereby promoting the malignant progression of kidney cancer cells. The binding of TCE to the active site of CASP3 may inhibit its enzymatic activity or interfere with its ability to bind substrates, ultimately suppressing apoptosis. This anti‐apoptotic effect may increase the likelihood of cellular transformation and tumor development. It is important to note that molecular docking is a computational predictive method. Although it has provided important theoretical insights into the molecular mechanisms of TCE‐induced kidney cancer, further experimental validation is required to confirm these findings and provide robust scientific evidence for the prevention of TCE exposure and the clinical management of kidney cancer.

  Through systematic analysis, this study established a comprehensive mechanistic framework for TCE‐induced kidney carcinogenesis, integrating computational predictions with experimental validation. TP53, ACTB, and CASP3 were identified as key targets, whose high expression in kidney cancer tissues correlated with advanced tumor stage, poor prognosis, and immune microenvironment remodeling. Molecular docking confirmed weak to moderate binding affinities between TCE and these targets, a finding further substantiated by in vitro experiments. Critically, TCE exposure dose‐dependently upregulated TP53 and ACTB expression while suppressing CASP3 activation by reducing its cleaved form. This experimental evidence validates the proposed mechanism that TCE directly interacts with these key targets, leading to dysregulation of the PI3K‐Akt signaling pathway, disrupting the balance between cell proliferation and apoptosis, and ultimately driving malignant progression. This multi‐dimensional validation from bioinformatics and molecular docking to functional assays significantly enhances the plausibility of these findings.

  This study has several limitations. First, database diversity may have led to the omission of key targets or introduced bias. Second, reliance solely on CB‐Dock2, without cross‐validation using other platforms or AlphaFold‐predicted structures, limits the robustness of interaction predictions; future studies will address this through multi‐platform validation. Third, considering the technical complexity of constructing a chronic TCE‐induced kidney cancer animal model, in vivo data are currently lacking, which leaves TCE‐target interactions in complex biological contexts unclear and represents a primary direction for future research. Finally, while in vitro experiments demonstrated protein expression changes, the lack of functional assays limits our ability to fully confirm the definitive oncogenic roles of these targets. Specifically, the observed TP53 upregulation may reflect the accumulation of mutant or dysfunctional protein rather than functional activation, and without loss‐of‐function experiments, designating ACTB as a definitive oncogenic driver remains an overinterpretation. Therefore, these conclusions remain preliminary, necessitating future functional validation.


## Conclusion
  The molecular pathogenesis of TCE‐induced kidney carcinogenesis is elucidated herein, with TP53, ACTB, and CASP3 identified as pivotal drivers. Validation of direct interactions between TCE and these targets reveals a mechanism by which environmental pollutants disrupt cellular homeostasis to drive malignant progression. These findings bridge a critical knowledge gap between exposure and pathology, offering a scientific basis for biomonitoring and intervention, thereby advancing environmental toxicology from correlation to causal elucidation.
