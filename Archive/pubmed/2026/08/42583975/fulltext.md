# Andrographolide inhibits hepatocellular carcinoma progression and programmed death ligand-1 expression by blocking STAT3 phosphorylation


## BACKGROUND:
  Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is an aggressive malignancy with frequent recurrence and strong immune evasion. Programmed death ligand-1 (PD-L1) facilitates immune evasion by suppressing T-cell activity. Andrographolide (AD), a natural diterpenoid with anti-inflammatory, antiviral, and immunomodulatory properties, has demonstrated antitumor potential.


## MATERIALS AND METHODS:
  Subcutaneous HCC mouse models assessed the antitumor and immune-activating effects of AD. CCK-8 assay, colony-formation assay, apoptosis analyses, and western blot investigated AD’s impacts on Huh-7 cell proliferation, apoptosis, and cell cycle. Immunofluorescence staining and flow cytometry collectively measured PD-L1 expression after AD treatment. Molecular docking and cellular thermal shift assay detected the targeted binding between AD and STAT3. Finally, recombinant interleukin-6 (rIL-6), a STAT3 activator, was used to verify whether AD-mediated effects on Huh-7 cell viability and PD-L1 expression were STAT3-dependent.


## RESULTS:
  In vivo, AD significantly inhibited mouse tumor growth, decreased PD-L1 expression in tumor tissues, and enhanced T-cell infiltration. In vitro, through cell experiments, AD bound to STAT3 and inhibited tumor cell proliferation, induced apoptosis, and downregulated PD-L1 protein levels by suppressing the phosphorylation of STAT3. Treatment with rIL-6 significantly reversed AD’s antitumor effects.


## CONCLUSION:
  AD inhibits HCC progression and PD-L1 expression by blocking STAT3 phosphorylation, providing a theoretical foundation for developing AD-based therapeutic strategies against HCC.


## Introduction
  Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) accounts for the vast majority of primary liver cancers and stands as a major contributor to global cancer burden and death.[1] Current epidemiology places HCC as the third most lethal malignancy worldwide, with a steadily climbing incidence that is particularly pronounced across the Asia-Pacific region.[2] Despite progress achieved through surgical resection, liver transplantation, targeted molecular agents, and immune checkpoint inhibitors, long-term therapeutic success is still hampered by frequent relapse, extensive tumor diversity, and potent immune evasion.[345] Programmed death ligand-1 (PD-L1) is a pivotal immune checkpoint molecule central to immune evasion. PD-L1 critically shapes the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) and mediates immune evasion; its high expression is consistently associated with poor prognosis in HCC.[67] Blocking PD-L1 can markedly enhance antitumor immunity and improve the effectiveness of immunotherapy.[8] However, for the majority of HCC patients, the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway is not the sole rate-limiting factor in antitumor immunity. Blocking the PD-1/PD-L1 axis alone is insufficient to elicit an effective antitumor immune response. Combination therapies may represent a superior alternative.[9] Gasdermin D (GSDMD) promotes PD-L1 expression. The combination of anti-PD-1 and GSDMD inhibitors provides an effective treatment option for HCC patients with upregulated GSDMD.[10] Furthermore, PRMT3 enhances PD-L1 expression in tumor cells, and anti-PD-L1 can inhibit the oncogenic effects of PRMT3 in HCC.[11] Targeting upstream regulators of PD-L1, thus, may be a novel strategy to overcome immunotherapy resistance in HCC.

  STAT3 is a master transcription factor that governs proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and immune responses. Its aberrant activation contributes to the development and progression of various cancers.[8] In HCC, sustained STAT3 phosphorylation fuels self-renewal of tumor stem cells, stimulates angiogenesis, and shapes an immune evasion-favored TIME.[1213] Phosphorylated STAT3 (p-STAT3) directly activates PD-L1 transcription and indirectly suppresses T-cell-mediated antitumor immunity.[1415] Due to the central role of STAT3 in regulating PD-L1 expression and the immunosuppressive environment that underpins HCC progression, targeting STAT3 emerges as a promising therapeutic avenue.

  Andrographolide (AD), a natural diterpenoid isolated from Andrographis paniculata (Burm. f.) Wall. ex Nees has long been employed in traditional Chinese and Southeast Asian medicine to treat infectious diseases for its potent anti-inflammatory, antiviral, and immunomodulatory properties.[161718] AD has shown an antitumor potential in several solid tumors.[192021] For example, in colorectal cancer and nonsmall-cell lung cancer, AD inhibits proliferation, induces apoptosis, and restrains metastasis by blocking the NF-κB, PI3K/AKT, and MAPK signaling pathways.[222324] The therapeutic promise of AD has also been preliminarily pointed out in HCC.[2526] However, the precise mechanisms, especially regarding whether AD modulates PD-L1 expression through the STAT3 pathway to counteract HCC immune evasion, remain to be systematically elucidated.

  The present study investigated the antitumor mechanism of AD in HCC. We found that AD directly binds to STAT3 and suppresses tumor progression and downregulates PD-L1 expression by inhibiting its phosphorylation. Our findings uncovered a potential mechanism by which AD restrained HCC progression by targeting the STAT3/PD-L1 axis, providing new insights and experimental evidence for developing safe and effective natural immunomodulatory agents.


## Materials and Methods

### Cell culture
  Mouse hepatoma cells H22 (CL-0341) and human HCC cells Huh-7 (CL-0120) were purchased from Procell (China). H22 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S). Huh-7 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. All cells were incubated at 37℃ with 5% CO2 under saturated humidity. Cells were observed daily under a microscope and passaged at a 1:3 ratio when reaching 80%–90% confluence.


### Animal experiments
  Six- to eight-week-old female BALB/c mice were purchased from Hangzhou Ziyuan Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (China). Mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free animal facility (22 ± 1℃, 50 ± 5% relative humidity, 12-h light/dark cycle) with free access to irradiated, sterilized food and acidified water. After injecting H22 cells (1 × 106 per mouse) subcutaneously into the right flank of the mice, the condition of the mice was observed daily. When tumors reached approximately 100 mm3, mice were randomized into two groups (n = 5): phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) control and AD-treated. AD (10 mg/kg) was administered intravenously at scheduled intervals, while mice in the PBS group were given equal volumes of sterile PBS. Tumor size was measured every 5 days and volume calculated as V = length × width2/2. After 30 days, mice were euthanized. Tumors were dissected, weighed, photographed, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for histological analyses.


### Histological analyses
  Tumor tissues fixed in 4% PFA at 4℃ for 24 h were paraffin-embedded and cut into 5-μm sections. Prior to sectioning, the paraffin blocks were prechilled at − 20℃ for 20 min to minimize wrinkling. Sections were dewaxed in xylene (twice, 10 min each) and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (100%, 95%, 85%, and 75%), with 3 min at each concentration. After two rinses with distilled water, 3% hydrogen peroxide was applied and incubated at room temperature for 10 min to block endogenous peroxidase activity. Following another rinse with distilled water, antigen retrieval was performed using a citrate-based solution in a 95℃ water bath for 20 min, followed by natural cooling to room temperature. Following three PBS washes, the slides were blocked with bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min.

  For Ki67 staining, the slides were incubated overnight with anti-Ki67 (Abcam, ab15580, UK, 1:400) at 4℃ and were then washed in PBS and incubated with Horseradish Peroxidase (HRP)-labeled goat antirabbit immunoglobulin G (IgG) (Sigma-Aldrich, SAB3700885, Germany, 1:500) at 37℃ for 30 min and developed with 3,3’-Diaminobenzidine (DAB) until brown staining appeared. After color development, the nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. The sections were then mounted with neutral balsam and observed under a microscope (Nikon, Japan).

  For immunofluorescence (IF), the BSA-blocked sections were incubated overnight at 4℃ with anti-CD4 (Abcam, ab288724, UK, 1:200), anti-CD8 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 14-0808-82, USA, 1:200), or anti-PD-L1 (Abcam, ab213480, UK, 1:200). Then, secondary antibodies, including Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit IgG (Abcam, ab150077, UK, 1:500) or Alexa Fluor 555 goat anti-rat IgG (Abcam, ab150158, UK, 1:500), were applied for 1 h in the dark. Nuclei were stained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Thermo Fisher, D1306, USA) for 5 min. Slides were mounted with an antifade reagent and imaged.


### CCK-8 assay
  Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 5 × 103/well, incubated for 24 h at 37℃, and treated with AD or AD + recombinant interleukin-6 (rIL-6) for another 24 h. Each cell then received 10% CCK-8 reagent (Beyotime, China) for 4 h of culture. Absorbance at 450 nm was read on a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher, USA).


### Colony-formation assay
  Cells (500/well) in 12-well plates were cultured at 37℃ overnight and then treated with 80 μM AD for 24 h. Treated cells were cultured in fresh complete medium for 2 weeks, with the medium changed every 3 days, until the cell community (≥50 cells/community) became visible to the naked eye. The supernatants were extracted for use. After PBS washes, cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 20 min and stained with 0.5% crystal violet (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) for 10 min. The staining solution was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane to remove particulates. Finally, the staining reagents were gently washed off with PBS. The cell colonies (≥50 cells) were observed under a microscope and counted automatically using ImageJ software (version 1.53k, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).


### Apoptosis assay
  Cells were cultured in 6-well plates and grew to 70% confluency. Then, 80 μM AD was added for 24 h of culture. Apoptosis was assessed using the Annexin V-FITC kit (Beyotime, China). All buffers were cooled to 4℃ before use. After trypsinization (ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid-free), centrifugation (1000 × g, 5 min), and twice PBS washes, cells were resuspended in 100 μL of 1× binding buffer and stained with 5 μL Annexin V-FITC and 10 μL Propidium Iodide (PI) for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. The sample was mixed with 400 μL of 1 × binding buffer, kept on ice, and analyzed on an Agilent flow cytometer (USA) within 1 h.


### Western blot
  Cells lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (Beyotime, China) on ice for 30 min were centrifuged to extract total protein, which was later quantified with the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) kit (Thermo Fisher, USA). Thirty-microgram protein per lane was separated on sodium dodecyl sulfate - polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes, blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature, and probed overnight at 4℃ with antibodies against CDK6 (ab124821), CDK4 (ab108357), CDK2 (ab32147), Bcl-2 (ab182858), Bax (ab32503), Caspase-3 (ab32351), cleaved caspase-3 (ab32042), STAT3 (ab68153), phosphorylated STAT3 (p-STAT3) (ab267373), and β-actin (ab8226). Secondary antibody HRP-labeled goat antirabbit IgG (ab205718) was then applied and incubated for 2 h at room temperature. After incubation, three tris-buffered saline with tween-20 (TBST) washes, each for 10 min, were performed. Bands were visualized with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate (Beyotime, China). Primary antibodies and the secondary antibody were purchased from Abcam (UK).


### Immunofluorescence staining of cells
  Cells were treated with 80 μM AD for 48 h, fixed with 4% PFA for 10 min, permeabilized with 0.2% triton X-100/PBS at room temperature for 15 min, blocked with 3% BSA/PBS for 1 h, and incubated with anti-PD-L1 (ab205921, Abcam, UK) overnight at 4℃. Alexa Fluor 555 goat antirabbit IgG (ab150078, Abcam, UK) was applied for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counter-stained with DAPI and imaged on an Olympus confocal microscope (Japan).


### Flow cytometry
  After AD treatment and centrifugation, cells were then fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min, blocked with TruStain FcX on ice for 30 min to avoid nonspecific binding, and then incubated with anti-PD-L1 (ab209962, Abcam) for 30 min on ice. After two PBS washes, cells were resuspended and analyzed on a flow cytometer.


### Molecular docking
  The 3D structure of AD (CID: 5318517) was downloaded from PubChem and converted to PDBQT format using Open Babel. The STAT3 protein sequence (ID: P40763) was obtained from UniProt. The crystal structure (6NJS) that has been resolved in the protein data bank (PDB) was preferentially selected. Preprocessing was conducted using PyMOL, including removal of water molecules, cofactors, and unrelated ligands. The optimal binding pocket was predicted using Protein.plus (https://proteins.plus/) to define the grid box. Molecular docking was performed on AutoDock (v1.5.7, The Scripps Research Institute, La Jolla, CA, USA). Protein interactions were visualized with LigPlot.


### Cellular thermal shift assay
  Huh-7 cells treated with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) or 80 μM AD for 24 h were washed with PBS containing protease inhibitors and moved to polymerase chain reaction (PCR) tubes. Samples were heated in a thermocycler (ThermoFisher, USA) for 3 min per temperature, lysed in NP-40 buffer, subjected to three freeze–thaw cycles, and centrifuged (20,000 × g, 4℃, 20 min). The supernatants collected were boiled for 5 min in buffer and analyzed by western blot (WB).


### RNA extraction and real-time-quantitative polymerase chain reaction
  Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol (Invitrogen, USA) and reverse-transcribed using a PrimeScript RT kit (Takara, Japan). Gene expression was quantified with a SYBR Green PCR kit (Takara, Japan) on a real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, USA). β-actin served as the internal control. Relative mRNA levels were calculated by the 2-ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences (5’→3’) are listed in Table 1.


### Cell cycle analysis
  Treated cells were fixed overnight in 1 mL 70% precooled ethanol, washed with precooled Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS), resuspended in 0.2 mL Tali® cell cycle solution (Thermo Fisher, USA) containing PI, RNase A, and Triton X-100, incubated for 30 min at room temperature in the dark, and analyzed by flow cytometry to determine the distribution of cell cycles.


### Statistical analysis
  Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviations (n ≥ 3). Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, California, USA) and Adobe Illustrator CS6 (Adobe Inc., San Jose, California, USA). Between-group differences were assessed by Student’s t-tests. Statistical significance was defined by P < 0.05 (*/#).


## Results

### Andrographolide suppresses hepatocellular carcinoma progression and remodels the tumor immune microenvironment
  To evaluate the anti-HCC efficacy of AD, we implanted subcutaneous HCC tumors in mice and administered AD intravenously. Tumor growth curves showed that AD markedly delayed tumor growth [Figure 1a]. At the endpoint, tumor size and weight in the AD group were significantly lower than in the PBS group [Figure 1b and c]. Ki67 staining revealed a pronounced reduction in Ki67-positive cells within AD-treated tumors, indicating suppressed proliferation [Figure 1d]. IF further demonstrated a significant decrease in PD-L1 expression after AD treatment [Figure 1e]. Given PD-L1’s role in T-cell immunity, we quantified T-cell infiltration in tumor tissues. The number of both CD4+ and CD8+ T-cells was significantly higher in AD-treated tumors [Figure 1f], demonstrating that AD remodeled the TIME and enhanced T-cell infiltration and activity. Collectively, AD exerted its antitumor effects by inhibiting cancer cell proliferation and reshaping the TIME.

  To validate the impact of AD treatment on PD-L1, apoptosis, cycling proteins, and STAT3 phosphorylation within tumor tissues in vivo, we performed WB analysis. AD treatment significantly downregulated PD-L1 protein levels. The levels of the apoptotic proteins Bax and cleaved caspase-3 were markedly increased, while the antiapoptotic protein Bcl-2 was significantly decreased. The expression of cell cycle-related proteins – CDK2, CDK4, and CDK6 – was also substantially reduced, indicating that AD treatment not only promoted cancer cell apoptosis but also participated in cell cycle regulation. In addition, protein level analysis revealed a significant decrease in p-STAT3 following AD treatment, suggesting that AD inhibited STAT3 phosphorylation [Figure 1g].


### Andrographolide promotes tumor cell apoptosis and affects the cell cycle in hepatocellular carcinoma
  After confirming AD’s antitumor and immunity-reshaping effects in vivo, we dissected the relevant cellular mechanisms. CCK-8 assays showed that AD reduced Huh-7 cell viability by 50% after 24 h [Figure 2a], and colony formation was markedly impaired [Figure 2b]. Flow cytometry revealed that apoptosis rose from 4.8% (control) to 21.8% after AD exposure [Figure 2c]. Meanwhile, WB showed upregulation of proapoptotic Bax and cleaved caspase-3, and downregulation of antiapoptotic Bcl-2, indicating that AD promoted apoptosis of cancer cells [Figure 2d]. Moreover, the WB results demonstrated a significant reduction of CDK2, CDK4, and CDK6 expression [Figure 2d]. In summary, AD participated in the regulation of cell apoptosis and the cell cycle.


### Andrographolide downregulates programmed death ligand-1 expression in hepatocellular carcinoma
  We next examined AD’s impact on PD-L1 in vitro. WB showed a significant decrease in PD-L1 protein after 24 h of AD treatment in Huh-7 cells [Figure 3a]. To directly present PD-L1 expression on the cancer cell membrane, IF staining was performed in AD-treated cells. Confocal microscopy showed a pronounced decrease in red fluorescence following AD exposure [Figure 3b]. Consistently, flow cytometry confirmed that PD-L1 levels were significantly lower in the AD group than in the DMSO group [Figure 3c]. Together, AD effectively suppressed PD-L1 expression in HCC.


### Andrographolide directly binds STAT3 and suppresses its phosphorylation
  The aforementioned experiments indicated that AD promoted the apoptosis of HCC cells, affected the cell cycle, and reduced the expression of PD-L1. The potential molecular mechanisms by which AD regulates HCC progression were next explored. JAK/STAT signaling influences tumor occurrence and progression by modulating cell metabolic progression, including proliferation, apoptosis, cell cycle, and PD-L1 expression.[27] Reportedly, AD inhibits STAT3 phosphorylation.[25] Therefore, we hypothesized that AD affects the JAK/STAT signaling pathway by inhibiting the activation of STAT3 protein, thereby alleviating HCC progression. STAT3 expression was detected in AD-treated cells. qPCR showed no change in STAT3 mRNA after AD treatment [Figure 4a], whereas p-STAT3 protein was markedly reduced without affecting total STAT3 [Figure 4b]. These results collectively demonstrated that AD indeed had an impact on the activation of STAT3, and this effect occurs at the protein level rather than at the transcriptional level. We subsequently explored the possibility of potential interactions between AD treatment and STAT3 activation through molecular docking. As shown in Figure 4c, molecular docking predicted three groups of hydrogen bonds between AD and STAT3 residues Gln247, Arg325, and Gln326, and hydrophobic interactions with Ala250, Cys251, Ile258, Glu324, Cys328, Pro333, and Pro336. Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) confirmed that AD stabilized STAT3 against thermal denaturation [Figure 4d]. In conclusion, AD directly bound to STAT3, and the interaction inhibited the phosphorylation of STAT3.


### Andrographolide regulates apoptosis, cell cycle, and programmed death ligand-1 in hepatocellular carcinoma through STAT3 inhibition
  To determine whether AD’s proapoptotic and PD-L1-suppressive effects hinge on STAT3 inhibition, we cotreated Huh-7 cells with AD and the STAT3 activator rIL-6 and examined metabolic changes in AD-treated and AD-rIL-6 cotreated groups. First, WB showed that AD treatment significantly upregulated the key apoptotic proteins Bax and cleaved caspase-3 and significantly downregulated antiapoptotic protein Bcl-2 and PD-L1 [Figure 5a]. Treatment with rIL-6 further decreased the expression of apoptotic proteins, increased the expression of antiapoptotic proteins, and restored PD-L1 levels. This indicated that promoting the activation of STAT3 could intervene in the effects of AD on cell apoptosis and PD-L1 expression. Further, AD significantly inhibited Huh-7 cell viability, which was reversed by adding rIL-6 [Figure 5b]. Cell cycle experiments showed a consistent trend that AD effectively inhibited the cell cycle progression and arrested the cell cycle at the G2/M phase, while rIL-6 weakened these effects [Figure 5c]. In conclusion, AD promoted HCC cell apoptosis, arrested cell cycles, and downregulated PD-L1 expression by inhibiting the activation of STAT3.


## Discussion
  PD-L1 is a master immune checkpoint molecule in HCC. By binding to PD-1, PD-L1 suppresses T-cell activity and facilitates tumor immune evasion. High PD-L1 expression is consistently associated with tumor progression, metastasis, and poor prognosis, making PD-L1 blockade a promising therapeutic strategy for HCC. Our study demonstrated that AD suppressed PD-L1 expression in HCC cells by inhibiting STAT3 activation and, concurrently, significantly increased T-cell infiltration in mouse tumor tissues. AD was identified as a naturally derived agent that may complement current immunotherapeutic approaches for HCC.

  AD, a natural diterpenoid lactone from a traditional medicinal herb, has attracted increasing attention for its antitumor function.[28] AD inhibits proliferation, induces apoptosis, and arrests the cell cycle in a variety of solid tumors.[293031] In colon cancer DLD1 cells, for example, AD dose and time dependently induce the generation of reactive oxygen species and inhibit nuclear condensation, thereby suppressing cell proliferation.[32] Beyond direct acting on tumor cells, AD modulates tumor-associated immune responses. Reportedly, AD enhances the efficacy of anti-PD-1 antibodies in colorectal cancer by suppressing cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2)-mediated prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) release.[33] AD can further regulate innate and adaptive immunity through macrophage repolarization and modulation of antigen-specific antibody production.[34] These findings indicate that AD also regulates the TIME apart from its direct antitumor effect. Our data corroborated and extended the observation. We found a substantial decrease in PD-L1 expression in the AD-treated HCC mice model and a simultaneous increase in the number of T-cells. Further in vitro experiments discovered AD’s binding to the STAT3 protein, its suppression of phosphorylation of STAT3, and the subsequent inhibition of PD-L1 expression. We extended the research scope of AD treatment in regulating antitumor immunity by revealing the molecular mechanism that AD downregulated PD-L1 by blocking p-STAT3.

  STAT3 is a transcription factor critically involved in oncogenesis.[3536] Abnormal STAT3 activation promotes the proliferation, survival, invasion, and metastasis of tumor cells and concurrently suppresses antitumor immunity.[37] Targeting STAT3 is therefore considered a promising antitumor strategy.[38] Current small-molecule STAT3 inhibitors, such as WP1066, stattic, and STA-2,[3940] have shown efficacy in in vivo and in vitro preclinical models. However, clinical translation is hindered by their suboptimal affinity, poor stability, low bioavailability, and potential toxicity.[4142] Natural compounds from herbs offer an alternative avenue and are receiving increasing attention. Compared to small-molecular inhibitors, herb compounds have many advantages. First, owing to sourcing from nature and their inherent chemical complexity, they usually exhibit lower toxicity because of balanced interactions with multiple targets, which is in line with the human physiological state.[4344] Second, the long history of herb use in traditional medical applications has proved their clarified safety.[45] Moreover, the additional antioxidant or anti-inflammatory benefits of constituents in traditional Chinese medicine could potentially enhance the overall clinical efficacy in cancer treatment.[46]

  In conclusion, applying the active constituents in traditional Chinese medicines could effectively avoid the toxicity of small-molecular inhibitors, holding potential for treating tumors. In this context, we systematically assessed the antitumor effects of AD. Our molecular docking and CETSA data confirmed the high-affinity binding of AD to STAT3 and inhibitory effects on STAT3 phosphorylation. Therefore, AD emerged as a safe antitumor alternative to small-molecular STAT3 inhibitors in cancer treatment.

  In summary, the present study elucidates the underlying mechanism whereby AD suppresses HCC progression and PD-L1 expression by targeting and suppressing the phosphorylation of STAT3. Our findings provide new theoretical support for the clinical application of AD in HCC immunotherapy. Nevertheless, the current data are constrained to cellular and animal models without validation in clinical samples. Moreover, the long-term efficacy and safety profile of AD in HCC patients remains to be established. We will develop and refine these research directions in the future.


## Conclusions
  This study reveals the potential mechanism by which andrographolide can target and bind to the STAT3 protein, inhibit PD-L1 expression and HCC progression by blocking STAT3 phosphorylation, providing a new theoretical basis for the development of AD-based HCC treatment strategies.
