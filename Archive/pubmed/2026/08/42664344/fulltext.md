# Programmable protein degraders enable selective knockdown of pathogenic β-catenin subpopulations in vitro and in vivo


## Abstract
  Aberrant activation of Wnt signaling results in unregulated accumulation of cytosolic β-catenin, which subsequently enters the nucleus and promotes transcription of genes that contribute to cellular proliferation and malignancy. Here, we sought to eliminate pathogenic β-catenin from the cytosol using designer ubiquibodies (uAbs), chimeric proteins composed of an E3 ubiquitin ligase and a target-binding domain that redirect intracellular proteins to the proteasome for degradation. To accelerate uAb development, we leveraged a protein language model–driven algorithm called SaLT&PepPr to computationally design “guide” peptides with affinity for β-catenin, which were subsequently fused to the catalytic domain of a human E3 called carboxyl terminus of Hsp70-interacting protein. Expression of the resulting peptide-guided uAbs in colorectal cancer cells led to the identification of several designs that greatly reduced the abnormally stable pool of free β-catenin in the cytosol and nucleus while preserving the normal membrane–associated subpopulation. This selective knockdown of pathogenic β-catenin suppressed Wnt/β-catenin signaling and impaired tumor cell survival and proliferation. Furthermore, one of the best degraders selectively decreased cytosolic but not membrane-associated β-catenin levels in livers of BALB/c mice following delivery as a lipid nanoparticle–encapsulated mRNA. Collectively, these findings reveal the unique ability of uAbs to selectively deplete abnormal proteins in vitro and in vivo and open the door to peptide-programmable biologic modulators of other disease-causing proteins.


## INTRODUCTION
  The Wnt signaling pathway is a crucial regulator of various cellular processes, including cell proliferation, differentiation, and migration, and determination of cell fate during embryonic development and tissue homeostasis (1–3). A key downstream component of this pathway is β-catenin, a dual-function protein that plays an important role in cell-cell adhesion through interaction with E-cadherin and transcriptional regulation of Wnt-responsive genes through interaction with T cell factor (TCF)/lymphoid enhancer factor (LEF) transcription factors (4, 5). Under normal conditions, β-catenin is kept in check by a destruction complex composed of several proteins including adenomatous polyposis coli (APC) and glycogen synthase kinase 3, which targets it for proteasomal degradation (Fig. 1). However, when Wnt signaling is aberrantly activated, such as from loss-of-function mutations in the APC gene or gain-of-function point mutations or deletions in CTNNB1, the gene encoding β-catenin, this degradation is blocked. Abnormally stabilized β-catenin accumulates in the cytosol and then translocates to the nucleus where it interacts with TCF/LEF to drive the expression of oncogenes such as c-Myc and Cyclin D1 (6–8). This unchecked β-catenin activity promotes uncontrolled cell proliferation and survival, contributing to the development of various malignancies including colorectal cancer (CRC) and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) (9, 10).

  Given the clearly delineated role of pathogenic β-catenin in tumorigenesis, pharmacological agents designed to prevent abnormal stabilization of β-catenin or facilitate its degradation represent promising approaches that could form the basis of an effective anticancer strategy. Unfortunately, even after decades of preclinical and clinical research, there are currently no approved therapies that target β-catenin directly. Conventional small molecule or monoclonal antibody approaches have met limited success because of β-catenin’s intracellular location, lack of a well-defined active site, and intrinsically disordered protein regions, which collectively contribute to its classification as an undruggable target (11). Beyond direct inhibition, RNA interference (RNAi) approaches such as small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), which silence protein expression at the transcript level, have been developed against β-catenin (12–14). However, while siRNA can target any protein-coding mRNA, it has certain limitations. Most critically, siRNA is incapable of distinguishing abnormal β-catenin that is free in the cytosol and nucleus from normal membrane–associated β-catenin, both of which are expressed from the same gene. This lack of selectivity is potentially problematic because the loss of β-catenin at the membrane decreases cell-cell adhesion, which in turn can promote tumor invasion and metastasis (15). Given this and other challenges associated with siRNAs, such as inefficient knockdown of proteins with long half-lives (16) and nonspecific knockdown of proteins due to partial complementarity with off-target mRNA (17), it is evident that alternative approaches for targeting β-catenin are needed.

  One such strategy is proteome editing, a powerful approach for targeted protein modulation that enables posttranslational degradation, stabilization, activation, or relocalization of proteins of interest (POIs). By functioning posttranslationally, proteome editing has the potential to dissect complicated protein functions at higher resolution than RNAi or gene-editing technologies like CRISPR that operate at the pretranslational level, while also overcoming limitations of these other methods such as irreversibility, lack of temporal control, and off-target effects (18, 19). Among the many proteome editing modalities, two of the most advanced are proteolysis targeting chimeras (PROTACs) and molecular glues, which both leverage small molecules to recruit endogenous E3 ubiquitin ligases of the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway (UPP) to POIs for proximity-induced degradation (20). However, while a peptide-based PROTAC and molecular glue have been developed for targeted degradation of β-catenin (21, 22), they both require very high doses for activity—in the tens of micromolar range—and are incapable of discriminating the cytosolic/nuclear and membrane subpopulations of β-catenin.

  An alternative proteome editing approach with the potential to selectively target cytosolic/nuclear β-catenin at lower doses is ubiquibodies (uAbs). Also referred to more recently as affinity-directed protein missiles and bioPROTACs, uAbs are chimeric proteins in which an E3 ubiquitin ligase or E3 adaptor is genetically fused with a targeting peptide or protein (warhead) with affinity for a POI. These biologics-based proteome editors redirect otherwise stable POIs to the UPP for proteasomal degradation, as was originally demonstrated with model proteins such as β-galactosidase and green fluorescent protein (23, 24). The modular design of uAbs offers exceptional engineerability, allowing precise customization of both the E3 ligase and the POI-binding warhead (19). In the case of E3 ligases, the design space is vast with more than 100 E3s across humans, bacteria and viruses having been functionally incorporated into the uAb architecture (25–27), which is in stark contrast to PROTACs that predominantly rely on only two E3s, cereblon and von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) (28). In the context of targetable proteins, the design space is similarly expansive, taking advantage of an immense collection of available POI-specific scaffolds, such as alpha repeat proteins, designed ankyrin repeat proteins (DARPins), fibronectin type III monobodies, single-chain Fv antibodies, VHH nanobodies, and peptides (23–25, 29, 30). Moreover, because uAbs function through protein-protein interactions (PPIs) across extensive contact areas, they can degrade POIs and related proteoforms that have been notoriously difficult to target with conventional small molecule–based modalities (25, 26, 31–36). Unfortunately, preexisting “off-the-shelf” binding domains are not available for many POIs, especially those that are conformationally disordered, placing such targets beyond the reach of the uAb approach.

  To address this challenge, we recently developed several structure-independent protein language models (pLMs) for computationally designing “guide” peptides that enable target engagement by uAb degraders (29, 37–39). One of these is SaLT&PepPr (Structure-agnostic Language Transformer and Peptide Prioritization) (29), a model that leverages fine-tuned ESM-2 pLM embeddings (40) to predict the interacting motifs on partner sequences of target POIs and, by integrating with PPI databases, enables isolation of continuous peptide candidates with affinity for an input POI. The resulting SaLT&PepPr-derived peptide warheads were used to construct uAbs that were experimentally confirmed to bind and degrade their target POIs, including many intrinsically disordered proteins such as regulatory proteins and transcription factors (29).

  In this study, we leveraged the SaLT&PepPr algorithm to computationally design uAb warheads for driving selective degradation of oncogenic β-catenin in the cytosol and nucleus while preserving membrane-associated β-catenin that is protective and maintains tissue integrity (Fig. 1). Specifically, a panel of putative β-catenin–specific uAbs was constructed by fusing SaLT&PepPr-designed guide peptides to the catalytic domain of human C terminus of Hsp70-interacting protein (CHIP, a.k.a. STUB1), a highly modular human E3 ubiquitin ligase domain that has been used to develop many successful uAbs previously (23, 29, 30, 37–39). Following expression in the CRC cell line DLD1, that accumulates abnormally high levels of cytosolic β-catenin, we identified several peptide-guided uAb designs that greatly reduced the cytosolic and nuclear pools of β-catenin while sparing the membrane-associated pool. Selective removal of cytosolic and nuclear β-catenin was accompanied by potent inhibition of Wnt/β-catenin signaling activity and impairment of tumor cell survival and proliferation. We also observed selective elimination of cytosolic β-catenin in livers of BALB/c mice that were intravenously injected with lipid nanoparticle (LNP)–encapsulated mRNAs encoding one of the top performing peptide-guided uAbs. Together, our findings establish peptide-guided uAbs as a robust proteome editing technology for precisely discriminating between pathogenic and nonpathogenic proteoforms in vitro and in vivo, with the potential for selectively targeting the primary oncogenic drivers of tumorigenesis.


## RESULTS

### Design of peptide-guided degraders of human β-catenin using SaLT&PepPr
  To develop uAbs for selective eradication of pathogenic β-catenin, we leveraged a warhead–linker–E3 ubiquitin ligase molecular architecture, with computationally predicted guide-peptide warheads specific for β-catenin connected by a flexible linker to the catalytic domain of human CHIP lacking its native substrate-binding domain (CHIPΔTPR) (Fig. 2A). We hypothesized that guide peptides based on β-catenin’s known interaction with E-cadherin (fig. S1A) (41) but with weaker affinity [i.e., >36 nM, which is the measured affinity between the cytosolic domain of E-cadherin (Ecad-CD) and β-catenin (42)] would preferentially bind cytosolic/nuclear β-catenin but would be outcompeted by endogenous E-cadherin for binding to the β-catenin subpopulation at the membrane. To find such β-catenin–specific warheads, we used the SaLT&PepPr algorithm (29) with the amino acid sequence of Ecad-CD as input (table S1). SaLT&PepPr first predicted the interaction sites along the Ecad-CD/β-catenin–binding interface and then isolated continuous peptide sequences that were scored for their probability of binding to β-catenin. A total of 33 candidate peptides of varying lengths (10 to 24 residues) were generated by this approach (table S2). For comparison, we also leveraged the known interaction between β-catenin and the WD40-repeat domain of the β–transducin repeat containing protein (β-TrCP) F-box protein (fig. S1B) (43) to identify 11 additional candidate peptides (10 to 20 residues) with potential for β-catenin binding (table S3).


### Identification of uAbs that knockdown β-catenin levels and activity
  To test the 44 designs, uAbs were constructed by genetically fusing each guide peptide candidate to CHIPΔTPR in plasmid pcDNA3. We chose to evaluate uAb-mediated β-catenin degradation in DLD1 cells, a CRC cell line in which β-catenin signaling is dysregulated because of a loss-of-function mutation in APC (Fig. 1), specifically a C-terminal truncation starting at amino acid 1427. This loss of APC canonical function causes aberrant stabilization of β-catenin, which has been associated with a wide variety of human malignancies including CRC (44). To determine whether any of the designed uAbs could promote the degradation of stabilized β-catenin, DLD1 cells were transiently transfected with uAb-encoding plasmid DNA (pDNA), and cytosolic β-catenin levels were analyzed by immunoblotting. This preliminary screening revealed that all but two of the uAb constructs were capable of lowering steady-state β-catenin levels in the cytosol, with 15 of 44 promoting a decrease of ≥50% (Fig. 2B and figs. S2A and S3A). In parallel, we assessed the effect of each uAb on β-catenin signaling using TOPFlash, a synthetic TCF/LEF reporter plasmid containing tandemly repeated TCF motifs upstream of the luciferase gene that provides a reliable luminescent readout of β-catenin transcriptional activity (32, 45). Consistent with the immunoblot results, all uAbs reduced TOPFlash luciferase activity to some extent, with several constructs decreasing β-catenin signaling activity by as much as 75 to 80% (Fig. 2B and figs. S2B and S3, B and C). While good agreement was observed between β-catenin levels and signaling activity for most constructs, there were a few notable exceptions. For example, a few constructs (e.g., Ecad-2–CHIPΔTPR and Ecad-27–CHIPΔTPR) showed measurable inhibition of β-catenin signaling but failed to measurably reduce β-catenin levels. While the exact reasons remain unclear, we speculate that these constructs were incapable of efficiently transferring ubiquitin to bound β-catenin but that binding alone was sufficient to sequester β-catenin in the cytosol and prevent it from entering the nucleus and activating gene expression.

  Among the productive degraders, Ecad-9–CHIPΔTPR and Ecad-30–CHIPΔTPR were the most effective on the basis of their performance across both immunoblot and TOPFlash screens and thus were downselected for further analysis. Two additional productive degraders, Ecad-19–CHIPΔTPR and Ecad-26–CHIPΔTPR, were also downselected because they showed consistent degradation profiles, and their guide peptides shared a 12-residue core motif (PPYDSLLVFDYE) with the Ecad-9 and Ecad-30 peptides. The selection of these degraders was substantiated by a more thorough TOPFlash analysis in which these four productive degraders were analyzed alongside three nonproductive constructs (Ecad-14–CHIPΔTPR, Ecad-21–CHIPΔTPR, and Ecad-33–CHIPΔTPR) and an siRNA directed against β-catenin (CTNNB1) that was previously shown to strongly decrease total β-catenin protein levels and TOPFlash reporter activity (13). All four productive uAbs significantly inhibited β-catenin signaling in DLD1 cells relative to nontransfected cells or cells transfected with pDNA encoding control constructs (e.g., “guideless” CHIPΔTPR, nonspecific poly-glycine–CHIPΔTPR), with uAb-mediated reductions in luciferase activity on par with that observed following transfection with the CTNNB1 siRNA (Fig. 2C). The downselection of these peptide-guided degraders was further substantiated by the observation that MG132, an inhibitor of the cytosolic proteasome, completely blocked uAb-mediated degradation of β-catenin (fig. S4A; shown for Ecad-30–CHIPΔTPR), thus confirming a UPP-dependent mechanism of action. As expected, the three nonproductive degraders showed no reduction in β-catenin signaling (Fig. 2C).

  To understand the impact of the Ecad-30–CHIPΔTPR degrader on protein levels globally, we performed shotgun proteomics following transfection, as has been reported previously for other uAb/bioPROTAC constructs (29, 46). Specifically, DLD1 cells were transfected with pDNA encoding Ecad-30–CHIPΔTPR or empty plasmid. Cells were harvested after 24 hours, and their cytosolic fractions were subjected to liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). A final dataset of 6994 proteins was identified and used for downstream analysis. Proteins were considered differentially expressed if the Log2 fold change (Log2FC) between Ecad-30–CHIPΔTPR and empty plasmid groups was either ≤−1 (down-regulated) or ≥1 (up-regulated) and the P value was <0.05. In cells transfected with Ecad-30–CHIPΔTPR, we observed the expected increase in uAb-derived tryptic peptides assigned to human CHIP (STUB1) and a significant decrease in β-catenin abundance compared to the empty plasmid control (fig. S4B). While β-catenin depletion did not meet the Log2FC threshold in the uAb-transfected group, it matched the expected depletion pattern in cytosolic samples collected simultaneously and analyzed by immunoblotting (fig. S4C). At the same time, relatively few other proteins showed significant changes in abundance as a function of uAb expression, confirming that off-target effects associated with uAb expression or degradation were minimal, as in previous reports (29, 46).


### Guide peptides direct binding and ubiquitination of β-catenin in vitro
  To better understand the mechanism of the productive peptide-guided degraders, we evaluated their binding activity and specificity. To this end, each uAb was expressed in Escherichia coli strain BL21(DE3) and purified from cell extracts (fig. S5A), after which binding was evaluated by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). All four uAbs exhibited strong binding to immobilized β-catenin but not immobilized bovine serum albumin, whereas CHIP-ΔTPR showed no meaningful β-catenin binding (fig. S5B), confirming the ability of the computationally designed peptides to direct β-catenin–specific binding. The same purified uAbs were also subjected to biolayer interferometry analysis, which revealed the equilibrium dissociation constant (Kd) of each construct to be in the midnanomolar range (250 to 430 nM) (fig. S5C), which was an order of magnitude weaker than the reported affinity between β-catenin and Ecad-CD (Kd = 36 nM) (42). For comparison, we generated a panel of uAbs composed of CHIPΔTPR fused to different VHH nanobodies that bind the N-terminal, core, or C-terminal domain of β-catenin with Kd values ranging from ∼2 nM up to >10 μM (47). When expressed in DLD1 cells, several of the VHH-based uAbs depleted β-catenin with an efficiency that was indistinguishable from the two best peptide-guided degraders, Ecad-9–CHIPΔTPR and Ecad-30–CHIPΔTPR (fig. S6, A to C). While two of these uAbs were composed of VHHs that have low nanomolar affinity for β-catenin (BC1 and BC2; Kd ≈ 2 to 5 nM), two others involved VHHs having order-of-magnitude weaker affinity (BC6 and BC9; Kd ≈ 5 to 10 μM) (47). Hence, our data indicate that a range of binder affinities can achieve equally strong proximity-induced target degradation and that the guide peptides satisfy our design criteria of binding less avidly to β-catenin than Ecad-CD.

  We also investigated the ability of our peptide-guided uAbs to promote ubiquitination of β-catenin in a reconstituted in vitro ubiquitination (IVU) assay. This assay involves mixing purified UPP components [E1, E2, ubiquitin, and adenosine triphosphate (ATP)] with one of the uAbs as the E3 ubiquitin ligase component and purified β-catenin as the target in a one-pot reaction (fig. S7A). β-Catenin has 27 potential ubiquitin attachment sites: 26 internal Lys residues as well as its N terminus. The E2 enzyme UbcH5α was chosen because of its demonstrated ability to function with human CHIP in vitro (23, 30, 48). When IVU reaction mixtures were probed with an anti–β-catenin antibody, we detected ubiquitinated β-catenin, which appeared in immunoblots as high–molecular weight (HMW) bands greater than 100 kDa (fig. S7B; shown for Ecad-30CHIPΔTPR). When these samples were immunoprecipitated using magnetic beads coated with a β-catenin–specific VHH nanobody and then immunoblotted with an anti-ubiquitin antibody, HMW ubiquitin species were detected that corresponded to ubiquitinated β-catenin (fig. S7C). The intensity of the HMW bands became more pronounced at later incubation times, which was characteristic of the polyubiquitination mediated by CHIP in the presence of native and non-native targets (23, 30, 48). In contrast, IVU reactions performed using guideless CHIPΔTPR as the E3 showed no detectable ubiquitination of β-catenin, confirming the essentiality of the guide peptide for redirecting the catalytic activity of CHIPΔTPR to the non-native β-catenin target.

  To unequivocally establish the formation of β-catenin–linked ubiquitin chains, we performed MS analysis to profile the ubiquitination patterns generated by Ecad-30–CHIPΔTPR on β-catenin in IVU reactions. When a ubiquitinated protein is subjected to tryptic digestion, a C-terminal Gly-Gly dipeptide derived from ubiquitin remains attached to the ubiquitinated Lys residue (fig. S7D) (49). For this analysis, HMW products (∼80 to 250 kDa) were excised from an SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gel, digested with trypsin, and analyzed by LC–MS/MS. A thorough scan of the MS data identified several ubiquitinated Lys residues in β-catenin–derived tryptic peptides, which all occurred in the N-terminal third of β-catenin (amino acids 1 to 233) (fig. S7, E and F). These results confirm the ability of our peptide-guided uAbs to transfer ubiquitin to multiple Lys residues in the target protein and corroborate the ubiquitin-mediated proteasomal degradation of β-catenin seen above.


### Guide peptides bind the armadillo repeats of β-catenin
  To further investigate the interaction between our productive degraders and β-catenin, we first turned to the previously solved crystal structure [Protein Data Bank (PDB) code 1I7X] of the complex formed by Ecad-CD (residues 577 to 728 of the mature E-cadherin sequence) and the proteolytically defined armadillo (arm) repeat domain of murine β-catenin (residues 134 to 671) (41). The interaction interface between these proteins involves the C-terminal 100 residues of Ecad-CD, which are defined by five regions (regions I to V) (Fig. 3, A and B). All our SaLT&PepPr-predicted peptide sequences except for Ecad-33 occurred in region III of Ecad-CD (residues 667 to 684). The observation that SaLT&PepPr enriched for peptide sequences almost exclusively from region III was notable when one considers that our input to SaLT&PepPr was the entire Ecad-CD, which contains four other regions in addition to region III that make extensive contacts with the β-catenin arm repeats. When we modeled the interaction between Ecad-30 and β-catenin using AlphaFold3 (AF3), the guide peptide adopted an extended orientation that closely resembled the region III polypeptide, running along the β-catenin groove toward its N terminus and contacting arm repeats R9–R4 in β-catenin (residues Tyr306 to Arg515) (Fig. 3C). To analyze the Ecad-30–β-catenin interaction more quantitatively, we enlisted PeptiDerive (50, 51) using the AF3-predicted complex as input. This analysis revealed that the greatest energy drop was between amino acids 379 and 394 of β-catenin (Fig. 3D), a motif that is central to the interaction with region III of Ecad-CD and where important hydrogen bonds and water-mediated contacts involving residues N387 and D390 of β-catenin are formed. On the basis of this structural analysis, we propose a model whereby Ecad-30–CHIPΔTPR interacts with the arm repeats of β-catenin in a manner that positions the CHIPΔTPR domain in proximity to the N terminus of β-catenin. This orientation would explain the IVU results above in which ubiquitin was found exclusively within the first 233 amino acids of β-catenin.

  To validate the structural model, we subjected Ecad-30–CHIPΔTPR to domain mapping analysis using a previously described ELISA strategy in which three different truncation mutants of β-catenin—the negatively charged N terminus (amino acids 1 to 119), the core domain composed of 12 arm repeats (amino acids 120 to 683), and the C terminus (amino acids 684 to 781)—were used as immobilized antigens (47). This analysis revealed that Ecad-30–CHIPΔTPR exclusively recognized the core arm repeat domain, with binding that rivaled that measured for full-length wild-type (WT) β-catenin (fig. S5B). We did not detect any domain cross-specificity for Ecad-30–CHIPΔTPR with either the N-terminal or the C-terminal domains of β-catenin, suggesting a strict domain-specific binding in line with our prediction that the Ecad-30 peptide binds β-catenin between amino acids 379 and 394.


### Peptide-guided uAbs promote degradation of mutant forms of β-catenin
  Besides loss-of-function mutations in genes encoding Wnt signaling components, β-catenin is also aberrantly activated by mutations within the CTNNB1 gene itself (52). For example, mutations affecting critical serine and threonine residues within the N-terminal phosphodegron sequence of β-catenin—such as S33, S37, T41, and S45—or deletions in exon 3, which encompasses this region, are well known to disrupt β-catenin’s interaction with the β-TrCP F-box protein that acts as a substrate-recognizing component of the Skp1-Cul1-F-box E3 ubiquitin ligase complex. This disruption results in β-catenin stabilization and the subsequent activation of oncogenic transcriptional programs. Because Ecad-30 is predicted to bind downstream of these activating β-catenin mutations, we hypothesized that our uAb constructs could promote degradation of both WT and mutant forms of β-catenin that are commonly observed in CRC and HCC. To test this hypothesis, we enlisted two human HCC cell lines that carry heterozygous β-catenin mutations: HepG2 that harbors a deletion of exons 3 and 4 corresponding to amino acids 25 to 140 in β-catenin (CTNNB1WT/Δex3–4), and HCT116 that harbors a 3–base pair in-frame deletion that eliminates the serine residue at codon 45 in exon 3 (CTNNB1WT/Δ45). When Ecad-30–CHIPΔTPR was expressed in these cells, strong knockdown of β-catenin was observed that rivaled that achieved in DLD1 cells (fig. S8). We also tested an isogenic line derived from HCT116 (clone W1) that harbored only the mutant β-catenin allele (CTNNB1−/Δ45) (53). A similar level of β-catenin knockdown was observed in these cells (fig. S8), confirming the ability of our Ecad-30–guided degrader to promote turnover of β-catenin irrespective of its mutational status and providing indirect validation of the predicted Ecad-30–binding site.


### Selective removal of cytosolic β-catenin by peptide-guided uAbs
  Given that β-catenin executes two principal activities that are closely tied to its subcellular localization inside cells, we next investigated the ability of our productive uAb degraders to disambiguate these dual roles. To this end, we transfected DLD1 cells with uAb-encoding plasmids or control plasmids and generated subcellular fractions that were subjected to immunoblot analysis. Cells transfected with peptide-guided uAb degraders exhibited strong, statistically significant knockdown of β-catenin in cytosolic but not membrane fractions (Fig. 4, A and B), indicating clear selectivity of our degraders for the pathogenic signaling pool of β-catenin. At the same time, no measurable degradation was observed in any fractions derived from controls including untreated cells (cells only control), cells transfected with empty pDNA, or cells transfected with pDNA encoding the guideless CHIPΔTPR construct or the nonspecific polyG-CHIPΔTPR construct. For comparison, we evaluated the extent to which the VHH nanobody–based degraders affected membrane-bound β-catenin levels. uAbs based on the BC1 and BC2 nanobodies, which both bind β-catenin with stronger affinity than Ecad-CD, induced strong degradation of both cytosolic and membrane β-catenin (fig. S6). In contrast, uAbs based on BC6 and BC9, which bind β-catenin with weaker affinity than Ecad-CD, only lowered cytosolic but not membrane β-catenin levels. These results further corroborate our hypothesis that selective degradation of β-catenin is achieved by moderate affinity uAbs that are outcompeted by membrane-associated E-cadherin for binding to β-catenin.

  To probe uAb selectivity more quantitatively, we took advantage of a CRISPR-Cas9–edited DLD1 cell line in which a sequence encoding the 11–amino acid High BiT (HiBiT) tag was knocked-in at the endogenous CTNNB1 loci, resulting in C-terminal tagging of the β-catenin protein. Following transfection of HiBiT reporter cells with peptide-guided uAbs, we measured strong knockdown (∼50 to 60%) of cytosolic β-catenin, whereas membrane β-catenin levels were unchanged (Fig. 4C), consistent with the immunoblotting results. Cytosolic and membrane β-catenin levels remained elevated in all control cells. We further tested nuclear fractions derived from the same cells and observed that only the productive degraders but none of the controls promoted significant reduction of β-catenin levels in the nucleus (Fig. 4C), mirroring the cytosolic β-catenin profiles. Collectively, these findings further demonstrate that peptide-guided uAbs preferentially degraded the soluble cytosolic/nuclear subpopulation of β-catenin while sparing the membrane-bound subpopulation that is associated with endogenous E-cadherin at the cell membrane. As expected, the CTNNB1 siRNA reduced cytosolic and nuclear β-catenin levels as strongly as the pDNA-encoded Ecad-30–CHIPΔTPR degrader but also significantly lowered the membrane pool of β-catenin (Fig. 4C and fig. S9, A and B) and thus lacked the subcellular location–dependent selectivity of the peptide-guided uAbs.


### Down-regulation of known target genes by uAb-driven β-catenin elimination
  Next, we evaluated whether uAb-mediated knockdown of cytosolic β-catenin concomitantly affected the endogenous expression of several known β-catenin target genes, namely Axin2 (54), Cyp1a2 (55), and c-Myc (6). Consistent with the signaling inhibition observed with the synthetic TOPFlash reporter, real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis revealed a significant decrease in transcript levels for all three β-catenin target genes in DLD1 cells transfected with pDNA encoding the Ecad-30–CHIPΔTPR construct (Fig. 5A). In contrast, transcript levels for these same genes were unaffected in control cells transfected with pDNA encoding guideless CHIPΔTPR or the nonproductive Ecad-14–CHIPΔTPR construct. The decrease in gene expression triggered by Ecad-30–CHIPΔTPR was indistinguishable from that caused by CTNNB1 siRNA treatment, which was also in agreement with TOPFlash results. Together, these results provide additional confirmation of uAb-mediated inhibition of β-catenin’s transcriptional regulatory function in CRC cells.


### Survival and growth of tumor cells is affected by peptide-guided degraders
  To address the biological impact of uAb-driven β-catenin removal, we investigated the extent to which peptide-guided degraders affected survival of CRC cells. Specifically, the viability of DLD1 tumor cells was assessed by 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-5-(3-carboxymethoxyphenyl)-2-(4-sulfophenyl)-2H-tetrazolium (MTS) assay following transfection with each of the peptide-guided uAbs and compared to transfection with control constructs. At both 24 and 48 hours posttransfection, a significant decrease in survival was apparent for DLD1 cells receiving pDNA encoding the four productive uAb degraders but not negative controls (Fig. 5B and fig. S10). The 35 to 50% reduction in DLD1 cell viability triggered by peptide-guided uAbs compared favorably to the 35% decrease in cell viability achieved by CTNNB1 siRNA treatment of DLD1 cells observed here or of Hep3B and HepG2 cells described previously (13). We also examined the effect of uAb-driven β-catenin knockout on tumor cell proliferation using a clonogenic assay in which the colony-forming ability of DLD1 cells was assessed by plating transfected cells at a low density and growing for 5 days. A dramatic decrease in the number of crystal violet–stained colonies was observed for DLD1 cells transfected with pDNA encoding the four productive uAbs relative to negative controls (Fig. 5C). Similar to the viability results, uAbs diminished the colony-forming ability of DLD1 cells to an extent that rivaled that of CTNNB1 siRNA treatment. Together, these results reveal the capacity of peptide-guided uAbs for affecting tumorigenic phenotypes via targeted β-catenin depletion.


### LNP-mediated delivery of uAb mRNA depletes cytosolic β-catenin in cultured cancer cells
  Despite the demonstrated proteome editing capability of uAb degraders, efficient cellular delivery represents a major obstacle in their clinical translation. To address this challenge, we investigated the delivery of uAb-encoded mRNAs using LNPs, which have emerged as reliable, therapeutically relevant vectors for targeted delivery, cellular uptake, and cytosolic release of RNA payloads. LNP components are widely regarded as safe, and Food and Drug Administration approval has been granted for LNP-encapsulated mRNAs and siRNAs (56–58).

  As an initial proof of concept, synthetic mRNAs encoding Ecad-30–CHIPΔTPR mRNA, guideless CHIPΔTPR, and CHIPΔTPR fused with a nonspecific scramble peptide (scr-CHIPΔTPR) were produced by in vitro translation (IVT) and subsequently formulated with LNPs in which the ionizable lipid was a unique trialkyl ionizable lipid called Lipid 10 (59). We chose Lipid 10 because it is a well-tolerated and potent ionizable lipid for siRNA and mRNA delivery in rodents and nonhuman primates (59). Moreover, unlike some other recently disclosed ionizable lipids (e.g., SM-102 and ALC-0315 used in COVID vaccines), Lipid 10 was designed for intravenous delivery and found to perform optimally for targeting hepatocytes (59).

  Because this LNP composition was expected to drive biodistribution to the liver following intravenous administration, we first evaluated our uAb-mRNA-LNP formulations using the human HCC cell line, Hep3B, which is known for its extensive expression of liver-specific proteins and abnormally stabilized β-catenin levels due to mutation of AXIN1. Transfection of Hep3B cells with LNP-encapsulated mRNA encoding Ecad-30–CHIPΔTPR resulted in strong knockdown of cytosolic β-catenin, whereas treatment with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) or control LNP formulations resulted in no detectable knockdown (fig. S11, A and B). A similar level of β-catenin knockdown was observed in Hep3B cells following transfection with pDNA encoding the uAb (fig. S11, C and D), indicating a comparable potency in vitro for the mRNA- and pDNA-based delivery methods. We also evaluated the effect of LNP-mediated delivery of uAb mRNA on β-catenin signaling. Specifically, Hep3B cells transfected with the TOPFlash reporter were treated with LNPs formulated with increasing concentrations of mRNA payloads. In line with the significant reduction of cytosolic β-catenin, delivery of LNP-encapsulated mRNA encoding Ecad-30–CHIPΔTPR resulted in robust, dose-dependent inhibition of β-catenin signaling, with a half-maximal inhibition of 2.9 nM at 48 hours posttreatment and an overall reduction in luciferase activity that was similar to that achieved in Hep3B cells transfected with pDNA encoding the uAb (fig. S12, A to C). As expected, PBS or LNP-encapsulated control constructs had no effect on luciferase activity.


### LNP-delivered uAb mRNA depletes cytosolic β-catenin in mice
  Given the ability of Ecad-30–CHIPΔTPR–mRNA–LNP to promote knockdown of cytosolic β-catenin in vitro, we next investigated whether the same formulations could promote knockdown of β-catenin in vivo following systemic administration. To this end, groups of WT BALB/c mice were administered single intravenous injections of PBS or LNP-encapsulated mRNA formulations (1.0 mg/kg mRNA dose) via the lateral tail vein. At 24 hours after the intravenous injections, we analyzed tissue-specific β-catenin silencing by homogenizing isolated liver tissue and performing subcellular fractionations to generate cytosolic and membrane fractions. As expected, LNP-delivered mRNA encoding guideless CHIPΔTPR or nonspecific scr-CHIPΔTPR control constructs showed no measurable changes in cytosolic or membrane β-catenin levels relative to the levels measured in mice receiving PBS (Fig. 6, A and B). Meanwhile, LNP-mediated delivery of Ecad-30–CHIPΔTPR mRNA resulted in statistically significant elimination of cytosolic β-catenin without any measurable effect on β-catenin levels in the membrane fraction (Fig. 6, A and B), thereby confirming that peptide-guided uAb selectivity was maintained following mRNA-LNP delivery in vivo. The duration of this effect was evaluated in a short-term study by examining time course changes in liver-specific β-catenin levels via immunoblotting analysis. Specifically, liver samples were harvested on days 1 to 7 after a single 1–mg/kg dose of LNP-encapsulated mRNA encoding either Ecad-30–CHIPΔTPR or CHIPΔTPR. Notably, in mice receiving the Ecad-30–CHIPΔTPR formulation, cytosolic β-catenin remained selectively decreased on days 1 to 5 postinjection but returned to steady-state levels by day 7, while membrane β-catenin levels were unchanged over this same interval (fig. S13). In contrast, mice receiving the guideless CHIPΔTPR formulation exhibited no measurable changes in cytosolic or membrane β-catenin levels. Collectively, these results lay the foundation for the potential clinical translation of peptide-guided β-catenin degraders in models of Wnt-driven CRC and HCC.


## DISCUSSION
  Here, we describe a method for rapidly designing uAb degraders in a CRISPR-analogous manner whereby short guide peptides were identified using a structure-agnostic pLM, SaLT&PepPr, and used to redirect the human E3 ubiquitin ligase CHIP to pathogenic β-catenin and accelerate its removal via proteasomal degradation. Our peptide-guided uAb degraders were shown to selectively deplete abnormally accumulated β-catenin in the cytosol and nucleus of CRC cells while preserving normal β-catenin at the membrane. In contrast, a β-catenin–targeting siRNA was unable to distinguish between these subpopulations as they are expressed from the same gene. This distinction arises because, unlike RNAi and CRISPR-Cas methods, uAb-mediated proteome editing operates at the posttranslational level. Consequently, uAbs have the potential to dissect complicated protein functions with higher resolution than their gene silencing counterparts. For example, by exploiting the customizable affinity and specificity of the warhead, uAbs have been engineered that selectively deplete particular protein states (e.g., active/inactive conformation, mutant/WT, posttranslationally modified/unmodified, etc.) as well as localizations, as we showed here and has been reported previously (25, 26, 30–34, 36, 60, 61).

  In the present study, we selectively targeted the pathogenic subpopulation of β-catenin by designing guide peptides based on β-catenin’s known interaction with E-cadherin but with weaker affinity than the native interaction. The resulting uAbs showed minimal competition with the E-cadherin–associated subpopulation at the membrane. These uAbs exhibited a midnanomolar affinity (250 to 430 nM) for β-catenin, which is measurably weaker than the low nanomolar affinity of warheads that are commonly deployed in uAb studies. Our peptide-guided uAbs performed as well or better than uAbs constructed with low-nanomolar affinity (∼2 to 5 nM) VHH domains specific for β-catenin, consistent with a previous study in which a DARPin-CHIPΔTPR chimera with low-micromolar affinity for ERK2 was still capable of achieving target degradation (30). Collectively, these results further emphasize that warheads with high affinity may not be optimal for constructing an efficacious uAb (34) and that other parameters in addition to binding kinetics, such as cooperativity, dynamics, epitope differences, and proximity considerations, are crucial determinants of ternary complex formation and ubiquitination efficiency (62). In the future, it will be important to more carefully map the relationship between affinity and efficacy, while also keeping in mind that naturally occurring E3 ligases often exhibit relatively modest affinities for their targets. For example, the measured affinity between CHIP and its native substrates Hsp70, Hsp90, and Hsc70 is in the low-micromolar range (Kd = 0.3 to 2.3 μM) (63).

  A key design feature that was leveraged here is the modularity of human CHIP, which derives from its conformational flexibility (48) and broad substrate specificity (64). This modularity allows CHIP to accommodate substantial rewiring of its target-binding domain without compromising its ubiquitin transfer activity, making it widely applicable as a versatile degrader of diverse POIs in both transiently and stably transfected cell lines, as evidenced by the many successful uAbs that have enlisted this E3 (23, 26, 29, 30, 36, 37, 65–67). Simple swapping of the warhead using preexisting off-the-shelf binders has proven to be an effective means for on-demand construction of functional uAbs against desired POIs (19, 62, 68). However, while exchanging uAb warheads is relatively straightforward, discovering warheads for previously untargeted proteins has remained a major bottleneck. This process has historically relied upon purely experimental methods, such as animal immunization or screening using display technologies (e.g., phage, yeast, etc.), which are time consuming, labor intensive, and often result in low hit rates. Fortunately, recent breakthroughs in generative methods have revolutionized de novo target binder design and quickened the pace of discovery. State-of-the-art pLMs, such as ESM-2, now offer unprecedented potential to create binders for virtually any POI, requiring little to no structural information (69). Consequently, structure-agnostic approaches like SaLT&PepPr (29), PepPrCLIP (38), and PepMLM (39) have proven particularly effective at designing binders that target disordered proteins, including difficult-to-drug transcription factors like β-catenin. Moreover, pLM-driven binder discovery has greatly accelerated the pace at which uAbs can be generated, as demonstrated here and in other recent studies (29, 37–39). With SaLT&PepPr, we rapidly constructed a panel of 44 bespoke uAbs that were evaluated for their ability to degrade pathogenic β-catenin and inhibit its signaling activity, with design-build-test cycles of roughly 1 week. This development speed is in stark contrast to small molecule–based degraders (e.g., molecular glues and PROTACs), which suffer from the lack of available POI- and E3-specific ligands and typically require extremely long and difficult campaigns to find ligands for new targets (19). Although we focused on four of the best uAb degraders for in-depth characterization, our preliminary screen identified 29 of 44 uAb designs that promoted >30% reduction in both β-catenin levels and Wnt/β-catenin signaling activity, reflecting a 66% success rate.

  While peptide-guided uAbs hold great potential for knockdown of biomedically important targets, their clinical application is limited by the fact that most protein biologics are incapable of spontaneously entering mammalian cells (70). Consequently, methods for efficient in vivo delivery are needed for uAbs to reach their full clinical potential. However, most studies that have evaluated uAb efficacy in vivo have relied on delivery methods that are unsuitable for clinical translation, such as stable transfection/transduction of tumor-cell lines with uAb genes before implantation in mice (31–34, 60, 67) or injection of recombinant adenovirus delivery vehicles via nonclinically relevant routes of administration (e.g., intratumoral and intra-amniotic) (66, 71–73). With an eye toward more clinically relevant, nonviral delivery strategies, LNP vehicles have been used to encapsulate anionically modified uAb proteins, leading to efficient intracellular delivery and target knockdown in cultured cells (46); however, whether this method is effective in vivo was not tested. In related work, our group demonstrated the use of cationic polypeptide–based nanoplexes to functionally deliver encapsulated uAb-encoding mRNAs to mice (25); however, this formulation has yet to be clinically validated.

  Here, we pursued a well-established path for enabling exogenous proteins to access intracellular targets by delivering their encoding mRNA via LNP carriers, which are one of the most advanced nonviral delivery systems and are approved for therapeutic use in humans (56–58). We found that uAb-mRNA-LNP formulations enabled highly selective removal of pathogenic β-catenin in vitro, resulting in potent suppression of Wnt/β-catenin signaling with doses of less than 10 nM. When the same formulations were administered in mice, we observed strong knockdown of cytosolic β-catenin that persisted for 5 days following intravenous injection of a 1.0–mg/kg dose, consistent with the duration of protein expression reported in other single-dose experiments where mRNA-LNP formulations were intravenously injected at comparable mRNA concentrations (74, 75). To further extend the duration of uAb-driven knockdown, several strategies could be envisioned. Besides improvements to mRNA payloads and LNP vehicles themselves, which have become areas of intense research (76), uAb-centric innovations will also be crucial for in vivo therapeutic applications. For example, nearly all E3s are subject to autoubiquitination, which is an essential part of their natural turnover but can also lead to self-destruction of uAbs following their cytosolic delivery (31, 34, 46). Thus, lysine-replacement strategies that render uAbs resistant to self-degradation without compromising specificity and ubiquitination efficiency (23, 34) (US Patent Application No. 18/845,621) will need to be integrated with in vivo delivery efforts in the future.

  Another important translational consideration is the level of β-catenin degradation, which in our colorectal cell experiments appeared to hit a ceiling of 70 to 75% depletion. We suspect that the combination of protein resynthesis together with the abnormally high levels of β-catenin in CRC cells make it difficult to achieve complete ablation. This ceiling is not unique to our study, having been observed with other targeted protein degradation (TPD) modalities directed against β-catenin. For example, a recent study using a β-catenin–targeting PROTAC also showed a similar ceiling of 70 to 75% depletion in the CRC cell line HCT116 as well as in human embryonic kidney–293T cells, even after treatment with very high doses (50 to 70 μM) of degrader compound (22). Under the same conditions, only about 50% depletion of β-catenin was observed in the CRC cell lines SW480 and LoVo. Likewise, incomplete elimination of β-catenin (56%) was also observed in a stable DLD1 cell line carrying an inducible copy of a protein-based degrader against β-catenin (32). While this ceiling appears to be a common threshold across different β-catenin–targeting TPD modalities, it raises an important question: How much degradation is enough (77)? For some therapeutic targets, partial degradation may be sufficient to achieve desired phenotypic and clinical effects, whereas others may require more complete removal (i.e., >90%).

  As uAb degraders and LNP delivery vehicles are further refined and optimized, we anticipate that uAbs will become an increasingly attractive modality for targeting intracellular proteins, especially given the remarkable pace with which unique warheads are being found using pML-driven algorithms (29, 37–39). Our demonstration of LNP-mediated delivery of these peptide-guided degraders into cells, both in vitro and in vivo, serves as an important proof of concept for translating the uAb technology platform and sets the stage for uAb-mediated treatment of challenging diseases in the future.


## MATERIALS AND METHODS

### Computational peptide design
  Binding peptides designed in this study were generated by inputting the Ecad-CD or β-TrCP interacting partner sequences (table S1) into the SaLT&PepPr algorithm that we developed previously (29). This algorithm fine-tunes the ESM-2 pLM with a per-position prediction task to identify PPI sites using data from the PDB and prioritizes motifs that are most likely to be involved within interchain binding. To predict binding peptides, the Ecad-CD or β-TrCP binding partners were passed as input to the model and the probability of each amino acid being a PPI site was determined as the output of the model. Continuous sequences of amino acids were then cut from the sequence along the curve via a local-maximum sampling approach to isolate peptides of user-specified length with highest predicted probability of binding that also represent local maxima in the predicted binding likelihood. All binder sequences and their cumulative binding site probability scores are provided in tables S2 and S3. The Ecad-30–β-catenin interaction was analyzed using PeptiDerive (50, 51) with the AF3-predicted complex as input and calculated binding energy contributions using the Rosetta energy function for a sliding window of 16 amino acids over the length of β-catenin.


### Plasmids
  For construction of all peptide-guided uAb plasmids, we used a pcDNA3-based plasmid containing an Esp3I restriction site immediately upstream of DNA encoding a flexible GSGSG linker followed by the CHIPΔTPR gene (29). Oligonucleotides encoding the candidate guide peptides (data S1) were annealed and subsequently ligated into the Esp3I-digested backbone using T4 DNA ligase (NEB). Assembled constructs were used to transform E. coli cells (DH5α) and plated onto Luria-Bertani agar supplemented with the appropriate antibiotic. All plasmids were isolated using a QIAprep Spin Miniprep Kit (Qiagen) and confirmed by DNA sequencing at the Genomics Facility of the Cornell Biotechnology Resource Center.


### Cell culture and transfection
  The human CRC cell line, DLD1, was purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (catalog no. CCL-221, RRID:CVCL_0248). DLD1 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with penicillin (100 U/ml), streptomycin (100 mg/ml), and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Corning) at 37°C with 5% CO2. The cells were seeded in a six-well plate 1 day before transfection. Plasmids encoding the peptide-guided uAb constructs were prepared using the PureYield miniprep kit (Promega) to eliminate endotoxins. An appropriate amount of pDNA was mixed with jetPRIME buffer by vortexing for 10 s, after which jetPRIME reagent (VWR) was added to the mixture and incubated for 10 min at room temperature. The mixture was then added to the cells. After 4 hours of incubation at 37°C, with 5% CO2, fresh medium was replenished. The cells were subsequently cultured under these conditions for an additional 48 hours before being harvested for analysis. For siRNA transfection, a final concentration of 100 nM CTNNB1 siRNA (Cell Signaling Technology, catalog no. 6225) was transfected in each well of a 96-well plate (100 μl per well) using jetPRIME reagent.


### Immunoblot analysis
  On the day of harvest, cells were detached by addition of 0.05% trypsin-EDTA (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and cell pellets were washed twice with ice-cold 1× PBS. The cells were then lysed, and subcellular fractions were isolated from lysates using a Subcellular Protein Fractionation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) per the manufacturer’s instructions. Specifically, ice-cold cytosolic extraction buffer was added to the cell pellet, and the mixture was placed at 4°C for 10 min with gentle mixing followed by centrifugation at 500g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was collected immediately to a prechilled PCR tube and placed on ice followed by immunoblotting or stored at −20°C for future usage. The pellet was then mixed with ice-cold membrane extraction buffer, and the mixture was incubated at 4°C for 10 min followed by centrifugation at 3000g for 5 min. The supernatant was immediately transferred to a prechilled tube. Protein concentration was quantified using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). An equivalent amount of total protein was loaded into Precise tris-Hepes 4 to 20% SDS–polyacrylamide gels (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and separated by electrophoresis. Immunoblotting was performed according to standard protocols. Briefly, proteins were transferred to poly(vinylidene fluoride) membranes (Millipore); blocked with 5% (w/v) nonfat dry milk (Carnation) in 1× tris-buffered saline with 0.05% (v/v) Tween 20 (TBST) at room temperature for 1 hour; washed three times with TBST for 10 min; and probed with rabbit anti–β-catenin (Cell Signaling Technology, catalog no. 8480S, RRID:AB_11127855; diluted 1:1000), mouse anti–glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH, Calbiochem, catalog no. CB1001, RRID:AB_2107426; diluted 1:5000), rabbit anti-SERCA2 (Abcam, catalog no. ab137020, RRID:n/a; diluted 1:1000), and or rabbit anti–β-tubulin (Cell Signaling Technology, catalog no. 2146, RRID:AB_2210545; diluted 1:1000). The blots were washed again three times with TBST for 5 min each and then probed with a secondary antibody, either donkey anti–rabbit–horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (Abcam, catalog no. ab7083, RRID:AB_955416; diluted 1:2500) or goat anti–mouse-HRP (Abcam, catalog no. ab97023, RRID:AB_10679675; diluted 1:4000) for 1 hour at room temperature. Blots were detected by chemiluminescence using a ChemiDoc MP imager (Bio-Rad). GAPDH predominantly localizes to the cytosol and was used as a cytosolic fractionation marker under short exposure times. At longer exposures, GAPDH signal was also detected in the membrane fraction, consistent with its known partial association with plasma membranes; therefore, GAPDH served as a general loading control across both cytosolic and membrane fractions under long exposure times. SERCA2, an endoplasmic reticulum–resident calcium adenosine triphosphatase, served as a membrane fractionation marker (78). Densitometry analysis of protein bands in immunoblots was performed using ImageJ software (79) as described at https://imagej.net. Briefly, bands in each lane were grouped as a row or a horizontal “lane” and quantified using the gel analysis function in ImageJ. Intensity data for the uAb bands were normalized to band intensity for CHIPΔTPR control unless stated otherwise.


### TOPFlash assay
  A total of 1 × 104 DLD1 cells were seeded on a white-bottom 96-well plate 24 hours before transfection. On the day of transfection, each well received the following plasmids: M50 Super 8x TOPFlash plasmid (Addgene, plasmid # 12456) or M51 Super 8x FOPFlash (TOPFlash mutant; Addgene, plasmid # 12457), pCMV-Renilla (32), and one of the pcDNA3 plasmids encoding a peptide-guided uAb or control construct (e.g., CHIPΔTPR). Cells were transfected with a total of 100 ng of pDNA in a ratio of TOPFlash/FOPFlash:Renilla:pcDNA3 = 1:0.1:3 using jetPRIME reagent. After 48 hours of incubation, the cells were lysed and the firefly and Renilla luminescence signals were measured sequentially by the dual-luciferase reporter kit (Promega). Luminescence was read on a microplate reader (Tecan Spark). All luciferase signals were measured and normalized against the control Renilla signals. For TOPFlash analysis of siRNA, an identical protocol was followed but with CTNNB1 siRNA at a final concentration of 100 nM per transfection instead of pcDNA3 plasmid.


### HiBiT assay
  A total of 0.3 × 106 DLD1 cells with CTNNB1-HiBiT CRISPR knock-in (Promega) were seeded per well in clear, flat six-well plates. After 24 hours of incubation at 37°C, 5% CO2, the cells were transfected with 2 μg of pcDNA3 pDNA encoding a uAb or control construct or with 100 nM of CTNNB1 siRNA using jetPRIME reagent. The cells were incubated for 48 hours and then detached and harvested with 0.05% trypsin-EDTA. The cells were pelleted at 500g for 10 min and lysed, after which the cytosol, membrane, and nuclear fractions were isolated from lysates using a Subcellular Protein Fractionation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) per the manufacturer’s instructions. For HiBiT analysis, 20 μl of each fraction was added per well in white-bottom 96-well plates. One hundred microliters of the reagent from the Nano-Glo HiBiT Lytic Detection System kit (Promega) was added to each well followed by incubation for 30 to 60 min at room temperature. Luminescence was read on a plate reader (Tecan Spark). The total protein concentration in each well was measured by BCA assay. Signals for samples derived from uAb-expressing cells were normalized first by total protein concentration and then by the signals of samples derived from the cells-only control.


### Real-time qPCR analysis
  DLD1 cells were seeded in a six-well plate at a density of 2.5 × 105 cells per well in 2 ml of RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 24 hours before transfection. On the day of transfection, DLD1 cells were transfected with 2 μg of pcDNA3 pDNA encoding a uAb or control construct or with 100 nM of CTNNB1 siRNA using jetPRIME reagent, after which the plates were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2. At 24 hours posttransfection, the cells were detached with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA and reseeded in a 60-mm dish in 5 ml of the medium. The cells were incubated at 37°C, with 5% CO2, for 3 days. On the day of cell harvest, medium was removed from the plate and 1 ml of TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added to each well. RNA was extracted and purified according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA was converted to cDNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). qPCR was performed with 25 ng of cDNA and SYBR Green Universal Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on a QuantStudio 7 Pro Real-Time PCR System. Cycle conditions were 95°C for 10 min followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 20 s and 60°C for 1 min. Primers for each target gene are provided in the Supplementary Materials (data S1).


### Colony formation assay
  DLD1 cells were seeded and transfected in the same manner as described above for the qPCR analysis. At 24 hours posttransfection, the cells were detached with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA and reseeded in a 100-mm dish at a density of 2000 cells per dish in 10 ml of medium. The cells were incubated at 37°C, with 5% CO2, for 5 days. On the day of colony staining, the medium was removed from the dish, and the cells as monolayers were gently rinsed two times with ample 1× PBS. A total of 5 ml of 0.5% (w/v) crystal violet (Sigma-Aldrich) was added to each dish, and the mixture was incubated for 15 to 20 min at room temperature. Crystal violet solution was removed, and culture dishes were rinsed four times with 8 ml of 1× PBS followed by imaging. Colony counting was performed by ImageJ using the same threshold for all samples. Data were normalized by colony counts of the CHIPΔTPR sample.


### MTS cell proliferation assay
  A total of 5 to 7 × 104 DLD1 cells were seeded the same way as for the TOPFlash assay but in clear, flat, 96-well plates, one for each time point. After 24 hours of incubation at 37°C, with 5% CO2, the cells were transfected with 100 ng of pcDNA3 pDNA encoding a uAb or control construct or with 100 nM of CTNNB1 siRNA using jetPRIME reagent. Fresh medium was replaced after 4 to 6 hours of incubation. The plates were further incubated for 24 and 48 hours posttransfection, and samples were collected at each of those time points. A total of 20 μl of MTS assay reagent (Promega) was added to each well, followed by another 1 hour of incubation. Absorbance was measured using a microplate reader (Tecan Spark) at a wavelength of 490 nm to assess cell viability and proliferation.


### Production of synthetic mRNA
  Synthetic mRNAs corresponding to CHIPΔTPR, scr-CHIPΔTPR, and Ecad-30–CHIPΔTPR were produced using IVT. Briefly, mRNA sequences including a 5′ untranslated region and a 3′ untranslated region were each cloned in DNA plasmids downstream of an RNA promoter and upstream of a poly(A)120 region. The pDNA was expanded in E. coli, purified, and linearized downstream of the poly(A) region with BspQI. The linearized pDNA was combined with RNA polymerase, ATP, cytidine triphosphate, guanosine triphosphate (GTP), N1MePseudoUTP, and Mg2+ and incubated at 37°C for 2 hours. Deoxyribonuclease I was added to the reaction to digest the pDNA template to stop the reaction, and the RNA was purified. To cap the RNA, the purified RNA was combined with Vaccinia capping enzyme, S-adenosylmethionine, GTP, and Mg2+, incubated at 37°C for 2 hours, and purified. Purity of the mRNA was evaluated by capillary gel electrophoresis, and content was evaluated by A260/280 absorbance spectroscopy.


### Preparation of mRNA-LNP formulations
  Synthetic mRNAs were encapsulated in LNPs with Lipid 10 (Genevant Sciences) serving as the ionizable lipid in all formulations. This lipid was designed for intravenous delivery and was synthesized as described previously (59). All mRNAs were encapsulated in LNPs using a controlled mixing process (US 9005654) in which an aqueous solution of mRNA in acetate buffer at pH 5 was combined with an ethanolic solution of lipids in a T-shaped impingement zone. The lipid mix contained a polyethylene glycol (PEG)–conjugated lipid, Lipid 10 as the ionizable lipid, cholesterol, and 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DSPC) at a molar ratio of 1.6:54.6:32.8:10.9, respectively, at a total lipid-to-RNA ratio of 20:1 w/w. Ethanol was removed by tangential flow ultrafiltration, followed by buffer exchange and concentration. The formulations were adjusted to 0.5 mg/ml and sterile filtered through a 0.2-μm polyethersulfone (PES) membrane. Aliquots were subsequently stored frozen at −80°C in tris-sucrose buffer (pH 8.0) until the day of dosing.


### LNP characterization
  LNP formulations were characterized by particle size analysis using a dynamic light scattering (DLS) instrument. Briefly, LNPs were diluted to 0.8 to 1.6 ng/μl total mRNA in PBS (pH 7.4) and transferred into a polystyrene cuvette to measure particle size and polydispersity by DLS (Malvern Nano ZS Zetasizer), using refractive index (RI) of 1.590 and absorption of 0.010 in PBS at 25°C and viscosity of 0.9073 cP and RI of 1.332. Measurements were made with 10-s run durations with the number of runs automatically determined. Each measurement had a fixed position of 4.65 mm in the cuvette with an automatic attenuation selection. Diameters were reported as Z average.


### In vivo administration of LNPs in mice
  LNP formulations encapsulating mRNA corresponding to CHIPΔTPR, scr-CHIPΔTPR, and Ecad-30–CHIPΔTPR were administered intravenously by tail vein injection at a dose of 1.0 mg/kg of mRNA to female BALB/c mice (strain # 000651, 6 to 7 weeks old; the Jackson Laboratory, RRID:IMSR_JAX:000651). Power analysis (G*Power version 3.1.9.6; α = 0.05, power = 0.80) indicated a minimum of four mice per group. All mice were analyzed equally with no subsampling, and thus there was no requirement for randomization. Blinding was also not deemed necessary as all data were acquired indiscriminately following well-defined preestablished criteria, and no data were excluded in this work. On the day of injection, the LNP stocks were filtered and diluted to the required dosing concentration with PBS. At 1 day postinjection, the animals were euthanized under carbon dioxide, and the livers were harvested and collected. The livers were also collected from mice receiving CHIPΔTPR and Ecad-30–CHIPΔTPR at 3, 5, and 7 days postinjection. Harvested livers were weighed and flash frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C overnight. The next day, the livers were homogenized to obtain lysates, which were subsequently fractionated into cytosolic and membrane fractions using a Subcellular Protein Fractionation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) per the manufacturer’s instructions. The isolated fractions were then subjected to immunoblotting analysis as described above. All animal experiments were reviewed and approved by the Institution of Animal Care and Use Committees of Cornell University under protocol # 2019-0063.


### Statistics and reproducibility
  To ensure robust reproducibility of all results, the experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates and at least three technical measurements. Sample sizes were not predetermined on the basis of statistical methods but were chosen according to the standards of the field (at least three independent biological replicates for each condition), which gave sufficient statistics for the effect sizes of interest. All data were reported as average values with error bars representing SD. For individual samples, statistical significance was determined by paired Student’s t tests (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001) using GraphPad Prism 11 for MacOS version 11.0.0. No data were excluded from the analyses. The experiments were not randomized. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment.
