# Multi-omics approaches reveal direct NF-κB p65 target genes in pancreatic islets during cytokine exposure and in Type 1 diabetes


## Abstract
  Autoimmune diseases, including Type 1 diabetes (T1D), are often characterized by overactive inflammatory signaling pathways. The pro-inflammatory cytokine interleukin-1β (IL-1β) elicits global gene expression changes in islet β-cells which overlap with islets obtained from human donors with T1D. The direct transcriptional link between NF-κB subunit p65 and target genes involved with autoimmune events was investigated. We used a multi-omics approach including bulk RNA-sequencing (RNA), single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-Seq), and chromatin immunoprecipitation coupled to deep sequencing (ChIP-Seq), alongside molecular docking simulations, and transcriptional assays. Through the various experimental modalities, we identified early response genes driven by IL-1β that were differentially expressed in pancreatic islets from human T1D donors and also conserved across mouse, rat, and human tissues. ChIP-Seq revealed genes that are direct genomic targets of the NF-κB p65 transcription factor. Moreover, regions that gained RNA polymerase II binding following cellular exposure to IL-1β were identified, complementing the early response gene profile induced by β-cell exposure to IL-1β. Molecular docking simulations predicted that mutations reducing p65 transcriptional capacity do not alter DNA binding ability. These findings clearly show that IL-1β signaling in pancreatic β-cells directs p65 to specific genomic regions congruent with increased gene expression relevant to T1D in β-cell lines as well as mouse and human islets exposed to cytokines. Islets from human donors with T1D express genes identified as direct p65 targets using unbiased approaches, implicating heightened NF-κB activity as a critical component of autoimmune disease etiology.


## Introduction
  Type 1 diabetes is an endocrine disease that arises from reductions in circulating insulin due to damage and dysfunction of the pancreatic islet β-cells (3). The disease progresses when immune cells infiltrate pancreatic tissue and ultimately eliminate a portion of the insulin-producing β-cells (47). The infiltration of various leukocytes likely arises through production of chemotactic cytokines (aka chemokines) within the islets of Langerhans and recognition of β-cell derived antigens (4, 10, 17, 22, 30, 33). Upon immune cell arrival, there is interplay among the innate and adaptive immune cells through discrete cytokines produced by such cells and through cell-cell contacts (51, 53, 72).

  Macrophages are an innate immune cell present near and within islets of non-obese diabetic (NOD) mice (6), pancreatic tissue from autoantibody positive donors, and donors with T1D (67). Macrophages and other immune cells secrete a variety of cytokines, many of which have been associated with islet inflammation and T1D pathology (30, 56, 57). Numerous cell surface and intracellular signaling pathways converge upon NF-κB to promote alterations in cellular phenotype, allowing an organism to respond to a variety of changing environmental conditions (5, 40). One prototypical example is the cytokine interleukin-1β (IL-1β). IL-1β signals through the cell surface receptor IL-1RI, which is connected to the NF-κB pathway, to induce intracellular changes in gene expression that alter cellular function (26).

  The predominant transcriptional components of the NF-κB pathway are the p65 (encoded by the RelA gene) and p50 subunits (65). The transcription factors p65 and p50 are capable of both homo- and hetero-dimer formation, but only p65 has a transactivation domain (TAD); the presence of the TAD allows p65 dimers to activate gene transcription in response to specific signals, such as IL-1β (75). Most cells, including pancreatic β-cells, express RelA/p65 which allows them to respond to various stimuli to remodel their transcriptional outputs (10, 75). When such pathways are chronically activated, this often results in autoimmune or autoinflammatory diseases.

  Herein, we have identified early IL-1β signaling events using bulk RNA-Seq, single cell RNA-Seq (scRNA-Seq) and chromatin immunoprecipitation coupled to massively parallel sequencing (ChIP-Seq) to understand how β-cells exposed to IL-1β regulate expression of genes. These data were compared with islets from humans with established islet autoimmunity. We identified RelA/p65 as critical for global gene expression alterations in response to IL-1β with key genes identified overlapping those present in human donors with T1D. Thus, dysregulated NF-κB activation likely represents one major factor driving transcriptional alterations occurring in pancreatic β–cells that ultimately participate in the onset and progression of T1D.


## Results

### Discrete and complementary gene expression patterns revealed by comparing human islets exposed to cytokines versus human islets from donors with Type 1 diabetes.
  We compared bulk RNA-Seq data obtained from human islets exposed to the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IFNγ for 24 hours (66) with bulk RNA-Seq data obtained from islets isolated from donors with established Type 1 diabetes (ref. (63); Figure 1A). We identified 107 genes in common between these two conditions (Figure 1A) with Gene Ontology pathway analysis and representative pathway enriched genes shown in Figure 1B. We next analyzed human islets exposed to IL-1β for three hours to identify early response genes with relevance to T1D (Figure 1C). The comparison of donors with T1D (established disease), 24h exposure of human islets to IL-1β + IFNγ (late responses), and 3h exposure of human islets to IL-1β (immediate early response genes; IEGs) yielded 10 genes in common across all three human islet data sets (Figure 1D). Examples of pathways in common across all three data sets in Figure 1D include: regulation of immune effector process, chemotaxis, response to Type II interferon, and leukocyte cell-cell adhesion.


### IL-1b exposure promotes analogous gene expression responses in mouse β-cells and the 832/13 rat b-cell line with dependence on the NF-κB transcription factor p65.
  We used the Enrichr software (19) to predict which transcription factors are likely to be associated with differentially expressed genes from human islets. Specifically, we included the genes that were present in at least two of the gene sets in Fig 1D for analysis using Enrichr. This approach, shown in Figure 2A, used the TRRUST database (38) in combination with the Enrichr platform. The Enrichr analysis identified the p65 subunit of NF-κB as the most likely candidate associated with differentially expressed genes across the human bulk RNA-Seq data sets (TFs in Figure 2A predicted from the data shown in Figure 1D). The NF-κB subunit p65 is a transcription factor encoded by the Rela gene. Indeed, mouse and human RelA/p65 were both represented in the top 5 predicted transcription factors (Figure 2A), which indicates very similar, if not identical, conserved binding sites recognized by p65 across the two species. Therefore, we next extracted RNA from islets isolated from mice with pancreatic-targeted p65 gene deletion exposed or not to IL-1β. Bulk RNA-Seq analysis from these islets revealed that the expression of multiple IL-1β responsive genes were dependent upon the presence of p65 (Figure 2B). In addition, many of the same pathways found to be enriched in the human islets, such as ‘cellular response to biotic stimulus’ and ‘response to virus’, were also represented in the mouse islets (Figure 2B).

  Rodent and human islets comprise predominately insulin producing β-cells (32). We used the rat-derived 832/13 insulinoma cells (42), a highly glucose responsive cell culture model of rodent β-cells, exposed to IL-1β for 3 hours to identify the immediate early gene response to cytokine exposure (Figure 2C). IL-1β increased the expression of 756 genes in 832/13 cells within 3 hours (Figure 2C). This includes genes encoding chemokines, components of the antigen presentation pathways, and transcription factors. For comparison of gene expression across mouse, rat, and human tissues, the results from Figure 2C were integrated with bulk RNA-Seq data from mouse and human islets exposed to IL-1β for 3 hours and analyzed by comparing human orthologs of the genes expressed in rodents (Figure 2D). This analysis revealed 30 human genes (or orthologs of human genes) rapidly expressed in all three species in response to IL-1β (Figure 2D). Genes identified in common across mouse, rat, and human cells, such as Birc3, Cxcl3, Icam1, and Stx11, also reflect genes observed in human islets from donors with established T1D (24) and from human islets exposed to IL-1β + IFNγ [ref. (31) and Figure 1]. Thus, we note that conserved cytokine-regulated genes occur in mouse, rat, and human with analogous timing of response to IL-1β and are also highly relevant to autoimmune events.


### scRNA-seq reveals similar clusters of cell types between mouse and rat cells during acute exposure to IL-1β.
  While the bulk RNA-Seq approach provides high sensitivity, it represents an aggregated result from all cell-types within a tissue without any information on possible cellular heterogeneity in the transcriptomic responses. Thus, in efforts to understand individual cell-type specific responses to IL-1β, we implemented single-cell RNA-Seq (scRNA-Seq) and clustering analyses using data generated from rat 832/13 cells (this study) and mouse islets (68) to identify expression changes with single cell resolution (Figure 3).

  Unsupervised clustering of the integrated mouse single-cell RNA-seq samples yielded groups that represent discrete cell types within mouse islets as noted by the inter-cluster distance (Figure 3A). This was expected due to the distinct endocrine and other cell populations within the pancreatic islets (71). Cluster 0 identifies the β-cell population (Figure 3A; boxed cluster). This population of cells was thus isolated and subjected to re-clustering using Seurat (Figure 3B). We observed a much tighter clustering, which likely represents different transcriptional states of the same cell type (e.g., β-cells). Cells from both the NT and IL-1β exposed conditions were broadly distributed among the four identified clusters (Figure 3C), indicating that no single cluster was responsible for driving overall transcriptional responses. Clustering of the rat 832/13 cells also revealed tighter clusters (compared to islets) once again reflecting the common origin for the majority of these cells which also displayed different transcriptional states (Figure 3D).

  In addition, expression of the Ins1 and Ins2 genes were positively correlated with each other in both 832/13 cells and mouse islets (Supplementary Figure 1). Thus, at the single cell level, the 832/13 β-cell line and mouse islets show similarities in their highly expressed genes under control conditions and in their respective transcriptomic responses to IL-1β treatment. Moreover, clustering revealed that the cellular heterogeneity in rat 832/13 cells and β-cells from mouse islets are consistent with and analogous to data from human β-cells, where similar analyses revealed four distinct clusters (28).

  The expression of specific candidate genes in the scRNA-seq data, split by cluster and treatment condition, is represented by the dot plots in Figures 4A and 4B. The expression of genes involved in inflammatory processes, such as Birc3, Cxcl10, Cxcl1, Cxcl2, Cxcl3, and Icam1 were greatly increased in response to IL-1β in both datasets. Similarly, the decrease in expression of Pdx1, Nkx6.1 and MafA occurred in response to IL-1β in mouse islets and the 832/13 β-cell line in all clusters (Figures 4A and 4B). We next employed a non-negative matrix factorization algorithm encoded within the GeneNMF package to identify gene expression programs in an unbiased manner. This method decomposes the expression matrix into gene programs, each comprising genes whose expression co-varies coordinately across cells, essentially offering a 'guilt-by-association' approach to identifying transcriptional changes (45). GeneNMF was applied to clusters from the scRNA-Seq, regardless of treatment conditions, to allow the algorithm to identify gene programs without prior knowledge of cytokine exposure. We identified which of the output gene programs were associated with IL-1β response and then scored individual cells using such gene lists. The mean module score per cluster, split by treatment after GeneNMF analysis, is presented in Figures 4C (mouse) and 4D (rat). The similarity between the mouse islet and 832/13 rat insulinoma IL-1β-associated gene programs revealed 45 genes in common (Fig 4E). Examples of pathways identified from these shared genes by Gene Ontology are shown in Fig 4F. We conclude that most β-cells exposed to IL-1β respond to the cytokine by coordinately regulating patterns of gene expression across a broad population of cells rather than a specific subset.

  We also calculated the cell-cycle status of each cell (G1, G2/M, S) based on the expression of a subset of human and mouse cell-cycle specific genes provided by Seurat (39, 69). After superimposing the cell-cycle status of each cell in the clustered data, we found that the rat β-cell line and the mouse islet contained cells in all three phases (Supplementary Figure 2A and B). We note that the 832/13 β-cells were relatively enriched for cells in the G1 phase (Supplementary Figure 2B). In contrast, the three cell-cycle phases were more evenly distributed in the mouse islets. This is congruent with the slower proliferation rate of primary cells within the mouse islet (e.g., β-cells which are the predominant cell type within the islets) compared with a proliferating cell line. These patterns did not shift substantially when the 832/13 β-cell line or mouse islets were exposed to IL-1β (Supplementary Figure 2).

  The total number of cells per cluster and the proportion of cells in each cluster originating from untreated and IL-1β treated samples are shown in Supplementary Figure 3, with the total number of cells per cluster for the mouse islet and β-cell-line datasets shown in Supplementary Figure 3A and 3C, respectively. In the β-cell line dataset, 4 clusters were generated with a larger proportion of cells originating from the IL-1β-treated samples (Supplementary Figure 3B). This is expected because the total number of cells collected from IL-1β treated samples (14,866) was larger than for the untreated samples (5637). The larger number of cells arises from technical replicates in the IL-1β exposed group. For the mouse islet data, the control and treated samples had a similar number of cells (2161 and 1951 individual cells, respectively) and consequently the clusters in the integrated data were not systematically enriched for any contributing sample (Supplementary Figure 3D).

  In addition, we compared the common genes that were differentially regulated in both islets and cell-line (FDR ≤0.05) and visualized the agreement in their direction of change (Ctrl vs. IL-1β) via a scatterplot (Supplementary Figure 4). Of the 305 genes that are differentially expressed in both the 832/13 β-cell line and mouse islets, 166 genes were matched in the direction of their fold-changes. Of these coordinately regulated genes, 74 had positive fold changes (red) and 92 had negative fold changes (blue). Thus, the overall transcriptomic responses of the β-cell line and the mouse islet were enriched for similar genes, indicating concordant regulation in response to IL-1β.


### Genomic occupancy of p65 is directed by IL-1β exposure and is associated with recruitment of RNA Polymerase II.
  Many genes induced by IL-1β were expressed across most, if not all, clusters of cells in the scRNA-Seq analysis (Figure 4) and required p65 for their expression (Figure 2; defined by reduced expression with p65 Pdx1-Cre deletion in isolated mouse islets). Therefore, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation coupled with massively parallel sequencing (ChIP-Seq) in the 832/13 β-cell line to identify global p65 DNA occupancy in response to IL-1β. We found that in the untreated state, 2513 binding sites for p65 were detected throughout the genome with most sites identified in distal intergenic regions (Figure 5A). By contrast, after exposure to IL-1β for 30 minutes, 19,656 sites across the genome were occupied by p65 (Figure 5A). The stacked bar graph shows that IL-1β induces a major change in p65 genomic localization, with 5183 peaks in gene promoter regions (representing 26% of the p65 bound sites in response to IL-1β). By contrast, only 255 peaks (10% of the total peaks) were in gene promoter regions in untreated conditions (Figure 5A).

  We next analyzed the distribution of p65 genomic occupancy in the control versus IL-1β condition by plotting average peak profile around regions associated with transcriptional start sites (TSS; Figure 5B). In general, IL-1β exposure promoted p65 localization near genomic regions with defined gene promoter sequences, often near the transcriptional start sites of IL-1β responsive genes. Furthermore, we identified the consensus DNA sequence bound most frequently genome wide by p65 when β-cells were exposed to IL-1β using the de novo calculation method within Hypergeometric Optimization of Motif EnRichment (HOMER) (Figure 5C; shown as sequence logo). This sequence logo format displays the height of each letter, which is proportional to its frequency at that position in the aligned genomic sequences. We compared the p65 binding motif identified by HOMER with the consensus RelA/p65 κB recognition sequence maintained in the JASPAR database (MA0107.1; Figure 5C), which shows a very high similarity between the nucleotides.

  Many genes enriched in our multi-species bulk RNA-Seq analysis (Figure 2D) were also represented in the ChIP-Seq dataset, indicating that they are direct targets of p65. As an example, we identified two distinct κB genomic elements within ChIP-Seq peaks contained within the Stx11 gene promoter. This genomic sequence from the Stx11 gene promoter was then cloned into a luciferase reporter construct and the identified κB sequences subjected to site-directed mutagenesis. We found that IL-1β was unable to promote transcriptional activity in each κB mutant relative to the wildtype promoter sequence (Figure 5D), indicating these κB sites were indeed functional. In addition, we selected three genes identified by ChIP-Seq (i.e., Birc3, Cxcl3, and Stx11) for ChIP – qPCR analyses in response to IL-1β using additional time points (Figures 5E, 5F, and 5G). We found that p65 was present within 15 minutes on κB sites of the Birc3 and Cxcl3 gene promoters, and remained at 60 minutes, after IL-1β exposure. The kinetics of Stx11 was slower, gaining significant p65 occupancy at 30 minutes (Figure 5E). We note that expression of these genes is enhanced in the human islets from donors with T1D, human islets exposed to the cytokines IL-1β + IFNγ for 24 hours, and human islets exposed to IL-1β for 3 hours (Figure 1D).

  Subsequently, we investigated whether Pol II genomic occupancy matched p65 target genes in response to IL-1β using ChIP-Seq by examining differentially bound (DB) peaks in both datasets. We found that in response to IL-1β, p65 occupied many sites in the genome that did not include corresponding increases in RNA Pol II occupancy (Figure 6A). However, during IL-1β exposure, differential binding of RNA Pol II predominately occurred near genomic regions occupied by p65 (Figure 6B). Thus, IL-1β signaling reroutes RNA Pol II to specific genes bound by p65. We next identified Gene Ontology pathways associated with either p65 (Figure 6C) or RNA Pol II genomic occupancy (Figure 6D). We also analyzed the pathways common to p65 and RNA Pol II from the collective ChIP-Seq datasets and found several pathways significantly enriched, including canonical NF-κB signal transduction and cytokine-mediated signaling pathway (Figure 6E). We interpret these overlapping regions, i.e., genomic sites containing concomitant enrichment of p65 and RNA Pol II occupancy, to be sites of active transcription, consistent with the patterns of genes expressed using bulk RNA-Seq.


### p65 overexpression is sufficient to promote gene expression in the absence of IL-1β.
  IL-1β promoted p65 occupancy on specific genomic regions globally identified using ChIP-Seq (Figure 6). In addition to DNA binding, various sites of phosphorylation-induced transcriptional control within the p65 Rel homology domain (RHD) and transactivation domain (TAD) have been reported in other systems (75). Thus, we made a cadre of phospho-acceptor site mutations, representing both RHD and TAD, across p65 to investigate which sites contribute to IL-1β induced transcriptional activity in β-cells. For this purpose, we used a hybrid reporter construct, which has p65 fused to the yeast GAL4 DNA binding domain. Mutations to p65 were made within this fusion protein system, then transfected into rat 832/13 β-cell line alongside the UAS-promoter-luciferase reporter to assess which phospho-acceptor sites are necessary for transactivation capacity in response to IL-1β (Figure 7A; inset). Mutations within the transactivation domain (TAD), which include S529, S535, and S536 did not have any impact on the cytokine-induced transcriptional activity in this transcriptional reporter assay (Figure 7A). In addition, a quad mutant (QM) containing four amino acid substitutions (S529A, S535A, S536A, and S547A) also did not affect IL-1β-induced transcriptional activity. By contrast, phospho-acceptor site mutations within the RHD produced a marked reduction in transcriptional capacity in response to IL-1β (Figure 7A; red bars).

  Based on the transcriptional assay results, we modeled the interaction of p65 with DNA using molecular docking simulations to determine whether changes in DNA binding would help to explain these outcomes. The in silico analyses compared p65 docking to DNA with the known crystal structure of p65 bound to a consensus κB DNA sequence [ref (48) and Figure 7B, 7C, and 7D]. The root mean square deviation (RMSD) values are shown in Figure 7B. RMSD values < 2.0 typically indicate high similarity between predicted structures. We note that our in silico binding analysis the wild-type p65 has very strong agreement with the reported crystal structure [9BDU; ref. (48)]. Moreover, the serine mutations that reduced transcriptional activity (Figure 7A) are not predicted to impair p65 DNA binding (Figures 7B, 7C, and 7D). We interpret these data to indicate that PTMs within p65 enhance transcriptional activity after DNA binding.

  We next used viral transduction to determine whether enhancing p65 abundance in 832/13 cells was sufficient to drive expression of genes in the absence of IL-1β. Using bulk RNA-Seq, we found that p65 overexpression was indeed capable of promoting active gene transcription with both distinct expression patterns as well as genes shared with cells exposed to IL-1β (Figure 7E). A key finding here is that p65 overexpression produced a gene expression profile that included subsets of genes that were also regulated by exposure to IL-1β (shared genes; Figure 7E).

  Finally, we compared the bulk RNA-Seq from cells exposed to either IL-1β (no p65 overexpression) or p65 overexpression (no IL-1β exposure) with genes identified by p65 and RNA Pol II ChIP-Seq (Figure 7F). We found that many genes identified in established human T1D or human islets exposed to cytokines were also represented in this multiway comparison. Such genes include Birc3, Cxcl3, Icam1, and Stx11. We interpret these data to indicate that a subset of genes conserved across species during exposure to IL-1β/p65 activation could be important regulatory points relevant to autoimmune pathways. We further found that overexpression of p65 in β-cells is sufficient to promote the expression of various subsets of genes associated with inflammation in the absence of a cytokine stimulus.


## Discussion
  Type 1 diabetes results from leukocyte-mediated reductions in the function and mass of the pancreatic islet β-cells. While the initial trigger for this disease has not been identified, there is an established complex interaction between a variety of immune cells and the pancreatic islet β–cells (47, 72). During T1D, tissue resident macrophages within the islets increase in both NOD mouse (6) and humans (67). Thus, we envisage that β-cells are being exposed to greater quantities of IL-1β, a macrophage secretory product, at the initial onset of an autoimmune and autoinflammatory response, during progression towards T1D, or all of these events. Because the β-cell is the target of the immune response in T1D, we have investigated the mechanistic determinants underlying IL-1β signaling paradigms in mouse, rat, and human tissue. Herein, we compared gene expression profiles from islets collected from human donors with T1D against isolated human islets exposed to IL-1β, as well as a combination of IL-1β plus IFNγ. This approach revealed similarities between true autoimmune disease observed in patient samples compared with direct cytokine exposure at the gene expression level.

  Using multi-omics methodologies, we identified genes conserved across species that are responsive to IL-1β, contain p65 occupancy at regulatory genomic regions, and show elevated expression in established T1D. Examples include Bcl3, Birc3, Cxcl3, and Stx11. The discovery of similar genetic reprogramming across species identifies plausible candidates influencing autoimmunity. Supporting this principle, genetic deletion of Bcl3 in NOD mice protects against development of autoimmune diabetes (60). The Bcl3 gene was detected in our multi-omics approach and is a genuine p65 target gene in response to IL-1β in β-cells (Figure 1, Figure 2C, Supplementary Figure 5 and data not shown).

  Using bulk RNA-Seq and scRNA-Seq, we found that most β-cells respond to IL-1β to induce gene expression rather than using a subset of cells to drive large gene expression changes. Islet resident macrophages basally produce IL-1β, suggesting physiological role(s) supporting islet β-cell mass, function, or both (16, 34). Corresponding requirements for cell surface-mediated interleukin-1 receptor agonism provides evidence that this pathway regulates appropriate insulin output and is important to maintain the health of cells within the islets (9, 29). However, if IL-1β rises above physiological quantities, or lacks appropriate regulation, we postulate enhanced overactive signaling initiates and/or sustains pathological outcomes (6, 30, 50).

  We propose a stepwise model for progression of autoimmunity that leads to T1D: early cytokine signaling enhances transcriptional activation of genes encoding cytokines/chemokines and antigen processing/presentation components. These events increase secreted proteins, such as chemokines, that promote or exacerbate immune cell infiltration into pancreatic tissue near and within pancreatic islets. This feedforward loop occurs with concomitant elevations in expression of genes within β-cells involved with antigen processing and presentation that ultimately allows for β-cells to assemble antigen presenting proteins (53, 59, 63). We postulate that these steps occur in parallel with interactions occurring among the immune cell milieu present within the pancreatic tissue. This collective combination of events promotes autoimmunity, selective targeting of pancreatic β-cells, and reduced circulating insulin (18, 22, 70).

  Several lines of evidence, including the data herein, are consistent with this premise. Rats exposed to pattern recognition signals and viral infection (25) induce similar patterns of gene expression in β-cells as noted in the present study. Human CD8+ T-cells recognizing pre-proinsulin are present in the healthy pancreas but are found more often near insulin containing islets of autoantibody positive and donors with T1D (2, 8). We interpret these findings to indicate that chemokine-mediated recruitment of immune cells towards insulin-positive cells within the pancreatic islets would occur steadily during the onset of and progression to T1D. In addition, once glucose levels start to rise, hyperglycemia may exacerbate or accelerate the process of autoimmunity (23, 35, 64).

  Our discovery of widespread genomic regions bound by p65 in β-cells, including intronic regions (Figure 5), parallels human observations (54). These transcription factor binding events indicate a distribution of κB response elements throughout the genome with highly specific p65 occupancy in response to IL-1β. We found a correlation between κB consensus sequences identified by ChIP-Seq and significantly upregulated transcripts quantified by bulk RNA-Seq (r = 0.22; p value = 0.008). The κB genomic elements recognized by p65 represent one important component for precise control of gene expression. Other critical factors include post-translational modifications (PTMs) to primary DNA binding factors (e.g., p65) and co-activators/co-repressors within a multi-regulatory transcriptional complex, recruitment of RNA polymerase II to gene promoter regions, and histone chemical modifications (11, 12, 14, 15).

  In addition, we identified amino acids critical for p65 transactivation capacity within the Rel Homology Domain (RHD) using a RelA-Gal4 DNA binding domain fusion protein driving a UAS promoter-luciferase reporter. These experiments demonstrated reduced p65 transcriptional responses in β-cells after exposure to IL-1β (Figure 7A; red bars). By contrast, removing multiple phospho-acceptor site residues in the transactivation domain (TAD) had no effect on p65 transcriptional activity in response to IL-1β (Figure 7A). In silico structural analyses indicated that p65 could bind DNA despite discrete RHD amino acid substitutions. (Figure 7B, 7C, and 7D). We interpret these data to indicate that precise PTMs enhance transcriptional events after p65 binds to DNA. In support of this notion, key amino acids within the RHD are ideally situated for protein-protein interactions to occur in response to post-translational modifications, such as phosphorylation of S276 (77). Furthermore, our structural analysis is supported by functional data showing various genes are readily expressed by p65 overexpression in the absence of IL-1β (Figures 7E and 7F). Genes not expressed by p65 overexpression, yet responsive to IL-1β exposure, may require specific regulatory events (e.g., cytokine-mediated PTMs, chromatin opening, etc.) in response to cytokine signaling to promote transcription.

  An additional interpretation for increases in gene expression of IL-1β responsive genes, which are not direct targets of p65, is regulation by mRNA stability rather than direct transcriptional control. One example from the present study is the Sgk1 gene, which does not contain p65 peaks within the gene promoter despite mRNA accumulation in response to IL-1β (Supplementary Figure 5 and data not shown). This highlights a key limitation of traditional RNA-Seq approaches, i.e., they cannot distinguish de novo transcription from increased mRNA stability. Thus, we have used p65 and RNA Pol II genomic co-occupancy in comparison with bulk RNA-Seq to identify genes likely to be responsive at the transcriptional level. Our data is consistent with the notion that p65 DNA binding is a substantial component linking transcriptional machinery with enhanced expression of genes associated with both IL-1β signaling and established autoimmunity.

  Using multi-omics approaches, we identified conserved genes across species that respond to IL-1β and are elevated in verified T1D. Several key findings emerged from this work: 1) Similar, but not exact, patterns of gene expression exist in islets from humans with T1D when compared with human islets exposed to IL-1β. 2) Mouse islets and the rat derived β-cell line 832/13 respond to IL-1β with comparable gene expression patterns with those observed in cytokine-exposed human islets. 3) p65 overexpression in the absence of any cytokine exposure drives gene expression in β-cells and identifies subsets of genes regulated by IL-1β exposure. 4) RNA polymerase II is recruited near genomic sites occupied by p65 after acute exposure to IL-1β, which corresponds to increased mRNA accumulation of genes containing κB genomic response elements. 5) Molecular docking simulations reveal that p65 binds to distinct κB DNA sequences with similar structural properties even when key amino acid substitutions within the Rel homology domain that reduce transcriptional capacity are present. Finally, NF-κB activation could synergize with Type I and Type II interferon signaling (11, 44, 50), ultimately exacerbating autoimmune events that promote losses in mass and function of the insulin producing cells. Thus, overactive NF-κB signaling is likely to be a critical contributor to events leading to the onset and progression of autoimmunity that is relevant to onset and progression of T1D.


## Materials and Methods

### Cell Culture and Islet Isolation.
  Establishment and culture of the rat 832/13 β-cell line has been previously described (42). Cytokine exposure was performed in serum-free RPMI 1640 using recombinant rat IL-1β (PeproTech 400–01B). Recombinant adenovirus expressing p65 and control virus expressing E. coli LacZ have been described (12, 58). Male mice were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory. RelA/p65 floxed (Jax#: 024342) and and Pdx1-Cre (Jax#: 014647) were crossed to generate mice with homozygous deletion of RelA in Pdx1–1 expressing cells. Mice at twelve weeks of age were used for islet isolation. Islets were isolated from three mice per genotype for analysis. All animals were multihoused with a 12-h/12-hlight/dark cycle and were allowed to acclimate to the facility for a minimum of one week before beginning experimentation. All animals had ad libitum access to food and drink prior to isolation of islets. The protocol for pancreas perfusion and isolation of islets has been previously described (13). Human islets were obtained from Lonza (Clonetics Fresh Human Pancreatic Islets) with donor information detailed previously (21). All animal procedures were approved the Pennington Biomedical Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.


### Plasmids and site-directed mutagenesis.
  Putative binding sites for p65 in the Stx11 proximal gene promoter were identified with JASPAR (61). Promoter luciferase constructs containing κB genomic elements were cloned into pNL1.2 (Promega) using PCR amplification of a 3kb region of genomic DNA upstream of the rat Stx11 transcriptional start site, digestion with FastDigest restriction enzymes (Thermo Fisher), and ligation into the aforementioned vector. Mutations of κB sites were performed using the QuikChange II Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Agilent).


### Bulk RNA-Seq and data analysis.
  RNA content and quality (260/280 ratio range 1.9–2.1) were initially assessed using a NanoDrop 1000 followed by additional RNA quality analysis using an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 with RNA Nano Chip. A RIN of 7 or higher was used for these studies. Library construction used the Lexogen QuantSeq 3’ mRNA-Seq V2 Library Prep Kit FWD with UDI (Catalog # 191.96). Sequencing was carried out on the Illumina NextSeq2000. RNAseq libraries sequenced 75bp forward read and 2 X 12bp dual barcodes for demultiplexing. Reads were aligned to the appropriate genome with STAR (2.7.10b) set to default values (27). Genomes for alignment were rat (rn7, accession GCF_015227675.2), mouse (mm39, accession GCF_000001635.27), and human (hg38, accession GCF_000001405.40). Gene counts were used for differential expression analysis with DESeq2 (49). Gene Ontology analysis was performed with clusterProfiler (74). All enrichment results were generated using the “enrichGO” function to characterize by Biological Process with the “simplify” function within clusterProfiler used to summarize GO:BP results. Predicted regulatory transcription factors were determined using Enrichr (19). Heat maps were generated by plotting gene expression z-scores using Complex Heatmap (36) after normalizing gene expression to control samples. Human islet data (52, 63, 66) have been deposited and can be accessed at the Gene Expression Omnibus using accession identifiers: GSE251911, GSE121863, and GSE124166.


### ChIP-Seq and data analysis.
  The rat 832/13 β-cell line was cultured as described above, split into 15 cm dishes, left untreated or exposed to 1 ng/mL IL-1β for 30 minutes. Lysis and imunoprecipitation of protein-DNA complexes was performed as previously described (52) with minor adjustments for ChIP-seq. Prior to lysis, cells were washed with PBS, crosslinked for 30 min using ChIP Crosslink Gold (Diagenode), washed with PBS again, crosslinked 10 min in 1% formaldehyde in PBS solution, and quenched 5 min with 1.25 M glycine. Cells were scraped and centrifuged to form pellets that were flash frozen in liquid nitrogen. Pellets were thawed on ice then lysed for 30 min in ChIP Lysis Buffer containing 1% SDS. Lysates were sonicated in Bioruptor Pico (Diagenode) for 12 cycles (each 30 sec on, 30 sec off) and then cleared of insoluble material by centrifugation. Proper sonication of DNA into 100–300 bp fragments was confirmed by subjecting a 1 uL sample of lysate to digestion by RNAse A (30 min at 37C) and decrosslinking by proteinase K (1 hr at 65C) before running on an agarose gel. Proteins immunoprecipitated were p65 (Cell Signaling 8242) and RNA Polymerase II (Diagenode C15200004). To perform the immunoprecipitation, antibodies were bound to Protein G Dynabeads (Thermo Fisher); Dynabead-antibody complexes were then blocked in 1% BSA solution before proceeding with the ChIP. Lysates were diluted 1:10 in ChIP Dilution Buffer containing Halt Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Thermo Fisher). Antibody-bound Dynabeads were added to this tube, which was rotated 2 hr at 4C to perform the IP. Beads were then precipitated with a magnetic rack to remove the supernatant; these samples were then washed successively with four ChIP Wash Buffers (Low Salt, High Salt, LiCl, and Tris-EDTA). Samples were then eluted in 100 uL SDS-containing ChIP Elution Buffer, digested in RNAse A (30 min, 37C), decrosslinked in proteinase K (overnight, 65C), and purified with the Qiagen PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen). Recovered DNA was used to build libraries with the NEBNext® Ultra™ II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Catalog #: E7645S). Sequencing was done on the Illumina NextSeq2000 at 2 x 100bp (forward and reverse). Reads from p65 and RNA Pol II ChIP-Seq experiments were aligned to the appropriate genome with STAR (2.7.10b), with the “–alignIntronMax” option set to 0 to prevent splitting DNA reads as introns (27). Peaks were called on alignments using Model-based analysis of ChIP-Seq [MACS3; (76)]. Peak calling on RNA Pol II samples was performed with default settings, while for p65 samples the “--call-summits” option was applied, and the 250 bases on each side of summits were considered peaks to prevent aggregation during downstream analysis. Peak calling was also performed on input samples to determine genomic regions to exclude due to sequencing bias. DiffBind was used to find differentially binding peaks between the NT and IL-1β conditions for both ChIP-seq experiments (62). ChIPseeker was used to annotate peaksets (73). Binding motifs were calculated using Hypergeometric Optimization of Motif EnRichment (HOMER) software (41). Peaks shared between p65 and RNAP datasets were found using the "subsetByOverlap” function in IRanges, allowing for a maximum gap of 5000 bp to qualify as overlapping (46). Gene Ontology enrichment was performed with clusterProfiler using the same parameters as in the RNA-seq analysis (74).


### scRNA-Seq and data analysis.
  The rat 832/13 β-cell line used for scRNA-Seq was grown in 60mm dishes, exposed or not to IL-1β (1 ng/mL) for 3 h in serum free media, then trypsinized to create a single cell suspension. Viability was assessed and found to be >90% prior to library construction using10x Genomics Chromium™ Next GEM Single Cell 3' Kit v3.1. The demultiplexing, barcoded processing, gene counting and aggregation were made using the Cell Ranger software v7.2.0. Single cell RNA-seq data was analyzed in Seurat V5 (39). Single-cell RNA-seq data from rat 832/13 β-cells was new to this study while data from mouse islet cells was from ref. (68). From both data sets, the control and IL-1β-treated samples underwent both individual and comparative analyses using Seurat (v5.3.0). Cells were filtered to retain 500–7,500 detected genes with <10% mitochondrial transcripts. Data were normalized using SCTransform (37), identifying 3000 variable features per dataset. DoubletFinder removed putative doublets using optimized parameters (pN = 0.25, dataset-specific pK, 30 PCs), with expected doublet rates of 6% (55). Expression data from mouse and rat were each integrated into single Seurat objects using RPCA integration. Principal component analysis was performed on the 3000 most variable genes, selecting the first 30 PCs. Clustering was performed by first constructing a k-nearest neighbors graph (k = 20) in the reduced dimensionality space and then applying the Louvain community detection algorithm for efficient clustering. The mouse dataset was subjected to an initial round of clustering at resolution 0.05 to identify the β-cell portion. This cluster was subsetted, split by batch, normalized, and integrated again. Resolution parameters for both datasets were systematically evaluated using the clustree R package (v 0.5.1) across a range of resolutions, with final resolutions of 0.1 (rat) and 0.15 (mouse) being selected. Clustering results were visualized in 2 dimensions via with UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection) function (7). Cell cycle annotation was added using “CellCycleScoring”, with rat and mouse orthologs to the Seurat cell cycle gene lists. Differential gene expression was determined by comparing the treatments with the “FindMarkers” function, setting the min.pct threshold to 0.25 and the logfc.threshold to 0.585. Gene programs were identified with the GeneNMF package (45). Seurat objects were split by cluster regardless of cytokine condition, and individual clusters were analyzed with multiNMF (k = 4:9, nFeatures = 3000). Rat cluster 3 was excluded from gene program identification due to low cell and nFeature_RNA counts. Metaprograms were then identified with these cluster-determined gene programs (nMP = 8, weight.explained = 0.8, max.genes = Inf). The metaprograms which corresponded to IL-1β response were identified by the presence of several known NF-κB response genes, such as Nfkbia and Cxcl10. The gene lists from the cytokine-responsive metaprograms were then used to rate individual cells with Seurat’s AddModuleScore function. The mean module score per cluster and treatment combination were then compared.

### Molecular docking/simulations.
  The p65 sequence (UniProt: Q04206) was modified with mutations for S205A, S276A, and S281A and de novo structures were generated using AlphaFold 3 (1). NF-κB response element 5’-ATCACTGGAATTTCCCAGT-3’ was used as the DNA template to model p65 homodimer binding. Molecular docking was performed using High Ambiguity Driven DOCKing (HADDOCK) v2.4 with the structures generated from AlphaFold (43). The residues residing in the TAD and DBD of p65 were defined as active residues for dimerization and DNA-binding based on the previous solved crystal structure (20). Structures were generated using default parameters. RMSD values were calculated based on distances from the solved crystal structure [PDB 9BDU; (48)].
