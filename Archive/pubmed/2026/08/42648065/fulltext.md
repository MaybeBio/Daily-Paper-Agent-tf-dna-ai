# Predicting transcriptional regulators in plants in the era of artificial intelligence


## Abstract
  Identifying transcriptional regulators that control important biological pathways in plants is fundamental to understanding regulatory mechanisms, network hierarchy, and phenotypic variation. This remains challenging because transcription factors (TFs) and their targets operate within highly interconnected, dynamic, and often redundant regulatory networks. Over the past two decades, advances in omics technologies, sequencing data generation, and computational tools have shifted gene discovery from single-gene studies to network-level investigation. At the same time, these advances have created a new challenge: how to extract biologically meaningful regulatory relationships from increasingly complex and high-dimensional datasets. Recent progress in multi-omics integration, machine learning, deep learning, and emerging foundation-model approaches is beginning to address this challenge and is reshaping how transcriptional regulators, targets, and regulatory relationships are predicted in plants. In this review, we summarize advances in network-enabled gene discovery, discuss how multi-omics and AI are transforming transcriptional target prediction, and consider how these developments may lead to predictive models of plant gene regulation with applications in crop improvement and synthetic biology.


## Introduction
  François Jacob remarked that “the logic of life is regulation” [1], underscoring how central regulatory control is to biological function. In plants, transcriptional regulation coordinates spatiotemporal gene expression programs that underlie development, growth, physiology, and responses to the environment [2]. At the heart of this system are TFs, which control target gene expression by binding specific DNA sequences known as cis-regulatory elements (CREs), typically located in promoters and enhancers, within chromatin environments that further shape regulatory activity [3,4]. Identifying the transcriptional targets of TFs is therefore essential for understanding how plants organize complex gene expression programs and adapt to changing environments. This task is challenging because transcriptional regulation in plants is highly interconnected. Regulatory interactions are embedded in gene regulatory networks (GRNs), in which a single TF may influence hundreds or even thousands of genes while integrating multiple developmental and environmental signals [5]. TFs also regulate other TFs, creating transcriptional cascades, and their activity often depends on cofactors that act cooperatively or antagonistically [6]. As a result, defining TF targets requires more than single-gene approaches; it calls for large-scale datasets that capture quantitative molecular changes across many biological contexts.

  Over the past two decades, advances in high-throughput genomics and transcriptomics have greatly expanded the scale at which transcriptional regulation can be studied [7]. Among these technologies, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) has become the most widely used platform for genome-wide expression profiling [8]. These transcriptomic datasets have enabled the development of network-based methods that infer regulatory relationships from patterns of coexpression and dynamic transcriptional responses across tissues, developmental stages, and environmental conditions [9-12]. Such network-enabled strategies have been especially valuable in plants, including non-model species, because falling sequencing costs, improved crop genome assemblies, and increasingly standardized pipelines have made transcriptome-based discovery broadly accessible. In parallel, genome-wide assays that measure TF–DNA binding and chromatin accessibility have provided additional layers of regulatory evidence that can validate or refine predicted TF–target relationships and strengthen GRN reconstruction [5,13]. Together, these advances have markedly improved our ability to identify TF target genes associated with agronomically important traits.

  More recently, AI and machine learning (ML) have begun to reshape transcriptional target prediction by integrating diverse genomic and regulatory datasets and by learning complex patterns directly from sequence and multi-omics data [14]. These approaches promise greater accuracy and scalability, but they also introduce new analytical challenges. Here, we discuss how network-enabled approaches have advanced gene discovery, how multi-omics integration and AI are changing the field, and how these developments may lead to predictive models of plant gene regulation with applications in crop improvement and synthetic biology.


## Network-enabled gene discovery in plants
  High-throughput transcriptomics shifted plant regulatory biology from single-gene studies toward systems-level approaches for discovering transcriptional relationships (Figure 1). A major strength of network-based analysis is that it helps prioritize candidate regulators within complex biological pathways. By leveraging transcriptomic datasets collected across developmental stages, environmental conditions, or genetic perturbations, researchers can construct transcriptional networks that suggest potential relationships between TFs and their target genes. In these networks, genes are represented as nodes and their expression relationships as edges, enabling the identification of gene modules that are likely to be coregulated and functionally related. We refer readers to recent reviews for broader discussion of transcriptional network approaches in plant science [11,12,15].

  Two types of transcriptional networks have been particularly useful for gene discovery. The first is the gene coexpression network, which is based on the premise that genes participating in the same biological process often show coordinated expression across conditions [16]. In practice, coexpression analysis is highly effective for reducing large candidate lists from thousands of genes to a more manageable set of strongly coexpressed candidates by grouping them into modules. Methods such as weighted gene coexpression network analysis (WGCNA) are widely used because they are relatively robust and straightforward to implement [17]. Their main limitation, however, is that coexpression alone cannot distinguish direct from indirect relationships and therefore does not define regulatory directionality.

  The second approach is GRN inference from gene expression data. Unlike coexpression analysis, GRN inference seeks to identify directional relationships between TFs and their candidate targets. A range of computational approaches has been developed for this purpose, including tree-based ML methods such as GENIE3, probabilistic graphical approaches such as GENIST, and other regression-based or information-theoretic methods [18-21]. More recently, plant-focused integrative tools such as MINI-EX and MINI-AC have extended GRN inference by incorporating single-cell expression, TF motif, and chromatin accessibility information to reconstruct cell-type-specific or accessible chromatin-derived regulatory networks in plants [22,23]. The broader use of these approaches has been facilitated by improved genome assemblies, more comprehensive TF annotations, and the growing availability of high-resolution regulatory datasets. Still, GRN inference remains challenging because large transcriptomic and regulatory datasets often require substantial filtering and integration before biologically meaningful TF–target relationships emerge.

  To bridge coexpression analysis and GRN inference, simplified workflows such as NEEDLE have been developed for TF target prediction in crop species [24]. In NEEDLE, standard RNA-seq analysis is linked to WGCNA-based module detection, after which selected modules are subjected to GRN inference using GENIE3. The resulting TF–target predictions are then evaluated through in silico analyses and experimental validation in planta. More broadly, network-enabled analyses that combine gene expression dynamics with knowledge of TF families and gene function can reveal regulatory hubs controlling development, hormone signaling, and stress responses [9,10,24,25]. These frameworks are particularly valuable in non-model species, where classical genetic resources remain limited, because they generate testable hypotheses from transcriptomic data alone.


## Integrating multi-omics data to predict transcriptional regulators
  Although transcriptome-based networks have yielded important insights, their predictive power is limited when expression data are analyzed in isolation [15,26]. Transcriptional regulation depends on several interacting molecular layers, including TF–DNA binding, chromatin accessibility, CREs, and epigenetic state, all of which influence whether a TF can access and regulate a target gene [6]. For this reason, integrating genomic, proteomic, and epigenomic information has become a key strategy for improving transcriptional target prediction [27-29]. By combining datasets that capture distinct aspects of regulatory activity, multi-omics approaches help separate direct TF–target interactions from downstream transcriptional effects (Figure 1).

  Several technologies have made this possible. DNase I hypersensitive sites sequencing (DNase-seq) and Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin with sequencing (ATAC-seq) identify open chromatin regions where regulatory elements are more likely to be active, narrowing the genomic space in which functional TF binding is likely to occur [30,31]. Chromatin Immunoprecipitation Sequencing (ChIP-seq) [32-34] and DNA Affinity Purification sequencing (DAP-seq) [35-37] provide genome-wide maps of TF–DNA interaction. When these data are integrated with motif analysis and gene expression profiles, they can substantially improve identification of candidate targets that are both transcriptionally responsive and physically associated with specific TFs [9,38,39].

  Single-cell and spatial transcriptomics add another level of resolution [40-43]. These approaches reveal cell-type-specific expression programs and uncover regulatory relationships that may be obscured in bulk tissue data. In plants, integration of high-resolution transcriptomic data with chromatin accessibility, TF binding, or motif information has been used to infer cell-type-specific regulatory networks and track regulatory remodeling during development, hormone signaling, immunity, and environmental stress [41,44,45]. These studies illustrate how multi-omics integration can improve the prediction of context-dependent transcriptional regulators and candidate targets.


## Artificial intelligence in transcriptional target prediction
  AI broadly refers to computational systems designed to perform tasks that typically require human intelligence [46]. In transcriptional target prediction, its main advantage over traditional statistical or network-based approaches is that it can learn complex, nonlinear relationships directly from large-scale omics data rather than relying mainly on predefined rules or simple correlation patterns. ML, a major subset of AI, has been increasingly applied to GRN inference, gene discovery, and candidate prioritization [27,28,47]. Notably, some established GRN inference tools already used in plant biology, such as GENIE3, are themselves ML-based, illustrating that machine learning has been contributing to transcriptional target prediction well before the recent surge of interest in AI.

  These approaches are especially useful when the goal is to connect regulatory patterns to phenotype, as reflected by the sharp rise in publications applying AI to plant gene regulation over the past decade (Figure 2). Cheng et al., for example, used nitrogen-responsive transcriptomic data and XGBoost models trained on evolutionarily conserved nitrogen-response genes shared between Arabidopsis and maize to predict nitrogen use efficiency (NUE) with high accuracy [14]. The ML-prioritized NUE-associated genes were then integrated into time-series GRNs to rank candidate TFs, several of which were subsequently validated experimentally [48]. This kind of workflow shows how AI can strengthen both gene prioritization and downstream regulatory interpretation (Figure 3).

  AI has also been applied to problems that are particularly difficult for conventional methods. Predicting TF binding sites is one example, because motifs are often highly similar among members of the same TF family and TF–DNA interactions are strongly context dependent. Tu et al. addressed this issue by generating protoplast-based ChIP-seq data in maize and training ML models on TF-bound regions to predict TF binding and co-localization genome-wide [32]. In a different application, Moore et al. used Random Forest and Support Vector Machine models to distinguish specialized metabolism genes from general metabolism genes in Arabidopsis [49], reducing a prioritized set of candidates for downstream functional analysis. Such studies illustrate how AI can help identify genes linked to agriculturally, medicinally, or industrially relevant traits.

  Deep learning, a subset of ML that uses multilayered neural networks to learn complex patterns from large datasets [50], is extending this analytical reach even further. In plant regulatory biology, deep learning models have been used to predict transcript abundance and regulatory variation from genomic sequence, and transformer-based approaches have been used to prioritize candidate genes from transcriptomic datasets [51-53]. As a general example from non-plant systems, DeFine, a convolutional neural network (CNN)-based model that learns local sequence features, predicts TF–DNA binding intensity from large-scale ChIP-seq data and uses differences in predicted binding signal between reference and variant sequences to prioritize functional non-coding variants [54]. Similar plant-trained models could provide an additional layer of evidence for identifying regulatory variants that alter TF-binding sites and improving TF target prediction in plants. Recently, an integrated neural network architecture termed the transcriptional activation domain activity (TADA) network was used to predict activation domains in more than 1,500 Arabidopsis TFs, revealing multiple distinct combinations of sequence features associated with transcriptional activation [55].

  Beyond models that predict TF binding or transcript abundance from sequence, emerging AI approaches may broaden the ways transcriptional regulators are identified and prioritized in plants. Structure-prediction tools such as AlphaFold [56] and protein language models such as ESM [57] can help infer functional properties of candidate regulators, including domain architecture, protein–protein interaction potential, and possible TF–DNA or TF–cofactor interfaces. Although these approaches do not directly assign TF targets, they can provide supporting evidence for regulator prioritization and interpretation of regulatory variants. DNA language models offer a more direct route to regulatory prediction. General nucleotide foundation models [58], together with plant-focused models such as AgroNT [59] and PlantCaduceus [60], are beginning to learn regulatory grammar from large genome collections and can be adapted to predict regulatory annotations, promoter activity, tissue-specific expression, and functional non-coding variants. Single-cell foundation models and GPT-style approaches may further help infer cell-type-specific regulatory programs and predict transcriptional responses to genetic or environmental perturbations [61]. In parallel, literature-mining resources such as PlantConnectome [62] illustrate how AI can extract functional relationships from large collections of plant biology literature and help prioritize candidate regulators for GRN reconstruction.

  Despite this progress, several challenges still limit AI-based transcriptional target prediction in plants. Many foundation and deep learning approaches remain more mature in mammalian systems, and recent benchmarks of single-cell perturbation-response models show that they do not always outperform simple baselines [63], underscoring the need for rigorous plant-specific evaluation. In addition, plant regulatory genomics still lacks sufficiently large and well-annotated training datasets, and gene regulation is highly context dependent across tissues, developmental stages, and environmental conditions, complicating model transferability [41]. Even so, as plant single-cell, spatial, perturbation, and multi-omics datasets expand, AI is likely to become increasingly useful for nominating candidate regulators, predicting context-specific TF activity, and reconstructing GRNs across diverse plant species (Figure 3).


## Toward predictive regulatory models of plant transcriptional networks
  The convergence of large-scale multi-omics data and AI-based analysis is moving plant regulatory biology from descriptive network reconstruction toward predictive modeling. Traditional GRN studies have focused mainly on identifying candidate regulatory relationships and generating hypotheses about TF–target interactions [11,12,15]. The next step is to build models that can predict how transcriptional programs respond to genetic perturbation or environmental change at a mechanistic level. That transition is being accelerated by the rapid accumulation of large omics datasets, but it will require frameworks that integrate multiple biological layers while preserving the dynamic and context-dependent nature of transcriptional control.

  Emerging methods are beginning to address this challenge by combining network inference with predictive modeling [44]. ML models trained on genomic sequence, chromatin accessibility, TF binding profiles, and transcriptomic responses can, in principle, predict how regulatory networks respond to TF overexpression, gene knockout, or environmental stress [28,29]. In plants, such perturbation-aware models could help forecast transcriptional responses to drought, heat, salinity, or pathogen challenge, thereby guiding experiments and accelerating the discovery of stress-resilience genes before experimental validation.

  Another promising direction is the development of generative and mechanistic models that aim to capture the causal architecture of GRNs rather than merely describing correlations. Coupled with genome editing tools, including CRISPR-based transient perturbation assays in crops where stable transformation is often difficult, such models could support systematic testing of TF–target relationships and iterative refinement of GRNs. As highlighted by the CropGPT project [64], predictive regulatory frameworks may ultimately support precision breeding and push plant biology toward a more predictive and engineering-oriented discipline.


## Conclusions and future perspectives
  Accurately predicting regulatory genes and modeling their mechanisms from large-scale datasets is becoming central to understanding how plants coordinate development, growth, and responses to changing environments. Over the past two decades, the field has moved from single-gene analysis to systems-level investigation of transcriptional networks. Transcriptome-based network approaches laid the foundation for this shift, multi-omics integration added mechanistic depth, and AI now offers new ways to uncover regulatory patterns that are difficult to capture with conventional approaches.

  The major challenge ahead is to turn increasingly detailed regulatory maps into models that can forecast transcriptional responses to genetic or environmental perturbation. Meeting that challenge will require deeper integration of single-cell and spatial transcriptomics, large-scale perturbation datasets, and AIdriven modeling. As these approaches mature, transcriptional target prediction is likely to move beyond hypothesis generation toward predictive and design-oriented frameworks that support crop improvement, precision breeding, and synthetic biology.
