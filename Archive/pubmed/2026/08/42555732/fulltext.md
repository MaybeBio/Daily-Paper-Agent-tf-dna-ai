# Development of a structurally distinct TopBP1 inhibitor that enhances PARP blockade and reverses osimertinib resistance


## Abstract
  Therapeutic resistance remains a major challenge in cancer treatment, driven by compensatory signaling and stress response pathways that sustain tumor survival. Topoisomerase IIβ-binding protein 1 (TopBP1), a multifunctional scaffold protein with nine BRCT domains, integrates replication stress signaling with oncogenic networks and is frequently overexpressed in aggressive cancers. Its BRCT7/8 domains mediate critical interactions with E2F1, mutant p53, MIZ1, PLK1, and CIP2A, making TopBP1-BRCT7/8 an attractive therapeutic target. Using docking-guided screening and structure-activity relationship–driven optimization, we developed CS18 as a potent and selective BRCT7/8 inhibitor that disrupts oncogenic TopBP1 complexes without interfering with DNA replication. CS18 suppresses MYC transcriptional programs, restores E2F1-mediated apoptosis, and induces mitotic catastrophe. It exhibits broad-spectrum anticancer activity and synergizes with poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors in multiple cancer types and enhances osimertinib sensitivity in EGFR-mutated non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cells. CS18 demonstrates efficacy in patient-derived breast cancer xenografts and overcomes osimertinib resistance in refractory NSCLC in vivo. These findings establish CS18 as a chemically distinct TopBP1 inhibitor with translational potential to overcome therapeutic resistance and advance precision oncology.


## INTRODUCTION
  Therapeutic resistance represents a central barrier to durable cancer control. Although targeted therapies and precision medicine have transformed cancer treatment, most patients eventually relapse due to the activation of compensatory and convergent oncogenic signaling pathways and increased reliance on stress response pathways. These redundant networks allow cancer cells to bypass single-agent inhibition and promote survival and progression despite initial therapeutic success. Therefore, strategies that simultaneously target convergent signaling hubs or exploit pathway interdependencies are critical to achieve durable responses and overcome resistance.

  Topoisomerase IIβ-binding protein 1 (TopBP1) has emerged as a critical hub linking replication stress signaling to multiple oncogenic pathways (1). This multifunctional scaffold protein contains nine BRCT domains that coordinate DNA replication, ATR activation, DNA repair, mitosis, and transcriptional regulation (2). TopBP1 promotes replication by binding Cdk2-phosphorylated Treslin and activates ATR under replication stress (3, 4). TopBP1 overexpression is associated with a shorter survival in many types of cancer, such as breast cancer (5, 6), lung cancer, ovarian cancer, and leukemia (7). TopBP1 overexpression drives tumor progression by suppressing E2F1-mediated apoptosis (8–10), enhancing mutant p53 (mutp53) gain of function (6), bypassing Cdk2 dependency (11), and impairing ATR/Chk1 signaling (12). The C-terminal BRCT7/8 domains of TopBP1 mediate phosphorylation-dependent self-oligomerization (10) and serve as a scaffold for interactions with multiple partners, including mutp53 (6), E2F1 (10), PLK1 (13), MIZ1 (10), PHF8 (14), and BACH1/FANCJ (15). Through these context-dependent interactions, TopBP1 regulates transcriptional programs controlling proliferation, MYC activity, and p21Cip1 repression and plays a critical role in homologous recombination (HR) repair and genome stability. Small-molecule inhibitors specifically targeting the BRCT7/8 domains of TopBP1, such as CalAM (1) and 5D4 (16), demonstrate a proof of concept by restoring E2F1-dependent apoptosis and blocking the activities of mutp53 and MYC without disrupting DNA replication. By blocking the TopBP1-PLK1 interaction, CalAM and 5D4 impair HR, thereby resensitizing cancer cells to poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors (13, 16). Consistently, the synergistic effect can also be found between PARP inhibitor rucaparib and auranofin, a Food and Drug Administration–approved drug for rheumatoid arthritis with the ability to bind TopBP1-BRCT7/8 (17). During mitosis, TopBP1 forms complex with CIP2A (Cancerous Inhibitor of Protein Phosphatase 2A), which serves as a molecular tether to connect fragmented chromosomes (18), recruit repair enzymes (19), and facilitate repairs through microhomology-mediated end joining (MMEJ) and mitotic DNA synthesis (MiDAS) to ensure stable chromosome inheritance under replication stress (20). The CIP2A/TopBP1 complex is critical for preventing mis-segregation of acentric chromosomes in BRCA-mutated cancer cells, making it a promising synthetic lethal target for BRCA-mutated cancers (21). Although CIP2A binds the region between the fifth and sixth BRCT domains of TopBP1 (21–23), the TopBP1-BRCT7/8 inhibitor 5D4 can nevertheless disrupt the interaction between TopBP1 and CIP2A (16), indicating that the BRCT7/8 domains contribute to the stabilization of the CIP2A/TopBP1 complex. Together, these diverse roles position TopBP1-BRCT7/8 as a very promising target for intervention.

  Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations represent a major oncogenic driver in non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), and EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) have substantially improved patient outcomes. Third-generation TKIs such as osimertinib provide durable responses and central nervous system penetration (24), yet acquired resistance inevitably develops through diverse mechanisms, including secondary EGFR mutations [e.g., Cys797→Ser (C797S)], activation of bypass pathways (amplification of MET or HER2 and overexpression of AXL, HER3, or MYC), and histologic transformation (25). MYC activity is elevated in most EGFR-mutated NSCLC tumors that relapse after EGFR-TKI therapy, and its silencing can resensitize osimertinib-resistant tumors to treatment (26). TP53 mutations also promote osimertinib resistance (27). Given that TopBP1-BRCT7/8 inhibitors can suppress MYC activity and mutp53 gain of functions (1, 16), the effects of these inhibitors on enhancing EGFR-targeted therapy and overcoming therapeutic resistance warrant further investigation.

  To broaden structural diversity of TopBP1-BRCT7/8 inhibitors and identify previously undescribed chemotypes beyond calcein and 5D4 derivatives, we used docking-guided screening combined with structure-activity relationship (SAR)–driven synthesis to find and optimize a previously unidentified chemical class of TopBP1 inhibitors. Here, we report the rational design and characterization of CS18, a potent and selective BRCT7/8 inhibitor that dismantles oncogenic TopBP1 complexes, induces mitotic catastrophe, and exhibits broad-spectrum anticancer activity. CS18 synergizes with PARP inhibitors and EGFR inhibitors, overcomes osimertinib resistance, and demonstrates efficacy in patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models, establishing a foundation for translational development.


## RESULTS

### Identification of Cpd 3B6 as a structurally distinct TopBP1-BRCT7/8 inhibitor through molecular docking
  Previously, we conducted a molecular docking screen of ∼200,000 compounds and identified several hundred candidates with strong predicted binding to the TopBP1-BRCT7/8 domains (16). From this pool, we initially prioritized 99 compounds for experimental testing, leading to the identification of Cpd 2H3 and subsequently its highly active derivatives, 5D4 and 5H3 (16). To expand the chemical diversity of potential inhibitors, we further selected 23 additional compounds that are structurally distinct from Cpd 2H3 (table S1), with the goal of identifying alternative classes of active compounds capable of targeting TopBP1-BRCT7/8. Among the 23 compounds tested, Cpd 3B6, a methylpiperazin-coumarin derivative (Fig. 1A), demonstrated notable activity by inducing apoptosis in TOV-112D ovarian cancer cells (fig. S1). Molecular docking analysis revealed that Cpd 3B6, like the other two classes of TopBP1-BRCT7/8 inhibitors, i.e., calcein (1) and Cpd 2H3 (16), contains a multi–benzene ring structure that occupies a subpocket within the BRCT7/8 domain (Fig. 1B and fig. S2). This subpocket corresponds to the binding site for the +4 aromatic residue (Ω, representing W/Y/F) of the conserved S/TxxxΩ motif (fig. S2), suggesting that the polycyclic aromatic moieties of these lead compounds mimic the +4 aromatic amino acid when engaging TopBP1-BRCT7/8. Furthermore, the docking model indicated that, to occupy the binding pocket more effectively, additional side chains could be introduced to Cpd 3B6, which could potentially enhance the stability of its binding to the BRCT7/8 domains (Fig. 1B).


### Hit expansion identifies Cpd 4B4 and 4B5 as more potent TopBP1 inhibitors
  Guided by docking analysis, we performed hit expansion using PubChem and ZINC databases and identified 21 derivatives of Cpd 3B6 (table S2), which have additional side chains to potentially adopt more stable interactions with TopBP1-BRCT7/8 domains. Among these derivatives, Cpds 4B4 and 4B5 consistently exhibited better cytotoxic activities against TOV-112D, MDAH-2774 ovarian cancer cells, and MDA-MB-468 triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cells (fig. S3). Comparison of 4B4 and 4B5 with 3B6 revealed that the addition of the methoxybenzene side chains positioned them similarly to the phosphoserine or phosphothreonine residues of the binding peptides, potentially allowing them to form hydrogen bonds with surrounding amino acids (Fig. 1, A and B). Compounds 4B4 and 4B5 bound to TopBP1-BRCT7/8 and inhibited its interaction with the pS1159-TopBP1 phosphopeptide, with median inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of 6 and 8 nM, respectively (Fig. 1, C and D). Similarly, 4B4 and 4B5 disrupted TopBP1-BRCT7/8 binding to mutant p53 DNA-binding domain (DBD) fragment containing the Arg273→His (R273H) mutation [mutp53R273H(DBD)], with IC50 values of 25 nM and 0.3 μM, respectively (Fig. 1, E and F). Compared with 3B6, 4B4 and 4B5 elicited a more pronounced apoptotic response in both MDA-MB-468 and MDAH-2774 cells, as indicated by the caspase-3/7 activity assay; in contrast, none of them had any effect in nontransformed AML12 mouse hepatocytes (Fig. 1G). The clonogenic survival assay revealed that compounds 4B4 and 4B5 inhibited MDA-MB-468 cell growth with IC50 values ranging from 1 to 2.5 μM (Fig. 1H).


### Synthesis of Cpd 3B6 derivatives
  Guided by molecular docking, next we synthesized a series of Cpd 3B6 derivatives to enable SAR analysis. The reaction sequence was outlined in Fig. 2A. The target compounds were prepared via a two-step synthetic route. In the first step, a Pechmann condensation between substituted phenols (1) and ethyl 4-chloroacetoacetate (2) yielded substituted 4-chloromethylcoumarins (3). Subsequently, 3 was coupled with the commercially available substituted piperazines and piperidines (4) in the presence of N,N′-dimethylformamide (DMF) at room temperature to generate the desired 3B6 derivatives. Details of the preparation and spectroscopic data of the target compounds are provided in the Materials and Methods section and in the Supplementary Materials.


### Lead compound B21 exhibits the expected characteristics of TopBP1-BRCT7/8 inhibitors
  In the initial round of synthesis, we generated seven derivatives of Cpd 3B6 (table S3). Among these compounds, B12, B21, and B28 (Fig. 2B) were able to induce apoptosis in MDAH-2774 and TOV-112D ovarian cancer cells (Fig. 2, C and D, and fig. S4). Notably, Cpd B21 exhibited the strongest activity compared to B12, B28 or the parent Cpd 3B6 (Fig. 2, C and D). Previously, we demonstrated that a small-molecule inhibitor, 5D4, targeted TopBP1-BRCT7/8 and synergized with PARP inhibitors to block cancer cell proliferation (16). Consistently, B21 exhibited synergistic activity with the PARP inhibitor rucaparib in MDAH-2774 cells and cisplatin-resistant A2780cis ovarian cancer cells, with a combination index (CI) of <1 (Fig. 2, E to G). Furthermore, B21 suppressed the expression of MYC and its downstream target gene, Rad51, while inducing p21Cip1 expression and activated caspase-8 (16) in the NCI-H1975 (hereafter referred to as H1975) NSCLC cells harboring the EGFR mutant [Leu858→Arg (L858R)/Thr790→Met (T790M)] (Fig. 2H).


### SAR study identifies CS18 as the most potent TopBP1 inhibitor among all the 3B6 derivatives
  Given the promising anticancer activity of B21, we then synthesized and characterized additional 51 derivatives of Cpd 3B6 for SAR analysis (Supplementary Materials/Spectroscopic Data). We developed thermal shift assay (TSA) to examine direct binding between the TopBP1-BRCT7/8 domains and the compounds. Of the synthesized derivatives, 15 compounds exhibited notable thermal stabilization of TopBP1-BRCT7/8 domains, as indicated by a melting temperature shift (ΔTm ≥ 2°C; table S4). All these 15 compounds induced apoptosis in MDAH-2774 ovarian cancer cells; in contrast, only 6 of 36 compounds that did not show notable ΔTm shifts were able to induce apoptosis (table S4). Statistical analysis with the chi-square test (χ2 = 30.36, P = 3.59 × 10−8) confirmed a strong correlation between thermal stabilization and apoptotic activity. Notably, compounds BS13 and CS18 induced the largest ΔTm shifts by increasing 10°C of the melting temperature, suggesting a strong binding to the TopBP1-BRCT7/8 domains.

  The SAR analysis of five pairs of compounds showed that a benzene group in the R2 position is critical for the binding (Fig. 3, A and B). Several side chains in the R3 position of the active compounds include methyl (CH3), methoxybenzene, ethyl (CH2CH3), and phenol. Among them, the phenol side chain resulted in the greatest ΔTm (10°C for both BS13 and CS18). Molecular docking of CS18 indicates that it fits well inside the phosphopeptide-binding pocket (Fig. 3C) and forms hydrogen bonds with residues S1273, S1274, R1314, S1406, R1407, and G1410 in a manner mimicking the natural ligands (Fig. 3D). S1273 and S1274 are known to form hydrogen bonds with phosphoserine or phosphothreonine, whereas R1314, R1407, and G1410 define the sides of the +3/+4 binding pocket (28).

  We next performed the in vitro binding assay and found that CS18 could compete with pS1159 peptide for the binding to TopBP1-BRCT7/8 at IC50 = 2.47 to 3.8 nM (Fig. 3E). The TSA showed that CS18 failed to bind Ser1273→Ala (S1273A) and Lys1317→Met (K1317M) TopBP1-BRCT7/8 mutants (Fig. 3F), which are defective in phosphoserine binding (29), confirming the specificity of CS18 binding to TopBP1-BRCT7/8 directly. Among these 3B6 derivatives, CS18 exhibited the most potent cytotoxic activity against a panel of cancer cell lines, including ovarian cancer cells (MDAH-2774 and A2780cis), TNBC (MDA-MB-468 and BRCA1-mutated HCC1937), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD; A549 and EGFR-mutated H1975), and lung squamous cell carcinoma [LUSC; NCI-H2170 (MYC-amplified, hereafter referred to as H2170), HCC95 (Hamon Cancer Center 95), and HCC2814 (Hamon Cancer Center 2814)] (Fig. 3G and fig. S5). Similar to 3B6, 4B4, and 4B5, CS18 did not exhibit cytotoxicity toward normal bronchial epithelial cells (NL20 and BEAS-2B) or nontransformed AML12 mouse hepatocytes (Fig. 3G).

  Comparing the compound pairs E13 versus BS13 and D13 versus CS18, the only structural difference lies in the R2 side chain, where a benzene ring is replaced by either a hydroxyl (─OH) group or a hydrogen atom (Fig. 3A). Despite this subtle modification, both E13 and D13 completely lost in vitro TopBP1-BRCT7/8 binding activity (Fig. 3H), indicating that a benzene ring at the R2 position is essential for compound binding. Consistently, E13 and D13 also lacked the cytotoxic activity against MDAH-2774 cells (Fig. 3I). Together, these concordant biochemical and cellular data provide strong evidence that the TopBP1 BRCT7/8 domains are the endogenous targets of BS13 and CS18. BS13 is structurally similar to CS18 except that a hydroxyl group on the R1 side chain is substituted by a hydrogen atom. Although BS13 retained notable activity, it was less potent than CS18 in MDAH-2774 and MDA-MB-468 cells (Fig. 3, I to K), suggesting that an OH group at the R1 position is preferable for optimal activity. To sum up, the biological activity of these 3B6 derivatives correlates with the altered binding to TopBP1-BRCT7/8, supporting the conclusion that the activity of CS18 is mediated through direct binding to TopBP1-BRCT7/8. Therefore, we focused subsequent studies on the characterization of CS18. The synthesis of CS18, along with its high-resolution electron impact–mass spectrometry (HREI-MS) and one-dimensional and two-dimensional nuclear magnetic resonance (1D/2D NMR) spectra, is provided in the Supplementary Materials.


### CS18 selectively disrupts TopBP1 interactions with E2F1, mutp53, MIZ1, PLK1, and CIP2A
  TopBP1 interacts with several proteins such as E2F1 (8), mutp53 (6), MIZ1 (30), and PLK1 (13) in a BRCT7/8-dependent manner, whereas other interactions, such as those with RPA2 and Rad9, are mediated through BRCT1/2 domains (2, 31). We previously showed that compound 5D4 also inhibits the TopBP1-CIP2A interaction, suggesting a role for BRCT7/8 domains in the association of TopBP1 with CIP2A as well. To determine whether CS18 specifically targets BRCT7/8-mediated interactions, we examined its effects in MDAH-2774 and MDA-MB-468 cells. CS18 inhibited TopBP1 binding to E2F1, mutp53, MIZ1, CIP2A, and PLK1 but did not affect its interaction with RPA2 or Rad9 (Fig. 4, A and B), indicating a selective disruption of BRCT7/8-dependent complex formation by CS18.


### CS18 treatment promotes MIZ1 binding to the p21Cip1 promoter and inhibits the expression of MYC target genes
  TopBP1 interacts with MIZ1 through its BRCT7/8 domains and suppresses MIZ1’s activity in p21Cip1 transactivation (10, 30). Therefore, inhibitors that target TopBP1-BRCT7/8, such as 5D4 and CalAM, can disrupt this interaction, thereby enhancing MIZ1’s transcriptional activity (1, 16). Likewise, we found that CS18 could release MIZ1 from TopBP1 binding (Fig. 4). Moreover, chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays showed that CS18 treatment substantially increased MIZ1 binding to the p21Cip1 promoter in MDA-MB-468, MDAH-2774, and two MYC-amplified lung cancer cell lines, HCC95 and H2170 (Fig. 5, A to D). Consistently, similar to 5D4 and CalAM, CS18 treatment induced p21Cip1 expression in MDA-MB-468, MDAH-2774, and H1975 cells (Fig. 5E). CS18 treatment also inhibited the expression of MYC target genes, such as Cyclin D1, Rad51, and ACTL6A, and induced apoptosis in MDAH-2774, MDA-MB-468, H2170, H1975, and A2780cis cells (Fig. 5, F to J). Similar to B21, CS18 also decreased MYC expression in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 5, F to I). In cancer cells, MYC promotes enhancer activity that contributes to its sustained overexpression through an indirect feed-forward loop, particularly in the context of super-enhancers or oncogenic transformation (32). This enhancer-driven self-reinforcement contributes to the phenomenon of MYC addiction observed in many cancers (33). Consequently, strong inhibition of MYC activity may lead to a reduction in its own expression.


### CS18 exhibits potent on-target inhibitory activity in cell growth across a broad spectrum of cancer cell lines
  We next performed clonogenic cell survival assays to evaluate the effect of CS18 on cell growth in multiple cancer models. CS18 demonstrated strong inhibitory activity in MDA-MB-468 TNBC cells (IC50 = 0.15 μM), MDAH-2774 ovarian cancer cells (IC50 = 0.18 μM), and cisplatin-resistant A2780cis ovarian cancer cells (IC50 = 0.46 μM) (Fig. 6, A to C). CS18 also markedly decreased cell proliferation in lung cancer cell lines, including A549 (IC50 = 0.5 μM; Fig. 6D), HCC95 (IC50 = 0.54 μM; Fig. 6E), HCC2814 (IC50 = 0.67 μM; Fig. 6F), and EGFR-mutated H1975 cells (IC50 = 0.32 μM; Fig. 6G). The Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay also showed that CS18 greatly decreased cell viability against Kasumi-1 acute myelogenous leukemia cells, which were derived from a patient with t(8;21) translocation and p53 (R248Q) mutation (IC50 = 0.46 μM) (Fig. 6H). Consistently, treatment with CS18 substantially inhibited the formation of 3D-cultured patient-derived xenograft organoids (PDxOs), which were established from the BCM2665 TNBC PDX model (34) (Fig. 6I), and reduced overall cell viability (Fig. 6J).

  To further confirm that TopBP1 is a direct target of CS18, we performed a cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) to assess the ability of CS18 to bind endogenous TopBP1 in MDAH-2774 cells. The result revealed that CS18 increased the melting temperature (Tm) of TopBP1, indicating direct target engagement within cells (Fig. 7A). In addition, TopBP1 depletion modestly increased basal caspase-3/7 activity but significantly attenuated CS18-induced caspase-3/7 activation in both MDA-MB-468 (Fig. 7B) and MDAH-2774 cells (Fig. 7C). Consistently, depletion of TopBP1 decreased the levels of active caspase-8 and cleaved PARP1 induced by CS18 treatment (Fig. 7, D and E). Moreover, TopBP1 depletion reduced the expression of c-Myc and its target genes, such as Rad51, Cyclin D1, and ACTL6A, and mitigated the effect of CS18 (Fig. 7, D and E, and fig. S6).

  Because the S1273A and K1317M TopBP1 mutants failed to bind CS18 (Fig. 3F), next we transiently expressed wild-type TopBP1 or these mutants in TopBP1-depleted H1299 cells that stably express mutp53(R273H) and then performed cell viability assay to assess their response to CS18. Only reconstitution with wild-type TopBP1, but not the S1273A or K1317M TopBP1 mutant, could restore the sensitivity to CS18 in TopBP1-depleted cells (Fig. 7F). Together with the chemical biology analyses (Fig. 3, H to J), these complementary genetic and biochemical approaches provide strong orthogonal evidence that the cytotoxic activity of CS18 is mediated through on-target inhibition of the TopBP1 BRCT7/8 domains.

  Collectively, these results demonstrate that CS18 directly targets TopBP1 and exerts potent cytotoxic effects across a broad range of established cancer cell lines, including TNBC, ovarian cancer, lung cancer, and acute myelogenous leukemia, as well as a clinically relevant ex vivo PDxO model.


### CS18 synergizes with PARP inhibitors and osimertinib
  CS18 disrupts the interaction between TopBP1 and PLK1 (Fig. 4), a key event in the regulation of HR repair (13), raising the possibility of synergism between CS18 and PARP inhibitors. To evaluate this potential, we performed CI analysis. The results demonstrated that combined treatment of CS18 with a PARP inhibitor, such as talazoparib, rucaparib, or veliparib, further promoted caspase-3/7 activity, with CI values less than 1 in both MDA-MB-468 and MDAH-2774 cells (Fig. 8, A to D). The synergistic effect of CS18 and talazoparib in growth inhibition of MDAH-2774 cells was also confirmed using the CCK-8 cell viability assay (Fig. 8E).

  In addition to the double-mutated EGFR (L858R/T790M), H1975 cells harbor oncogenic alterations including mutp53(R273H), mutant PIK3CA Gly118→Asp (G118D), and elevated MYC expression. These pathways can be targeted by CS18, independently of EGFR signaling. To evaluate potential synergy, we assessed the combination effect of CS18 and osimertinib in H1975 cells. CS18 and osimertinib exhibited strong synergistic activity in cell viability assay (Fig. 8F). The synergistic effect was also validated in another EGFR-mutated NSCLC PC-9 cell line, which carries the EGFR exon 19 deletion (ΔE746–A750) (Fig. 8G).


### CS18 treatment overcomes osimertinib resistance in H1975 cells
  MYC expression is elevated in most of the osimertinib-resistant lung cancers, and MYC knockdown has been shown to restore sensitivity to osimertinib (26). To investigate whether CS18 can target MYC and overcome osimertinib resistance in refractory NSCLC, we generated osimertinib-resistant H1975 (H1975-OsiR) cells by gradually increasing osimertinib concentrations more than 3 months and confirmed the acquired resistance using the CCK-8 cell viability assay (Fig. 8H). We collected H1975 cells at different stages during the development of osimertinib resistance and assessed the expression of EGFR, TopBP1, and MYC target genes. We found that the levels of EGFR and TopBP1 were progressively increased along with the induction of MYC-regulated genes, such as Rad51 and Cyclin D1 (Fig. 8I). Notably, CS18 treatment effectively overcame osimertinib resistance and demonstrated strong synergy with osimertinib in H1975-OsiR cells (Fig. 8J). Consistently, depletion of TopBP1 in H1975-OsiR cells was able to enhance sensitivity to osimertinib (Fig. 8K), supporting a role for TopBP1 overexpression in osimertinib resistance.

  The synergy between CS18 and osimertinib was further validated by clonogenic viability assays in both parental and resistant H1975 cells (Fig. 8L). Western blot analysis revealed elevated MYC and TopBP1 expression in H1975-OsiR cells. Combined treatment with CS18 and osimertinib synergistically suppressed the expression of MYC and its targets (Rad51 and Cyclin D1) while enhancing PARP1 cleavage and activating caspase-8 in both cell lines (Fig. 8M).


### CS18 arrests cancer cells in prometaphase, thereby blocking progression to metaphase and triggering apoptotic cell death
  In addition to its functions during interphase, TopBP1 also plays important roles in mitosis (35) by interacting with several key proteins, including PLK1, CIP2A, and Top2A (21–23, 36, 37). During mitosis, the TopBP1-CIP2A complex prevents chromatid breakage caused by incomplete DNA replication and reduces acentric chromosome mis-segregation, protecting against micronucleation and cell death (21). Through the BRCT7/8 domains, TopBP1 binds to Top2A, which is required for the recruitment of Top2A to ultrafine anaphase bridges (UFBs) during mitosis (37). Therefore, disruption of TopBP1 complex formation with CIP2A, PLK1, or Top2A by TopBP1-BRCT7/8 inhibitors may activate the spindle assembly checkpoint, thereby arresting cells in prometaphase. To investigate the effect of TopBP1-BRCT7/8 inhibitors on mitotic progression, MDAH-2774 cells were synchronized at the G2-M phase border by a CDK1 inhibitor RO-3306, followed by the treatment with TopBP1-BRCT7/8 inhibitors for an hour. After washing twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove RO-3306, cells were allowed to enter mitosis in the presence or absence of TopBP1-BRCT7/8 inhibitors for 45, 90, 120, or 210 min. The interaction between TopBP1 and either CIP2A, PLK1, mutp53, or Top2A, but not SLX4 [mediated by TopBP1-BRCT5 (38)], was disrupted after 45-min treatment with TopBP1-BRCT7/8 inhibitors (Fig. 9A). These TopBP1-BRCT7/8 inhibitors, particularly CS18 and CalAM, induced a pronounced prometaphase arrest, preventing cell progression into metaphase (Fig. 9, B and C). By 3.5-hour treatment with TopBP1-BRCT7/8 inhibitors, cells underwent apoptosis, as indicated by the activation of caspase-3/7 and cleaved caspase-8 (Fig. 9D). Thus, CS18 halts cancer cells in prometaphase, preventing their transition to metaphase and inducing apoptotic cell death.


### CS18 inhibits the growth of breast cancer PDXs
  We next implanted the BCM2665 TNBC PDXs into NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ (NSG) mice. This PDX model harbors a mutant p53 Arg249→Ser (R249S) allele, four copies of MYC, and wild-type BRCA1/2 (16). Treatment with CS18 (40 mg/kg, twice weekly) significantly suppressed tumor growth (Fig. 10A). The mice tolerated CS18 well, with no significant changes in body weight (Fig. 10B) or observable side effects. Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis of BCM2665 tumors revealed that CS18 induced apoptosis, as evidenced by increased PARP1 cleavage (Fig. 10C). In contrast, CS18 treatment neither induced apoptosis nor altered Ki-67 staining in the intestinal crypts (Fig. 10, C and D), suggesting that CS18 exerts a specific anticancer effect. To assess target engagement, we performed CETSA on xenograft tumor tissues. Consistent with the results done in cultured cells (Fig. 7A), CS18 increased the thermal stability (Tm) of TopBP1 protein, supporting its binding to TopBP1 directly in vivo (Fig. 10E).

  We next evaluated the dose-response by administrating CS18 over a range of 15 to 60 mg/kg three times weekly (Fig. 10, F and G). The results showed a dose-dependent in vivo antitumor activity. Mice tolerated doses up to 60 mg/kg well (Fig. 10H), and no plateau in efficacy was observed within this range, suggesting that higher doses may yield additional therapeutic benefit.


### Synergy between CS18 and osimertinib in H1975-OsiR xenograft models
  To assess the impact of CS18 on osimertinib resistance in vivo, parental H1975 or H1975-OsiR cells were injected into the flanks of NSG mice, followed by treatment with osimertinib, CS18, or their combination. To evaluate potential synergy with osimertinib, a suboptimal intermediate dose of CS18 (30 mg/kg, three times weekly) was selected. Osimertinib effectively suppressed the growth of parental H1975 xenografts without affecting mouse body weight (Fig. 10, I to K) but had minimal activity in H1975-OsiR tumors (Fig. 10, L and M). Treatment with CS18 significantly reduced the growth of H1975-OsiR tumors, and when combined with osimertinib, they elicited even more robust antitumor responses (Fig. 10, L and M). The combination treatment was well tolerated, with no significant changes in body weight or overt signs of toxicity (Fig. 10N). Neither single-agent nor combination treatment altered Ki-67 staining or triggered apoptosis in mouse intestinal crypts (Fig. 10O).


## DISCUSSION
  In this study, we describe the development and characterization of CS18, a structurally unique and highly potent inhibitor that specifically targets the TopBP1-BRCT7/8 domains. Through an integrated approach combining molecular docking, SAR-guided synthesis, biophysical TSA, and functional assays, we identified CS18 as a lead compound exhibiting high specificity and potent efficacy in disrupting TopBP1-mediated oncogenic interactions. Biochemical assays confirmed strong binding affinity and selective inhibition of BRCT7/8-dependent interactions with E2F1, mutp53, MIZ1, PLK1, CIP2A, and Top2A, highlighting its ability to disrupt critical signaling hubs that sustain tumor growth.

  The TSA revealed that CS18 failed to bind the S1273A and K1317M TopBP1-BRCT7/8 mutants (Fig. 3F), which are defective in phosphoserine recognition, indicating engagement of the phosphoserine-binding interface rather than nonspecific contacts. Accordingly, these two mutants also failed to restore CS18 sensitivity when transiently expressed in TopBP1-depleted cancer cells (Fig. 7F). Likewise, structurally related CS18 analogs that do not engage TopBP1 BRCT7/8 also lacked the cytotoxic activity, supporting an on-target specificity of CS18 (Fig. 3, H and I). Consistent with this finding, CETSA showed robust thermal stabilization of endogenous TopBP1 upon CS18 treatment in cultured cells (Fig. 7A), confirming target engagement in a cellular context. Collectively, these findings provide compelling orthogonal evidence that TopBP1 BRCT7/8 is the endogenous target of CS18. These observations were corroborated in vivo using xenograft tumor samples, where CETSA revealed increased thermal stabilization of TopBP1 following systemic CS18 administration, confirming effective target engagement under therapeutic conditions (Fig. 10E). In contrast, CS18 did not disrupt the BRCT1/2-dependent interactions of TopBP1 with Rad9 or RPA2 nor the BRCT5-dependent interaction with SLX4, indicating selective targeting of the BRCT7/8 domains while sparing other BRCT-mediated interfaces (Figs. 4M and 9A). Together, these findings define a precise mechanism of action centered on TopBP1-BRCT7/8 and support a favorable therapeutic window by preserving other BRCT-dependent protein-protein interactions essential for DNA replication while disrupting BRCT7/8-dependent oncogenic signaling.

  Mechanistically, CS18 inhibits multiple oncogenic pathways by targeting the BRCT7/8 domains of TopBP1. Disruption of TopBP1 interactions with E2F1 and mutp53 relieves suppression of E2F1-mediated apoptotic signaling and attenuates mutp53 gain-of-function activities. In addition, by promoting MIZ1 recruitment to the p21Cip1 promoter and suppressing transcription of MYC target genes, CS18 inhibits critical transcriptional networks involved in cellular proliferation and survival. CS18 also interferes with BRCT7/8-dependent TopBP1 interactions required for mitotic fidelity, including those with CIP2A, PLK1, and Top2A, while sparing BRCT5-mediated binding to SLX4. Disruption of the TopBP1-Top2A interaction is expected to impair Top2A recruitment to ultrafine anaphase bridges (37), promoting persistence of DNA catenanes and chromosome segregation defects, whereas loss of the CIP2A-TopBP1 complex may compromise chromosome tethering under mitotic stress (23). In parallel, interference with the TopBP1-PLK1 interaction is predicted to impair PLK1-BLM–dependent resolution of recombination intermediates before mitotic entry, resulting in persistent DNA linkages (39). The combined disruption of these BRCT7/8-dependent processes provides a mechanistic basis for the observed prometaphase arrest, chromosome congression failure, spindle abnormalities, and mitotic catastrophe, particularly in highly proliferative tumor cells. Collectively, these activities underlie the broad antiproliferative effects of CS18 observed across multiple cancer models, including TNBC, ovarian cancer, NSCLC, and acute myeloid leukemia, while sparing normal epithelial cells and hepatocytes, which underscores its therapeutic selectivity.

  Compared to normal tissues, many types of cancer express higher levels of TopBP1 protein (fig. S7). However, the observed selectivity is unlikely to be explained solely by differences in TopBP1 expression. Our prior studies support a model in which cancer cells exhibit functional dependence on BRCT7/8-mediated TopBP1 signaling. Oncogenic deregulation of the pRb pathway leads to aberrant E2F1 activation in many cancers, thereby increasing reliance on TopBP1 to restrain E2F1-driven apoptotic signaling (9, 10). In addition, BRCT7/8-dependent TopBP1 interactions contribute to mutp53 gain of function and MYC-driven transcriptional programs that further promote tumor cell survival (6, 10, 16). Collectively, these findings suggest that cancer cells depend on these regulatory interactions to sustain survival and proliferation under oncogenic stress, rendering them selectively vulnerable to BRCT7/8 disruption by CS18.

  Although CIP2A binds to a conserved region in TopBP1 located between its fifth and sixth BRCT domains (BRCT5/6) (21–23), all three TopBP1 inhibitors, including CalAM, 5D4, and CS18, can inhibit the interaction between TopBP1 and CIP2A (Figs. 4 and 9A) (16). These results suggest that either BRCT7/8 domains are also involved in the association with CIP2A or CS18, CalAM, and 5D4 can serve as allosteric inhibitors and cause a conformational change of TopBP1, thereby hindering its binding to CIP2A.

  In addition to synergizing with PARP inhibitors to inhibit cancer cell growth, CS18 demonstrated robust synergy with osimertinib, thus overcoming osimertinib resistance and enhancing therapeutic efficacy in EGFR-mutated NSCLC xenograft models. These findings suggest that TopBP1 inhibition disrupts compensatory pathways that enable EGFR-mutated tumors to bypass osimertinib inhibition, providing a strong rationale for clinical evaluation in combination strategies. The resistance to EGFR inhibitors remains a highly heterogeneous process, which is driven by alternative signaling convergence on oncogenic hubs such as MYC, co-occurring TP53 mutations, bypass pathways including MET amplification or AXL activation, and adaptive survival mechanisms. This complexity underscores the need to identify targets for the disruption of these convergent networks. Our study highlights TopBP1-BRCT7/8, a key regulator of MYC and mutp53, as a rational therapeutic target to reverse osimertinib resistance. Notably, TopBP1 expression is up-regulated during the acquisition of osimertinib resistance in EGFR-mutated NSCLC cells (Fig. 8I). Depletion of TopBP1 enhances osimertinib sensitivity (Fig. 8K), supporting a role for TopBP1 overexpression in the development of osimertinib resistance.

  Last, in vivo studies using TNBC PDX and H1975-OsiR xenografts confirmed CS18’s antitumor activity and target engagement, with minimal toxicity to normal tissues. Collectively, these results position CS18 as a promising candidate for further preclinical development and clinical translation. Although current in vitro and in vivo data support a favorable therapeutic window, more comprehensive pharmacologic and toxicity studies will be required to rigorously define its safety profile and therapeutic index. Future studies will focus on optimizing its pharmacokinetic properties, evaluating its efficacy in combination therapies, and exploring its impact on tumor microenvironment and immune modulation.


## MATERIALS AND METHODS

### Cell culture and transfection
  MDA-MB-468, HCC1937, MDAH-2774, A2780cis, TOV-112D, NCI-H1975, PC-9, human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293T, and AML12 (alpha mouse liver 12) mouse hepatocytes were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). A549, H2170, HCC95, HCC2814, NL20, and BEAS-2B were maintained in RPMI with 10% FBS. Kasumi-1 cells were maintained in RPMI with 20% FBS. The A2780cis cell line (40) [European Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (ECACC), catalog no. 93112517] was purchased from Public Health England (PHE) Culture Collections through Sigma-Aldrich. HCC95 and HCC2814 cell lines were purchased from UT Southwestern. H1975 and PC-9 were purchased from Cytion. The H1975-OsiR cell line was established by exposing cells to gradually escalating doses of osimertinib for more than 3 months. The resistant cell lines were maintained in regular growth medium containing 4 μM osimertinib. The nontumorigenic AML12 hepatocyte cell line and all other cancer cell lines were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). All cell lines were tested negative for mycoplasma contamination. The pLKO.1-Scrambled vector was purchased from Addgene (plasmid #1864). The pLKO-shTopBP1 expression vectors were purchased from RNAi Consortium. The lentivirus harboring TopBP1 short hairpin RNA (shRNA) or scrambled shRNA was produced in Lenti-X HEK293T (Takara), followed by the transduction in MDA-MB-468, MDAH-2774, H1975-OsiR, or H1299 stably expressing mutp53(R273H). After selection with puromycin (3 μg/ml) for a week, stable cell lines were established for further experiments.


### Virtual screening and molecular docking
  Virtual screening was conducted using the Schrödinger Suite (version 2010, Schrödinger LLC, New York, NY) as previously described (1). The TopBP1-BRCT7/8 protein structure [Protein Data Bank (PDB) ID: 3AL3] was prepared in Maestro 9.1 with default parameters: Water molecules were removed, hydrogen atoms were added, and the BACH1 peptide ligand was extracted for docking. An initial screen of over 200,000 compounds from three libraries (drug-like molecules, natural product derivatives, and compounds tested in men) was performed as previously described (16). Molecular docking of positive hits and their derivatives were carried out using Mcule’s 1-Click Docking tool.


### Synthesis of target compounds

#### General
  All chemicals were of reagent grade and were used as received. The reactions were monitored by thin-layer chromatography (TLC) analysis using silica gel plates (Kieselgel 60 F254, E. Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). Compounds were visualized by ultraviolet (UV) irradiation. NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker Avance 500 spectrometer and were measured in pyridine-d5, chloroform-d, or dimethyl sulfoxide-d6 (DMSO-d6). Chemical shifts were reported in parts per million (ppm) with respect to the corresponding solvent as an internal standard, and coupling constants (J) were in hertz (Hz). High-resolution mass spectra were measured with a JEOL (JMS-700) EI mass spectrometer using an inlet direct system and with an AB SCIE X (QSTAR XL) high-resolution electrospray ionization mass spectrometer. Melting points were determined using a Yanagimoto micromelting point apparatus or Mel-Temp II (Laboratory Devices, USA) and were uncorrected.


#### General procedure for the synthesis of substituted 4-chloromethylcoumarins
  A 73% sulfuric acid (30 ml) solution was added dropwise to a mixture of substituted phenols (50 mmol) and ethyl 4-chloroacetoacetate (50 mmol) at 0°C in an ice bath. The cooling bath was then removed, and the reaction mixture was stirred overnight at room temperature. The reaction mixture was poured onto crushed ice after the completion of the reaction. The resulting precipitate was collected by filtration and washed with ice water until the filtrate was neutral and dried under vacuum. The crude compound was recrystallized from an adequate solvent to form the required substituted 4-chloromethylcoumarins.


#### General procedure for preparing 3B6 derivatives
  Substituted piperazines or piperidines (2.2 mmol) were added to a solution of the properly substituted 4-chloromethylcoumarin (1 mmol) in DMF (2 ml) and held for 24 hours at room temperature (completion of reaction determined by TLC). The reaction mixture was poured slowly into ice-cold water. The formed precipitate was filtered off and washed with water and recrystallized from an adequate solvent to obtain the desired product. The compounds were transformed into the corresponding hydrochloride or used as a free base. Compounds were named following IUPAC rules as applied by the ChemDraw Professional 17.0 software.


#### General procedure for preparing hydrochloride of target compounds
  To a round bottle containing target compound (20 mg) was added 3 M methanolic HCl (1 ml), and the reaction mixture was stirred overnight at room temperature. The reaction mixture was evaporated in vacuum. The residue was recrystallized from methanol or ethanol to obtain corresponding hydrochloride salts.

  Spectroscopic data for all compounds, synthesis of CS18, HREI-MS spectrum, and 1D/2D NMR spectra of CS18 are provided in the Supplementary Materials.


### In vitro peptide binding
  Recombinant GST-TopBP1-BRCT7/8 was purified as described previously (8). The glutathione S-transferase (GST) tag of GST-TopBP1-BRCT7/8 was removed by PreScission protease (Pharmacia). Two biotinylated peptides that contain the TopBP1-S1159 residue, including pP (Btn-REERARLApSer1159NLQWPS) and nP (Btn-REERARLASNLQWPS), were synthesized and purified (>98%) by Sigma-Genosys. Purified TopBP1-BRCT7/8 was incubated with Cpd 4B4, 4B5, or CS18 in NETN-A buffer [50 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 20 mM Tris (pH 8.0), and 0.5% NP-40] at 4°C for 2 hours, followed by the addition of the purified biotinylated pP or nP peptide at 4°C overnight. The biotinylated peptides were then pulled down with Streptavidin Sepharose (Amersham). The beads were washed four times with NETN-B buffer (100 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, and 0.2 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride) and then subjected to SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) and immunoblotting using an anti-TopBP1 rabbit antibody against the C terminus of TopBP1 (Bethyl Laboratories).


### GST pull-down assay
  Purified TopBP1-BRCT7/8 was incubated with 4B4 or 4B5 in NETN-A buffer at 4°C for 2 hours, followed by the addition of purified GST-mutp53R273H(DBD) (41) or GST at 4°C overnight. GST-mutp53R273H(DBD) was pulled down with Glutathione Sepharose 4B beads (Amersham). The beads were washed four times with NETN-B buffer and then subjected to SDS-PAGE analysis and immunoblotting with an anti-TopBP1 antibody as described above.


### Thermal shift assay
  Protein thermal stability was assessed by differential scanning fluorimetry using a Stratagene/Agilent Mx3005P quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) system according to a published protocol (42). Each TSA reaction (10 μl) contained 2 μM purified TopBP1-BRCT7/8 protein and a compound titrated between 20 and 100 μM in assay buffer [10 mM Hepes (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, and 5× SYPRO Orange dye]. Samples were dispensed into optical 96-well PCR plates, sealed, and centrifuged briefly to remove bubbles. SYPRO Orange was monitored using the filters 492/610 nm (excitation/emission) in the Mx3005P qPCR machine while the temperature was increased from 25° to 95°C at 1°C/min with continuous data acquisition. Melting temperatures (Tm) were determined from the peak of the first derivative of fluorescence versus temperature (−dF/dT). ΔTm values were calculated relative to vehicle controls.


### Cell viability, clonogenic survival, and caspase-3/7 activity assays
  Cell viability was determined by the CCK-8 assay according to the manufacturer’s instruction (APExBIO). Ten microliters of WST-8 solution was added to the cells for 1 to 2 hours at 37°C, and the amount of WST-8 formazan generated by the activity of dehydrogenases in the living cells was measured by reading the absorbance at an optical density (OD) at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek). To investigate whether CS18 inhibits cell viability through the specific binding to TopBP1, an empty vector, wild-type TopBP1, or a TopBP1 mutant defective in CS18 binding (S1273A or K1317M) was transiently expressed in TopBP1-depleted H1299 cells that stably express mutp53(R273H) and a TopBP1 shRNA. After 48 hours, cells were treated with various concentrations of CS18 for 45 hours, followed by the CCK-8 assay to determine the cell viability. To determine the clonogenic survival, MDA-MB-468, MDAH-2774, or A2780cis cells were treated with various concentrations of 4B4, 4B5, or CS18 for 24 hours. After PBS washing, viable cells were cultured in fresh growth medium for another 6 to 9 days, followed by fixation with 3% formaldehyde and staining with 0.5% crystal violet in 25% methanol. HCC95, HCC2814, A549, or H1975 cells were treated with various concentrations of CS18 every other day for 6 days before fixation and crystal violet staining. To determine the combination effect of CS18 and osimertinib on clonogenic survival, parental or H1975-OsiR cells were treated with various concentrations of CS18, osimertinib, or both every other day for 6 to 8 days. Cells were then fixed and stained with crystal violet as described above. To determine the viability of PDX tumor spheres, BCM2665 breast cancer PDX tissues were trypsinized and cells were cultured in Complete DMEM/F-12 Media for Patient-Derived In Vitro and Organoid Cultures as described in the NCI Patient-Derived Models Repository (SOP30101). After culturing for 10 days, tumor spheres were aliquoted on 48-well plates and treated with DMSO vehicle or 3 μM CS18 every other day for 5 days, followed by the CCK-8 assay to determine the viability of tumor spheres. Apoptosis was determined by the Caspase-Glo 3/7 activity assay (Promega), which measures the caspase-3/7 activity by cleavage of the luminogenic substrate containing the DEVD sequence, and was normalized to protein concentrations.


### Immunoblotting, coimmunoprecipitation, and immunostaining
  Immunoblotting was performed using an antibody specific to Cyclin D1, c-Myc, Rad51, p21Cip1, Top2A, active caspase-8, HSP90, α-tubulin (Cell Signaling), ACTL6A, GAPDH (Santa Cruz Biotechnology), SLX4 (Bethyl Laboratories), or PARP1 (BD Pharmingen). To detect the effect of CS18 on the association of TopBP1 with its interacting partners, MDA-MB-468 cells or MDAH-2774 cells treated with CS18 for 4 or 20 hours were harvested in a buffer containing 0.1% Triton-X, 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 1 mM EDTA, and 1 mM EGTA. Endogenous TopBP1 was immunoprecipitated with an anti-TopBP1 mouse monoclonal antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) or control mouse immunoglobulin G (IgG) and was resolved by SDS-PAGE. The coimmunoprecipitated proteins were detected by immunoblotting using a rabbit antibody specific to MIZ1, CIP2A, PLK1, p53, E2F1, RPA32, or Rad9 (Cell Signaling). The immunoblot was stripped and reprobed with an anti-TopBP1 rabbit antibody (Bethyl Laboratories). Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as the primary loading control, with HSP90 or α-tubulin substituted when necessary to avoid molecular weight overlap with target proteins. To determine whether TopBP1 inhibitors disrupt the association of TopBP1 with its interacting partners while cells enter mitosis, MDAH-2774 cells were treated with 10 μM RO-3306 (APExBIO) for 24 hours, followed by the addition of 1 μM CS18, 4 μM 5D4, or 2 μM Calcein-AM for 1 hour. After washing twice with PBS to remove RO-3306, cells were cultured in fresh growth medium containing one of the TopBP1 inhibitors for 45 min. Coimmunoprecipitation of TopBP1 with PLK1, CIP2A, mutp53, Top2A, or SLX4 was performed as described above.


### Mitotic progression
  Subconfluent MDAH-2774 cells were treated with 5 μM RO-3306 (APExBIO) for 2 days to arrest cells at the G2-M phase border, followed by the addition of 3 μM 5D4, 1 μM CS18, or 2 μM Calcein-AM for 1 hour. After washing twice with PBS to remove RO-3306, cells were cultured in regular growth medium containing one of the TopBP1 inhibitors for 45, 90, or 120 min. After fixation with 3% formaldehyde, the nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33258 (Sigma-Aldrich). To determine the population of cells in different phases of mitosis, at least 200 mitotic cells per sample were counted under a Zeiss digital Axio Observer inverted fluorescence microscope using the 20X objective.


### Chromatin immunoprecipitation
  Treated cells were fixed and nuclei were isolated as previously described (43). Nuclei were resuspended in shearing buffer [0.1 mM EDTA (pH 8.0), 0.1 mM EGTA, 10 mM tris-HCl (pH 6.8), 100 mM NaCl, 0.1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.5% N-lauroylsarcosine, and protease inhibitors] and sonicated to an average fragment size of ∼800 base pairs (bp). Chromatin concentrations were determined from processed aliquots, and equal amounts of chromatin were used for immunoprecipitation. ChIP assays, washes, and downstream processing were performed as previously described (43). Immunoprecipitations were performed with 3 μg of anti-MIZ1 antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific) or control mouse IgG, followed by capture with protein G magnetic Dynabeads (Invitrogen). Enriched DNA was analyzed by qPCR using ChIP primers (table S5); the Gene Desert primer was designed by Active Motif.


### In vivo xenograft experiments
  Breast cancer PDX BCM2665 (obtained from the BCM PDX Core) was implanted into the mammary fat pads of female NSG mice. Once tumors became measurable, mice were treated via intraperitoneal injection with vehicle (a cosolvent containing 7.5% DMSO, 40% polyethylene glycol, molecular weight 300, 5% Tween 80, and 47.5% saline) or CS18 (40 mg/kg) twice weekly for 15 days (a total of five treatments) or with escalating doses of CS18 (15, 30, 45, or 60 mg/kg) three times per week for 3 weeks. To establish parental H1975 xenografts, 2 × 107 cells were suspended in 50 μl of PBS and mixed with 50 μl of Matrigel supplemented with EGF (50 ng/ml), followed by injection subcutaneously into the right flank of NSG mice. To establish osimertinib-resistant H1975-OsiR xenografts, 5 × 106 cells suspended in 100 μl of PBS were injected subcutaneously into the right flank of NSG mice. NSG mice bearing parental H1975 xenografts were treated with vehicle or osimertinib [6 mg/kg, oral gavage (o.g.), once daily, 5 days per week] for 17 days. NSG mice bearing H1975-OsiR xenografts were treated with vehicle, CS18 (30 mg/kg, intraperitoneally, three times per week), osimertinib (6 mg/kg, o.g., once daily, 5 days per week), or the combination of CS18 and osimertinib for 17 days. The mouse weight and tumor size were measured on the indicated day with a caliper and calculated on the basis of the formula π/6 (length x depth x width). The evaluator was blinded to the group allocation during monitoring. Animals were euthanized on the indicated dates, and tumors were harvested, weighed, and further processed for histopathological analysis. All experiments were performed under a Baylor College of Medicine Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC)–approved protocol (AN-5431), and all experiments confirm to IACUC standards and ethical regulations.


### Histology and immunohistochemistry
  PDX tumors and mouse small intestines were placed in cassettes and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin. The next day, the samples were sent to The Pathology and Histology Core of Baylor College of Medicine for further processing. The paraffin-embedded sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) or Ki-67 using standard operating protocols.


### Cellular thermal shift assay
  MDAH-2774 cells were treated with 5 μM CS18 at 37°C for 2 hours. After washing three times with PBS, cells were resuspended in PBS supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail. The cells were then aliquoted and either kept at 25°C or heated individually at different temperatures ranging from 42° to 46.4°C for 3 min, followed by cooling down for 3 min at room temperature. The cell suspensions were freeze thawed three times using liquid nitrogen. After centrifugation at 20,000g for 20 min at 4°C to remove the cell debris, the supernatants were transferred to new microtubes and subjected to SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting. For xenograft experiments, the frozen tumors were thawed on ice and homogenized in cold PBS followed by three cycles of freeze-thawing using liquid nitrogen. Tissue lysates were separated from the cellular debris by centrifugation at 20,000g for 20 min at 4°C. The supernatants were diluted with PBS supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail, aliquoted and then heated at different temperatures ranging from 42.4° to 47.6°C for 3 min, followed by cooling down for 3 min at room temperature. Soluble fractions were isolated by centrifugation and analyzed as mentioned above.


### CI analysis
  To assess and quantify dose-dependent interactions between CS18 and either a PARP inhibitor or osimertinib, cells were treated with CS18, a PARP inhibitor (or osimertinib), or their combination at a fixed concentration ratio for 20 hours, followed by the caspase-3/7 activity assay, or for 45 hours, followed by the CCK-8 cell viability assay. The CI was analyzed as described previously (44, 45). The mode of drug interaction (synergy, antagonism, or additivity) was determined by CI analysis using the CompuSyn software program (CompuSyn Inc., Paramus, NJ): CI = 1, additive effects; CI < 1, synergy; and CI > 1, antagonism.


### Statistical analysis
  The two-tailed t test was used for comparison of treatment groups. Data were expressed as means ± SD from at least three biological replicates or means ± SEM (or SD) from at least three independent experiments. P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Sample size was based on estimations by power analysis with a level of significance of 0.05 and a power of 0.9. For xenograft experiments, linear mixed-effects models were fitted in JASP (version 0.18.3). Tumor size was used as the dependent variable, with treatment and day specified as fixed effects and biological replicate included as a random effect to account for repeated measurements.
