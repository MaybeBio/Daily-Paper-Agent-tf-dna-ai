# Machine learning of sunitinib resistance genes reveals that SP5 regulates angiogenesis and sunitinib resistance in RCC via the VEGFA/AKT axis


## Highlights
  •A 117‑algorithm machine‑learning framework identified SP5 as a core sunitinib resistance gene and established a robust prognostic risk model for RCC.•SP5 directly binds to the VEGFA promoter to transcriptionally upregulate VEGFA, which activates AKT phosphorylation via autocrine and paracrine loops.•SP5 knockdown suppresses RCC proliferation, migration, invasion, angiogenesis, and resensitizes tumor cells to sunitinib treatment.•Suramin, identified through virtual screening, disrupts the SP5–VEGFA interaction and synergizes with sunitinib to overcome acquired resistance.

## Introduction
  Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) represents a prevalent malignancy, with an estimated lifetime prevalence of 2.3% among males and 1.3% among females in the United States. In 2023, approximately 81,800 new cases of RCC were projected to be diagnosed nationwide, ranking it as the sixth most common cancer in men and the ninth in women [1]. Histologically, RCC comprises three major subtypes: clear cell (accounting for 75–80% of cases), papillary (10–15%), and chromophobe (5%) [2]. Clear cell RCC, characterized by its golden-yellow cytoplasm, exhibits inactivation of the VHL tumor suppressor gene in up to 90% of tumours [3]. This inactivation, typically resulting from a deleterious mutation coupled with loss of chromosome 3p, disrupts the oxygen-sensing pathway by abrogating the VHL protein–mediated degradation of hypoxia-inducible factors (HIF-1α and HIF-2α), thereby driving constitutive activation of hypoxia and angiogenesis programmes and giving rise to highly vascularised tumours with an increased bleeding risk [4]. These molecular aberrations have been therapeutically exploited: for metastatic disease, systemic therapy now includes tyrosine kinase inhibitors that impair angiogenesis by blocking vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) receptor signalling, as well as immune checkpoint inhibitors that target the PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 pathways to restore CD8⁺ cytotoxic T-cell activity against tumour cells [5]. However, despite initial responses, many patients progress after a median of 6–15 months on sunitinib, indicating acquired resistance [6]. The underlying mechanisms are multifactorial and include: alternative angiogenic pathway activation, tumour microenvironment remodelling, ER stress-induced autophagy, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, lysosomal drug sequestration and efflux-transporter polymorphisms, non-coding RNA dysregulation, and epigenetic alterations [7,8].

  This study aims to provide novel insights into the early molecular diagnosis and treatment of renal cell carcinoma. We first screened for sunitinib resistance-related differentially expressed genes (DEGs) using multiple RNA-bulk datasets of renal carcinoma with sunitinib resistance, ultimately yielding 118 common DEGs. By applying 117 machine-learning algorithms to these 118 genes, we developed a risk-scoring model to precisely stratify patient subgroups. Patients in the high-risk group exhibited significantly poorer prognosis and showed marked differences in mutation burden, immune infiltration, and other features compared with the low-risk group. For high-risk patients, we performed drug screening to enable precision therapy. Among the screened genes, SP5 was the most frequently identified candidate and was selected for experimental investigation. We found that SP5 knockdown significantly suppressed tumor proliferation, migration, and invasion, while also mediating sunitinib resistance and angiogenesis. Further mechanistic studies revealed that SP5 promotes VEGFA transcription by binding to the VEGFA promoter, which in turn activates the pAKT pathway in both tumor cells and vascular endothelial cells through autocrine and paracrine signaling, thereby driving tumor angiogenesis and sunitinib resistance. Through subsequent drug screening, we discovered that suramin can bind to the C2H2 zinc-finger domain of SP5, reducing VEGFA transcription, and that its combination with sunitinib significantly ameliorates sunitinib resistance.


## Materials and methods

### Data collection and initial processing
  The bulk RNA sequencing data for sunitinib resistance were obtained from three datasets: GSE172165, GSE173572, and GSE216494. Bulk RNA transcriptomic data accompanied by prognostic information were obtained from four independent cohorts, namely TCGA-KIRC, EMTAB, CTPAC, and GSE167573. Of these, 534 tumor samples with survival data were derived from TCGA-KIRC, 101 tumor samples with prognostic information from EMTAB, and 110 and 55 tumor samples with prognostic data from CTPAC and GSE167573, respectively.

  Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data for RCC were obtained from the GEO database (GSE156632), which comprised 7 tumor tissues and 5 matched adjacent normal tissues. The preprocessing of the single-cell data, including quality control, normalization, dimensionality reduction, and clustering, was performed using the Seurat package following the pipelines established in our previously published studies [9,10].


### Construction of the machine learning model
  We leveraged the Mime1 package to build a unified computational platform that incorporates ten established algorithms: RSF, Enet, Stepwise Cox, CoxBoost, plsRcox, superpc, GBM, survivalsvm, Ridge, and Lasso [11,12]. The TCGA-KIRC dataset, with its sizable cohort, was partitioned into training (70%) and internal validation (30%) sets. To ensure robust external validation, we also included three complete datasets, EMTAB1980, CTPAC, and GSE167573, without any further splitting.


### Gene enrichment analysis
  For gene enrichment analysis and network mapping, we utilized Metascape, a web-based portal that integrates comprehensive annotation and analytical resources for gene lists [13].


### Somatic mutation analysis
  We first estimated tumor mutational burden (TMB) for each sample using the maftools package with TCGA SNV data, while recurrent amplifications and deletions were determined by GISTIC 2.0 using CNV data [14]. Based on the median TMB score, KIRC patients were classified as high-TMB or low-TMB. To compare their prognostic outcomes, we performed survival analysis by merging the TMB stratification with clinical information from the TCGA-KIRC cohort.


### Immune infiltration analysis
  We utilized the built-in functions of the Mime1 package to map the immune landscape via multiple algorithms and robustly quantified their correlations; additionally, immune cell infiltration was estimated by ssGSEA using gene sets for 28 and 13 immune cell types, and the levels were compared across risk groups [15,16].


### Drug prediction
  Drug sensitivity data for cancer cell lines were obtained from CTRP v2.0 and PRISM 19Q4, providing AUC-based dose-response profiles (lower AUC indicates higher sensitivity). Compounds with >20% missing AUC values were excluded, and remaining missing values were imputed via k-NN, consistent with our previous study [9].


### Cell culture and transfection
  769-P and 786-O cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, whereas HEK293T cells were cultured in DMEM high-glucose medium containing 10% FBS. For lentiviral production, HEK293T cells were co-transfected with the shRNA-expressing vectors and packaging plasmids. After 48 hours, viral supernatants were collected and used to infect 769-P and 786-O cells, which had been seeded in 6-well plates at 3 × 10⁵ cells per well. Stable transfectants were selected with 2 μg/mL puromycin (Solarbio, Cat#P8230) for approximately two weeks. Knockdown efficiency was subsequently verified by quantitative RT-PCR and Western blot analysis. All shRNA sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1.


### qRT-PCR
  RNA was extracted with TRIzol, reverse-transcribed using Vazyme's All-in-One Ultra RT SuperMix (R433-01), and amplified via qPCR with the One Step SYBR Green Kit (Q221-01) on a qPCR system. Relative expression was determined by the 2−ΔΔCT method (normalized to internal controls); primer sequences are in Supplementary Table S2.


### Western blot
  Total proteins were extracted from tissues or cells using RIPA buffer (Solarbio, Cat# R0010) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Solarbio, Cat# P1260), and quantified via the BCA assay (Beyotime, Cat# P0012S). Equal amounts of protein were then separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (BioRad, Cat# 1620115). After blocking with 5% nonfat milk or BSA for 2 hours at room temperature, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies (2 hours at RT or overnight at 4°C), followed by secondary antibody incubation for 1 hour at RT. All antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table S3.


### CUT&RUN assay
  The CUT&RUN assay was performed using the CUT&RUN Assay Kit (Vazyme) according to the manufacturer's instructions, and qRT-PCR was carried out to verify the SP5-binding sites within the VEGFA promoter.


### CCK-8 and colony formation assay
  Two complementary assays were performed to evaluate cell proliferation and clonogenicity. For the CCK-8 assay, cells were seeded at 2,000 per well in 96-well plates, and at indicated time points, CCK-8 reagent (Apexbio) was added. After a 1.5-hour incubation, absorbance at 450 nm was recorded. For colony formation, cells were plated at 1,000 per well in 6-well plates and cultured for 7 to 14 days. The resulting colonies were then fixed, stained with 0.5% crystal violet, and quantified using ImageJ, with a threshold of >50 cells per colony.


### Wound-healing assay
  To evaluate migratory capacity, we seeded cells into 6-well plates at 5 × 10⁵ cells per well and incubated them to ∼90% confluence. A linear defect was created in the monolayer using a sterile 200- μL pipette tip, after which detached cells were removed by PBS rinsing. Wound images were captured with a Nikon microscope immediately (at 0 h), and at 24 h and 48 h post-scratching. The extent of migration was determined by calculating the remaining wound area in ImageJ at both time points.


### Transwell assay
  Transwell assays were used to evaluate both migration and invasion. For migration, 2 × 10⁴ cells suspended in serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chamber, while the lower chamber was filled with DMEM containing 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. After 24 hours of incubation, cells that had migrated through the membrane were fixed, stained, and enumerated. The invasion assay followed the same protocol except that the upper chambers were pre-coated with Matrigel (1:20 dilution; Corning, Cat# 354234) prior to cell seeding.


### Tubule formation assay
  Matrigel (100 µL per well) was added to 24-well plates and polymerized at 37°C for 60 minutes. HUVECs were first co-cultured with treated cells for 24 hours, then harvested and resuspended in conditioned medium at 1.5 × 10⁵ cells per 200 µL. The cell suspension was seeded onto the solidified Matrigel and incubated for 4–6 hours. Tube formation was photographed under an inverted microscope (Leica), and the number of branch points was counted using ImageJ.


### Subcutaneous xenograft tumor model
  All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanjing Medical University and performed according to the protocols established in our previous work [17]. For subcutaneous xenograft models, 6‑week‑old male nude mice were injected subcutaneously into the right flank with 1 × 10⁶ 786‑O cells stably expressing shControl or shSP5. Tumor dimensions were measured with calipers every 8 days, and tumor volumes were calculated using the formula: length × width² × 0.5. At the end of the experiment, mice were sacrificed, tumors were excised, weighed, and photographed. All procedures complied with institutional guidelines.


### Statistical analysis
  All data processing and statistical analyses were carried out using R software (version 4.3.2), while data visualization and supplementary statistical tests were performed with GraphPad Prism 9. All experiments were repeated at least three times independently, and results are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance thresholds were set at *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001.

  To identify key genes associated with sunitinib resistance, we first analyzed DEGs across multiple GEO datasets. The volcano plots illustrate the differential gene expression between resistant and sensitive states in GSE172165, GSE173572, and in A498 and 786-O cells from GSE216494, respectively (Fig. 1A–D). We identified a set of core genes potentially involved in sunitinib resistance by intersecting the DEGs from these four datasets, providing targets for subsequent functional validation at the single-cell level (Fig. 1E). To further elucidate the cellular origins and communication characteristics of these resistance genes within the RCC microenvironment, we performed an in-depth analysis using scRNA-seq data from the GSE156632 dataset (Fig. 1F–H). The t-SNE dimensionality reduction clustering results revealed that the cell populations could be clearly divided into multiple clusters and annotated into seven major subpopulations based on sample origin and cell type: Epithelial Cells, Endothelial Cells, Fibroblasts, Myofibroblasts, T and NK Cells, and B Cells (Fig. 1F). The expression heatmap of cell type marker genes and the distribution of specific markers on the t-SNE plots further validated the accuracy of these cell type annotations (Fig. 1G, H). Building on this, we evaluated the enrichment of the aforementioned resistance gene set at the single-cell level using the AUCell algorithm, and all single cells were divided into a high-risk score group and a low-risk score group according to the gene set enrichment scores (Fig. 1I, J). Subsequently, we compared the cell–cell communication differences between the two groups. Chord diagram analyses indicated significant differences in both the number and strength of cell–cell interactions between the high- and low-risk groups (Fig. 1K, L). Furthermore, heatmap analyses of signaling patterns revealed marked heterogeneity in both outgoing signaling and incoming signaling between the two groups (Fig. 1M, N). ### Immune infiltration profiling and drug prediction
  To delineate the immune landscape associated with our risk model, we performed an immune infiltration analysis using six distinct algorithms (xCell, EPIC, ABIS, ESTIMATE, CIBERSORT, and CIBERSORT-ABIS), and the correlations among these algorithms were visualized (Fig. 5A, B). Subsequently, the infiltration levels of 28 and 13 distinct immune cell subtypes were compared between the high- and low-risk groups to further characterize the tumor microenvironment (Fig. 5C, D). Notably, the high-risk group exhibited significantly higher infiltration levels of CD4 and CD8 T cells compared to the low-risk group (Fig. 5C, D). This observation indicates that the high-risk group harbors an activated but potentially exhausted T-cell microenvironment, which may contribute to the distinct immune evasion mechanisms and clinical outcomes observed in these patients. Given the unfavorable prognosis and distinct immune profiles observed in the high-risk group, we further investigated potential therapeutic agents by screening drug sensitivity data from the PRISM and CTRP databases. The correlation analysis and comparative box plots for drug sensitivity in both the PRISM and CTRP databases were systematically presented (Fig. 5E–H). SMER-3 is a small‑molecule enhancer of rapamycin that selectively inhibits the Met30 ubiquitin E3 ligase, blocking substrate ubiquitination [18]. Dofetilide is a class III antiarrhythmic agent that selectively blocks the rapid component of the delayed rectifier potassium current (IKr) [19]. ### SP5 modulates malignant progression and sunitinib resistance in RCC
  To investigate the functional role of SP5 in RCC, stable SP5-knockdown cell lines were established in 769-P and 786-O cells, and the knockdown efficiency was confirmed at both the protein and mRNA levels (Fig. 6A). Functional assays revealed that SP5 knockdown significantly suppressed colony formation, cell migration, and invasion in these RCC cell lines (Fig. 6B–D). CCK-8 assays demonstrated that SP5 knockdown sensitized 769-P and 786-O cells to sunitinib treatment, as evidenced by a marked reduction in cell viability upon combination treatment and a significantly decreased IC50 for sunitinib (Fig. 6G, H). ### SP5 potentiates renal cancer angiogenesis and sunitinib resistance via VEGFA-mediated pAKT activation through autocrine and paracrine loops
  To elucidate the molecular mechanism through which SP5 promotes angiogenesis, we first examined the mRNA expression of multiple angiogenesis-related genes. To identify the key downstream effectors mediating SP5-induced angiogenesis, we performed a tube formation rescue experiment using conditioned media from SP5-knockdown cells supplemented with individual recombinant proteins.   We next explored the transcriptional regulation of VEGFA by SP5. The SP5 binding motif was identified, and three putative SP5 binding sites within the VEGFA promoter region were predicted with the corresponding primer sets spanning these sites designed accordingly (Fig. 7G, H). CUT&RUN assays revealed that SP5 specifically and significantly bound to binding site 1 within the VEGFA promoter, rather than binding sites 2 and 3 (Fig. 7I). Dual-luciferase reporter assays further demonstrated that SP5 markedly enhanced the transcriptional activity of the VEGFA promoter containing the wild-type binding site 1; however, mutation of binding site 1 completely abolished this enhancement (Fig. 7J). Subsequently, Western blot analysis revealed that SP5 knockdown led to a marked reduction in VEGFA protein expression and decreased phosphorylation of AKT in RCC cells (Fig. 7K). Notably, the phosphorylation of AKT was also significantly suppressed in HUVECs incubated with conditioned media from SP5-knockdown cells, further confirming the paracrine activation of the VEGFA-AKT axis (Fig. 7L). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that SP5 directly binds to binding site 1 within the VEGFA promoter to upregulate its expression, thereby activating the AKT signaling pathway in a VEGFA-dependent manner to promote angiogenesis and confer sunitinib resistance in RCC.


### Suramin enhances sunitinib sensitivity via disruption of SP5-VEGFA promoter interaction
  To screen for potential SP5 inhibitors, virtual docking against the Selleck L1300 database was performed using AutoDock, and suramin sodium salt emerged as the top-ranked compound with a docking score of −12.3 kcal/mol (Fig. 8A). Suramin was predicted to stably occupy a specific pocket composed of residues including ARG345, ARG348, PHE356, PHE365, ARG367, SER368, ASP369, HSD370, LYS373, and HSD374. Suramin is a reversible, competitive inhibitor of protein tyrosine phosphatases (PTPases) and DNA topoisomerase II, with antiparasitic, antitumor, and anti‑angiogenic activities [20,21]. Preclinically, suramin has demonstrated dose-dependent inhibition of renal cell carcinoma cell proliferation and metastasis in vitro and in vivo [22]. We then evaluated the biological effects of suramin in RCC cells. Treatment with suramin significantly reduced both VEGFA mRNA expression and its secretion in 769-P and 786-O cells (Fig. 8B, C). Based on the reported IC₅₀ range of suramin in RCC cells and its noncytotoxic sensitizing concentrations, we chose 35 µM and 70 µM for combination experiments [23,24]. Functionally, CCK-8 assays demonstrated that suramin synergized with sunitinib to inhibit cell proliferation, as evidenced by a reduced cell viability in the combination group and a significantly decreased IC50 of sunitinib following suramin treatment (Fig. 8D, E). Furthermore, conditioned media from suramin-treated cells markedly impaired HUVEC tube formation, suggesting that suramin blocks SP5-mediated paracrine angiogenesis (Fig. 8F). Consistently, colony formation assays revealed that co-treatment with suramin and sunitinib exhibited a synergistic suppression of RCC cell colony formation compared with either agent alone (Fig. 8G). ## Discussion
  Sunitinib, a standard oral tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI), is widely utilized in the treatment of multiple malignancies, including renal cell carcinoma, gastrointestinal carcinoid tumors, and gliomas [25]. However, with its expanding clinical use, an increasing number of patients develop resistance to sunitinib, posing a significant therapeutic challenge [26]. The mechanisms underlying sunitinib resistance in renal cell carcinoma are multifactorial and can be broadly categorized into five interconnected aspects: activation of bypass or alternative signaling pathways, insufficient intracellular drug accumulation, enhanced drug efflux or metabolic inactivation, metabolic reprogramming, and epigenetic regulation [[27], [28], [29], [30]]. Therefore, multidisciplinary efforts to explore innovative therapeutic strategies are urgently required to overcome this resistance and improve clinical outcomes.

  SP5, a member of the Specificity Protein (Sp) transcription factor family, encodes a protein characterized by three conserved Cys2His2 zinc finger domains at its C-terminus, enabling sequence-specific binding to GC-boxes or related motifs in the promoters of target genes to modulate transcription [31,32]. In the context of tumorigenesis and tumor progression, SP5 displays a dualistic and context-dependent function. As a direct downstream target of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, SP5 is upregulated in various malignancies, including hepatocellular carcinoma, gastric cancer, and colorectal cancer [33]. Functional studies have demonstrated its pro-tumorigenic role; for instance, in prostate cancer cells, SP5 activates PDPK1 transcription, thereby initiating the AKT/mTOR signaling cascade to promote cell viability and glycolysis [34]. Moreover, the circular RNA circSP5 has been shown to facilitate colorectal cancer liver metastasis through SP5-mediated transcriptional upregulation of BAMBI [35]. Conversely, SP5 also exhibits tumor-suppressive activities. In lung adenocarcinoma, SP5 acts as an inhibitor of the Wnt pathway by promoting SERPING1 transcription, which in turn suppresses tumor progression via the TSC2/mTOR axis [36]. In colon cancer HCT116 cells, SP5 negatively regulates cell proliferation through transcriptional induction of the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor p27 [31]. Collectively, these observations underscore that SP5 can assume either oncogenic or tumor-suppressive roles depending on the tumor type and microenvironment, rendering it a promising yet challenging candidate for targeted cancer therapy.

  VEGFA is a member of the PDGF/VEGF growth factor family that encodes a heparin-binding, disulfide-linked homodimeric protein[37,38]. The human VEGFA gene spans approximately 16 kb on chromosome 6p12 and consists of eight exons. Alternative splicing of exons 6, 7 and 8 generates multiple isoforms, with VEGF₁₂₁, VEGF₁₆₅ and VEGF₁₈₉ being the major ones [39]. Exons 3 and 4 encode the receptor-binding domains for VEGFR1 and VEGFR2, respectively, while exons 6 and 7 encode heparin-binding domains that determine the isoforms' affinity for the extracellular matrix [40]. Functionally, VEGFA is a critical regulator of angiogenesis, vasculogenesis and endothelial cell growth [37,41]. It induces endothelial cell proliferation, promotes cell migration, inhibits apoptosis and increases vascular permeability [42]. In the tumor microenvironment, VEGFA is the principal driver of pathological angiogenesis [38,43]. Its expression is upregulated in many known tumors and correlates with tumor stage and progression. Beyond its role in neovascularization, VEGFA also modulates the tumor immune microenvironment by promoting immunosuppression. In renal cell carcinoma, particularly the clear cell subtype, which accounts for approximately 80% of kidney cancers—VEGFA plays a central and well-characterized role [44]. The majority of RCCs harbor inactivating mutations in the von Hippel-Lindau tumor suppressor gene, leading to stabilization of hypoxia-inducible factors (HIF-1α and HIF-2α) and subsequent transcriptional overexpression of VEGFA [45,46]. This renders RCC a paradigm for anti‑angiogenic therapy, and agents targeting the VEGFA/VEGFR pathway, including monoclonal antibodies, tyrosine kinase inhibitors and VEGF‑trap molecules, have become standard of care for advanced disease [42]. Elevated VEGFA expression in RCC patients is associated with worse overall survival, disease-specific survival and progression-free survival, and correlates with tumor size, stage and metastasis [47]. Moreover, VEGFA upregulation in RCC can result from impaired repressor function of seryl-tRNA synthetase or from epigenetic activation by chromatin remodelers such as SMARCD1, and its expression is positively associated with immune cell infiltration within the RCC microenvironment [48]. Notably, although direct transcriptional regulation of the VEGFA promoter by SP5 has not been conclusively established, Sp-family transcription factors, particularly Sp1 and Sp3, are well-characterized activators of VEGFA transcription through their binding to GC-rich elements in the promoter [[49], [50], [51]]. Given that SP5 shares the same DNA-binding domain and target motif specificity, it is plausible that SP5 may similarly modulate VEGFA expression in a context-dependent manner, though this possibility awaits direct experimental validation.

  In summary, this study comprehensively elucidates the molecular mechanism by which SP5 drives angiogenesis and sunitinib resistance in clear cell renal cell carcinoma. Through integrative multi-omics analyses and a robust 117-algorithm machine learning framework, we identified SP5 as a critical hub gene linking sunitinib resistance to malignant progression. Mechanistically, we demonstrated that SP5 directly binds to binding site 1 within the VEGFA promoter, thereby transcriptionally upregulating VEGFA expression and subsequently activating the AKT signaling pathway via both autocrine and paracrine loops in tumor cells and vascular endothelial cells. Furthermore, the application of structure-based virtual screening identified suramin as a potent SP5 antagonist, which effectively disrupts the SP5-VEGFA interaction and resensitizes RCC cells to sunitinib treatment.

  The methodological strength of this study lies in its comprehensive integration of bulk transcriptomics, single-cell resolution, and in vitro and in vivo functional validation. The use of the Scissor algorithm coupled with 117 algorithmic combinations provides a rigorous and unbiased framework for identifying phenotype-associated proliferative subpopulations and constructing a reliable prognostic model. Moreover, the discovery of suramin as a targeted inhibitor of SP5 offers a promising translational avenue for overcoming acquired resistance to first-line targeted therapy.

  However, several limitations should be noted. First, the relatively modest sample size of the single-cell cohort and the retrospective nature of the public datasets may limit the generalizability of our findings, necessitating validation in larger, prospective cohorts. Second, while suramin showed encouraging efficacy in vitro, further preclinical evaluations of its pharmacokinetics, in vivo toxicity, and off-target effects are required before clinical translation. Additionally, the prognostic utility of the constructed risk score warrants prospective verification in real-world clinical settings.


## Conclusion
  SP5 drives sunitinib resistance and angiogenesis in RCC by directly activating VEGFA transcription and downstream AKT signaling via autocrine/paracrine loops.Disrupting the SP5–VEGFA axis with suramin resensitizes tumors to sunitinib, offering a promising strategy to overcome acquired resistance.
