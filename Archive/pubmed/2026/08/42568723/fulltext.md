# Activation of BMPR1b–SMAD pathway by Ginsenoside F1 restores osteoblast function in ovariectomy-induced osteoporosis


## Introduction
  Osteoblasts are bone-building cells that drive osteogenesis by producing osteoid and promoting its mineralization during development and remodeling. Although ginsenosides from Panax species can enhance bone formation and inhibit resorption, the role of ginsenoside F1 in osteoblast differentiation and bone metabolism has not been defined.


## Objectives
  This study aimed to elucidate the molecular mechanism by which ginsenoside F1 promotes osteoblast differentiation and evaluate its therapeutic potential in an ovariectomy-induced osteoporosis model.


## Methods
  Bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells and primary osteoblasts were treated with ginsenoside F1. Osteogenic differentiation was analyzed through gene and protein expression of key markers, including Runx2, Sp7 and Alpl. RNA-sequencing, molecular docking, and siRNA-mediated gene silencing were performed to identify and validate F1's target signaling pathway. The in vivo efficacy of F1 was assessed in ovariectomized mice using micro-computed tomography, histological staining, and biochemical assays.


## Results
  Gene and protein expression analyses showed higher levels of osteogenic transcription factors in F1-induced osteoblasts than in untreated cells. RNA-sequencing analysis and molecular docking studies revealed an association between bone morphogenetic protein receptor type 1b (BMPR1b) and SMAD proteins important for mediating osteoblast differentiation following F1 induction. Furthermore, BMPR1B knockdown attenuated the inhibition of downstream SMAD1/5/9 signaling, indicating that BMP-activated SMAD signaling was required for the pre-osteogenic action of F1. In addition, F1 treatment increased bone mass in ovariectomy-induced osteoporosis.


## Conclusion
  Collectively, these findings suggest that ginsenoside F1 enhances osteoblast differentiation and promotes bone formation under osteoporotic conditions, highlighting its therapeutic potential for bone metabolism disorders.


## Introduction
  Osteoporosis is a major skeletal disorder characterized by reduced bone mass and deterioration of the bone microarchitecture, resulting in an increased likelihood of fractures even from minor incidents [1]. Globally, over 200 million people are affected, and the lifetime fracture risk exceeds one in three for women and one in five for men over the age of 50 [2]. Hip and vertebral fractures substantially increase morbidity, mortality, and healthcare expenses, with hip fractures associated with a 1-year mortality rate of 20–30% [2,3]. With population aging, the global burden of osteoporosis is expected to rise, underscoring the need for efficient preventive and treatment strategies [4].

  Bone remodeling depends on osteoblastic bone resorption and formation. In postmenopausal women, a lack of estrogen disrupts this balance, hastening bone turnover and promoting resorption [5,6]. Currently available therapies for osteoporosis are designed to either suppress bone resorption or stimulate new bone formation. Anti-resorptive agents include bisphosphonates, selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs), and the receptor activator of nuclear factor-kappa B ligand (RANKL) inhibitor denosumab [7]. Anabolic treatment options comprise parathyroid hormone analogs, such as teriparatide and abaloparatide, along with the sclerostin-targeting antibody romosozumab [8,9]. Although these medications can effectively lower fracture incidence, their long-term clinical application is limited by adverse effects. Prolonged bisphosphonate use, in particular, has been associated with complications such as osteonecrosis of the jaw and atypical femoral fractures [10]. SERMs provide modest skeletal benefit and increase thromboembolic risk [11]. Discontinuing denosumab can precipitate rapid bone loss and rebound fractures [12]. In addition, the use of anabolic agents is restricted in duration due to safety, cost, and access considerations [8]. Accordingly, pursuing new therapeutic agents that combine safety with long-term efficacy is critical for advancing osteoporosis management.

  Natural compounds from medicinal plants are attractive candidates for bone health, due to their structural diversity, multitarget properties, and generally favorable safety profiles [13]. Phytochemicals, such as flavonoids, polyphenols, and terpenoids, can exert osteogenic effects by promoting osteoblast activity and inhibiting osteoclast differentiation [14]. Among these, ginseng (Panax ginseng C.A. Meyer) has long been used in traditional medicine for anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory purposes [15]. Triterpenoid saponins are the bioactive components of ginsenosides, and evidence of their roles in bone metabolism is increasing. Several ginsenosides, including Rb1, Rg1, and Rd, can promote osteoblast differentiation and mineralization, while also inhibiting osteoclastogenesis [16,17]. Mechanistic findings have indicated that ginsenosides act via multiple pathways, including the Wnt/β-catenin, mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), and nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) pathways, underscoring their potential as multifunctional modulators of bone remodeling [16].

  Ginsenoside F1, a derivative metabolite of ginsenoside Rg1, exhibits higher bioavailability and biological activity than the parental compound [18]. Although F1 has been studied in cardiovascular and neuroprotective contexts [19,20], its skeletal activities remain underexplored. Previous evidence suggests that ginsenoside F1 can promote osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow-derived cells [21]. Major ginsenosides show limited gastrointestinal absorption due to size-related, low solubility and membrane permeability, impeding their absorption in the human body. However, previous reports showed that the transformation of major ginsenosides into smaller, deglycosylated, rare and minor ginsenosides (such as F1 and compound K) enhances the physiological activity in vivo [22]. Among various ginsenosides, F1 does not inhibit uridine 5′-diphospho-glucuronosyltransferases, which are crucial for detoxifying and eliminating drugs and toxins from the body [23]. Furthermore, another report showed that F1 was detectable after oral administration for a longer duration than were major ginsenoside metabolites, suggesting a higher likelihood of complete absorption [24]. Computational analyses, including molecular docking and dynamic simulations, further indicated that F1 satisfies drug-likeness criteria and has absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) properties suitable for drug development [25]. Together, these observations suggest ginsenoside F1 as a promising therapeutic candidate with suitable pharmacological characteristics. However, its molecular role in bone metabolism has not been clarified yet.

  The bone morphogenetic protein (BMP)-SMAD signaling pathway represents a critical regulator of osteoblast lineage commitment and maturation [26]. Activation of BMP receptors leads to the phosphorylation of receptor-regulated SMAD family proteins (SMAD1/5/9), which then translocate to the nucleus and drive the transcription of osteogenic genes, such as runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2) and specificity protein 7 (Sp7) [27,28]. Dysregulation of BMP-SMAD signaling contributes to impaired bone formation, whereas BMP-SMAD signaling activation is a key target of anabolic therapies [29]. To date, whether ginsenoside F1 promotes osteogenesis by engaging the BMP-SMAD signaling axis has not been systematically investigated.

  In this study, we evaluated the osteogenic potential of ginsenoside F1 in osteoblast differentiation and bone formation. Specifically, we investigated whether F1 activates BMP-SMAD signaling, thus driving osteogenesis, and assessed its therapeutic potential in an ovariectomized (OVX) mouse model of postmenopausal osteoporosis. Our findings indicate that ginsenoside F1 stimulated osteoblast differentiation through BMP-SMAD activation and mitigated OVX-induced bone loss in vivo, providing mechanistic insight into its osteoprotective action and supporting its development as a natural therapeutic agent for osteoporosis.


## Materials and methods

### Chemicals and preparation of diets
  Ginsenoside F1 (>98% purity) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). To evaluate the in vivo effects of F1, we replaced the gelatin-jelly vehicle with a direct chow formulation. Briefly, F1 powder was dissolved in 20% (v/v) ethanol to prepare a concentrated stock (e.g., 20–50 mg/mL). Standard rodent chow was milled to powder, and the calculated volume of F1 solution was sprayed onto the chow and mixed to achieve the desired intake-normalized concentration, assuming 3.0–4.0 g/day food consumption per adult mouse. As a guideline, for a 25 g mouse (food intake ∼3.5 g/day), 20 mg/(kg·day) corresponds to 0.5 mg F1/day, equivalent to ∼0.14 mg F1 per gram of chow, to achieve the target dose of 20 mg/(kg/day) in a chow diet formulation. The batch concentration was adjusted weekly based on cage-level mean body weight and measured food intake. After thorough mixing, the ethanol was allowed to evaporate completely under a Class II biosafety hood (≥1 h with intermittent mixing) before re-pelleting the chow into small pellets and storing it at 4 °C in airtight containers (≤7 days). Control (vehicle) chow was prepared identically using 20% ethanol only, followed by the same evaporation step. Mice received F1-containing chow ad libitum for 8 weeks; chow intake was recorded thrice per week to verify the delivered dose, and fresh chow was provided at least every 2–3 days.


### Isolation and culture of BMSCs
  For BMSC isolation, femora and tibiae from 8 to 10-week-old mice were collected, flushed, and filtered through a 70 μm strainer. Cells were plated at a density corresponding to one mouse (four bones) per 10 cm dish in alpha-minimum essential medium (α-MEM) containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. The cultures were expanded to confluency and then induced toward osteoblast differentiation by supplementing the medium with 10 mM β-glycerophosphate and 50 μg/mL ascorbic acid, with media changes occurring every 2–3 days. This BMSC isolation and culture strategy was adapted from a report by Rosen and colleagues that provides standardized procedures for murine marrow stromal cell culture [7].


### Isolation and culture of primary osteoblasts
  Primary osteoblasts were isolated from the femora and tibiae of mice following a modified protocol adapted from Chevalier et al. (2021) to standardize murine osteoblast isolation for downstream differentiation studies [30]. Briefly, bone marrow was completely flushed, and bone shafts were minced into ∼1 mm fragments and digested with 1 mg/mL collagenase II in α-MEM containing 10% heat-inactivated FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin for 90 min at 37 °C. After sequential washes, bone fragments were cultured in basal medium for 3–4 days to enable outward osteoblast migration. The cells were subsequently released via sequential collagenase I and trypsin digestion, centrifuged, and resuspended in basal medium. Cultures were maintained at 37 °C in 5% CO2, and cells between passages 2–5 were used for experiments.

  Details regarding animal models, cell cultures and experimental methods are listed as the supplementary materials and methods section.


### Statistical analysis
  For the results obtained in this study, the means and standard deviations were calculated using GraphPad Prism 10.6.1 (GraphPad Software Inc., CA, USA). Statistical significance was verified by performing one-way or two-way ANOVA and with an additional unpaired multiple t-test. ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, #p < 0.05, ##p < 0.01, and ###p < 0.001 were considered to reflect statistically significant differences.


## Results

### Ginsenoside F1 promoted osteoblast differentiation in bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs)
  Initially, BMSCs were isolated from the long bones of wild-type (WT) littermates (Fig. 1A) and cultured for 7–14 days in vitro with or without osteogenic induction, e.g., in the presence or absence of 10 μM ginsenoside F1 (Fig. 1B). A screening of multiple doses (1, 5, 10, 20 μM) with F1 ginsenoside treatment in primary osteoblasts was conducted, (Supplement Fig. S1A), and since 10 μM was sufficient to induce a significant increase in osteogenic markers, this was the concentration selected for subsequent experiments as an effective working dose. Although ginseng is a naturally occurring compound, the toxicity was also validated with MTT (3-[4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]-2,5 diphenyl tetrazolium bromide) assay in BMSCs prior to final treatment (Supplementary Fig. S1B). Consistent with a prior report, osteoblastogenesis from BMSCs began after day 7 [31]. To visualize morphological differences in the cells, we obtained time-course phase-morphological images on days 3, 5, 7, and 10. These images revealed increased matrix deposition in osteogenic medium that was further augmented by F1 on days 7 and 10 comparison, when compared with that observed on days 3 and 5 (Fig. 1C). To confirm whether this matrix accrual reflected differentiation, we measured the mRNA-expression levels of master osteogenic transcription factors, such as Runx2 and Sp7 (Fig. 1D), and other osteogenic genes alkaline phosphatase (Alpl), osteocalcin (Bglap), collagen type I alpha 1 (Col1a1), and osteopontin (Opn) (Fig. 1E). All these transcription-regulating genes were dramatically upregulated in F1-treated BMSCs, especially after differentiation, compared with the expression levels found in the untreated groups. These results were in line with a previously reported major ginsenoside compound K (CK) [32]. Using CK as a positive control for F1, morphological differences in BMSCs were observed in a time-dependent manner (Supplement Fig. S1C). This was followed by a significant rise in gene expressions in the F1-treated cells, comparable to CK (Supplement Fig. S1D). ### F1 promoted osteogenic differentiation and proliferation in primary osteoblasts
  Osteoblast precursor cells generated from BMSCs are responsible for bone deposition and have a restricted capacity for division. The final stage in the bone lineage comprises post-mitotic osteocytes, which are generally isolated within the bone and may become embedded within, forming osteoid cells [28]. Thus, validating the above findings directly from primary bone cells is essential. To this end, primary osteoblast cultures were prepared from tibia and femora as described by Chevalier et al. [30]. As expected, treatment with ginsenoside F1 induced the expression of osteogenic transcription factor genes (Fig. 3A) and proteins (Fig. 3B). A similar expression pattern was observed with ALP staining, which showed higher bone matrix formation (Fig. 3C and D). In addition, ARS staining revealed higher calcium deposition in primary bone cells (Fig. 3E and F). ### In-silico analyses implicated BMPR1B in F1-regulated osteogenesis
  To explore downstream regulators, RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis was conducted with mice BMSCs in undifferentiated and differentiated conditions. Several differentially expressed genes were noted, one of which (Bmpr1b; represented in the heatmap) was significantly upregulated in differentiated samples (Fig. 4A). The gene exhibited consistent upregulation, mirroring the expression pattern of Smad4 in the differentiated group versus that in the undifferentiated control group (Fig. 4B and C; box plot) and showing an >2-fold expression change (Fig. 4D; volcano plot). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis [34] showed enrichment for positive regulation of osteoblast differentiation (Fig. 4E). Molecular docking analysis was performed using the BMPR1b protein structure (Fig. 4F), indicating that the F1 ligand can bind directly to the BMPR1b active site with a predicted docking score of −5.89 kcal/mol, interacting with ASP265 and ARG198 (Fig. 4G). Other essential residues involved in osteogenic differentiation were also analyzed in molecular docking studies (Supplementary Fig. S2 and Supplementary Table S2). ### Bmpr1b signaling was required for F1-mediated osteoblast differentiation
  To confirm these in-silico findings, the mRNA-expression levels of BMPR1b and BMPR1a were assessed, which showed F1 treatment significantly upregulated BMPR1b but not BMPR1a (Fig. 5A). The mechanistic downstream effects of silencing BMPR1b expression in a human osteoblast cell line with siRNA was also monitored, both with or without F1 treatment. While F1 initially promoted differentiation, the absence of BMPR1b abolished F1-induced upregulation of osteogenic transcription factors (Fig. 5B), suggesting that F1 interacts with BMPR1b to activate SMAD signaling, consequently affecting osteogenesis. Following Bmpr1b knockdown, the expression levels of proteins associated with BMP–SMAD signaling were similar (Fig. 5C), as supported by representative quantitative measurements (Fig. 5D–G). Confocal microscopy images further showed that OSX expression under osteogenic conditions was higher after F1 treatment only when Bmpr1b was present (Fig. 5H–J). ### F1 mitigated OVX-induced bone loss and improved bone microarchitecture
  To evaluate whether F1 administration can ameliorate OVX-induced osteoporosis, we first monitored body weight changes in each experimental mouse group (Fig. 6A). OVX mice and F1-treated mice exhibited gradual increases in body weight compared with those of SHAM controls. Our micro-computed tomography (μCT) analyses of the distal femora revealed pronounced trabecular bone loss in the OVX group, whereas F1 partially restored trabecular microarchitecture toward the patterns of SHAM mice (Fig. 6B). Quantitative morphometric parameters confirmed these observations (Fig. 6C). OVX mice displayed significantly reduced bone mineral density (BMD), bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), and trabecular number (Tb.N) than did SHAM controls (p < 0.01–0.001). Notably, F1 supplementation significantly increased all four indices relative to OVX mice, indicating robust rescue of bone mass and architecture. Additionally, the histological examination further substantiated these findings (Fig. 6D). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of femoral sections showed severe trabecular deterioration and enlarged marrow cavities in OVX mice.   Osteoporosis arises when the balance between osteoblast-driven bone formation and osteoclast-driven bone resorption is disrupted, with the condition primarily characterized by increased bone resorption. To validate the effect of F1 on osteoclast activity, gene expressions for osteoclast regulating markers osteoprotegerin (Opg), receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand (Rankl), acid phosphatase 5 tartrate resistant (Acp5), and nuclear factor of activated T-cells, cytoplasmic 1 (Nfatc1) were analyzed in primary bone cells with and without F1 treatment which showed reduced osteoclast expressions in the presence of F1 ginsenoside (Supplementary Fig. S3A). To further investigate whether the protective effects of F1 on bone mass were associated with reduced resorption of osteoclasts activity, tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining from the femoral sections was conducted. TRAP staining showed a marked increase in osteoclast-positive staining in OVX group compared to SHAM controls, whereas F1-fed OVX mice bones showed a striking reduction in staining intensity (Fig. 6E). Individual TRAP stained bone images are provided in the supplementary figure (Fig. S3B–D). Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant increase in TRAP positive cells in OVX mice, which was significantly reversed following F1 administration (Fig. 6F).

  Consistent with these observations, Western blot analysis with direct bone tissue samples further demonstrated that the protein expression levels of osteogenic markers, including RUNX2 and osteocalcin (OCN), were significantly decreased in OVX mice relative to SHAM controls (Fig. 6G). Importantly, F1 treatment markedly upregulated the expression of both RUNX2 and OCN compared with OVX mice. Densitometric quantification verified that these increases were statistically significant (Fig. 6H), reinforcing the notion that F1 promotes osteoblast differentiation and function. In addition, protein expression of SMAD4 showed reduced expression in OVX bone tissues in comparison to SHAM, while F1-fed OVX mice samples showed a slight increase (Supplementary Fig. S3E–F), contributing to the activation of the BMP-SMAD signaling pathway for induction of osteoblastogenesis by F1 ginsenoside.

  Collectively, these results indicate that F1 not only alleviates OVX-induced bone loss but also enhances osteogenic activity, contributing to the restoration of bone homeostasis. Thus, F1 counteracted OVX-induced trabecular deficits and improved bone mass and architecture without materially altering systemic body weight.


### F1 restored osteoblast activity and bone matrix formation in OVX mice
  To assess whether F1 treatment can rescue impaired osteoblast function in OVX mice, flow cytometry was performed on differentiated osteoblasts from isolated BMSCs of SHAM, OVX and OVX + F1 group, to determine the percentage of viable osteoblast populations [35]. OVX mice exhibited a significant reduction in viable osteoblasts compared with that in SHAM controls (approximately 45.76% versus 90.84%), whereas F1 supplementation markedly increased the percentage of live cells to 64.78% (Fig. 7A and B). Confocal immunofluorescence staining further revealed reduced OSX expression in OVX mice, which was restored upon F1 treatment (Fig. 7C). Quantitative analysis confirmed significantly higher OSX fluorescence intensity in the OVX + F1 group than in the OVX group (Fig. 7D). In addition, histochemical staining showed diminished ALP activity and mineralized nodule formation in OVX mice, both of which were significantly increased by F1 treatment (Fig. 7E). Quantification indicated that ALP activity (Fig. 7F) and ARS-measured calcium deposition (Fig. 7G) were restored toward SHAM levels in the OVX + F1 group. ## Discussion
  The results of this study provide the first evidence that ginsenoside F1 can promote osteoblast differentiation and attenuate estrogen deficiency-induced bone loss through BMP-SMAD pathway activation. Our findings show that F1 significantly upregulated key osteogenic transcription factors, including Runx2 and Osx, as well as matrix genes such as Alpl, Bglap, Col1a1, and Opn, in both BMSCs and primary osteoblasts. These data align with earlier reports showing that other ginsenosides, such as Rb1 and Rg1, enhanced osteoblast differentiation and mineralization via osteogenic signaling cascades [24,36]. However, distinct from these parent compounds, our data implicate BMBR1b as a critical mediator of F1 activity, which was supported by favorable in-silico docking interaction predictions. Moreover, BMBR1b knockdown abrogated F1-induced osteogenic signaling, supporting a model wherein F1 engages BMBR1b to activate BMP-responsive SMAD1/5/9.

  Previous data showed that BMP signaling was indispensable for osteoblast commitment and bone formation [17]. The ability of F1 to bind BMPR1b and activate downstream SMAD1/5/9 phosphorylation suggests a unique molecular action, positioning F1 as both an osteogenic stimulator and a potential natural BMP mimetic. These findings represent a distinct advance over previous findings, where most ginsenosides tested exerted skeletal effects indirectly through antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, or Wnt/β-catenin pathways [31]. Accordingly, F1 may be considered a BMBR1b-responsive osteoanabolic modulator that can potentially complement existing therapies.

  Current osteoporosis pharmacotherapy relies on either anti-resorptive or anabolic agents. Bisphosphonates remain first-line agents due to their proven efficacy in reducing fracture risk; however, their long-term use is associated with an increased risk of atypical femoral fractures and osteonecrosis of the jaw [10]. Denosumab, although effective in suppressing bone resorption, can lead to rebound-associated vertebral fractures upon discontinuation [12]. Anabolic therapies, such as teriparatide and romosozumab, increase bone mass yet are constrained by duration limits, high costs, and cardiovascular safety considerations [32,37].

  In contrast, F1 offers several attractive features. As a natural metabolite of ginsenosides with improved bioavailability relative to that of the parental compound, Rg1 [16], F1 combines safety with potent osteoanabolic activity. Unlike bisphosphonates or SERMs, which primarily limit resorption, we found that F1 enhanced osteoblast differentiation and bone formation via BMP-SMAD activation. Moreover, in contrast to parathyroid hormone (PTH) analogs, which act through cAMP-PKA signaling, the mechanism of F1 was rooted in BMPR1b-SMAD signaling; thus, it can potentially provide complementary effects [38,39]. These mechanistic differences, along with its natural origin, highlight F1 as an attractive candidate for integrative osteoporosis management.

  Although our findings established the osteogenic potential of F1, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the in vivo analysis was restricted to trabecular bone in an OVX mouse model, and cortical bone responses were not systematically evaluated. Second, although BMPR1b-SMAD activation was identified as a primary pathway, the possibility of cross-talk with Wnt/b-catenin or mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling was not excluded, and future studies should address these pathways. Third, the pharmacokinetic properties of F1, including its absorption, metabolism, distribution, and systemic clearance, require further definition. Given that ginsenosides are extensively metabolized by the intestinal microbiota [40,41], optimizing delivery methods would be essential for clinical translation. Finally, the long-term safety and efficacy of F1 in humans remains to be established. Moreover, the current study is restricted to osteoblast formation, lacking a detailed analysis of the osteoclasts in mouse model. Future study direction would imply testing the effect of F1-ginsenoside on osteoclast activity.

  Despite these limitations, our findings extend the understanding of ginsenosides in skeletal biology by identifying BMPR1b-SMAD activation as a mechanistic basis for the osteoanabolic activity of F1 and by demonstrating mitigation of OVX-induced bone loss. Future studies should further evaluate the long-term skeletal effects of F1 across both trabecular and cortical compartments, delineating its influence on osteoclast-mediated bone resorption and improving systemic bioavailability through optimized formulation strategies. In addition, studies exploring combinatorial potential with established anabolic agents, including teriparatide or romosozumab, will help advance F1 toward clinical translation and to define its therapeutic positioning and potential synergy in osteoporosis management.


## Conclusions
  In summary, our findings demonstrate that ginsenoside F1 can stimulate osteoblast differentiation and bone formation via BMPR1b-SMAD signaling activation. F1 supplementation effectively rescued trabecular bone mass and microarchitecture in OVX mice, highlighting its potential as a natural anabolic agent for treating osteoporosis. Its distinct mechanism relative to current agents supports further preclinical development and prospective clinical evaluation as a natural osteoanabolic candidate for treating postmenopausal osteoporosis.
