# STAT3 Signaling Inhibitors for Cancer Treatment


## Abstract
  Pharmacological targeting of Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 3 (STAT3) in cancer has a demonstrable antitumor efficacy. However, suitably potent, efficacious, and safe STAT3 inhibitors are scarce, and only a handful have entered clinical trials, limiting our knowledge of the extent of clinical benefit. Outcomes of recently completed trials in advanced cancers range from improved overall survival and complete responses in a cohort of patients with phospho-tyrosine STAT3 positivity to partial responses and progressive disease in unselected patients. Advancements in oligonucleotide technologies and the integration of E3 ligase-specific proteolysis-targeting chimera and molecular glue protein degrader strategies are accelerating the transition of STAT3 inhibitors into clinical testing. This review examines recent STAT3-targeted modalities and their preclinical and clinical activities. It concludes by underscoring the value of biomarker-informed approaches to optimize patient outcomes, combination therapies to improve clinical benefits, and artificial intelligence/machine learning (AI/ML) tools to accelerate development.


## The pathological outcomes of aberrant STAT3 signaling necessitate its therapeutic targeting
  The Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription (STAT) family of cytoplasmic proteins and transcription factors mediates the physiological responses to cytokines and growth factors, including cell growth and differentiation, development, and immune cell functions [1–6]. The family comprises seven members, STAT1, STAT2, STAT3, STAT4, STAT5a, STAT5b, and STAT6 (Figure 1), which share a structurally conserved N-terminal Domain (ND), Coiled-coil Domain (CCD), DNA-Binding Domain (DBD), Linker Domain (LD), Src Homology 2 Domain (SH2D), and the C-terminal Transactivation Domain (TAD). Classical STAT activation requires transient tyrosine (Tyr, Y) phosphorylation (e.g., Y705; See Glossary) mediated by epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), Janus Kinases (JAKs), or Src family kinases (Figure 2). Tyrosine phosphorylation induces dimerization between two Tyr-phosphorylated (pY) STAT monomers (STAT:STAT) via reciprocal pY peptide:SH2D interactions and the translocation of the dimers into the nucleus, where they bind to the consensus DNA response elements in the target gene promoters. Historically, STAT3 is known to be a transcriptional activator for genes promoting cell proliferation, survival, and other biological processes. However, non-canonical STAT3 signaling and functions across several biological contexts have emerged, including roles outside of the nucleus, as a transcriptional repressor, or in the unphosphorylated form [6]. Moreover, the phosphorylation of a conserved serine (Ser, S) in TAD (S727 for STAT3) is known to promote maximal transcriptional activation, and the same S727 phosphorylation also drives STAT3 localization into the inner mitochondrial membrane to support oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and glucose utilization [6–8]. Activated STAT signaling is tightly regulated by protein Tyr phosphatases (PTPs), suppressors of cytokine signaling (SOCS), and protein inhibitors of activated STATs (PIAS) (Figure 2). Conversely, dysregulated STAT pathways drive human diseases like cancer and autoimmunity. While aberrantly active STAT3 drives tumor progression [1–6], targeting it is complex, depending heavily on its cellular context and post-translational modifications [1–6].

  The discovery that STAT3 plays a causal role in human cancers was the initial impetus for targeting STAT3 in therapeutic development. The identification of the first direct STAT3 inhibitor, a peptide, twenty-five years ago marked the beginning of research aimed at developing therapeutic modalities targeting STAT3 activity and its functions. Discovery efforts for STAT3-targeting modalities largely prioritized the STAT3 SH2D and its interactions with the pY peptide, given the domain’s critical importance to dimerization. Many small molecules in this category have demonstrated antitumor efficacy in vivo. Notwithstanding the extensive discovery efforts, SH2D inhibitors have consistently been limited by suboptimal potency and lack of target selectivity, which have limited their clinical development. Other approaches targeting DNA-binding, including small molecules and oligonucleotides (ODNs), have also inhibited STAT3 function and in vivo tumor growth. ODN research has advanced significantly, improving chemical stability, nuclease resistance, and the development of cyclic versions. Additionally, natural products (Box 1) inhibit STAT3 signaling and block in vivo tumor growth, though their poorly understood mechanisms likely involve multiple targets. Finally, a few early SH2D binders and natural product inhibitors act via covalent engagement with STAT3, further muddling the understanding of their mechanisms. The significant challenge in these endeavors has been achieving high potency. In this context, the recently incorporated E3-ligase-mediated proteolysis-targeting chimeras and molecular glue degrader strategies are leading the way in overcoming this rather intractable barrier. Recent successes in integrating protein degrader strategies with both small-molecule and optimized ODN designs have increased the pace of development of STAT3-targeting therapeutics. Beyond traditional approaches, chemical biology strategies and the targeting of STAT3 gene expression have yielded STAT3 inhibitors, including pyrimethamine and atovaquone that advanced to clinical trials [8,9].

  Clinical trials show STAT3 inhibitors successfully suppress pY705-STAT3 in patient tissues. However, therapeutic efficacy varies significantly, ranging from complete responses to disease progression. The modalities have generally been well-tolerated. Collectively, clinical studies prove that routine, biomarker-driven patient selection significantly optimizes therapeutic outcomes. Despite progress, developing suitable STAT3-targeting therapeutics that achieve sustained clinical efficacy while minimizing adverse effects remains a significant challenge. This review will discuss the latest STAT3-targeting modalities in loosely-defined classes for both context and meaning, highlighting their pre-clinical biological activities or clinical outcomes, and conclude with comments on the value of biomarker-stratification and combination therapy with other agents to improve clinical outcomes, and the potential for artificial intelligence/machine learning (AI/ML) to move the field forward faster.


## Mechanism-based classification of STAT3 inhibitors

### STAT3 SH2D binders and dimerization disruptors are improving in potency
  A general trend towards improved potency is emerging for newer SH2D-targeting compounds, albeit incrementally (Table 1). Compound 12 inhibited interleukin-6 (IL-6)/JAK/STAT3 signaling with IC50 of 4.26 μM [10]. Its N-(benzimidazol-5-yl)-1,3,4-thiadiazol-2-amine analog, compound 40, showed improved potency (IC50 of 0.65 μM) in the same assay; however, compound 40 disrupted pY peptide:SH2D interaction in fluorescent polarization (FP) assay, with a weaker potency (IC50 of 10.4 μM). This discrepancy occurs because the former assay is a reporter gene-based that lets inhibitors interfere at any step in the IL-6-induced STAT3-dependent gene expression pathway, while the FP assay isolates just the direct binding of STAT3 to the pY-peptide Compound 40 induced cell growth inhibition, cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in vitro in human prostate (IC50 of 2.97 μM) and breast (IC50 of 3.26 μM) cancer cells that harbor constitutively-active STAT3, and inhibited tumor growth in vivo in prostate cancer xenografts [10]. Compound DB-2B was created through computational modeling as a derivative of the previous inhibitor, LY17. Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) and isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) quantified distinct SH2D binding affinities for DB-2B, with KD values of 1.06 μM and 7.5 μM, respectively [11]. Finally, a cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) validated direct STAT3 engagement and stabilization in colorectal cancer (CRC) cells [11]. Using three complementary assays to verify target binding (Table 1) increases confidence in STAT3 engagement. DB-2B inhibited both constitutive and IL-6-induced pY705-STAT3 without affecting pS727-STAT3 or JAK1/JAK2 induction in CRC cells, suppressed cell proliferation, induced apoptosis and cell cycle arrest, and attenuated stemness at 1–4 μM [11]. It exhibited favorable oral bioavailability and safety and inhibited tumor progression in vivo in CRC models at 10–50 mg/kg delivered orally [11]. Another compound, YY002, showed a high-affinity binding to the both SH2D and STAT3 using complementary biophysical methods [12]. In microscale thermophoresis (MST) assay, YY002 exhibited high-affinity binding to the SH2D (KD 2.24 nM) and full-length STAT3 (KD 18.27 nM), which was further independently confirmed via SPR assay, (KD of 65.3 nM) [12]. It inhibited both STAT3 Tyr705 and Ser727 phosphorylation, STAT3-dependent gene transcription, including cyclin D1, Bcl-2, survivin, and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) induction, mitochondrial oxygen consumption rate and cell growth in vitro in human pancreatic cancer (PDAC) models at nanomolar concentrations [12]. The antitumor effects against PDAC models aligns with the inhibition of STAT3 activation. However, the mechanism through which YY002 inhibits pS727-STAT3, and its subsequent effects on mitochondrial STAT3 localization, remain to be defined. The compound further suppressed PDAC xenograft growth and liver metastasis in mouse syngeneic models and prolonged survival [12]. SS-4 was identified via computational modeling and virtual screening targeting the STAT3 SH2D domain. It inhibited pY705-STAT3 in glioblastoma (GBM) cells, with IC50 of 0.38 μM and cell proliferation, with IC50 of 148–154 nM, and induced apoptosis of GBM cells in vitro, with IC50 of 77–81 nM [13]. Dependency of the biological responses on STAT3 was demonstrated in studies that showed that STAT3 knockout GBM cells were resistant to the antiproliferative effect of SS-4 [13]. The compound suppressed growth of orthotopic tumor xenografts in vivo in glioblastoma (GBM) models [13] (Table 1). The efficacy against intracranial GBM tumors suggested that SS-4 breached the blood-brain barrier to regulate GBM growth. However, the mechanism of how SS-4 inhibited pY705-STAT3 is yet to be determined.

  Micromolar activity, however, continued to be observed for other recent compounds. Compound 10g (a naphthoquinone-furo-piperidine derivative) bound STAT3 with a KD of 8.3 μM in SPR, inhibited pY705-STAT3 and pS727-STAT3 induction, cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro, at 2–7.5 μM, and blocked tumor xenografts growth in vivo in human TNBC models (15–30 mg/kg intra-peritoneal) [14]. The mechanism of inhibition of pS727-STAT3 is unknown. Overall, the antitumor cell responses of 10g against TNBC cells that harbor aberrantly-active STAT3 correlate with the inhibitory effects on STAT3 phosphorylation and downstream target gene expression. Imidazo[1,2- a]pyridine tethered pyrazolines (ITPs), exemplified by 3f, also inhibited both STAT3 Y705 and S727 phosphorylation and the viability of breast cancer cell lines in vitro, with IC50 values of 9.27–23.51 μM [15]. These results were corroborated by the latest studies of other ITPs, including compound 3p and 3n, which inhibited pY705-STAT3 at 2.5–10 μM and the viability of estrogen receptor-positive (ER+) breast cancer cells in vitro with IC50 values of 15 and 55 nM, respectively [16]. Although in silico docking predicts their interactions with SH2D, key questions remain, including the STAT3 binding site(s), the mechanism of engagement with STAT3, and the discrepancy between the inhibitory activities against pY705-STAT3 and cell viability [16]. A quinoline carboxamide, YHO-1701, which was derived from an earlier STAT3 dimerization disruptor, STX-0119, inhibited pY705-STAT3 induction at 0.6–10 μM, and the growth of ovarian cancer cells and ovarian cancer patient-derived cells, with IC50 of 0–67-2.83 μM [17]. Other than detecting decreased STAT3 dimerization in treated ovarian cancer cells, evidence that the YHO-1701 directly binds to STAT3 is absent. Oral administration of 20 mg/kg of YHO-1701 inhibited tumor growth in human ovarian cancer xenograft models and ovarian cancer peritoneal metastasis in mice [17]. Another compound, JKB887, which was designed to target SH2D, inhibited the pY peptide:SH2D interaction in FP assay, with IC50 of 3.5–5.5 μM, blocked STAT3 DNA-binding activity in vitro, with IC50 of 2.2–4.5 μM, inhibited pY-STAT3 and cell growth at 3–10 μM in TNBC cells, and suppressed TNBC xenograft growth in vivo at 3–10 mg/kg [18] (Table 1). The antitumor responses to JKB887 are consistent with its inhibitory effects on STAT3 signaling, including the inhibition of STAT3 target gene expression.

  Recent studies of two previously reported STAT3 SH2D inhibitors, TTI-101 and SH5–07, showed that they suppressed pY705-STAT3 induction, cell proliferation, and cyclin D1 and PCNA expression, promoted cell death in bladder cancer spheroids and organoids, and downregulated CD44 and CD133 expression in cancer stem cells [19]. The two compounds also induced reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, although the mechanism remains unknown, and their overall activities remained in the single- to double-digit micromolar range. The previously reported compound, C188–9, was recently shown to inhibit cell proliferation, viability, and c-Myc expression in breast cancer patients’ specific primary cells (PSPCs) in vitro and the growth of human breast tumor xenografts in vivo [20]. However, the data showed that C188–9 suppressed pY705-STAT3 levels only in a subset of samples, suggesting that its antitumor effects against PSPCs only partly depend on STAT3 signaling (Table 1). Despite the noted progress, the STAT3 SH2D domain or the pY-binding pocket remains a formidable target site, as reflected in the continued low potency of some newer SH2D-targeting inhibitors. The observation that cellular potencies sometimes are better than those measured in cell-free assays also suggests that cellular responses may be driven by off-target effects rather than by STAT3 inhibition alone. A bright spot in the targeting of SH2D is the integration of targeted protein degradation concepts into their design strategy, which is already achieving better STAT3-inhibitory potencies [21–24].


### DNA-binding domain has not been a major focus of targeting
  Not surprisingly, small molecule STAT3 inhibitors have typically blocked STAT3 DNA-binding activity (Table 1), including those that directly bind the SH2D. It has now emerged that some of the originally proposed SH2D binders, including BP-1–102, Cpd188, and Stattic, interact with sites in the STAT3 DBD via covalent mechanisms [25]. More recent azetidine-based small molecules, including H172 and H182, which are derived from BP-1–102 and its analogs, SH4–54 and SH5–07, were observed to bind to the STAT3 DBD via a covalent mechanism that is time-dependent. Accordingly, H172 and H182 inhibited STAT3 DNA-binding activity, with an IC50 of 0.38–0.98 μM [26,27]. Site-directed mutagenesis and mass spectrometry confirmed that azetidine compounds inhibit the STAT3 through a covalent mechanism, specifically mediated by bonds between their para-fluoro motifs and residues STAT3 Cys426 and Cys468. The inhibition of STAT3 activity by H182 suppressed cell viability and proliferation, and induced apoptosis of tumor cells in vitro, and the oral or intraperitoneal administration of H182 at 10–30 mg/kg led to tumor regression in vivo in xenograft models of human TNBC (Table 1). Newly conducted molecular docking and virtual screening of acrylamide chemical library, supported by biophysical assays and mass spectrometry measurement, discovered compound K2 and its analogs that target STAT3 at the CCD and DBD interface [28]. K2 appears to function as a covalent inhibitor. Despite its obvious significance in mediating STAT3 function, the DBD site has not been a major focus of targeting in the past until recently. The collective evidence of strong inhibition of STAT3 functions by the few existing DBD inhibitors highlights the significance and feasibility of targeting this domain. More intentional, thoughtful strategies are needed to advance the development of direct DBD inhibitors.


### Covalent Inhibitors of STAT3 predominantly target cysteine residues
  An expanding group of inhibitors targets STAT3 through covalent interactions (Table 1). Many of these molecules were not intentionally designed as covalent inhibitors; rather, covalent mechanisms of action were discovered by chance while studying the compounds or reviewing their structural characteristics. This is the case for some of the earlier SH2D-binders, which were initially modeled as reversible inhibitors, including S3I-201 and its analogs BP-1–102, SH4–54, and SH5–07, all of which were originally proposed to target the STAT3 pY peptide:SH2D interaction. The discovery of the covalent binding with STAT3 was made through protein thermal shift assay, differential scanning fluorimetry, and high-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry of ligand-protein engagements [25]. The recent azetidine-based small molecules, exemplified by H182 and H172, covalently interacted with the Cys328, Cys426, and Cys468 residues in the STAT3 DBD [26,27,29]. For these compounds, kinetic studies, site-directed mutagenesis, and mass spectrometry revealed that the interaction with Cys468 is the key event driving STAT3 inhibition [26,27]. The four-membered azetidine ring is a critical substructure that imparts structural rigidity and facilitates facile covalent engagement of the para-fluorine motif with the Cys residues at the STAT3 binding site [26,27]. For Stattic and Cpd188, subsequent studies showed they covalently bind to STAT3 [25]. How directed the covalent interactions are and what structural determinants guide them remain unknown. A more recent intentional approach has identified inhibitors that covalently interact with STAT3 at Cys259 in the CCD. A hit compound, K2, and its more potent analog, 6b, covalently bound Cys259 through an allosteric mechanism and inhibited STAT3 with a reasonable potency (IC50 of 2.91 ± 0.66 μM in FP assay) [28] (Table 1). Compared to reversible inhibitors, the covalent inhibition of STAT3 can enhance potency and effectively eliminate the protein and its functions, with a lasting impact on tumor cells. Yet, STAT3-targeting covalent inhibitor design research remains underexplored. A caveat is how to achieve high STAT3 selectivity and thus minimize toxicity.


## Oligonucleotide-based modalities are effective inhibitors of STAT3 functions
  Oligonucleotide modalities (ODNs), including decoys, G-quartets, G-quadruplexes, antisense, siRNA, and shRNA, have long been deployed against STAT3 to achieve effective outcomes (Table 1) [30–34]. These include high-affinity decoy ODNs, which mimic the consensus STAT3 binding sites in target gene promoters and represent one of the earliest ODN strategies to emerge [34]. Decoy ODNs bind STAT3 with a competitive advantage over the endogenous promoter sequences, thereby preventing STAT3:target gene promoter engagement and target gene expression. In earlier in vitro and in vivo studies in models of head and neck squamous carcinoma (HNSCC) and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), a 15-mer double-stranded ODN from the c-fos gene promoter and its subsequent generations repressed STAT3 functions and cell growth and proliferation, induced apoptosis, and inhibited tumor angiogenesis and tumor growth in vivo [30,34]. Subsequent ODN designs incorporated chemical modifications to keep the linear ODNs stabilized against nucleases [30].

  Substantial progress has been made in the development of this class of molecules. Among them, the linear double-stranded 15-mer ODN was ligated to form a cyclic decoy ODN (CS3D) that improved thermal stability and resistance to nucleases (Table 1) [34]. A notable achievement for the decoy ODN strategy is the Phase 1 clinical trial in pet cats with HNSCC for safety and efficacy, which showed that CS3D induced significant differences in selected peripheral blood immune parameters and elevated Programmed Cell Death 1 (PD-1) expression in responder tumors (35% disease control rate) compared to non-responders [34]. Without causing toxicity up to a 10 mg/kg dose, this agent induced dose-dependent antitumor activity in murine and feline HNSCC models, including organoids containing immune components and immunocompetent xenografts [34]. It was observed that CD8+ T cells were required to elicit the antitumor response, underscoring the importance of a competent, functional tumor microenvironment for a full response [34] (Table 1). These studies together highlight advances with the decoy ODN strategy.

  Other ODN examples include the minicircles (mc-1STAT3, MCs), which are 95 bp and contain a STAT3 binding site. These modalities inhibited STAT3 target gene expression and cell proliferation, and induced cell cycle progression changes and apoptosis in human and murine TNBC cells [35]. Intratumorally-injected mc-1STAT3 inhibited tumor growth and metastasis in murine TNBC models [35]. Moreover, demonstrating the broad application of ODNs and the therapeutic significance of targeting STAT3 in non-oncogenic indications, the hydrodynamic tail vein injection of STAT3 ODN in liposomes suppressed liver inflammation and fibrosis in both liver cancer and drug-induced liver injury models in mice [36] (Table 1).

  Antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) are the other types of ODNs that have been shown to be potent inhibitors of STAT3 function and to have biological effects. These modalities are typically single-stranded, 5–20 nucleotides, and are chemically modified to enhance physicochemical properties and stability against nucleolytic degradation. Stabilized ASOs targeting STAT3 mRNA (STAT3 ASOs) decreased both STAT3 mRNA and protein levels, induced antitumor cell effects against hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells in vitro, and inhibited HCC tumor growth in vivo [32]. The other types of ODNs are the G-rich oligonucleotide sequences that are capable of forming inter- and intra-molecular four-stranded structures, including G-quartet (a single, completely flat square plane created by four guanine bases) and G-quadruplex (GQ-ODN) (formed from two or more flat G-quartets layered on top of each other) [37]. In recent studies, T40214, a 16-mer GQ-ODN that targets the STAT3 SH2D and STAT3 dimer, inhibited STAT3 DNA-binding activity and functions in tumor cells [37]. Its stabilized version with a single N2-aryl deoxyguanosine modification suppressed pY705-STAT3 levels, STAT3 target gene expression (Bcl-2, VEGF, and cyclin D1), and cell proliferation, with an IC50 of 0.37–0.39 μM, and induced cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in prostate cancer cells in vitro [37] (Table 1). Significant breakthroughs in the overarching ODN strategy have successfully addressed key physicochemical limitations, advancing these therapies toward clinical application.


## Targeted STAT3 protein degraders have improved potency

### PROTAC STAT3 degraders have higher potency and induce antitumor efficacy in vivo
  Recently incorporated into STAT3 inhibitor discovery, the E3 ligase-specific proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) strategy is showing progress in improving potency. This strategy enables the creation of hetero-bifunctional molecules that, when bound to the target proteins, subjects them to degradation via the ubiquitin-proteasome mechanism [38,39]. SD-36 (Table 1) is one of the first PROTAC-STAT3 degraders and is based on the high-affinity STAT3 SH2D-binding peptidomimetics with pY-mimetic groups that selectively interacted with STAT3 [21]. SD-36 potently induced STAT3 degradation in vitro and in vivo with DC50 of 0.06 μM [21]. It suppressed STAT3-dependent transcriptional network, induced cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, and inhibited cell growth in vitro and tumor xenograft growth in vivo in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and ALCL models [21]. Other recent STAT3-selective PROTAC degraders are SD91 [40], the hydrolytic product of SD36, and SD436 [41], which is based on a new, high-affinity STAT3 ligand with improved chemical stability and a cereblon ligand. Both compounds induced selective and durable STAT3 depletion in tumor tissues and tumor regression in vivo in leukemia and lymphoma models [40,41]. Also in this group are SDL-1, a PROTAC version of S3I-201 [42], which is rather weak and only partially degraded STAT3 in gastric cancer cells at 40 μM concentration [23], and S3D5, a BP-1–102-based PROTAC inhibitor, with a DC50 of 110 nM, which inhibited HepG2 cell proliferation, invasion and migration in vitro [24] (Table 1). Another entity is the PROTAC TSM-1, based on the natural product, Toosendanin (TSN), which bound STAT3 with a KD of 296 nM [43]. Studies of TSM-1 showed it induced STAT3 degradation, with a single-digit micromolar potency, inhibited cell viability and STAT3 target gene expression in vitro, and blocked growth of cell line-derived xenografts (CDX), PDX, and patient-derived organoid models of HNSCC and CRC [43].

  Non-small molecule, oligonucleotide-based PROTAC STAT3 inhibitors have also emerged [44–46] (Table 1). These include POM-STAT3, a double-stranded STAT3 decoy ODN that is conjugated to the E3 ubiquitin ligase ligand [44]. It inhibited NSCLC cell proliferation, with an IC50 of 792 nM [44]. D11-PROTAC is another bifunctional biomolecule composed of a decoy ODN that is fused with the E3 ligases Von Hippe-Lindau (VHL-L) through click chemistry. Treatment with this modality induced STAT3 degradation in multiple cancer cells at concentrations of 0.5–1.5 μM, inhibited cell growth and cell cycle progression, induced apoptosis, suppressed tumor immune evasion, and inhibited tumor xenograft growth in vivo in human breast tumor models [45]. STAT3DPROTAC, which was created from a STAT3 decoy ODN that was conjugated to thalidomide, induced STAT3 degradation in mouse and human B cell lymphoma cells at nanomolar concentrations [46]. To make this treatment target only specific cells, STAT3DPROTAC was conjugated to a CpG oligonucleotide that targets Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9). This created C-STAT3DPROTAC that spontaneously and selectively internalized in TLR9-overexpressing cells, such as lymphoma, myeloid, and B cells, which similarly induced STAT3 degradation and tumor regression in human lymphoma xenografts in mice [46] (Table 1).

  Nanoengineered peptide PROTAC modalities (NP-PROTACs) have also emerged that combine peptide inhibitors and nanoparticles to overcome bioavailability, stability, and permeability barriers. A specific case is NP-PROTACs designed from STAT3-targeting and β-catenin-targeting peptides, each in a PROTAC background, and coupled with DSPE-PEG (1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-polymer polyethylene glycol) via disulfide bonds that facilitated their self-assembly into nanoparticles. However, the NP-PROTACs were less potent and induced STAT3 and β-catenin protein degradation at double-digit micromolar doses, inhibited cell proliferation, with IC50 values of 6.67–9.43 μM in CRC cells in vitro, reversed immunosuppressive microenvironment, and induced synergistic antitumor effects in CRC tumor models in vivo [47] (Table 1). The PROTAC STAT3 degrader strategy is advancing rapidly, with a few modalities already in clinical trials. A major limitation of the PROTAC strategy is the bulkiness of the compounds and the physicochemical challenge that could pose a cell and tissue permeability barrier. The fact that STAT3 protein degradation is being observed in cells and tissues, associated with antitumor efficacy responses in pre-clinical studies, suggests that the PROTAC compounds can cross the cell membrane despite their sizes. Their mechanisms of cellular entry need to be clarified.


### Molecular Glue Degrader inhibitors of STAT3
  Molecular glues (MGs) are monovalent small molecules that can specifically induce complexation between two or more proteins to modify biological functions, such as protein degradation and complex stabilization [39]. Molecular glue-targeted protein degradation strategy has been applied to STAT3 inhibitor design (MG-STAT3 inhibitors) (Table 1), albeit there are no published studies yet of MG-STAT3 inhibitors. A publicly disclosed study at scientific conferences of a STAT3 degrader, KT-333, shows a selective degradation of STAT3 in four anaplastic T cell lymphoma (ALCL) lines, with DC 50 of 2.5–11.8 nM, downregulation of STAT3 target genes (SOCS3, IL-2RA and GRZMB), and tumor growth inhibition of SU-DHL-1 and SUP-M2 ALCL xenograft models in response to treatment with the agent (see Conference Abstracts/Websites, I–IV). A pre-print of a study of KT-333, in the AML and Venetoclax (Ven)-resistant AML models showed STAT3 degradation, induction of apoptosis in vitro, decreased Mcl-1 expression, and tumor growth inhibition in vivo in xenografts after a two-week treatment, and a significant improvement in survival in Ven-resistant PDX models [48]. Another molecule, KYM-003 was publicly disclosed at scientific conferences to potently and selectively induce STAT3 protein degradation in diverse blood cancer cells and PBMCs, with a DC50 <100 nM, leading to inhibition of STAT3 downstream genes (SOCS3, MYC, and PIM1) [39] (Table 1) (see V, Conference Abstracts/Websites). KYM-003 also strongly induced STAT3 degradation and inhibited tumor growth in vivo in xenograft models of heme malignancies [39]. The MG strategy holds great promise for advancing STAT3-targeting therapeutics development, although it remains to be broadly harnessed.


## Clinical trial outcomes of STAT3-targeting modalities are mixed, albeit with some bright spots
  While no direct STAT3 inhibitors are currently approved for clinical use, several are in trials for various tumors (Table 2) and have proven well-tolerated. Because biomarker-driven patient stratification maximizes efficacy, clinical outcomes improve significantly when selection is guided by STAT3 activation markers like pY705-STAT3 expression. Napabucasin was one of the first modalities that entered clinical trials, has undergone multiple Phase I, II, and III studies with varying responses [49]. A highlight from these studies is the critical value of biomarker stratification. Specifically, it was observed that pY705-STAT3-positive status correlates with prolonged overall survival in refractory advanced CRC patients. In contrast, evaluating the total unselected population yielded no significant survival differences [50].

  Results from a first-in-human Phase I trial of the JAK/STAT3 pathway inhibitor,WP1066, in recurrent malignant GBM patients showed progressive disease in all eight patients with radiographic follow-ups, despite the inhibition of systemic immune pY705-STAT3, and no treatment-related deaths (ClinicalTrials.gov. NCT0194123)I [51]. WP1066 was evaluated in a Phase I clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov. NCT04334863)II as a single agent in children with refractory and progressive or recurrent malignant brain tumors, with results showing the drug was safe, with treatment-related adverse events being Grade 1–2 (diarrhea and nausea most common), overall survival was 4.9 months [52]. Moreover, the same treatment flipped the immune profile in patients’ PBMCs by down-regulating suppressive regulatory T-cells (Tregs), expanding circulating CD4+ and CD8+ cytotoxic T-cells, and promoting inflammatory TNFα signaling in myeloid lineages (ClinicalTrials.gov. NCT04334863)II [52]. A Phase 1 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov. NCT03195699)III of TTI-101 in sixty-four patients with advanced solid tumors showed it was well tolerated, no DLTs, and confirmed partial responses across tumor types, suggesting TTI-101 as a promising indication in advanced metastatic solid tumors, which has led to a Phase II clinical trial in patients with locally advanced or metastatic, unresectable HCC (ClinicalTrials.gov. NCT05440708)IV that is currently ongoing [53] (Table 2).

  One of the first STAT3 degraders, KT-333, has advanced to Phase 1a/1b clinical trial for safety, tolerability and PK/PD in adult patients with relapsed or refractory (R/R) lymphomas, large granular lymphocytic leukemia (LGL-L), T-cell prolymphocytic leukemia, and solid tumors (Phase 1a), and the safety, tolerability and the PK/PD in peripheral T-cell lymphoma, cutaneous T-cell lymphoma, LGL-L, and solid tumors (Phase 1b) (ClinicalTrials.gov. NCT05225584)V (Table 2) (see Resources). Collectively, the clinical trial results are mixed, although the number of trials is too small to support any comprehensive assessment of the clinical impact of inhibiting STAT3 activity in patients.


## Combining STAT3 inhibitors with other agents enhanced biological and clinical responses.
  While studies of STAT3 inhibitors prioritize its use as a standalone agent in pre-clinical and clinical trial settings, a few other studies lend support for their combinations with other therapeutics (Table 2). However, past combination studies have largely taken a simplistic approach, pairing STAT3 inhibitors with conventional chemotherapy. They often miss the opportunity to use agents with complementary mechanisms that maximize the impact of STAT3 inhibitors on STAT3-dependent gene expression, mitigate resistance, and maintain safety. Recent pre-clinical combination therapy studies showed that LLL12B enhanced the antitumor effects of cisplatin in medulloblastoma and ovarian cancer models in vitro and vivo [54,55]. LLL12B and olaparib (PARP inhibitor) combined produced significantly enhanced responses in both BRCA-mutant and BRCA-wild-type TNBC models [56]. Moreover, combining H182 and cisplatin or paclitaxel led to enhanced responses in TNBC cells compared to either agent alone [26]. Similarly, the inhibition of STAT3 activity by R001 sensitized TNBC cells to cisplatin and paclitaxel [57]. Additionally, YHO-1701 and sorafenib together produced stronger antiproliferative effects in vitro and antitumor efficacy in vivo in OSCC models [58]. The combination of C188–9 and paclitaxel induced stronger antitumor effects against human breast cancer xenografts in mice than either inhibitor alone [20]. Furthermore, treatment with stabilized modified STAT3 ASO and sorafenib combined led to enhanced efficacy against sorafenib-resistant HCC xenografts in vivo [32]. Moreover, treatment of paclitaxel-resistant ovarian cancer cells with both WP1066 and paclitaxel reversed the drug resistance to paclitaxel [59]. Treatment with both napabucasin and chemotherapeutic agents, including doxorubicin, also led to enhanced responses against DLBCL and neuroblastoma models [60,61]. Pre-clinical studies showed treatment with napabucasin and the proteasome inhibitor, MG-132, together led to an enhanced response against human ovarian cancer cells [62], and napabucasin further synergized with Paclitaxel to induce cytotoxic responses against paclitaxel-resistant TNBC cells [63] (Table 2).

  Clinical trial data for combination therapies (Table 2) are mixed, highlighting that future pairings with STAT3 inhibitors require a more strategic, mechanism-based approach rather than relying solely on conventional chemotherapy. A Phase III trial of napabucasin with paclitaxel (BRIGHTER) in pretreated advanced gastric or gastroesophageal junction (GEJ) adenocarcinoma showed improved survival (ClinicalTrials.gov. NCT02178956)VI [64]. In advanced cancer, combining BBI608 with immunotherapies (ipilimumab, pembrolizumab, or nivolumab) was well-tolerated (ClinicalTrials.gov. NCT02467361)VII (See Resources) Table 2. Notably, BBI608 combined with nivolumab yielded durable disease stabilization (7.4 to 12.1 months) in five patients, and extended overall survival (48.2 to 53.0 months) in four patients [65]. In a Phase I trial for metastatic CRC (mCRC), adding FOLFIRI and bevacizumab to napabucasin yielded variable outcomes; the best response was stable disease in two patients (50.0%) (ClinicalTrials.gov. NCT02641873)VIII [66] (Table 2). A randomized Phase III CanStem303C study of napabucasin with FOLFIRI showed that in patients with previously treated mCRC, adding napabucasin to FOLFIRI did not improve overall survival (ClinicalTrials.gov. NCT02753127)IX [67] (Table 2). Moreover, in the open-label, Phase III CanStem111P study (ClinicalTrials.gov. NCT02993731)X, adding napabucasin to nab-paclitaxel and gemcitabine yielded no overall survival improvement in adults with treatment-naive metastatic PDAC, although its safety profile aligned with previous reports [68].

  The STAT3 ASO danvatirsen (AZD9150) has proven safe but lacks clear clinical benefits across multiple combinations. In relapsed/refractory DLBCL, combinations with durvalumab (anti-PD-L1) antibody) (ClinicalTrials.gov. NCT02549651)XI [69] and the BTK inhibitor acalabrutinib (NCT03527147)XII [70] demonstrated limited antitumor efficacy, though the latter was generally tolerable with only one dose-limiting toxicity (Grade 3 liver transaminase). Similarly, the randomized Phase II PEMDA-HN study in recurrent/metastatic HNSCC (ClinicalTrials.gov. NCT05814666)XIII showed no improvement in outcomes compared to pembrolizumab alone (see Conference Abstracts/Websites VI) (Table 2). While STAT3-targeting combinations show mixed clinical outcomes, their full therapeutic potential hinges on optimizing synergy. A key step is determining how these inhibitors alter the TME to boost immune cell activity.


## Concluding remarks and future perspective
  Extensive preclinical data highlights the antitumor potential of STAT3 inhibitors, yet the transition to clinical-stage drugs remains constrained. This lack of clinical modalities impedes the development of biomarker-driven patient selection and obscures optimal treatment strategies. Moving forward, the fundamental challenge to therapeutic success is the absence of a clearly druggable pocket on the STAT3 protein. Yet, the field has advanced, driven by highly potent preclinical agents like covalent inhibitors and stable ODN therapeutics. Notably, integrating protein degradation strategies into STAT3 drug design has unlocked unprecedented nanomolar-to-sub-nanomolar potencies, accelerating the pipeline toward clinical trials (Tables 2 and 3). PROTAC and MG degrader strategies have produced potent clinical candidates like the STAT3 degrader KT-333 (Tables 2 and 3), which recently completed trials in adults with refractory LGL leukemia and solid tumors (NCT05225584)V. Additionally, ODN-based platforms, such as AZD9150 (danvatirsen) in humans and CS3D in companion animals with HNSCC (Tables 2 and 3), further confirm the safety and tractability of targeting STAT3 in a clinical setting.

  Biomarker-driven cohort selection is crucial for improving clinical outcomes. In a Phase III trial for refractory advanced CRC, for example, napabucasin prolonged overall survival in pY705-STAT3-positive patients compared to placebo, but offered no benefit in the unselected population (Table 2) [50]. The biomarker strategy expands beyond pY705-STAT3 to include STAT3 target genes, serving as established indicators of pathway activation and targeted inhibition [71,72]. For example, by using this gene signature to stratify GBM patients into STAT3-high and -low cohorts, inhibitor treatment demonstrated selective suppression of cell viability and tumorigenicity in orthotopic mouse xenografts [72]. Questions remain regarding the specific cell types that populate a bulk tumor and their clinical contributions to treatment outcomes. Additionally, the impact of STAT3 inhibition on systemic immunity must be defined to prevent severe adverse effects.

  Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) are accelerating STAT3 inhibitor discovery by optimizing binding prediction, toxicity profiling, and biomarker identification. There are already efforts to improve the predictive performance and interpretability of current models, as exemplified by the building of a fingerprint-enhanced graph (FPG) attention network model that integrates sequence-based fingerprints and structure-based graph representations to predict STAT3 inhibitors [73]. During the testing of FPG against many other predictive models with diverse representations and algorithm combinations, it was reported that the FPG-based model achieved the best predictive performance and outperformed all the others for identifying suitable STAT3 inhibitors [73]. Moreover, using transfer learning and virtual screening across a comprehensive dataset of STAT3 inhibitors, a generative AI model was developed for the discovery of novel candidate inhibitors [74]. The library of diverse compounds that emerged from AI-based model was then subjected to molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations, leading to the discovery of two benzotriazole compounds, HG106 and HG110 [74]. Studies of the benzotriazoles showed they inhibited pY705-STAT3 and nuclear translocation, suppressed cell growth and colony formation, and induced apoptosis in NSCLC cells in vitro [74]. The mechanism of inhibition of STAT3 phosphorylation by the two benzotriazoles has yet to be elucidated. The AI/ML platforms are also guiding the choice of modalities for combination therapy regimens with STAT3 inhibitors [75].

  While STAT3 inhibitors are primarily used in oncology due to the broad impact of dysregulated STAT3 signaling, pre-clinical research suggests they also hold promise for autoimmune diseases [76]. Consequently, optimism remains high for STAT3 inhibitors, though clinical experience with JAK/STAT inhibitors cautions that gastrointestinal perforations may signal systemic toxicity [77]. Combining STAT3 inhibitors with complementary therapeutics could mitigate these risks. This approach offers a path to durable efficacy at lower, less toxic doses.
