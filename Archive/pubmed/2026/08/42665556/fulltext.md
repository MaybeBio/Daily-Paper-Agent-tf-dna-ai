# Sodium Channel Blockers Demonstrate Binding Affinity for the Tandem Tudor Domain of the Epigenetic Hub UHRF1


## Abstract
  The tandem Tudor domain (TTD) of UHRF1 is a compelling epigenetic target for novel cancer therapeutics. Here, we integrate theoretical simulations, sophisticated biophysical evaluations and cellular assays to characterize potent TTD binders. Screening of small drug‐ and fragment‐like collections identifies several TTD ligands, with the most promising hit being the local anesthetic hydroxyprocaine. The ligand is characterized in terms of its binding requisites by calorimetry, affording a K d of 1.46 μM and a well‐balanced thermodynamic profile. Molecular dynamics simulations combined with heat capacity measurements and osmotic stress titrations confirm that hydroxyprocaine binds stably to the TTD by displacing approximately 26 interfacial water molecules upon complexation. A targeted follow‐up screen focusing on sodium channel blockers yields two additional, although less promising hits, mexiletine and triamterene. In the DU145 prostate cancer cell line, hydroxyprocaine treatment significantly up‐regulates key downstream targets including the tumor‐suppressor p53 and, to a lesser degree, the stress and inflammation regulators p38 and p65, respectively, while exhibiting very low cytotoxicity. Finally, a previously undocumented interdomain interaction between TTD and its N‐terminal adjacent Ubiquitin‐like domain is reported, introducing a novel, potentially druggable UHRF1 regulatory feature. Together, these findings establish hydroxyprocaine as a highly viable chemical scaffold for TTD‐targeted drug development.


## Introduction
  Drug discovery targeting epigenetic proteins aims at chemically reprogramming the genome in a therapeutic way [1]. The specific focus is on reversible post‐translational modifications of histones and DNA methylation [2, 3]. These targets can be categorized into functional groups such as writers (e.g., DNA methyltransferases and histone acetyltransferases), erasers (e.g., histone deacetylases and lysine demethylases), and readers (e.g., bromodomain‐containing proteins) [4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. Their primary therapeutic perspective is to reverse aberrant gene expression patterns such as silencing of tumor suppressor genes or activation of oncogenes without altering the primary DNA sequence. Current discovery pipelines leverage sophisticated approaches to navigate the highly conserved binding pockets of these proteins. For instance, the development of BET bromodomain inhibitors requires isoform selectivity among closely related bromodomains. Alternative approaches involve targeted protein degradation using PROTACs for specifically degrading epigenetic targets rather than just inhibiting their enzymatic activity. Furthermore, the integration of AI‐driven generative models is accelerating the identification of epigenetic module ligands, reducing the limitations of traditional first‐generation epi‐drugs [9]. This precision‐led approach aims for therapeutic interventions restoring the cellular memory to a homeostatic state, thus marking a paradigm shift in molecular medicine.

  The epigenetic module UHRF1 (Ubiquitin‐like with PHD and Ring Finger domains 1) represents a particularly attractive target for drug discovery, as it acts as a molecular hub coordinating DNA methylation and histone modifications [10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. Its main functionality is maintenance methylation of DNA by recruiting DNMT1 to hemi‐methylated CpG sites during the S‐phase of the cell cycle [15]. From a structural perspective, UHRF1 is a multidomain protein comprising a tandem Tudor domain (TTD), a PHD, an SRA, and a RING domain, hence offering considerable opportunities for being targeted by traditional orthosteric inhibitors [16, 17]. However, a limited number of compounds targeting either of its domains have been reported to date [17, 18]. With respect to the SRA domain, molecules designed to mimic the cytosine ring aim to effectively occupy the 5‐methylcytosine (5mC) binding pocket. Optimization thus focuses on achieving high shape complementarity to the flipping mechanism UHRF1 uses to recognize DNA. Previous efforts of our group have resulted in the identification of uracil derivatives NSC20116, NSC232003, and NSC232005 [19, ‍20]. These were among the first small molecules identified to specifically target the SRA domain and have been extensively used as chemical tools to study the role of UHRF1 in different settings [14, 21, 22]. By occupying the 5mC pocket, they are thought to competitively inhibit UHRF1 ability to recognize methylated cytosine, thus preventing DNMT1 recruitment and leading to global DNA hypomethylation. This activity has been demonstrated in HCT116, MCF7, and U251 cancer cells, with the related IC50 values ranging from 15 to 25 μM. Another aspect of the uracil‐based ligands lies in their role as scaffolds for hybridization. A representative example is compound H93 which integrates the uracil functional group of NSC232003 with the hydroxamate moiety of the HDAC inhibitor Vorinostat [23]. This hybrid allowed for the simultaneous disruption of DNA methylation and histone deacetylation, overcoming the limited anticancer efficacy seen with NSC232003 alone.

  In the present study, our efforts are focused on the TTD of the UHRF1 protein. In the architecture of UHRF1, the TTD serves as a critical epigenetic reader that specifically recognizes and binds to di‐ and tri‐methylated Lysine 9 of Histone 3 (H3K9me2/3) [24, 25]. In this sense, the TTD is a sophisticated regulatory module that dictates the spatial recruitment of UHRF1 to heterochromatin. It consists of two subdomains (Tudor1 and Tudor2) folded into a structure that gives rise to two distinct druggable sites. The first is a small hydrophobic cavity composed of four aromatic residues and characterized as the TTD aromatic cage. It is responsible for the interaction between UHRF1 and the methylated histone lysine quaternary ammonium group through extensive cation–pi interactions. The second druggable cavity of TTD can be found in close proximity to the aromatic cage and is defined as the arginine binding site. This site has been found to contribute to the UHRF1 internal dynamics toward defining the equilibrium between a closed, inactive and an open, fully functional conformation of the protein with capacity to bind H3 and hemimethylated DNA [26]. This is achieved by a complex network of interactions involving TTD in addition to separate UHRF1 components such as a key arginine residue on the linker connecting TTD with the adjacent PHD domain and a C‐terminal polybasic region (PBR) [24, 25, 27]. Regarding the biological functionality of UHRF1, the tandem nature of TTD is essential; while a single Tudor domain often shows weak affinity, coordinated action of both domains can increase binding affinity of substrates and provide the specificity required to distinguish H3K9me3 from other methylated species. The TTD is part of a crosstalk mechanism where binding of H3K9me3 induces a conformational change that promotes SRA binding to hemi‐methylated DNA. Thus, TTD is thought to be a high‐value target for disrupting the UHRF1‐mediated epigenetic inheritance loop. Early discovery efforts utilized H3‐like peptide sequences as templates to design stabilized, non‐natural peptide analogs that compete for the aromatic cage. By exploring tert‐butyl norleucine as a neutral and hydrophobic bioisoster of the cationic trimethyl‐lysine, the TTD has been found to demonstrate unique binding properties which could thus sustain the development of highly specific UHRF1‐targeting inhibitors [28]. Of high interest is the mouse ortholog of STELLA protein, which acts as a natural UHRF1 inhibitor by showing remarkably high affinity (K d of ≈5.8 nM) for the linked TTD‐PHD segment of UHRF1 [29]. Efforts in developing mSTELLA‐derived peptidomimetics and mRNA‐delivered versions aim at developing therapeutics to prevent UHRF1 from initiating aberrant DNA methylation in colorectal and breast cancers [29].

  The greatest interest in current research in terms of medicinal chemistry, however, focuses on identifying small molecules that mimic the trimethyl‐lysine group and could hence be used as inhibitors of the TTD functionality. Only limited TTD ligands have been reported and they totally center on competing with either the aromatic cage or the Arg‐site of the domain. In this direction, the tricyclic thienopyrrolotriazine derivative NV01 was identified via high‐throughput screening as a ligand of the H3K9me3 pocket with low micromolar affinity and was subsequently optimized to analogs such as NV03 affording a K d of ≈2.4 μM [30]. In another study, 2,4‐lutidine was reported as a TTD ligand discovered by a fragment‐based screen, affording an IC50 value of 29 μM [31]. The same publication introduced the naphthyridine fragment‐like analogue F1957−0088 as a suitable compound for validating TTD druggability, thus demonstrating the feasibility of using small molecules to block UHRF1‐chromatin association [31]. The affinity of 2,4‐lutidine for TTD was determined by a different study, affording a K d value of 53.9 μM [32]. In the same study, simple analogs of lutidine such as 5‐amino‐2,4‐dimethylpyridine were also found to bind TTD, with X‐ray crystallography providing a structural rationale for specifically targeting the Arg‐site of the domain [32]. An additional low MW molecule, the iminopyridine Compound‐1, was discovered as a TTD ligand of UHRF1 with a K d of 16 μM, although this original screening endeavor was designed to discover ligands for the Tudor domain of the survival motor neuron protein [33].

  In this report, we follow on our previous results on the SRA domain to extend our investigation of UHRF1 druggability by focusing on its TTD domain [19, 20]. We screen two selected compound collections and we identify molecules with low micromolar binding affinities for the protein, including the most potent TTD ligand reported to date. We pursue these compounds by a sub‐scaffold search and we confirm a small group of TTD ligands amongst which, interestingly, clinically used drugs with a high repurposing potential are included. We apply isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) experiments to explore their binding thermodynamics with emphasis on the most promising ligand, an ester type local anesthetic. We then use advanced calorimetric approaches involving titrations under osmotic stress and heat capacity measurements combined with long unbiased molecular simulations in an effort to offer a theoretical basis for understanding the binding requisites of TTD ligands. We then provide preliminary proof demonstrating a promising bioactivity profile of TTD ligands toward modulating expression of key UHRF1‐related genes. Moreover, we provide the first experimental evidence for a previously unconfirmed intramolecular interaction between TTD and its adjacent UBL domain, which can possibly serve as a highly original target for developing bioactive compounds pharmacologically modulating UHRF1 functionality.


## Results and Discussion

### Initial Screening and Thermodynamic Validation of Hits
  The chemical space that was opted to be evaluated for TTD ligands was comprised by two distinct in‐house compound collections with high structural diversity. The first has been assembled by molecules available at the NCI/DTP Repository and contained 205 entries freely provided by NCI/DTP to NKUA. This collection demonstrates high conformity with drug‐likeness structural and physicochemical requisites, low occurrence of PAINS features and a notable lead‐like character (summarized in Figure S1). The second collection was a fragment‐like set consisting of 105 low molecular weight molecules which show high agreement with Rule‐of‐3 features (summarized in Figure S2) originally assembled at NKUA. Compounds from the drug‐like collection were screened in silico by the implementation of two orthogonal methods, docking‐scoring calculations (Glide algorithm, Schrodinger Inc.) and 3D similarity evaluation (ROCS algorithm, OpenEye Inc.), and the top‐ranked poses were visually inspected with respect to their predicted interactions with key residues either at the aromatic cage (F152, Y188, and Y191) or at the Arg‐site (D142, M224, W238, and F278) of the TTD domain. After visual inspection, a number of 25 compounds were advanced to experimental assessment of their TTD binding by the use of differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF) and of these, seven compounds were progressed to a second DSF round for validation. Several molecules affording unreasonable ΔT m values at the primary screen (such as NSC204956, NSC278006, and NSC647098) were discarded, as they demonstrated either serious interference issues with the DSF dye or autofluorescence. After the confirmatory DSF round, only three of the assessed compounds resulted in a significant and reproducible shift of their T m values with no interference uncertainties; these were NSC12480 (ΔT m: −2.4 ± 0.1 °C), NSC251964 (ΔT m: +4.7 ± 0.3 °C), and NSC640647 (ΔT m: +4.6 ± 0.3 °C). The three molecules were advanced to ITC experiments for the determination of their binding affinities (K d) against TTD. However, only two out of the three initial hits could be confirmed as TTD binders and these were compounds NSC12480 (or hydroxyprocaine) and the purine derivative NSC251964. Hydroxyprocaine afforded a K d value of 1.46 ± 0.29 μM (at 25 °C), a 1:1 stoichiometry (n: 1.04 ± 0.03) and a balanced enthalpic–entropic binding, with a ΔH value of −2.93 ± 0.11 kcal/mol and a −TΔS value of −5.03 kcal/mol (ligand efficiency of 0.44 kcal/mol per heavy atom). On the other hand, the analogue NSC251964 afforded purely endothermic binding, with a K d of 1.05 ± 0.71 μM (at 25 °C) rendering it the most potent TTD ligand reported to date, a less expected 1:2 stoichiometry (n: 0.48 ± 0.04) probably indicating strongly—or merely—entropic binding with a ΔH value of +2.76 ± 0.28 kcal/mol and −TΔS of −10.92 kcal/mol (Figure 1D), affording a ligand efficiency value of 0.41 kcal/mol per heavy atom. Notably, the ligand efficiency of lutidine was determined at 0.72 kcal/mol per heavy atom based on the published K d value at 20 °C [32].

  With respect to the smaller fragment‐like collection, all compounds were subjected to DSF screening without any previous prioritization by in silico techniques. From this set, seven structurally diverse compounds afforded a significant ΔT m value and their binding modes were explored by subsequent docking‐scoring calculations. Their structures and related T m shifts can be found at the Supporting Information section (Table S1). Interestingly, while the confirmed hits derived from the previously discussed drug‐like collection afforded both positive and negative ΔT m values, all seven hits derived from the fragment collection resulted in positive shifts, ranging from +1.9 to +10.2 °C. The structures and related ΔT m values are shown in the (Table S2). However, none of these was deemed as a promising hit fitting the purpose of the present study in terms of their capacity to validate and explore the drug‐likeness character of TTD. Hence, they were not further pursued in this work, but they comprise useful starting points for a fragment‐based TTD drug or chemical probe development endeavor.

  As discussed above, the two most promising hits of the screen were NSC12480 and NSC251964. Hydroxyprocaine (NSC12480) is a sodium channel blocker, which has been used in the past as a local anesthetic. This hydroxylated analogue of procaine belongs to the ester type of local anesthetics and has been withdrawn from current clinical practice mainly due to allergic reaction concerns. Decomposition of the ITC results for hydroxyprocaine into enthalpic and entropic contributions suggests its affinity for TTD involves both stabilizing pairwise intermolecular interactions as well as disorder increase upon complexation, possibly arising from favorable solvent reorganization dynamics. Conversely, NSC251964, which is a rather lipophilic phenyl‐substituted purinone, demonstrates marginally higher affinity than NSC12480, yet binding in this case seems to originate exclusively by a favorable entropic term combined with a destructive enthalpy. The most problematic issue was, however, its binding stoichiometry. In particular, ITC afforded an equilibrium achieved at a ratio of one protein molecule versus two NSC251964 molecules. The isotherm was optimally fitted to an independent binding model and, notably, an effort to analyze the data by a two‐site sequential binding fit did not afford sound thermodynamic values or credible statistics. As TTD has two experimentally confirmed binding cavities (presented in detail before), a 2:1 ratio does not seem unreasonable. On the other side though, an equal K d value for both sites seems highly unlikely. Two possible explanations would be either a very weak interaction at the secondary binding site (whichever this is), a very low thermal signature at this site, or a combination of both factors, which would bring this particular secondary site K d beyond ITC measurement limits. Notably, analogous ambiguities have been observed in the case of 2,4‐lutidine, a typical TTD ligand. Whereas 2,4‐lutidine has been studied by ITC and afforded an unambiguous 1:1 binding ratio [32], its cocrystal structure with TTD shows two lutidine molecules bound to one TTD polypeptide, simultaneously occupying the aromatic cage and the Arg‐site [31]. This finding can be possibly explained as a crystallographic artifact formed either by the high concentration of 2,4‐lutidine used at the cocrystallization stage or by nonbiologically relevant stabilizing crystal contacts. The chance of a completely silent enthalpic binding at the secondary site could also explain the stoichiometry recorded by ITC, yet this was deemed as a less likely explanation. Nonetheless, the fact that NSC251964 diverges from a definite 1:1 binding interaction combined with the documented uncertainties in the stoichiometry of lutidine were factors strongly arguing against prioritization of this particular hit for further exploration. An additional drawback related to NSC251964 was the difficulty in assessing its exact structural identity. As those bioactive compounds were derived from public collections, validation of their structures was imperative to ensure relevancy of biological assays with any subsequent structure–activity relationships notions. Hence, a detailed analysis based on high‐resolution MS/MS was performed, which unambiguously confirmed the identity of NSC12480 (as well as the two follow‐up hits presented in the next section) but could not fully confirm the structure of NSC251964 (see Figures S10–S13 and Table S3). This particular analogue carries a labile N‐methoxy group which, under intense conditions such as those at the ionization stage of MS, is susceptible to various cleavage or rearrangement reactions with the most probable being a Meisenheimer‐type addition [34, 35]. In its MS spectrum there are indications for the existence of positional isomers with respect to the N‐methoxy group (Figure S13). As a result, this compound was not further pursued in the current study.


### Targeted Secondary Screening and Thermodynamic Validation of Derived Hits
  After reasoning for a lower priority as to the most potent hit, the investigation focused on hydroxyprocaine, a highly competent hit by itself. In this direction, its promising TTD binding affinity combined with its highly favorable physicochemical properties and well‐established drug‐likeness prompted the investigation of different sodium channel blockers as potential TTD ligands. To this direction, the LOPAC collection of pharmaceutically active compounds was considered as a targeted chemical space for identifying bioactive scaffolds characterized by this specific mechanism of action (MoA). A second round of screening was thus performed. After a MoA assessment of the 1280 entries of LOPAC, a number of 20 molecules with an established pharmacological MoA related to their affinity toward Na+ ion channels were selected and subjected to DSF experiments. Of those compounds, eight afforded absolute ΔT m values higher than 1.5 °C and were advanced to validation of their TTD binding by ITC (Figure 2). The local anesthetics lidocaine (amide type) and tetracaine (ester type) were also included in this set regardless of their lower absolute T m shifts (+0.7 ± 0.6 and −1.3 ± 0.6 °C, respectively) due to their apparent structural similarity and almost identical pharmacological properties with the hydroxyprocaine hit. The ITC experiments confirmed only two of the total ten assessed compounds as TTD binders. These were mexiletine (a cardiac Na+ channel blocker) and triamterene (a renal Na+ channel blocker), both clinically approved drugs with wide therapeutic applications. Mexiletine afforded a K d of 2.72 ± 0.99 (at 25 °C), a 1:1 stoichiometry (n: 1.27 ± 0.07) with the ΔH value at +2.20 ± 0.17 kcal/mol and a favorable −TΔS at −9.80 kcal/mol (ligand efficiency of 0.58 kcal/mol per heavy atom) while triamterene resulted to a K d value of 2.60 ± 1.49 (at 25 °C), a 1:1 stoichiometry (n: 1.24 ± 0.1), a ΔH value of +3.84 ± 0.47 kcal/mol and an also favorable −TΔS of −11.46 kcal/mol with a ligand efficiency value of 0.40 kcal/mol per heavy atom. The stoichiometric ratio was determined as 1:1 for both drugs. However, neither of these two follow‐up hits was characterized by a favorable enthalpic component in their binding profile, which would permit a more straightforward future optimization of their TTD affinities by means of rational design.


### Exploration of Hydroxyprocaine Binding Mode
  The thermodynamic study of the four hits verified hydroxyprocaine as the most promising among the identified binders of TTD. Although no longer in clinical use, its overall safety and optimal pharmacokinetic characteristics render it a highly promising scaffold for drug repositioning studies. For any such endeavor to succeed yet, the SAR exploration of its features is utterly required. In the absence of any crystallographic data, the study was oriented toward exploring its exact interaction geometry by the use of calorimetric experiments and molecular simulations. The first effort to determine at which of the two druggable cavities (Arg‐site or aromatic cage) the recognition between hydroxyprocaine and TTD takes place was performed by molecular simulations. Several models were developed for either cavities by fine‐tuning critical docking parameters [36] (please see Experimental section) and their success in reproducing the exact crystal pose of a template TTD ligand (iminopyridine analogue Compound‐1 and respective structure 4QQD) was assessed (Table S2). The parameters affording the lowest root–mean‐square displacement (RMSD) of the ligand with respect to its reference crystallographic position in each cavity were subsequently used to perform docking simulations of hydroxyprocaine but also of NSC251964, mexiletine, and triamterene. The results indicated the Arg‐site as the most probable cavity for all four confirmed ligands, as these docking scores systematically outperformed the respective scores recorded at the aromatic cage. This notion is in accordance with the overall topology of the two cavities and their apparent differences in terms of concavity and available volume, which render the Arg‐site more competent to accommodate bulkier ligands compared to the aromatic cage. To quantify this hypothesis, the CASTpFold algorithm was employed. This tool is based on the alpha shape method and it can accurately locate and measure concave volumes on protein surfaces [37]. The calculation was performed on the 5YY9 crystal structure, in which both TTD cavities are fully occupied by parts of the LIG1K126me3 peptide and thus open and properly shaped. Results unambiguously ranked the Arg‐site cavity at the top, with an area of 156.6 Å2 and a volume of 41.4 Å3 compared to the second‐ranked aromatic cage, with significantly lower area and volume values of 113.5 Å2 and 24 Å3, respectively (Figure S4).

  As a second step, the assessment of the heat capacity change (ΔC p) upon hydroxyprocaine binding to TTD was performed. By repeating the ITC experiments at 5 °C, a ΔC p value of −0.18 kcal/mol·K (ΔH 0 temperature of 8.8 °C) was measured (Figures 3A and S5). This slightly negative change is typical for such protein–ligand systems and usually (but not solely) reflects a change in solvent‐accessible surface areas (SASA) [38]. To further explore this prospect, osmotic stress titrations were applied. The basic result of such an experiment is the change in the number of water molecules upon the process of ligand binding [39, 40, 41, 42, 43]. In case of water release upon binding, the osmotic pressure applied by the osmolyte to water and the subsequent decrease in its activity facilitates its removal from the protein interface toward the bulk solvent where the osmolyte molecules are concentrated. In this study, betaine was used as the osmolyte and the osmolality ranged from 0 to 800 mOsm, as previously utilized in similar systems [44, 45]. Prior to the ITC experiments with betaine, its effects on TTD stability were assessed by DSF in a similar range of osmolalities (Figure 3B), where no destabilization issues were determined. This result may additionally indicate that decreasing water activity by adding osmolytes stabilizes the protein state with the smaller exposed surface area, in this case the ligand bound state. Concerning the ITC titrations, the increase of betaine concentration resulted to hydroxyprocaine binding becoming more endothermic (Figure S6) and slightly stronger. The fact of increasing betaine concentrations strengthening hydroxyprocaine affinity for TTD suggests a release of water molecules upon binding. By applying a modified Wyman linkage formalism correlating the derivative of solvent activity with respect to ligand ΔG binding with the net change in solvent molecules Δn w (Equation in Figure 3C), hydroxyprocaine binding to TTD was determined to release 26 water molecules [39, 46, 47]. This favorable solvent reorganization event is reflected on the gradual changes of the entropic term towards more positive values (−TΔS becoming more negative) upon increasing betaine concentration. As the osmolyte competes with TTD for the interaction with solvent molecules and the activity of water is consequently reduced, releasing waters from the protein ligand interface becomes much more energetically feasible. Enthalpy–entropy compensation effects are also evident, with the change of enthalpy to less favorable values also indicating a net decrease presumably of the strength of hydrogen bonds. This can likely be attributed to the removal of water molecules from highly ordered configurations trapped at hydrophobic protein surfaces and their transfer to the bulk. To this direction, a highly interesting result was derived by comparing the osmotic stress experiments with MD simulations of TTD. A series of unbiased simulations of 1 μs each were performed at 298 K for the apo‐TTD as well as for the TTD–hydroxyprocaine complex (three independent replicas for each system). The trajectories converged to a net decrease of the SASA of 366 Å2 induced by ligand binding to TTD (Figure 3D). As the estimate for the mean surface occupied by an individual water molecule is 10–15 Å2, this value is in excellent agreement with the total number of 26 waters estimated to be displaced upon ligand interaction [48, 49]. Having an experimentally determined approximation for the number of solvent molecules displaced by the ligand, an effort to derive a prediction by a statistical mechanics‐based approach and compare it with experiment was deemed meaningful. The SZmap algorithm was thus applied to the structure of TTD [50]. The cocrystal structure of TTD with compound‐1 (pdb code: 4QQD) was selected for analysis. The simulation was highly successful in predicting 28 hydration sites either overlapping with or located within 3 Å of the crystallographic ligand (Figure S7). This result also strengthens the hypothesis of hydroxyprocaine binding to the Arg‐site, as this cavity and not the aromatic cage is wide enough to accommodate a ligand of the size of hydroxyprocaine (see also Figure S4). The high TTD–hydroxyprocaine complex stability is demonstrated by the mean values of several key metrics monitored over the MD trajectories of the complex as well as on the RMSD of ligand within the TTD pocket, which is 1.97 ± 0.22 Å (Figure 3F). The respective interactions are summarized in Figure 3F graphs. It is worth noting that whereas several residues contribute to hydroxyprocaine binding, the two most common and apparently strong interactions based on MD trajectories involve the two carboxylate sidechains of Asp142 and Glu153, which mainly form salt bridges with the protonated diethylamine substituent of hydroxyprocaine (Figure 3E,F). Future studies employing either site‐specific mutagenesis or competition ITC experiments will hopefully verify the exact TTD cavity targeted by hydroxyprocaine and facilitate rational analogue design.


### Preliminary Exploration of Hydroxyprocaine Effects on Cancer Cells
  The affinity of hydroxyprocaine for TTD prompted the investigation of its potential effects on the regulation of genes related to UHRF1 and therapeutic possibilities. Interestingly, a hybrid compound (H93) based on one of our previously reported UHRF1 ligands (NSC232003) and developed by a separate research group showed a particularly promising activity against prostate cancer cells [23]. This cancer type appears to be a particularly promising target for UHRF1 modulators as suggested by a search on public databases (e.g., CCLE, DepMap) and literature. According to CCLE transcriptomic datasets (RNA‍‐‍seq), UHRF1 is robustly and stably expressed in DU145 cells. These cells express it at a high to moderate level relative to all human pan‐cancer cell lines, typically registering a value of ≈5.5–6.2 log2(TPM + 1) (Transcripts Per Million). Moreover, DU145 and PC3 lines often align with or slightly exceed UHRF1 expression in androgen‐sensitive lines like LNCaP [51]. This correlates with clinical database trends such as TCGA/GTEx, indicating that higher UHRF1 expression tracks aggressively with prostate cancer tumor progression and biochemical recurrence [52]. UHRF1 is officially labeled as a strongly dependent gene in DU145, as the deployment of shRNA‐mediated knockdown severely disrupts DU145 cell viability and triggers cell cycle arrest or mitotic catastrophe [53]. When DU145 cells are exposed to microenvironmental pressures, variations in DNA methylation mediated by UHRF1 are actively required to lock in epithelial to mesenchymal transitions and stemness characteristics [54]. In this study, the effect of hydroxyprocaine on the androgen‐insensitive prostate cancer line DU145 was assessed by monitoring the levels of a small representative set of genes involved in pivotal cell functions and signaling pathways such as the cell cycle, inflammation, stress, and metabolism. These proteins were p38 and phospho‐p38, p53, p65, and phospho‐p65, AMPK and phospho‐AMPK. Their levels were measured after 72 h of treatment with 25 μM of hydroxyprocaine to permit for at least one complete cell duplication. An increase was determined for p38 (1.53‐fold, p: 0.0041), p53 (1.57‐fold, p: 0.0451), and p65 (1.24‐fold, p: 0.038), whereas no statistically significant variations were observed for the phosphorylated states of both p38 and p65 (Figure 4A). The kinase AMPK did not show a significant variation at either its states as well.

  The cytotoxicity of hydroxyprocaine was also determined at 72 h and was found significantly low, with a calculated CC50 value over 100 μM (Figure 4B). This low toxicity agrees with the notion suggesting that chemical modulation of epigenetic mechanisms results to reprogramming of gene expression and hence less severe (and rapid) responses in terms of cancer cell proliferation and apoptosis. Overall, the effects of hydroxyprocaine on the assessed proteins of DU145 cells seem to be limited in increasing their levels but not affecting their phosphorylation states. Although this is a preliminary evaluation of the bioactivity of TTD ligands, the results are highly encouraging and prompt for a more systematic assessment of their properties, which is underway with various cell types, different biomarkers, appropriate negative controls and, notably, measurement of protein regulation via mRNA quantification. These additional studies will hopefully provide a more detailed delineation of the exact transcriptional regulation mechanisms underlying the activity of TTD ligands and also assist in ruling out potential off‐target effects related with the relatively high concentrations applied in this study. Another aspect which needs to be addressed is the possibility that at least some of the observed gene expression changes induced by hydroxyprocaine may be attributed to inhibition of sodium channels rather than modulation of UHRF1. To this end, utilizing sodium channel inhibitors such as lidocaine or tetracaine, which did not show measurable affinity against TTD, may assist in deconvoluting the observed cellular phenotypes and provide further mechanistic understanding regarding the cellular effects of ligand binding to TTD.


### Determination of TTD Affinity for the UBL Domain of UHRF1
  Although the structure of the full length UHRF1 has not been determined experimentally, there are several hypotheses as to the internal dynamics of this protein. There is numerous evidence implying UHRF1 exists in an equilibrium between an open, “active” and a closed, “inactive” state [26, 27, 55]. In this balance, the TTD domain seems to play a critical role. Specific confirmed interdomain interactions like this of C‐terminal PBR with the TTD and PHD finger and this of TTD with the TTD‐PHD linker, among others such as this of PHD with the SRA‐spacer, have been suggested to regulate this equilibrium. Yet, there are no reports describing any interactions between TTD and its N‐terminal adjacent UBL domain. To further characterize TTD in terms of its possible drug targeting opportunities, its interaction with UBL was assessed by the use of ITC (Figure 5A). The results indicate a moderately strong interaction, with a K d value of 6.01 ± 1.76 μM, which is mainly entropic, with a ΔH value of −2.68 ± 0.54 kcal/mol and a −TΔS of −4.48 kcal/mol. As expected, the stoichiometry is this of a 1:1 interaction (n: 0.72 ± 0.11). A crystallographic study was not possible at this time, thus an effort to predict the interaction geometry between TTD and UBL was performed by using two of the most accurate algorithms for protein–protein docking, ClusPro and HADDOCK [56, 57]. Several models were derived and ranked by the corresponding scores applied in each algorithm (ClusPro: 29 clusters, 979 total models; HADDOCK: 4 clusters, 18 total models). However, there was no clear consensus between the two methods, with each one suggesting a different heterodimer as the top‐ranked configuration (Figure 5B). Most importantly, this previously undetermined intramolecular binding aspect can possibly be targeted in a fashion enabling the perturbation of UHRF1 internal dynamics (open to closed states) in a pharmacologically exploitable way. Notably, these preliminary results are reported with the aim to encourage further research toward this direction. The predictions of ClusPro or HADDOCK can be valued in the future to assist mutagenesis studies for validating this interaction within the conformational context of the full‐length protein and, subsequently, to map the interfacial residues and structural requisites of this intramolecular UHRF1 interaction.

  The AlphaFold 3 algorithm was also employed for deriving a theoretical model of the full‐length UHRF1 and explore whether it would predict an interaction between TTD and UBL [58]. In this model, the two domains did not seem to participate in a direct interaction. However, based on the AlphaFold 3 metrics (primary metric for evaluating local confidence of structural predictions (plDDT); predicted template modeling score (pTM)), this model was deemed to be of particularly low confidence. Although the individual domains were predicted with high plDDT scores over 70, the interdomain segments which are the most critical in determining interdomain interactions were modelled with particularly poor performance. The corresponding plDDT scores of these segments were lower than 70 and in many cases even lower than 50. Moreover, the pTM was below the high quality threshold of 0.8 and as low as 0.49, indicating a wrong prediction according to the AlphaFold 3 documentation (Figure S15). The poor model quality regarding interdomain interactions was also evident on the PAE (predicted aligned error) matrix, where the off‐diagonal elements indicated high errors regarding the interdomain segments (Figure S15). As UHRF1 is known to exist in a highly dynamic equilibrium between “open” and “closed” states involving interdomain interactions and AlphaFold often predicts a single static conformation, the algorithm seems to be highly prone to suboptimal performance in predicting transient or complex dynamical interdomain crosstalks.


## Conclusion
  A carefully elaborated combination of computational and biophysical tools enabled the successful identification of drug‐like molecules as binders for the TTD domain of the epigenetic hub protein UHRF1. The chemotypes reported here as TTD ligands are of high originality, pronounced drug‐likeness (see Table S8 and Figure S14 for predicted ADME properties) and, together with the derived thermodynamic SAR knowledge, they will hopefully offer optimal starting points for the development of potent and selective TTD modulators. The repurposing potential of hydroxyprocaine even further advocates for its utilization as a superior scaffold for developing compounds targeting TTD. Unlike binders of the SRA domain, TTD ligands are not expected to have a direct effect on DNA methylation, but rather on the expression of regulation of related genes. Such a phenotype was indeed confirmed in this study, although at a preliminary level, and most notably without severe cytotoxic effects, in agreement with the reprogramming concept attributed to epigenetic drugs. Synthetic efforts are definitely needed to further explore and rationally optimize the activity of the local anesthetic scaffold toward TTD. Moreover, mechanistic studies are required for identifying other genes affected by ligand binding to TTD as well as elucidating the exact mode by which Na+ channel blockers can modulate the expression of these pivotal regulators involved in tumor suppression, inflammation and stress responses. There are several reports confirming the interplay between UHRF1 and several of the genes shown in this study to be upregulated by hydroxyprocaine. Those reports, for example, include the interaction between UHRF1 and p53 toward the in vivo evolution of cellular senescence or of p65 and the related to NPY1R gene pathogenesis of inflammatory bowel disease [59, 60]. Particularly with respect to prostate cancer, current research reveals complex mechanisms by which UHRF1 might be involved with disease progression and thus pharmaceutically targeted, such as its role on phase separation and silencing of tumor suppressor genes or the UHRF1‐dependent ubiquitination and degradation of the androgen receptor, the levels of which affect resistance to therapy [61, 62]. The results presented in this study establish hydroxyprocaine aside lutidine as an alternative, highly tractable, safe and promising structural scaffold for the design of bioactive compounds modulating UHRF1 function. Whereas lutidine is a highly efficient fragment‐like TTD ligand particularly privileged by its small size (0.72 kcal/mol of ΔG bind attributed to each heavy atom), hydroxyprocaine is a lead‐like scaffold that offers broad synthetic versatility alongside favorable physicochemical and pharmacokinetic properties. If developed, such derivatives could function through targeting either the TTD domain or the potential interplay between TTD and UBL, thus accelerating the development of drug candidates or chemical probes for this emerging and particularly interesting epigenetic entity.


## Experimental Section

### Virtual Screening and Molecular Simulations
  The cheminformatics analysis and physicochemical parameter calculation of the screened compound collection were performed using the DataWarrior v06.05.04 program [63]. The PAINS features were identified by DataWarrior modules based on related filters previously reported [64]. Regarding the virtual screening stage, docking was performed by the Glide SP algorithm on a grid derived by the TTD crystal structure with pdb code 5YY9, with no Van der Waals scaling applied to protein and ligand atom radii [65, 66, 67]. This specific structure is the complex of TTD with the histone‐mimetic peptide LIG1K126me3 and it was selected as it encompasses interactions both at the Arg‐site and the aromatic cage of TTD. The exact binding geometry exploration regarding the hits was performed by Glide on the crystal structure with pdb code 4QQD (TTD in complex with the iminopyridine derivative compound‐1) and, in this case three docking parameters were assessed and the combination affording optimal reproduction of the crystal structure pose was used for subsequent docking calculations [36]. Those parameters were the presence or not of crystallographic water molecules and the Van der Waals scaling of protein (grid) and ligand (docking) atom radii (Table S2). The resulting poses were subsequently subjected to an MM/GBSA rescoring calculation (Prime, Schrodinger Inc.) and the values of ΔG binding were derived [68, 69]. The 3D similarity screen was performed by ROCS using compound NV‐01 as a structural query and Tanimoto combo as the ranking metric [50]. The CASTpFold method was applied at the 5YY9 structure where LIG1K126me3 atoms were deleted and a probe radius of 1.4 Å was used. For the protein–protein docking, the processed 4QQD and 2FAZ structures were used for TTD and UBL, respectively. ClusPro 2.0 and HADDOCK 2.4 were utilized at their respective online versions with default settings [56, 57, 70, 71, 72, 73, 74]. The unrestrained molecular dynamics simulations were performed by using the Desmond software (D.E. Shaw Research) using the 5YY9 crystal structure where both the Arg‐site and the aromatic cage cavities are open and well‐shaped. The MD systems were prepared by charge neutralizing and solvating the protein–ligand complexes in SPC waters and 0.150 M NaCl. Periodic boundary conditions were applied to a triclinic system with a buffer range of 10 Å and multiple replicate simulations with different random seeds were performed at the NPT ensemble (Nose–Hoover chain thermostat, Martyna–Tobias–Klein barostat) at 298 K for 1 μs each. All simulations were performed at Linux mint local workstations with Intel core i9 operating Nvidia 2080 Super and RTX4000 Quadro GPU chipsets. The SZmap analysis was performed at the 4QQD crystal structure of TTD applying the stabilization mode. The pK a values for the four TTD ligands were predicted by the microscopic g‐xTB‐based aqueous pK a module of Rowan Scientific package (https://www.‍rowansci‍.com, accessed 2026‐05‐13) [75, 76, 77]. The predictions were compared with the experimental pK a values, when available (Table S7) [78]. The ADME properties of the four confirmed ligands were calculated by the SwissADME software (Table S8 and Figure S14) [79].


### Protein Expression and Purification
  The plasmids encoding the TTD domain of UHRF1 (pdb id: 4QQD) and the UBL domain of UHRF1 (pdb id: 2FAZ) were gifts from Cheryl Arrowsmith and Structural Genomics Consortium (Addgene plasmid #61 947; http://n2t.net/addgene: 61 947; RRID: Addgene_61947 and Addgene plasmid #25 596; http://n2t.net/addgene: 25 596; RRID: Addgene_25596). Competent Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) cells were transformed by heat shock and grown at 37 °C to an OD600 of ≈0.6 in Luria‐Bertani medium at the presence of 50 μg/mL kanamycin. Protein overexpression was induced by 1 mM of isopropyl‐β‐D‐thiogalactopyranoside at 18 °C overnight. Cells were harvested by centrifugation and sonicated (20 min, 75% amplitude, 1 s on‐pulse, 9 s off‐pulse, three repeats if needed) in lysis buffer (Tris pH 8.0 50 mM, NaCl 300 mM, imidazole 10 mM, TCEP 1 mM) and subsequently the protein was purified in a single step by immobilized metal affinity chromatography using 1 mL of PureCube 100 Ni‐NTA agarose of bead size 90–100 μm (Cube biotech id. 74 103) and an elution buffer (Tris pH 8.0 50 mM, NaCl 300 mM, TCEP 1 mM, imidazole 250 mM). The purified protein was concentrated using Amicon Ultra 2 mL tubes (10 kDa cutoff for TTD, 3 kDa cutoff for UBL, Merck Millipore) and their purity was assessed by SDS‐PAGE (Figures S8 and S9). Quantification of the purified proteins was performed at 280 nm by an Implen N60 Nanophotometer. Very limited impurities of nucleic acids were determined in the purified protein fractions of TTD, with the A260/280 nm ratio at 0.52, while the UBL respective A260/280 ratio spanned from 0.70 to 0.85.


### DSF
  Experiments were performed in a BioRad CFX‐Connect real‐time PCR machine. Compound screening utilized a continuous heating rate protocol of 0.5 °C per minute repeated for 65 cycles after an initial 3 min incubation at 25 °C. The protein was diluted at a concentration of 2–5 μM, depending on protein batch, in a buffer comprising 10 mM HEPES at pH 7.4 and 150 mM NaCl, while for monitoring protein unfolding the ProteOrange stain reagent (Lumiprobe code 10210) was used in concentrations between 5x and 25x depending on the protein batch. At primary screening, compounds were diluted in the protein buffer from 10 mM stock solutions in 100% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and were evaluated at a concentration of 200 μM. Data analysis and calculation of ΔT m values were performed by BioRad Maestro software, Microsoft Excel 365 and the fitting tools of the DSF‐World application by the Gestwicki group [80].


### Isothermal Titration Calorimetry
  A low‐volume Nano ITC (TA instruments) was used for all calorimetry experiments. The purified protein was concentrated and buffer exchanged using Amicon Ultra 2 mL tubes (Merck Millipore). The ITC protein buffer was 10 mM HEPES at pH 7.4 and 150 mM NaCl adjusted to 0.1% DMSO for matching compound samples. All samples were degassed prior to titration under a high vacuum. Isotherms were obtained by reverse titrations where the cell was filled with ≈300 μL of ligand at 10 μM diluted in the same buffer used for concentrating the protein and the syringe was filled with 50 μL of protein at 120 μM matched with the addition of 0.1% DMSO. Titrations were performed at 5 and 25 °C. For titrations, 20 injections of 2.5 μL each (first injection: 0.95 μL) with an interval of 300 s between injections were utilized at a stirring rate of 200 rpm. Blank experiments were performed by titrating the ligand sample into protein buffer and dilution heats were checked for linearity and low heat signal. Analysis was performed by the NanoAnalyze v. 4.1.0.1 software (TA instruments) using an independent binding model. Baseline correction in case of linear blank experiments was performed by fitting the last five or six points of each ligand to protein titration to a constant model and subtracting this value to obtain corrected heats. All osmotic stress experiments were performed at 25 °C under the same conditions and with the same buffers applied for the regular titrations. Betaine (TCI Chemicals) was used at osmolal concentrations of 0.20, 0.40, 0.60, and 0.80 Osm. As NaCl is present at the same concentration (150 mM) in all osmolyte containing samples, its contribution to total osmolality is systematically cancelled out. The TTD to UBL titration was performed at 25 °C using a concentration of 200 μM in the syringe and of 3 μM in the cell. For the particular titration, 20 injections of 2.5 μL each (first injection: 0.95 μL) with an interval of 300 s between injections were utilized at a stirring rate of 300 rpm.


### Structural Identification and Verification of Bioactive Compounds
  Compounds from the NCI/DTP repository were provided free of charge (https://dtp.cancer.gov). The fragment collection was assembled from fragment‐like reagents purchased by various commercial vendors. The LOPAC collection of compounds was kindly offered to NKUA. All collections were prepared in 100% DMSO stock solutions and were stored at −20 °C. The bioactive compounds have been verified in terms of their structural identity and purity by the use of high‐resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS). Individual stock solutions of NSC12480 (hydroxyprocaine) at 2523 mg/L, NSC251964 (7‐methoxy‐1,3‐dimethyl‐8‐phenyl‐2H‐purin‐6‐one at 2723 mg/L, mexiletine at 1793 mg/L and triamterene at 2533 mg/L were prepared in DMSO. Working solutions (1 mg/L) were subsequently prepared by appropriate dilution in 95:5 H2O:MeOH (v/v) and injected into the LC‐QTOF system in positive ionization mode. The HRMS structural elucidation was performed using an ultra‐high‐performance liquid chromatography system (Dionex UltiMate 3000 RSLC, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Dreieich, Germany) coupled to a hybrid quadrupole time‐of‐flight mass spectrometer (Maxis Impact II, Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany). Chromatographic separation was achieved at 40 °C using an Intensity Solo 1.8 C18‐2 (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 µm; Bruker Daltonics; Bremen, Germany) equipped with a VanGuard BEH C18 precolumn (2.1 × 5 mm, 1.7 µm; Waters, Ireland). The mobile phase consisted of (A) 5 mM ammonium formate in H2O: MeOH (99:1 v/v) acidified with 0.1% formic acid and (B) 5 mM ammonium formate in MeOH acidified with 0.1% formic acid. The elution program was based on LC gradient for the solvents and the flow rate. The analysis was conducted in positive electrospray ionization (ESI+) mode, utilizing data‐dependent acquisition with over a mass range of m/z 50–1200. The MS settings are the following: capillary voltage 4500 V, end plate offset 500 V, nebulizer gas pressure 2.0 bar, dry gas flow 8.0 L/min, and a dry temperature of 200 °C. Isotope pattern analysis was utilized to calculate the theoretical m/z of [M+H]‍+ ions for the investigated compounds. The Data Analysis software (v.2026, Bruker, Bremen, Germany) was employed for chromatographic processing, while the SmartFormula Manually tool was used for molecular formulae assignment to the corresponding m/z values, incorporating mass error (ppm) and isotopic fit (mSigma). MetFrag was utilized for in silico fragmentation of the compounds [81]. MZmine (v. 4.9.14) was employed for peak picking, and the resulting feature list (exported in. mgf format) was used as input for SIRIUS (v.6.4.0) for compound annotation [82, 83, 84]. Additionally, MS‐Finder (v 3.5.2) was utilized for further compound annotation [85]. All metrics supporting the annotation of the aforementioned compounds, obtained via multiple software platforms and annotation tools, are summarized in Tables S3–S6. The chromatograms and MS/MS spectra for NSC12480 (hydroxyprocaine), mexiletine, triamterene, and NSC251964 are illustrated in Figures S10–S13, respectively.


### Cytotoxicity Determination and Western Blotting Analysis
  Cells of the DU145 (RRID: CVCL_0105) line were cultured and maintained in RPMI containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin as antibiotics. The cell cultures were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2 flow inside a humidified incubator. Cell viability was assessed using the MTT colorimetric assay. The cells were seeded in 96‐well plates at a cell density of 1 × 104 cells per well and incubated for 24 h to reach confluency. Cells were then treated with varying concentrations (100, 50, 25, 10, 5, and 1 μM) of compound NSC12480 diluted in RPMI medium. A vehicle control containing the maximum concentration of DMSO used in the treatments was included to account for solvent effects. After 72 h of exposure, the medium was removed and replaced with 100 μL of MTT solution (0.5 mg/mL in PBS) and incubated for 1–3 h at 37 °C. The resulting formazan crystals were dissolved by the addition of 100 μL of DMSO. The absorbance was measured at 540 nm (reference wavelength 620 nm), using a microplate reader. Regarding western blotting analysis, cells were seeded in six‐well plates at a density of 2.5 × 105 cells/well and treated with NSC12480 for 72 h. After treatment, cells were washed with PBS and lysed in ice‐cold RIPA buffer with proteinase and phosphatase inhibitor. The protein supernatant was collected and protein concentrations were determined using the Lowry method. Samples containing 30 μg of protein were separated by 15% SDS‐PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Following a 1 h blocking step in 5% nonfat dry milk, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. GAPDH was used as an internal loading control to ensure equal protein distribution. Membranes were then incubated with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h. Protein bands were visualized with ECL chemiluminescent reagents using the Amersham ImageQuant LAS 500 system and band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software. The antibodies used were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology, except where noted otherwise: p38 (dilution 1:1000, Rabbit mAb #8690), phospho‐p38 (dilution 1:1000, Rabbit mAb #4511), p53 (dilution 1:1000, Mouse mAb #sc‐126, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), p65 (NFκB, dilution 1:1000, Mouse mAb #6956), phospho‐p65 (NFκB, dilution 1:1000, Rabbit mAb #3033), AMPK alpha (dilution 1:1000, Rabbit mAb #2603), phospho‐AMPK alpha (dilution 1:1000, Rabbit mAb #2535), GAPDH (dilution 1:2500 Rabbit mAb #5174), goat antirabbit secondary antibody (dilution 1:2000 #7074) and goat antimouse secondary antibody (dilution 1:2000 #7076). The analysis of western blotting results was performed by two‐tailed unpaired Student’s t‐test using GraphPad Prism v. 8 after rejecting the observation with the highest residual from each of the six‐point sets, deriving datasets with n = 5. The p values were: ns, not significant; *, p ≤ 0.05; **, p ≤0.01; and ***, p ≤ 0.001.
