# Mechanisms of Rhubarb–Peach Kernel in treating gastric cancer: A network pharmacology and molecular docking study


## Abstract
  This study investigates the potential mechanisms through which the Rhubarb–Peach Kernel herb pair may exert effects in gastric cancer (GC) using network pharmacology and molecular docking approaches. Active compounds and their corresponding targets were retrieved from the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology Database and Analysis Platform (TCMSP), with additional compound–target information obtained from DrugBank. Gastric cancer-related targets were collected from GeneCards, Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM), the Therapeutic Target Database, and DrugBank. The overlapping targets were used to construct a compound–target–disease network and a protein–protein interaction network. Gene ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway enrichment analyses were subsequently performed. Nine candidate compounds were docked against 8 core targets to assess their predicted structural interactions. A total of 27 active compounds were identified, among which β-sitosterol, aloe-emodin, and hederagenin were identified as major candidate compounds. Tumor protein p53, caspase 3, and JUN were among the principal network targets. Enrichment analysis indicated that the overlapping targets were mainly associated with the p53 signaling pathway and other pathways involved in cancer-related processes, including apoptosis, cell survival, and inflammatory regulation. Molecular docking revealed differences in predicted binding scores among the 9 candidate compounds and 8 core targets, with several compound–target pairs showing comparatively lower docking energies. This in silico study suggests that the Rhubarb–Peach Kernel herb pair may act through multiple compounds, targets, and biological pathways relevant to GC. However, the identified targets are not necessarily specific to GC, and the molecular docking findings represent computational predictions rather than evidence of actual biological binding. These results should therefore be considered preliminary and hypothesis-generating, and further in vitro and in vivo studies are required to evaluate their biological and therapeutic relevance.


## 1. Introduction
  Gastric cancer (GC) is a heterogeneous epithelial malignancy and remains a major global health burden. Despite an overall decline in incidence and mortality in some regions, GC is still among the most commonly diagnosed cancers and one of the leading causes of cancer-related death worldwide.[1,2] Because early symptoms, such as indigestion, bloating, and weight loss, are often nonspecific, many patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage. Current treatment strategies for GC include surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted therapy. However, treatment-related toxicity, drug resistance, and tumor heterogeneity continue to limit therapeutic efficacy, resulting in poor prognosis and unsatisfactory survival outcomes.[3]

  Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) has long been used as an adjunctive approach in cancer management. Within the theoretical framework of TCM, GC is associated with heterogeneous syndrome patterns, among which spleen deficiency, blood stasis and toxin accumulation, phlegm-dampness, and disharmony between the liver and stomach have been frequently reported.[4] The Rhubarb–Peach Kernel pair is a recognized herb combination in TCM and is incorporated into prescriptions used for disorders characterized by blood stasis, accumulation, and inflammatory pathological changes.[5]

  Rhubarb is traditionally used to purge accumulation, clear heat, detoxify, and dispel blood stasis, whereas Peach Kernel is used to invigorate blood, remove blood stasis, and moisten the intestines. Their combination, therefore, integrates the accumulation-removing action of Rhubarb with the blood-activating and stasis-dispersing effects of Peach Kernel. From the perspective of TCM compatibility, this pairing is intended to address the coexistence of pathological accumulation and blood stasis rather than to target a single disease-specific pathological factor.

  Nevertheless, direct clinical or experimental evidence evaluating the Rhubarb–Peach Kernel pair specifically in GC remains scarce. The 2 herbs have more commonly been investigated as components of multicomponent prescriptions or in disease models other than GC.[6] Therefore, existing evidence provides a theoretical and pharmacological rationale for investigating this herb pair but should not be interpreted as direct evidence of its clinical efficacy in GC.

  Rhubarb is derived from the dried root and rhizome of Rheum palmatum L., Rheum tanguticum Maxim. ex Balf., or Rheum officinale Baill.[7,8] Phytochemical studies have shown that Rhubarb contains multiple bioactive anthraquinones, including emodin, aloe-emodin, and rhein, which exhibit anticancer activities in several tumor models. These compounds have been reported to regulate signaling pathways such as mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/Akt), and nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells, thereby suppressing proliferation, promoting apoptosis, and inhibiting tumor progression.[9,10] Emodin and aloe-emodin have also been reported to inhibit proliferation and induce apoptosis in human GC cells.[11]

  Peach Kernel is the dried mature seed of Prunus persica (L.) Batsch or Prunus davidiana (Carr.) Franch.[12,13] It contains multiple bioactive constituents, including fatty acids, sterols, glycosides, and other phytochemicals with potential anti-inflammatory and antitumor activities. Although direct evidence regarding the effects of Peach Kernel itself in GC remains limited, Peach Kernel extracts and peach-derived constituents have demonstrated antiproliferative or antitumor activities in other cancer models.[14] In addition, β-sitosterol has been reported to regulate cell proliferation, apoptosis, angiogenesis, and tumor-associated signaling pathways, supporting its potential value as an anticancer compound.[15] Hederagenin has also demonstrated antitumor activity in experimental cancer models and may enhance the chemosensitivity of oxaliplatin-resistant GC cells.[16,17]

  From a pharmacological perspective, the Rhubarb–Peach Kernel pair may provide complementary chemical constituents capable of modulating overlapping or interconnected biological processes. Anthraquinones derived from Rhubarb have been associated with the regulation of cell proliferation, apoptosis, inflammation, and oxidative stress, whereas sterol- and triterpenoid-related compounds may influence angiogenesis, inflammatory responses, cell survival, and treatment sensitivity. The concurrent presence of these constituents may, therefore, broaden the range of molecular targets and signaling pathways affected by the herb pair. However, this possibility should be regarded as a theoretical pharmacological complementarity rather than demonstrated synergy because direct combination experiments and formal synergy analyses of Rhubarb and Peach Kernel in GC have not yet been performed.

  Because herbal medicines typically contain multiple active constituents that may act on multiple molecular targets simultaneously, conventional single-target approaches may not adequately capture their mechanisms of action. Network pharmacology provides a systems-level strategy for exploring interactions among compounds, targets, pathways, and diseases and is therefore particularly suitable for investigating complex herbal formulas and herb pairs.[18] Molecular docking can further assess the predicted structural compatibility between selected compounds and candidate protein targets. Nevertheless, docking scores provide only computational evidence of potential interactions and cannot establish actual binding affinity or biological activity.

  In the present study, network pharmacology and molecular docking were used to investigate the potential mechanisms of the Rhubarb–Peach Kernel pair in GC. Candidate active compounds, overlapping disease-related targets, protein–protein interactions (PPIs), and enriched biological pathways were systematically analyzed, followed by molecular docking of selected compound–target pairs. This study aimed to provide an exploratory in silico framework for understanding the multicomponent and multitarget characteristics of the herb pair and to generate testable hypotheses for subsequent experimental investigation. ## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1. Collection and screening of active compounds and their predicted targets in the Rhubarb–Peach Kernel pair
  The chemical constituents of Rhubarb and Peach Kernel were separately retrieved from the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology Database and Analysis Platform (TCMSP, version 2.3; University of Chinese Academy of Sciences).[19] The Chinese pinyin terms “Dahuang” and “Taoren” were used to search for Rhubarb and Peach Kernel, respectively. Candidate compounds were screened using the commonly adopted pharmacokinetic thresholds of oral bioavailability (OB) ≥ 30% and drug-likeness (DL) ≥ 0.18.[20-22] Compounds meeting both criteria were retained for subsequent analysis. Compounds for which no corresponding target information was available in TCMSP were excluded.

  To improve the coverage of compound–target associations, the retained compounds were additionally searched in the DrugBank database, and available compound–target associations were incorporated into the target dataset.[23] Targets obtained from TCMSP and DrugBank were subsequently integrated. Protein names were standardized and mapped to official human gene symbols using the Universal Protein Resource (UniProt) database, with the organism restricted to Homo sapiens.[24] Unmapped entries and duplicate targets were removed to generate the final predicted target set for the Rhubarb–Peach Kernel pair.


### 2.2. Identification of gastric cancer-related targets and construction of the Venn diagram
  Gastric cancer-related targets were retrieved from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org),[25] the Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) database (https://omim.org),[26] DrugBank (https://go.drugbank.com),[23] and the Therapeutic Target Database (TTD; https://db.idrblab.net/ttd/).[27] The search terms “gastric cancer” and “stomach cancer” were used in each database. For the GeneCards dataset, the median relevance score was calculated, and genes with relevance scores greater than or equal to the median value of 5.89 were retained to reduce low-confidence disease associations. Targets retrieved from the 4 databases were merged, standardized to official gene symbols, and deduplicated to generate the final GC-related target dataset.

  The predicted targets of the Rhubarb–Peach Kernel pair were intersected with the GC-related target set using R software (version 4.4.1; R Core Team). The overlapping genes, considered potential therapeutic targets of the Rhubarb–Peach Kernel pair in GC, were visualized using a Venn diagram.


### 2.3. Construction of the protein–protein interaction network and identification of core targets
  The overlapping targets between the Rhubarb–Peach Kernel pair and GC were submitted to the search tool for the retrieval of interacting genes/proteins (STRING) database (https://cn.string-db.org) to construct a PPI network.[28] The organism was restricted to Homo sapiens, and the minimum required interaction score was set to 0.900, corresponding to the highest confidence interaction level. Nodes without connections to other proteins in the network were excluded.

  The resulting interaction data were exported from STRING in tab-separated values (TSV) format and imported into Cytoscape software (version 3.10.2; Cytoscape Consortium) for network visualization and topological analysis.[29] The CytoNCA plugin was used to calculate 6 centrality parameters for each node: betweenness centrality, degree centrality (DC), closeness centrality, eigenvector centrality, network centrality, and local average connectivity.[30-32] Nodes with values greater than the corresponding median values for all 6 parameters were retained as candidate core targets.

  In the visualized PPI network, each node represented a protein target, and each edge represented a predicted or documented PPI. Node size was proportional to the DC value, whereas edge thickness was proportional to the STRING combined interaction score.


### 2.4. GO functional enrichment and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses
  The overlapping targets were submitted to the Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery (DAVID, version 6.8; https://davidbioinformatics.nih.gov/) for gene ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis.[33] The organism was restricted to Homo sapiens, and the default DAVID background was used. GO enrichment analysis included the 3 major categories of biological process, cellular component, and molecular function.

  Enriched GO terms and KEGG pathways were retained according to the following criteria: gene count ≥ 5, nominal P value < .01, and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.01. The enrichment results were exported from DAVID and visualized as bar plots and bubble plots using the ggplot2 package in R software (version 4.4.1).


### 2.5. Construction of the compound–target–pathway network
  The candidate active compounds, core targets identified from the PPI network, and representative KEGG pathways obtained from the enrichment analysis were imported into Cytoscape software (version 3.10.2) to construct a compound–target–pathway network. In this network, nodes represented active compounds, core targets, or enriched pathways, whereas edges represented the predicted associations among compounds, targets, and pathways. Topological analysis was performed based on DC, and nodes with higher degree values were considered to have more extensive connections within the network.


### 2.6. Molecular docking between candidate compounds and core target proteins
  Nine candidate compounds identified from the Rhubarb–Peach Kernel pair were selected for molecular docking with 8 core target proteins. The 2-dimensional structures of the selected compounds were obtained from the TCMSP and saved in MOL2 format.[19]

  The 3-dimensional structures of the 8 core target proteins were obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank.[34] The protein structures were imported into PyMOL software (version 3.0.3; Schrödinger, LLC) for preprocessing, including the removal of water molecules and original ligands, and the addition of hydrogen atoms.

  The processed compounds and target proteins were subjected to molecular docking using AutoDock4 software (The Scripps Research Institute [TSRI]), with 50 independent docking runs performed for each compound–target pair. Grid parameters were defined according to the corresponding docking configurations, and representative grid parameters for the 8 target proteins are summarized in Table 1. Predicted binding energies were used to evaluate the interactions between the candidate compounds and target proteins. Representative docking conformations were subsequently visualized using PyMOL software (version 3.0.3). The binding energy data were imported into R software (version 4.4.1), and the docking results were visualized as a heatmap using the ggplot2 package.


## 3. Results

### 3.1. Screening of active ingredients in the “Rheum palmatum–Peach Kernel” medicinal pair
  Based on the TCMSP (v2.3) database, a total of 158 compounds were retrieved, including 92 from Rheum palmatum (Rhubarb) and 66 from Peach Kernel. By applying the screening criteria of OB ≥30% and DL ≥0.18, a total of 28 candidate compounds were initially identified – 10 from Rhubarb and 18 from Peach Kernel. After excluding those lacking corresponding protein targets, 27 compounds were retained for subsequent analysis. Notably, β-sitosterol was found to be a common compound present in both herbs. The results are summarized in Table 2.


### 3.2. Construction of the herb–compound–target network
  The screened active ingredients were subjected to target prediction using the TCMSP database, supplemented by additional drug-target interactions retrieved from the DrugBank database (https://go.drugbank.com). All protein targets were then standardized to gene symbols using the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org). This process yielded 64 potential targets for Rhubarb and 48 for Peach Kernel. After merging and removing duplicates, a total of 74 unique targets were retained.

  To visualize the multicomponent–multitarget relationships, the herbs, active compounds, and their corresponding targets were imported into Cytoscape 3.10.2 to construct a “herb–compound–target” interaction network. One compound (GA119) without an identified target was excluded from the analysis. The final network (Fig. 2) consists of 2 herb nodes, 27 active compound nodes, and 74 target nodes.

  Topological analysis showed that each compound was connected to an average of 6.89 targets, while each target was associated with 2.51 compounds, highlighting the multicomponent and multitarget characteristics of the herb pair. Among the compounds, β-sitosterol exhibited the highest degree (35 targets), followed by aloe-emodin (21) and hederagenin (20), indicating their potential as core active constituents. Likewise, hub targets such as prostaglandin-endoperoxide synthase 2 (PTGS2, COX-2) (linked to 17 compounds), nuclear receptor coactivator 2 (11), and GABRA1 (10) may serve as central nodes mediating the pharmacological actions of the Rhubarb–Peach Kernel combination. The full network is illustrated in Figure 2.


### 3.3. Gastric cancer disease targets and Venn diagram construction
  Disease-related targets were collected from 4 major databases: GeneCards, OMIM, DrugBank, and the TTD. After removing duplicates, a total of 3450 unique targets associated with GC were identified.

  These disease-associated targets were subsequently cross-referenced with the 74 predicted targets of the Rhubarb–Peach Kernel pair using R software (version 4.4.1). A Venn diagram was generated to visualize the overlapping targets (Fig. 3). A total of 46 overlapping targets were identified between the Rhubarb–Peach Kernel pair and GC, indicating their potential relevance to the disease. These shared targets represent the core candidates that may mediate the therapeutic effects of the Rhubarb–Peach Kernel pair in the context of GC.


### 3.4. Results of PPI network analysis
  The 46 overlapping targets were imported into the STRING database for PPI analysis. After the removal of disconnected nodes, the resulting network contained 33 nodes and 64 edges (Fig. 4). Topological analysis using CytoNCA identified 10 candidate core targets with values above the median for the 6 selected network parameters. Among these, tumor protein p53 (TP53), caspase 3 (CASP3), and JUN showed relatively high degree values, suggesting that they may occupy central positions in the PPI network and play important roles in the potential therapeutic effects of the Rhubarb–Peach Kernel pair on GC.


### 3.5. Results of GO functional enrichment and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis

#### 3.5.1. GO functional enrichment analysis
  A total of 51 significantly enriched GO terms were identified (P < .01, count ≥ 5, FDR < 0.01), including 30 biological process terms, 7 cellular component terms, and 14 molecular function terms. The top 10 terms in each category are shown in Figure 5. The enriched biological processes were mainly related to response to estradiol, response to xenobiotic stimulus, and positive regulation of apoptosis. The enriched cellular components mainly included protein-containing complexes, nucleoplasm, and chromatin, whereas the enriched molecular functions were primarily associated with enzyme binding, nuclear receptor activity, and identical protein binding.


#### 3.5.2. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis
  A total of 64 KEGG pathways met the predefined enrichment criteria (P < .01, count ≥ 5, and FDR < 0.01). The top 10 enriched pathways ranked by FDR are presented in Figure 6. Among the highly ranked pathways were hepatitis B, pathways in cancer, and the p53 signaling pathway. Several of the enriched pathways contained signaling components involved in tumor-associated biological processes, including cell proliferation, apoptosis, and cellular stress responses. These results indicate that the predicted targets of the Rhubarb–Peach Kernel pair are enriched in multiple signaling pathways related to cancer-associated biological processes.


### 3.6. Construction of the compound–target–pathway network
  A compound–target–pathway network was constructed based on the selected enriched pathways (Fig. 7). The network contained 25 nodes and 75 edges, illustrating the predicted associations among candidate compounds, core targets, and enriched pathways. Among the pathways, hepatitis B, pathways in cancer, and lipid and atherosclerosis were connected to a relatively large number of targets. Among the candidate compounds, aloe-emodin exhibited relatively high network connectivity, indicating that it occupied a prominent topological position within the constructed network.


### 3.7. Molecular docking results of active compounds and core targets
  To further assess the predicted interactions between representative active compounds and core targets, molecular docking was performed using 9 compounds and 8 target proteins selected from the herb–compound–target network and PPI analysis. All compound–target pairs exhibited predicted binding energies below −5.0 kcal/mol, and several pairs showed binding energies below −7.0 kcal/mol. The average predicted binding energy across all compound–target pairs was −7.64 kcal/mol.

  Among the target proteins, B-cell lymphoma 2 (BCL2) and interleukin 1 beta (IL1B) exhibited relatively low average predicted binding energies across the tested compounds. Among the active compounds, β-sitosterol and hederagenin showed relatively low average predicted binding energies across multiple core targets. The lowest predicted binding energy was observed for the BCL2–β-sitosterol pair (−10.41 kcal/mol). Detailed docking scores are presented in Figure 8.

  Representative docking conformations are shown in Figure 9, including BCL2–β-sitosterol, CASP3–β-sitosterol, CASP8–β-sitosterol, ESR1–hederagenin, IL1B–hederagenin, JUN–GA120, TNF–β-sitosterol, and TP53–β-sitosterol. These docking results indicate favorable predicted interactions between the selected compounds and core target proteins; however, further experimental validation is required to confirm their actual binding and biological relevance.


## 4. Discussion
  In the present study, network pharmacology and molecular docking were integrated to investigate the potential pharmacological basis of the Rhubarb–Peach Kernel pair in GC. Several candidate active compounds, including β-sitosterol, aloe-emodin, and hederagenin, and a group of highly connected targets, including TP53, CASP3, JUN, and BCL2, were identified. Functional enrichment analysis showed that the predicted target set was associated with multiple cancer-related biological processes and signaling pathways, particularly those related to apoptosis and p53 signaling. Molecular docking further indicated relatively favorable predicted interactions between several representative compounds and core target proteins. Collectively, these findings suggest that the predicted pharmacological network of the Rhubarb–Peach Kernel pair involves multiple compounds, targets, and pathways. However, these associations are derived primarily from database-based prediction and computational analyses and should not be interpreted as direct evidence of therapeutic mechanisms.

  Among the identified candidate compounds, β-sitosterol, aloe-emodin, and hederagenin occupied relatively prominent positions in the constructed network and have also been implicated in cancer-related biological processes in previous studies. β-Sitosterol is a phytosterol with reported anticancer activity in multiple tumor types.[15] Previous studies have suggested that β-sitosterol may exert antitumor effects in GC and modulate signaling pathways associated with tumor progression.[35] In addition, β-sitosterol has been reported to regulate several pathways involved in cell proliferation and apoptosis, including MAPK, PI3K/Akt, and mechanistic target of rapamycin signaling.[36] β-Sitosterol has also been reported to suppress GC cell proliferation and angiogenesis through regulation of prostaglandin-endoperoxide synthase 1 (PTGS1) and vascular endothelial growth factor A, thereby potentially affecting tumor-associated biological processes.[37] These previous observations provide biological context for the relatively high network connectivity of β-sitosterol identified in the present analysis, although its contribution to the effects of the Rhubarb–Peach Kernel pair requires direct experimental confirmation.

  Aloe-emodin has been reported to induce apoptosis in GC cells through mitochondrial- and death receptor-associated pathways and to modulate apoptosis-related proteins, including BCL2.[38,39] Hederagenin, another candidate compound identified in the present study, has been reported to inhibit the proliferation, adhesion, invasion, and migration of GC cells.[40] Recent evidence has also suggested that hederagenin may influence chemosensitivity in oxaliplatin-resistant GC cells through PI3K/Akt- and MAPK-related signaling.[17] These findings are broadly consistent with the apoptosis- and survival-related processes highlighted by our network analysis. Nevertheless, the present study did not experimentally compare individual compounds or directly evaluate interactions between Rhubarb- and Peach Kernel-derived constituents. Therefore, the relative contributions of individual compounds and any potential additive or synergistic effects of the herb pair remain to be determined.

  Protein–protein interaction analysis identified TP53, CASP3, JUN, and BCL2 as highly connected nodes within the predicted interaction network. These proteins are closely involved in apoptosis, cellular stress responses, proliferation, and tumor survival. TP53 plays a central role in cell cycle control, apoptosis, and maintenance of genomic stability,[41,42] whereas JUN participates in transcriptional programs associated with cell proliferation and invasion.[43,44] CASP3 is a major executioner caspase in the apoptotic cascade,[45] and BCL2 family proteins regulate mitochondrial apoptotic signaling and contribute to tumor-cell survival and treatment resistance.[46-48] The identification of these targets, therefore, provides a biologically plausible link between the predicted compound network and apoptosis-related tumor biology. However, these proteins are not specific to GC and are frequently implicated across a wide range of malignancies. Accordingly, their high connectivity may partly reflect their central roles in general cancer-related molecular networks rather than a gastric cancer-specific mechanism.

  Previous network pharmacology research by Zhang et al investigating Rheum palmatum in lung cancer likewise highlighted apoptosis-related biological processes and broadly cancer-associated molecular networks.[9] The partial convergence between that study and the present findings suggests that some predicted effects of Rhubarb-derived compounds may involve conserved tumor-associated processes, particularly apoptosis and cell-survival regulation. At the same time, such cross-cancer overlap emphasizes that highly connected targets identified through network pharmacology may not necessarily be disease-specific. Therefore, these shared molecular features may be more appropriately interpreted as potential pan-cancer biological processes predicted for Rhubarb-derived compounds rather than evidence of a mechanism unique to GC.

  This issue is particularly relevant given the substantial molecular heterogeneity of GC. The Cancer Genome Atlas classified gastric adenocarcinoma into 4 major molecular subtypes: Epstein–Barr virus-positive, microsatellite instability, genomically stable, and chromosomal instability tumors, each characterized by distinct molecular alterations.[49] Among the core targets identified in the present study, TP53 is particularly relevant to the molecular heterogeneity of GC because TP53 alterations are strongly represented in chromosomal instability-associated GCs. By contrast, CASP3, JUN, and BCL2 are not established subtype-defining markers in the TCGA classification and are more broadly involved in apoptosis, stress responses, and tumor cell survival. Thus, the core target profile identified here may reflect biological processes shared across multiple GC subtypes rather than a mechanism restricted to a single molecular subgroup. Because the present analysis was based on aggregated disease-associated targets rather than subtype-specific genomic or transcriptomic data, however, the relative relevance of these predicted targets to individual GC subtypes cannot be determined. Future studies integrating molecular subtype information and patient-derived molecular datasets may help clarify whether the predicted pharmacological effects differ among biologically distinct GC populations.

  The enrichment analyses further indicated that the predicted targets were associated with response to estradiol, apoptosis-related biological processes, and multiple cancer-associated signaling pathways, including the p53 signaling pathway. These findings are compatible with biological processes involved in gastric tumorigenesis, including dysregulated apoptosis, proliferative signaling, and cellular stress responses. Previous evidence has also suggested that estradiol-related signaling may influence GC cell proliferation and apoptosis.[50] However, pathway enrichment should be interpreted as evidence of statistical overrepresentation of predicted targets rather than direct activation or inhibition of the corresponding pathways. In particular, hepatitis B was among the highly ranked KEGG pathways in the present analysis. This finding should not be interpreted as indicating that hepatitis B itself represents a direct mechanism through which the Rhubarb–Peach Kernel pair affects GC. KEGG disease pathways may contain molecular components that are also involved in broadly conserved processes such as apoptosis, inflammation, and cellular stress responses. Accordingly, enrichment of hepatitis B and other disease-related pathways may reflect shared genes or overlapping pathway annotations within the predicted target set rather than direct disease-specific pharmacological activity.

  Molecular docking was used to further evaluate the predicted structural compatibility between representative compounds and the core target proteins identified through network analysis. All tested compound–target pairs showed predicted binding energies below −5.0 kcal/mol, and several pairs exhibited values below −7.0 kcal/mol, with an overall average predicted binding energy of −7.64 kcal/mol. Among the target proteins, BCL2 and IL1B showed relatively low average predicted binding energies across the tested compounds, whereas β-sitosterol and hederagenin exhibited relatively low average predicted binding energies across multiple targets. The lowest predicted binding energy was observed for the BCL2–β-sitosterol pair (−10.41 kcal/mol). These results are consistent with the network-based identification of several potential compound–target associations and provide additional computational evidence of structural compatibility. Nevertheless, docking scores are dependent on the scoring algorithm and structural assumptions used in the computational model and do not directly demonstrate physical binding, target modulation, or biological activity. Therefore, the docking results should be regarded as hypothesis-generating evidence that prioritizes candidate compound–target pairs for subsequent validation rather than as confirmation of the predicted pharmacological mechanisms.

  The present findings should also be interpreted within the increasingly biomarker-driven therapeutic landscape of GC. HER2-directed therapy, immune checkpoint inhibition, and CLDN18.2-targeted therapy illustrate the importance of molecular stratification in advanced gastric and gastroesophageal junction adenocarcinoma. The ToGA trial established the clinical benefit of adding trastuzumab to chemotherapy in HER2-positive advanced gastric or gastroesophageal junction cancer,[51] whereas the CheckMate 649 trial demonstrated improved outcomes with nivolumab plus chemotherapy in advanced gastric, gastroesophageal junction, or esophageal adenocarcinoma.[52] More recently, the phase III SPOTLIGHT and GLOW trials demonstrated clinical benefit from adding the CLDN18.2-targeted antibody zolbetuximab to chemotherapy in patients with CLDN18.2-positive, HER2-negative advanced gastric or gastroesophageal junction adenocarcinoma.[53,54] These trials support the distinct clinical relevance of HER2-, immune-, and CLDN18.2-directed therapeutic strategies.

  The core targets identified in the present study, including TP53, CASP3, JUN, and BCL2, do not directly overlap with HER2, PD-L1, or CLDN18.2. Nevertheless, the predicted apoptosis-, cell-survival-, and stress-response processes may intersect at a broader biological level with signaling programs that influence tumor progression and treatment response. Therefore, the present findings may suggest potential pathway-level complementarity rather than direct target overlap with currently established GC therapies. Importantly, such complementarity remains hypothetical. Of note, previous evidence suggesting that hederagenin may influence chemosensitivity in oxaliplatin-resistant GC cells through PI3K/Akt- and MAPK-related signaling raises the possibility that individual constituents identified in the present network could interact with pathways involved in treatment response.[17] However, this observation cannot be extrapolated to the Rhubarb–Peach Kernel pair as a whole. The present study did not directly investigate interactions with chemotherapy, HER2-directed agents, immune checkpoint inhibitors, or CLDN18.2-targeted therapy. Consequently, no conclusion regarding complementary or combination treatment can be drawn from the current computational findings, and dedicated pharmacological and experimental studies will be required to examine this possibility.


## 5. Conclusion
  In this study, we integrated network pharmacology and molecular docking approaches to investigate the potential mechanisms of the traditional Chinese medicinal pair Rheum palmatum–Peach Kernel in GC. Key active compounds (e.g., β-sitosterol, aloe-emodin, and hederagenin), core targets (e.g., TP53, CASP3, and BCL2), and important signaling pathways (e.g., apoptosis- and cancer-related pathways) were identified. These findings suggest that the Rhubarb–Peach Kernel pair may exert potential regulatory effects in GC through a multicomponent, multitarget, and multi-pathway mode of action.

  Nevertheless, this study represents a preliminary in silico investigation. The findings were derived from public databases and computational predictions, which may be affected by incomplete or outdated data sources. In addition, the complex pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic interactions among multiple herbal constituents were not evaluated. Therefore, the present results should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Further experimental validation, particularly through in vitro and in vivo studies, is required to confirm the biological relevance of the predicted compound–target interactions and the proposed mechanisms.


## 6. Limitations
  Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the identification of candidate compounds and predicted targets relied primarily on public databases, including TCMSP, DrugBank, GeneCards, OMIM, and TTD. Differences in database coverage, update frequency, and underlying prediction algorithms may introduce selection bias, incomplete target capture, and false-positive associations. In addition, the use of predefined OB and DL thresholds may exclude compounds with potential biological activity that do not meet these screening criteria.

  Second, network topology reflects connectivity within a constructed interaction network and does not necessarily indicate biological importance or disease specificity. Several core targets identified in this study, such as TP53, CASP3, JUN, and BCL2, are broadly involved in cancer-related processes and may therefore represent shared tumor-associated mechanisms rather than pathways specific to GC. Likewise, enrichment analysis identifies statistical overrepresentation of annotated genes and pathways but does not directly demonstrate pathway activation or inhibition. Disease-related pathways, such as hepatitis B, may therefore arise from shared genes and overlapping pathway annotations rather than direct disease-specific effects.

  Third, molecular docking provides computational estimates of structural compatibility between compounds and proteins but cannot confirm actual binding affinity, target modulation, or downstream biological activity. In addition, the present docking analysis did not include experimental binding validation or reference ligand controls. Therefore, the docking results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory evidence.

  Finally, the predicted compounds, targets, and pathways were not validated through in vitro or in vivo experiments, and the study did not incorporate GC molecular subtype-specific data. Consequently, it remains unclear whether the predicted effects differ among distinct molecular subtypes or whether the Rhubarb–Peach Kernel pair may interact with existing therapies, including chemotherapy, HER2-directed therapy, immune checkpoint inhibition, or CLDN18.2-targeted treatment. Moreover, the present study cannot establish additive or synergistic effects between Rhubarb and Peach Kernel. Further experimental and translational studies are required to validate the predicted compound–target relationships and clarify their biological and potential therapeutic relevance in GC.
