# TNK1 is a targetable JAK-independent driver of STAT signaling and inflammation


## Abstract
  Deregulated inflammatory signaling via STAT family transcription factors, particularly STAT1, underlies a variety of immune-related diseases, including inflammatory bowel disease. Whereas activation of STATs by JAKs via canonical receptor-driven JAK–STAT signaling is well understood, little is known about JAK-independent mechanisms of STAT activation. Here, we identify the understudied nonreceptor tyrosine kinase TNK1 as a therapeutically targetable, JAK-independent activator of STAT signaling. Using a multiomics approach, we mapped a network of TNK1 substrates associated with protein condensates and proinflammatory signaling, including STAT1. We found that TNK1, but not its sister kinase ACK1, directly phosphorylates STATs at well described STAT-activating JAK sites. In cells, TNK1-mediated STAT1 phosphorylation and activation occurs independently of JAKs. Imaging and interactomics data suggest that TNK1 interacts with STAT1 in cytosolic condensates, which likely compartmentalize TNK1-substrate interactions. We show that an intrinsically disordered proline-rich region in TNK1, which includes a 14-3-3 docking phosphorylation site, is required for the formation of kinase-active TNK1 condensates and STAT1 phosphorylation. Mutations within the proline-rich region that eliminate 14-3-3 binding increase formation of TNK1 condensates, suggesting a model in which 14-3-3 acts as a clamp that constrains the flexible PRR to inhibit condensate formation and STAT1 activation. Finally, we show that TNK1 is a targetable driver of STAT1-mediated inflammation in the gut as inhibition of TNK1 reduces active STAT1 in the colon and ameliorates colitis symptoms in mice.


## Results

### A multiomics approach identifies TNK1 substrates involved in inflammatory signaling
  With the goal of identifying TNK1 substrates, we took advantage of the Hodgkin lymphoma cell line L540, which carries a chromosomal inversion resulting in a C-terminally truncated and active form of TNK1 (Gu et al. 2010; Chan et al. 2021; Ahmed and Miller 2022). In previous work, we found that this truncation eliminates an inhibitory 14-3-3 phospho-binding site, which is key to constitutively activating the kinase (Chan et al. 2021). Importantly, these L540 cells are dependent on endogenous TNK1 for the bulk of their phospho-tyrosine signaling. Indeed, treatment of these cells with a small molecule inhibitor of TNK1, TP5801, which we developed in a previous study (Chan et al. 2021), abolishes most of their global phospho-tyrosine signal (Fig. 1A). This suggests that the L540 cells could provide a good initial model for identifying TNK1-dependent phospho-tyrosine sites by proteomics.

  To identify TNK1 substrates, L540s were treated with or without TP5801, then processed for phospho-tyrosine peptide antibody-based enrichment and quantification of phospho-peptides by LC-MS/MS in biological triplicate (Fig. 1B). Consistent with Figure 1A, we found that TNK1 inhibition led to a drop in numerous LC-MS/MS-detected phospho-tyrosine peptides (Supplemental Fig. S1A). In total, 874 phospho-tyrosine sites were more than fourfold decreased by TP5801 with a P-value of ≤0.05 across replicates (Supplemental Fig. S1A; Supplemental Table S1). We then aligned the sequences surrounding each of these 874 potential TNK1 substrate sites and uploaded them to the recently updated kinase library tool from Cantley, Johnson, and colleagues (Johnson et al. 2023; Yaron-Barir et al. 2024), which allowed us to compare our list of potential TNK1 substrates with the in vitro phospho-motif sequence preferences of all catalytically active tyrosine kinases. Strikingly, this analysis showed that among all tyrosine kinases, the phospho-motif preference of TNK1 was the most significant match to the phospho-tyrosine motif sequences in our candidate substrate list (Fig. 1C), providing confidence that our MS-identified phospho-sites included direct TNK1 substrates.

  From our list of potential TNK1 substrates, a few gene ontology (GO) categories stood out. First, we saw an enrichment in RNA processing proteins and condensate scaffolding proteins, many of which are associated with P-body (PB) and stress granule (SG) condensates (Supplemental Fig. S1B,C; Youn et al. 2019; Curdy et al. 2023). For example, ~20% of the PB-associated proteins identified in a recent proteomics study by Franchini and colleagues (Curdy et al. 2023) were scored as putative TNK1 substrates in our data. These PB-associated proteins include RNA-binding and condensate scaffolding proteins, but also curiously include STAT3, and STAT1—although why STAT proteins localize to cytosolic condensates is not known (Curdy et al. 2023; Fujikawa et al. 2023). The next most prominent GO categories include immune-related and inflammatory signaling proteins with the notable inclusion of STAT1 as a common gene across several categories (Fig. 1D; Supplemental Fig. S1D,E).

  To help narrow our focus and prioritize direct TNK1 substrates from these pY proteomics data, we took a coimmunoprecipitation and proteomics approach to identify TNK1-binding partners. As a complement to the L540 cells and to gain deeper mechanistic insight with mutant forms of TNK1, we shifted focus to a different cell system in which we could fairly compare a panel of TNK1 mutants. In short, we used CRISPR/Cas9 to delete endogenous TNK1 from HCT-116 Trex FlpIn cells, then inserted WT or TNK1 mutants, C-terminally fused to GFP, into a single doxycycline-inducible locus to allow for titratable TNK1 expression. These TNK1 mutants, depicted in Figure 1E, include “AAA,” which carries three Ser-to-Ala mutations (S500A, S502A, S505A) that disrupt 14-3-3 binding to TNK1. Although we had previously shown that phosphorylation at S502 is the primary 14-3-3 binding site phosphorylation, our previous data suggested that minor levels of phosphorylations at the adjacent S500 and S505 may mediate a low level of 14-3-3 binding (Chan et al. 2021). Thus, mutation of all three completely eliminate 14-3-3 binding (Chan et al. 2021). We also included a TNK1 mutant lacking its C-terminal ubiquitin-associated domain (ΔUBA), and an additional mutant that combines both mutations (AAA-ΔUBA), which mimics the C-terminal TNK1 truncation in L540 cells in that it removes the C-terminal UBA domain and inhibitory 14-3-3 binding site (Chan et al. 2021). After a 48 h doxycycline induction of TNK1 (or GFP control), cells were lysed and TNK1 was immunopurified with an anti-GFP nanobody resin (Fig. 1E; Supplemental Fig. S1F), followed by LC-MS/MS in biological triplicate. A full list of TNK1-interacting partners for each TNK1 variant that rises above the threshold of twofold or greater change and P-value of ≤0.05 is in Supplemental Table S2.

  To focus our effort on finding TNK1 substrates, we looked for overlap between these TNK1-interacting proteins and putative substrates from the pY proteomics data set (Fig. 1F; Supplemental Table S3). A subset of these overlapping proteins was common across all TNK1 variants and many showed a trend of increased interaction with the TNK1-AAA mutant (Fig. 1G), suggesting that release of TNK1 from 14-3-3 perhaps frees TNK1 to interact with these potential substrates. Among proteins that overlapped between the data sets were several inflammation-associated proteins, including TRAF4, STAT3, and STAT1 (Fig. 1G)—all of which showed enhanced interaction with the 14-3-3 binding-defective TNK1-AAA mutants.


### TNK1 promotes the transcription of STAT1 targets
  To further narrow our focus on TNK1 substrates from the proteomics data, we performed RNA-seq looking for TNK1-mediated transcriptional effects that could be linked bioinformatically to substrates. For this experiment, we used the HCT-116 Trex FlpIn cells expressing doxycycline-inducible WT and mutant forms of TNK1 from Figure 1, E and G (Fig. 2A). We found that disruption of 14-3-3 binding with the AAA mutation led to increased TNK1-mediated gene expression broadly, both in the context of full-length and UBA-truncated TNK1 (Fig. 2B; Supplemental Fig. S2A). GSEA analysis showed a significant enrichment in IFN-related and STAT1 target genes in cells expressing all forms of TNK1, but particularly the 14-3-3-binding-defective AAA mutants (Fig. 2C–F; Supplemental Fig. S2B,C). Among the top 40 most significantly upregulated genes in TNK1-AAA-expressing cells (sorted by adjusted P-value), all but four genes were previously annotated as STAT1 targets (Fig. 2D), an observation that was also supported by GSEA analysis (Supplemental Fig. S2C; Rouillard et al. 2016; Diamant et al. 2025). Among all TNK1-AAA-induced genes that rise above the threshold of twofold or greater change and P-value of ≤0.05, ~50% are known STAT1 targets (Fig. 2E,F; Supplemental Fig. S2D–G), indicating a strong STAT1 gene signature. Collectively, these data suggest that disruption of 14-3-3 binding to TNK1 leads to enhanced interactions with other proteins (Fig. 1F,G), including STATs, and increased transcription of STAT1 gene targets. Thus, together, the phospho-tyrosine proteomics, interactomics, and RNA-seq data pointed us to STAT1 as a functionally important TNK1 substrate candidate.


### Phosphorylation of STAT proteins by TNK1 is independent of JAKs
  The pY substrate site on STAT1 identified in our proteomics data is Y701, a well-described JAK phosphorylation site that promotes STAT1 activation (Philips et al. 2022). To validate our proteomics data, we confirmed that inhibition of endogenous TNK1 with TP5801 in L540 cells results in reduced phosphorylation of STAT1 at Y701, as well as STAT2 and STAT3 at their canonical JAK sites (Fig. 3A). RNAi-mediated depletion of TNK1 in L540 cells also inhibited STAT1 phosphorylation (Fig. 3B). These data support a small but growing body of data correlating TNK1 expression with activation of STAT proteins (Ooi et al. 2014; Armacki et al. 2018; Chan et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2021b). However, whether TNK1 promotes STAT signaling indirectly via JAKs or other mechanisms is unclear (Ooi et al. 2014; Bao et al. 2020). In canonical JAK–STAT signaling, a cytokine receptor scaffolds the JAK–STAT interaction, allowing JAKs to phosphorylate STATs, which leads to STAT dimerization via phospho-tyrosine-SH2 binding. Thus, we asked whether our data represent an unusual scenario in which TNK1, as a nonreceptor tyrosine kinase, acts upstream of JAKs (Fig. 3C). First, we tested the impact of multiple JAK inhibitors on TNK1-mediated STAT1 phosphorylation. We saw subtle inhibition of TNK1-mediated STAT1 phosphorylation by the JAK3 inhibitor decernotinib in our HCT-116 TRex system; however, according to proteinatlas.org and our own immunoblots, JAK3 is not expressed in these cells so we reasoned this was likely an off-target effect of the inhibitor on TNK1 (Fig. 3D,E). Other JAK inhibitors had no effect on TNK1-mediated STAT1 phosphorylation at concentrations that inhibited canonical JAK-dependent STAT1 phosphorylation by IFN-γ (Fig. 3F,G), whereas the TNK1 inhibitor TP5801 showed near complete inhibition of STAT1 phosphorylation in TNK1-induced cells (Fig. 3D,E). Similarly, JAK inhibitors had little effect on STAT1 phosphorylation in the L540 cells whereas the TNK1 inhibitor completely blocked STAT1 phosphorylation (Supplemental Fig. S3A). In agreement with the JAK inhibitor data, siRNA-mediated depletion of all individual JAKs alone or in combination did not reduce TNK1-mediated STAT phosphorylation (Fig. 3H–J). Lastly, to determine whether TNK1 is capable of directly activating STATs, we used in vitro kinase assays with purified components. We found that TNK1 could robustly phosphorylate all STATs at their activating JAK substrate sites, whereas its sister kinase ACK1 showed little activity toward STAT1, STAT2, STAT3, and STAT6 and marginal activity toward STAT4 and STAT5 (Fig. 3K; Supplemental Fig. S3B–F). Taken together, our data indicate that STAT proteins are direct TNK1 substrates.


### 14-3-3 binding to TNK1 inhibits the formation of kinase-active puncta that colocalize with STAT1
  Using the panel of doxycycline-inducible TNK1 WT and mutants from Figure 1E, we found that disruption of 14-3-3 binding with the AAA mutation enhances TNK1-mediated STAT1 phosphorylation (Fig. 4A–D), which supported the observation that TNK1-AAA shows increased interaction with STAT1 (Fig. 1G) and transcription of STAT1 target genes (Fig. 2). To understand mechanistically how 14-3-3 controls TNK1 activity toward STATs, we noted that in the absence of 14-3-3 binding, TNK1 forms cytosolic puncta that contain active kinase as indicated by an autophosphorylation at Y277(Fig. 4E–G; May et al. 2010). This phenotype is most dramatic with TNK1-ΔUBA, which shifts from diffuse and mostly inactive with an intact 14-3-3 phospho-binding site to punctate and active with the 14-3-3 binding site mutated (cf. TNK1-ΔUBA and AAA-ΔUBA in Fig. 4E,G). Next, we asked whether STAT1 colocalizes with TNK1 in these puncta. As shown in Figure 4, F and H–J (see also Supplemental Fig. S4A,B), proximity ligation assays showed that the 14-3-3 binding-defective AAA mutation causes a marked increase in TNK1 interaction with STAT1 in cytosolic puncta. We also see a corresponding increase of phosphorylated STAT1 (pY701) within these TNK1-AAA puncta compared to WT TNK1 (Supplemental Fig. S4B).


### Loss of 14-3-3 binding allows formation of TNK1 condensates, which promote STAT1 phosphorylation
  We first considered the idea that the kinase-active TNK1 puncta in Figure 4E are associated with cellular structures, such as organelles or endosomes. However, all attempts to colocalize TNK1 puncta with various organelles and endosomal markers failed (data not shown); thus, we explored other possibilities. One important clue came from an initially puzzling observation that although loss of 14-3-3 binding with the AAA mutation activates TNK1 toward substrates (e.g., STAT1 in Fig. 4A–D), it also reduces TNK1 protein levels by immunoblot, which is most visible when cells are lysed by nonionic detergents as in Figure 5, A and B. We then asked whether loss of 14-3-3 binding causes TNK1 to shift into a detergent-insoluble fraction, making active TNK1 essentially disappear from our detergent lysate immunoblots. Similar shifts from detergent soluble to insoluble have been seen with other enzymes upon activation (Moquin et al. 2013; Najjar et al. 2016; Amin et al. 2018; Robeson et al. 2018). Indeed, as shown in Figure 5, C and D, lysis of the TX-100 insoluble cell pellet with urea showed that mutation of the 14-3-3 binding site on TNK1 leads to a relative enrichment of TNK1 in a TX-100-insoluble fraction.

  Previous studies have shown that protein insolubility in nonionic detergents can be caused by proteins undergoing liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) to form droplet-like condensates (Berry et al. 2015; Sridharan et al. 2022). To evaluate the possible condensate properties of TNK1 puncta, we took advantage of our TRex FlpIn HCT-116 TNK1 KO cells reconstituted with GFP-tagged versions of TNK1 at a single dox-inducible locus—allowing low-level titratable expression of TNK1 WT and mutants. Live-cell imaging showed that in the absence of 14-3-3 binding, TNK1 forms dynamic puncta with the liquid-like properties of fission and fusion (Fig. 5E; Supplemental Movies S1A–D, S2A–D, S3A–D). Furthermore, fluorescence-recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) indicated dynamic diffusion of TNK1 in and out of the puncta, consistent with TNK1 puncta being phase-separated protein condensates (Fig. 5F,G). Some ubiquitin-rich condensates are targets of autophagy (Turco et al. 2021), thus we asked whether these condensates represent a pool of TNK1 being targeted for autophagy. However, inhibition of autophagy with bafilomycin A1 in cycloheximide chase experiments did not consistently elevate baseline TNK1 levels over control or block TNK1 degradation in contrast to the positive control autophagy adapter p62/SQSTM1 (Supplemental Fig. S4C). In addition, we found that disruption of TNK1 condensates with 1,6 hexanediol reduced STAT1 phosphorylation, suggesting that these condensates are active signaling compartments for TNK1 (Supplemental Fig. S4D,E). Thus, together our data suggest a model in which 14-3-3 binding holds TNK1 in an inactive conformation that inhibits phase separation, making TNK1 appear diffuse in the cytosol, whereas release from 14-3-3 allows TNK1 to form kinase-active condensates where it likely interacts with STAT1 (Fig. 5H; Supplemental Fig. S4F).


### Modeling and deletion mapping points to sequences within the disordered TNK1 PRR as essential for condensate formation, STAT1 activation, and oncogenic truncation
  The primary 14-3-3 docking phosphorylation in TNK1, pS502, sits within a long intrinsically disordered PRR that has features of an LLPS-prone sequence, including stretches of low amino acid complexity and roughly 20% proline content. Online LLPS prediction tools score the TNK1 PRR highly for LLPS features (Fig. 6A). Molecular dynamics simulation of the impact of 14-3-3 binding on the TNK1 PRR suggests that 14-3-3 binding would constrain the flexibility of the PRR (Fig. 6B–D; Supplemental Fig. S5A–D), which in turn would inhibit the multivalency and LLPS propensity of the PRR. Indeed, deletion of the PRR reduces TNK1 puncta formation, instead shifting TNK1 to a diffuse pattern in the cytosol with some nuclear localization and limited nuclear or tightly perinuclear puncta (Fig. 6E). This stands in contrast to TNK1-AAA, which has an intact PRR but lacks the 14-3-3 binding site and is almost exclusively in large cytosolic condensates (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, consistent with the idea that TNK1 acts on STAT1 within cytosolic condensates, deletion of the TNK1 PRR also reduces STAT1 phosphorylation (Fig. 6F,G). These data suggest that 14-3-3 acts as a clamp on the disordered TNK1-PRR to inhibit the formation of cytosolic TNK1 condensates and, in turn, block TNK1-mediated STAT1 phosphorylation.

  One data point that seemingly conflicts with the idea that the PRR is essential for STAT phosphorylation is that in the Hodgkin lymphoma L540 cell line, the activating TNK1 truncation, which occurs at amino acid 472, removes most of the PRR—yet STAT phosphorylation is TNK1-dependent in these cells (Fig. 3A). To shed more light on this possible discrepancy, we mapped the critical regions of the PRR with finer deletions and truncations (Supplemental Fig. S5E). As shown in Figure 6, F and G, the internal PRR deletions of Δ442–510 and Δ501–588 pointed to the 442–500 region as essential for full TNK1 activity toward STAT1. In addition, C-terminal truncations of TNK1 down to amino acid 450 are still capable of strong STAT1 phosphorylation, whereas truncation to amino acid 441 eliminates STAT1 phosphorylation, further narrowing the essential PRR sequence to amino acids 442–449 (Fig. 6F,G). We also noted that deletions in the 531–588 region reduced TNK1 activity, but reasoned this is most likely due to these constructs maintaining an intact inhibitory 14-3-3 binding site, and our prior studies had found that truncations C-terminal to the 14-3-3 phospho-binding site (S502) increase inhibitory 14-3-3 binding (Chan et al. 2021). All together, these data suggest that for oncogenic TNK1 truncations to preserve the TNK1-STAT connection, they must leave the early sequence of the PRR (442–449) intact while also eliminating the 14-3-3 binding site. This suggests a narrow window for oncogenic truncations between amino acids ~450 and ~503—the precise location of the Hodgkin lymphoma truncation (Fig. 6G).


### Inhibition of TNK1 ameliorates DSS-induced colitis and blocks STAT1 activation in the colon
  The goal of this study was to understand the biological function of TNK1 by elucidating its substrate network, which could ultimately guide the development of a TNK1 inhibitor. Together with the data presented here, several lines of evidence point to a proinflammatory role of TNK1 in the gut. For example, while TNK1 expression is limited in mature organisms, it is relatively high in cells of the intestinal epithelium, including gastric mucous cells, parietal cells, and chief cells (proteinatlas.org). In addition, preclinical models and human data support STATs as drivers of IBD pathology in the intestinal epithelial barrier (Supplemental Fig. S6A), and TNK1 deletion in IECs abrogates CD45+ immune cell infiltration in the DSS-induced model of acute colitis (Günther et al. 2016; Armacki et al. 2018; Crnčec et al. 2018; Nakanishi et al. 2018; Günther et al. 2019; Metwally et al. 2025). Together with our observation that TNK1 directly activates STATs, the data suggest that TNK1 may be a therapeutic target to block STAT activity and inflammation in the gut. To test this idea, we applied our TNK1 inhibitor TP5801 to the DSS colitis mouse model. As shown in Figure 7, treatment of mice with TP5801 significantly improved all colitis metrics tested, including weight loss, colon length, stool score, goblet cell counts, CD45+ immune infiltration into the colon, and overall colitis severity scores (Fig. 7A–G). Furthermore, inhibition of TNK1 showed nearly complete abrogation of DSS-induced phosphorylation of STAT1 in colon tissue and expression of STAT1 gene targets (Fig. 7H–K). A similar loss of DSS-induced STAT1 phosphorylation was also observed with gut-specific deletion of TNK1 in mice, suggesting that the effect of the inhibitor is due to on-target TNK1 inhibition (Supplemental Fig. S6B). Moreover, using a tet-on system for doxycycline-induced ectopic expression of TNK1 in the intestinal epithelium of mice (Armacki et al. 2018), we found that TNK1 expression activates STAT1 within 24 h (Supplemental Fig. S6C). Together, our data provide a mechanism of direct activation of STAT1 by TNK1 and suggest that TNK1 plays a physiological role in the gut as a JAK-independent activator of STAT1 signaling, highlighting TNK1 as a potential therapeutic target for IBD.


## Discussion
  Although decades of work on STAT regulation have demonstrated a common hierarchy of receptor-mediated JAK-to-STAT signaling, there are contexts in which activation of STATs may involve alternative mechanisms, including via TNK1 or other non-JAK NRTKs. For example, certain subsets of triple-negative breast cancer with high phospho-STAT3 are not responsive to JAK inhibition and do not show amplification or activating point mutations in JAKs (Banerjee and Resat 2016; Stover et al. 2018). In addition, perturbations in TNK1 expression or mutations that disrupt 14-3-3 binding to TNK1 could underlie JAK-independent STAT activation in some cancers. One clear example in this study is the HL cell line L540, in which a truncated TNK1 that lacks the 14-3-3 binding site promotes STAT activation (Fig. 3A; Gu et al. 2010; Chan et al. 2021). Similar truncations have been seen in cutaneous lymphoma (Bastidas Torres et al. 2022), but the overall frequency of these truncations and their association with oncogenic STAT activity is still unclear.

  In IBD, the sources of STAT1 activation may be more complex than typically appreciated, which could help explain the relatively high percentage of patients that do not respond to JAK inhibitors (Lei et al. 2021; Honap et al. 2024). JAK-independent STAT activation has been seen downstream from tyrosine kinases, including FLT3, SRC, and ABL (Turkson et al. 1998; Zhang et al. 2000; Nakamura et al. 2005), yet examples of specific non-JAK tyrosine kinases directly activating STATs are limited. Perhaps the best comparison to the data presented here is the NRTK spleen tyrosine kinase (SYK), which, in the early stage of RNA virus-triggered RIG-I signaling, was reported to activate STAT1—potentially providing a means for early intracellular STAT1 activation ahead of cytokine-driven JAK–STAT signaling (Liu et al. 2021a). Similarly, TNK1 may respond to intracellular stress to initiate early STAT1 activation in the gut. If so, inhibition of TNK1 would blunt downstream signal amplification by eliminating a root source of inflammatory signaling, which could explain the effectiveness of TNK1 inhibition or deletion in blocking STAT1 activation (Fig. 7; Supplemental Fig. S6). In the future, more sensitive measures of TNK1 activation (e.g., phosphorylation at pY277) could help delineate temporal relationships between TNK1-dependent and JAK-dependent STAT activation.

  Our data suggest a new mechanism of STAT activation wherein the loss of 14-3-3 binding sends TNK1 into kinase-active condensates (Fig. 4E) to interact with and phosphorylate STAT1 (Fig. 4F–J). Interestingly, STAT1 and STAT3 have been identified in recent proteomics studies as components of cytosolic condensates (Curdy et al. 2023; Fujikawa et al. 2023). It is notable that within these condensates, STAT1 and STAT3 stand out as rare transcription factors among a sea of RNA-regulatory, ubiquitin-related, and condensate scaffolding proteins, begging the question of why they are there. Our data suggest that at least certain cytosolic condensates are compartments for intracellular STAT activation, perhaps acting as a platform for induced proximity—bringing kinases and substrates together. In addition, our work adds to the recent concept that 14-3-3 regulates the phase separation of its binding partners (Liu et al. 2023, 2024; Segal et al. 2023; Carey et al. 2025; Thomas et al. 2025). Indeed, the interaction between 14-3-3 and an IDR, like the PRR in TNK1, is a theme of 14-3-3 binding sites (Bustos and Iglesias 2006; Sluchanko and Bustos 2019; Egbert et al. 2023) and we suspect that many more examples, like TNK1, will emerge showing 14-3-3 regulating the phase separation of its client proteins.

  The model described in Figure 5H raises important questions for future study. First, what is the upstream signal that activates TNK1? Interestingly, TNK1 lacks domains that related kinases use to interact with receptors, including the SH2 domains of JAKs and Mig6 homology region of ACK1 (Prieto-Echagüe and Miller 2011), and we have yet to find any receptor requirement for TNK1-mediated STAT activation—although a handful of receptors were identified as potential TNK1 interactors in Supplemental Table S3 (e.g., RIG-I, TRADD, and FGFR4). Nevertheless, our data suggest that any signal that activates TNK1 would need to inhibit its interaction with 14-3-3. We previously found that microtubule affinity regulating kinases (MARKs) phosphorylate the 14-3-3 docking site on TNK1 at S502 (Chan et al. 2021), suggesting that MARKs may be a key brake on TNK1 activation—either by sequestering a pool of TNK1 in the cytosol and only releasing TNK1 upon some upstream signal or perhaps by phosphorylating and inhibiting TNK1 at the end of an inflammatory reaction. Unfortunately, little is known about MARK regulation and function. MARK4 has been implicated in inflammatory signaling (Liu et al. 2016; Li et al. 2017), but how this might link to TNK1 and 14-3-3 is still unknown. Future work should address these questions.

  Second, can other TNK1 substrates help explain the function of TNK1? Condensate-associated proteins, including UPF1 (Sun et al. 2020) and ADAR1 (Corbet et al. 2021), were enriched in our substrate list from phospho-tyrosine proteomics (Supplemental Fig. S1B,C), and it will be exciting to understand how these phosphorylations affect condensate dynamics. We also identified other inflammatory signaling proteins as potential substrates, including NF-κB subunits, A20, and TRAF4, many of which have also been implicated in IBD (Rogler et al. 1998; Chang et al. 2009; Vereecke et al. 2010; Garcia-Carbonell et al. 2018). We suspect that many of these substrates, in addition to STATs, account for the role of TNK1 in inflammatory signaling.

  In summary, our study provides the first substrate network of the understudied kinase TNK1 and reveals an unusual JAK-independent mechanism of STAT activation in cytosolic condensates. Most importantly, our data highlight TNK1 as a potential therapeutic target to suppress inflammation in the gut. It will be important to identify other disease contexts in which TNK1 inhibition could have benefit. One such context may be the systemic inflammation caused by traumatic injury, as TNK1 was reported to mediate inflammation-induced tissue damage in models of hemorrhagic shock (Halbgebauer et al. 2020). This and other acute and chronic inflammatory conditions should be explored to fully realize the potential of TNK1 as therapeutic target.


## Materials and methods

### Maintenance of cell lines
  HEK293T, HEK293A, and HCT116 cells were purchased from ATCC and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% Pen/Strep. TRex HCT116 cells were a kind donation from Dr. Jonathan K. Alder and were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% Pen/Strep. L540 were purchased from DSMZ-German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures and were cultured in RPMI supplemented with 20% FBS. All cells are incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2.


### Generation of cell lines and pY277 antibody
  To generate the TRex HCT116 TNK1 cell lines, TRex HCT116 cells were cotransfected with the pDNAs POG44 and the respective pcDNA 5.0 FRTTO constructs at a 9:1 ratio. Forty-eight hours after transfection, the transfected cells were treated with 150 μg/mL hygromycin for 14 days. The hygromycin-resistant TRex HCT116 clones were screened for TNK1 stable expression by treating them with doxycycline for 24 h, harvested and immunoblotted for TNK1. The TNK1-expressing cells were frozen and stored at −80°C. The TNK1 pY277 phospho-specific antibody was developed in rabbits by Pacific Immunology against a synthetic peptide, GGARGR-pY-VMGGPR, targeting the TNK1 Y277 phosphorylation site (amino acids 269–287). Rabbit serum was subjected to affinity chromatography with pY277 peptide to obtain the pY277 phospho-specific antibody.


### Mutagenesis and cell transfection
  FLAG-TNK1 expression plasmid was purchased from GenScript. All mutagenesis was performed using the Q5 site-directed mutagenesis kit (NEB), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were plated 1 day before transfection to obtain 40%–50% cell confluency on the day of transfection. Transfection complex was prepared at 1:4 DNA/transporter five ratio in DMEM without FBS and incubated for 15 min at room temperature. After DNA-transporter five complexes were added to the cells, cells were returned to the incubator. Media were replaced after 4 h.


### Immunoprecipitation and Western blot
  For TNK1 immunoprecipitation, transfected or stably expressing cells were washed and harvested in ice-cold PBS. Cell pellets were lysed in co-IP lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl at pH 6.8, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 0.3% Triton X-100) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors and rotated for 10 min at 4°C. Lysates were then homogenized by passaging through a 25 gauge needle and centrifuged at 21,000g for 10 min to clarify. Clarified lysate were collected for immunoprecipitation and/or western blot. For FLAG pulldown, lysates were incubated with anti-DYKDDDDK G1 Affinity Trap (ProteinTech) for 1 h at 4°C with rotation. The beads were washed with PBS three times. The coimmunoprecipitated proteins were eluted with SDS sample buffer by boiling for 5 min at 95°C. Lysates were resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membrane using iBlot3 Western blotting system. Membranes were blocked with 5% phosphoblocker (Cell Biolabs) in PBST for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies against proteins of interest were diluted at 1:500 or 1:1000 in blocking buffer and incubated with blot overnight at 4°C. Proteins were visualized and quantified using infrared fluorescent secondary antibodies IRDye 800CW goat antirabbit (1:10,000; LI-COR 92632211) and IRDye 680RD goat antimouse (1:10,000; LI-COR 926–68070). Sapphire FL and Image Studio Lite software were used for analysis.

  L540 cells were harvested and lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.1% Triton X-100, 0.1% SDS, 140 mM NaCl, 0.1% deoxycholate) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Lysates were incubated on ice and vortexed every 2 min for 10 min. Lysates were then centrifuged at 21,000g for 10 min, and the supernatants were transferred to new tubes. Lysates were then mixed with 5× SDS sample buffer and boiled for 5 min at 95°C. Western blotting was performed as described above.

### Quantitative LC-MS/MS analysis
  Quantitative LC-MS/MS was performed on 4 μL of each sample, using a Vanquish Neo UPLC system (Thermo) coupled to a Thermo Orbitrap Astral high-resolution accurate mass tandem mass spectrometer (Thermo). Briefly, the sample was first trapped on a Symmetry C18 20 mm × 180 μm trapping column (5 μL/min at 99.9/0.1 [v/v] water/acetonitrile), after which the analytical separation was performed using a 1.5 μm EvoSep 150 μm ID × 8 cm performance column (EvoSep) with a 30 min gradient of 5%–30% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid at a flow rate of 500 nL/min with a column temperature of 50°C. Data collection on the Orbitrap Astral mass spectrometer was performed in a data-independent acquisition (DIA) mode of acquisition with a r = 240,000 (at m/z 200) full MS scan from m/z 380–1080 with a target AGC value of 4 × 105 ions. Fixed DIA windows of 5 m/z from m/z 380–1080 DIA MS/MS scans were acquired in the Astral with a target AGC value of 5 × 104 and maximum fill time of 8 msec. HCD collision energy setting of 27% was used for all MS2 scans. The total analysis cycle time for each sample injection was ~45 min.

  Following nine total UPLC-MS/MS analyses (Supplemental Table S1), data were imported into Spectronaut (Biognosis) and individual LCMS data files were aligned based on the accurate mass and retention time of detected precursor and fragment ions. Relative peptide abundance was measured based on MS2 fragment ions of selected ion chromatograms of the aligned features across all runs. The MS/MS data was searched against a SwissProt Human database (downloaded in December 2023), a common contaminant/spiked protein database (bovine albumin, bovine casein, yeast ADH, etc.), and an equal number of reversed sequence “decoys” for false discovery rate determination. A library free Spectonaut perform the database searches. Database search parameters included fixed modification on Cys (carbamidomethyl), variable modification on Met (oxidation) and Ser/Thr, and Tyr (phosphorylation). Full trypsin enzyme rules were used along with 10 ppm mass tolerances on precursor ions and 20 ppm on product ion. Spectral annotation was set at a maximum 1% peptide false discovery rate based on q-value calculations. Note that peptide homology was addressed using razor rules in which a peptide matched to multiple different proteins was exclusively assigned to the protein has more identified peptides. Protein homology was addressed by grouping proteins that had the same set of peptides to account for their identification. A master protein within a group was assigned based on % coverage. The mass spectrometry proteomics data in this study have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE (Perez-Riverol et al. 2025) partner repository with the data set identifier PXD066275.

### Molecular dynamics
  To investigate how 14-3-3 inhibits the formation of phase-separated TNK1 condensates, we extracted a 100 amino acid region of TNK1 and conducted equilibrium molecular dynamics (MD) simulations both in isolation and in complex with 14-3-3. We then evaluated the structural dynamics of this region using several metrics that reflect conformational flexibility. Specifically, we analyzed structural of TNK1 properties including the radius of gyration (Rg) over time, which reflects the evolution of the protein’s overall compactness; the solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), which indicates the extent to which residues are exposed to solvent; the root mean square deviation (RMSD) of TNK1 α carbon (Cα) atoms from their initial positions; and the root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) of the Cα atoms, which indicates the extent of local atomic fluctuations around their mean positions. Additionally, we performed principal component analysis (PCA) to assess the variability and dominant modes of motion of TNK1 in its bound versus unbound states. If TNK1 becomes less conformationally flexible (i.e., less “floppy”) upon binding to 14-3-3, we would expect to observe a lower Rg (indicating a more compact structure), a reduced SASA (reflecting decreased solvent exposure), lower RMSD (suggesting more structural stability over time), and decreased RMSF values (indicating less local fluctuation). PCA would similarly be expected to show reduced conformational variance, with motions being more constrained in the bound state.


### All-atom molecular dynamics simulations
  The TNK1 sequence (based on the construct provided by J.L. Andersen; UniProt ID: Q13470) was used to generate 3D structural models both in isolation and in complex with 14-3-3 using AlphaFold. Simulation boxes were constructed separately for the isolated TNK1 and the TNK1–14-3-3 complex and solvated using the TIP3P water model, with box dimensions adjusted to prevent boundary artifacts. A physiological concentration of 150 mM NaCl was added to approximate biological ionic strength and stabilize protein–water interactions (Ross et al. 2018).

  The TNK1–14-3-3 simulation system consisted of 252,039 water molecules, 479 ions, and 6049 protein atoms, whereas the TNK1-only system included 255,909 water molecules, 499 ions, and 2196 protein atoms. To preserve the structural integrity of the TNK1–14-3-3 complex, an intermolecular distance-based restraint was applied between residue K49 of 14-3-3 and residue S306 of TNK1, specifically between the NZ atom of K49 and the OG atom of S306. The restraint was set with an initial distance of 0.475 nm and a force constant of 1000 kJ mol−1 nm−2. Both systems underwent energy minimization, equilibration, and production simulations to evaluate their structural dynamics under equilibrium conditions. Two replicas were performed per system, and 500 frames were analyzed across a 0–50 nsec simulation window.


### Energy minimization
  Energy minimization was first conducted to relax the initial structures and eliminate steric clashes. The steepest descent algorithm was used with a convergence threshold of 1000 kJ mol−1 nm−1 or a maximum of 5000 steps. Nonbonded interactions were calculated using the Verlet cutoff scheme. Van der Waals interactions employed a cutoff method with a force-switching function between 1.0 and 1.2 nm, and electrostatic interactions were computed using the particle mesh Ewald (PME) method with a real-space cutoff of 1.2 nm. The neighbor list was updated every 10 steps with a cutoff radius of 1.2 nm. Hydrogen bonds were constrained using the LINCS algorithm. Positional restraints were applied to backbone and side-chain atoms with force constants of 400 and 40 kJ mol−1 nm−2, respectively.


### Equilibration
  Following energy minimization, the TNK1 and TNK1 + 14-3-3 systems underwent equilibration to stabilize temperature and volume. Equilibration used a molecular dynamics integrator over 125,000 steps with a time step of 0.001 psec, totaling 125 psec. Initial velocities were generated at 303.15 K with a random seed to ensure a Boltzmann distribution of atomic velocities. Temperature was maintained at 303.15 K using the V-rescale thermostat with a coupling time constant of 1.0 psec, applied separately to nonprotein and protein groups. Nonbonded interactions were handled with the Verlet cutoff scheme, with van der Waals interactions calculated via a force-switching function between 1.0 and 1.2 nm, and electrostatic interactions managed by the PME method with a real-space cutoff of 1 of 1.2 nm. Hydrogen bonds were constrained using the LINCS algorithm to preserve structural integrity. Positional restraints were applied with force constants of 400 kJ mol−1 nm−2 for backbone atoms and 40 kJ mol−1 nm−2 for side-chain atoms, stabilizing key components during minimization.


### Production runs
  Production simulations were performed over 50 nsec for the TNK1-only system and 5 nsec for the TNK1–14-3-3 complex using a molecular dynamics integrator with a 0.002 psec time step, totaling 250,000,000 and 25,000,000 steps, respectively. For TNK1, simulations resumed from the equilibrated state, while the TNK1–14-3-3 simulations were initiated with velocities at 303.15 K using a random seed. Temperature was maintained at 303.15 K using the V-rescale thermostat (coupling constant: 1.0 psec), and pressure was regulated at 1.0 bar using the C-rescale barostat with a 5.0 psec coupling time and compressibility of 4.5 × 10−5 bar−1, applied isotropically. Nonbonded interactions followed the Verlet cutoff scheme, with van der Waals forces switched between 1.0 and 1.2 nm and electrostatics computed using PME with a 1.2 nm real-space cutoff. The neighbor list was updated every 20 steps, and hydrogen bonds were constrained using the LINCS algorithm. Center of mass motion was removed every 100 steps using linear mode for both protein and nonprotein groups. Trajectories were saved every 50,000 steps, and energies were recorded every 1000 steps. Two replicas were simulated for each system, and 500 frames sampled over the 0–50 nsec window were used for analysis.


### Analysis of structural fluctuations
  Root mean square deviation (RMSD) was utilized to evaluate the structural deviations of TNK1 and TNK1 + 14-3-3 during simulations relative to their initial configurations. The RMSD was calculated for each timestep of the simulations using the following equation:  RMSD=1N∑i=1Nδi2,  where N represents the number of Cα atoms in the protein, and δi is the distance between the ith pair of corresponding atoms in the reference and the current frame along the MD trajectory.

  Root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) was applied to measure the flexibility and fluctuations of individual residues in TNK1 and TNK1 + 14-3-3. Unlike RMSD, RMSF focuses on per-residue dynamics, with values plotted against residue numbers. The RMSF was computed as follows:  RMSF=1N∑jNxi(j)-xi2,  where xi(j) indicates the position of the ith Cα atom in the jth frame, xi represents the average position of the ith Cα atom over the entire trajectory, and N is the total number of frames.


### Principal component analysis (PCA)
  To evaluate the dominant conformational dynamics of TNK1 and TNK1 + 14-3-3, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed. The Cα trajectories from two independent replicas per condition were aligned to a reference structure (the first frame of the simulation) to remove translational and rotational movements. A covariance matrix was constructed based on the atomic coordinates of the Cα atoms across 500 frames sampled from the 0–50 nsec simulation window. The matrix was diagonalized to obtain the principal components (PCs), where each PC represents a direction of motion, and the corresponding eigenvalues indicate the magnitude of variance along that direction. The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2), which account for the largest contributions to the overall motion, were used to project the conformational states of TNK1 and TNK1 + 14-3-3 into a reduced two-dimensional space. This projection enabled the visualization of conformational ensembles and the comparison of structural variability between TNK1 alone and TNK1 in complex with 14-3-3.
