# An ML-Based QSAR Web Server for KEAP1 Inhibitor Bioactivity
Prediction: Composite-Score-Driven Training and Advanced Validation


## Abstract
  Cardiovascular disorders and neurodegenerative diseases are among the leading causes of death worldwide, with oxidative stress being a prominent etiological factor in their development. Lowering chronic oxidative stress has been proposed as a strategy for improving or treating these conditions. The body does this naturally through the KEAP1:NRF2 pathway in healthy individuals. Inhibiting the KEAP1 regulatory protein releases the NRF2 transcription factor, leading to the biosynthesis of the antioxidant proteins. Novel molecules that activate this pathway have recently been proposed as potential mechanisms to halt diseases driven by oxidative stress, but this approach has not yet reached clinical translation. To advance this approach to drug development, there is a strong need to rapidly identify KEAP1-specific molecules. Incorporating machine-learning tools into the drug development process reduces the risk of failure. Hence, this study presents a quantitative structure–activity relationship-based machine-learning model that can predict the potential of novel KEAP1 inhibitors before synthesis and biological evaluation. To achieve this goal, molecular fingerprints of KEAP1 inhibitors retrieved from ChEMBL and BindingDB were generated by using PaDEL, Mordred, and RDKit. Subsequently, these fingerprints were screened using a novel composite-score-based feature selection method, and the resulting features were then used to train 30 models. Their performances were rigorously evaluated and ranked using the coefficient of determination (R 2), root-mean-square error (RMSE), and mean absolute error (MAE). The best model was further validated using the concordance correlation coefficient (CCC), external validation (Q F1 2, Q F2 2, and Q F3 2), k-fold testing, and y-scrambling. On completion of the study, CatBoost demonstrated the highest predictive power with strong R 2 values for the test (0.8373) and training (0.9548) sets. A significant improvement was observed in CCC, external validation, cross-validation, and the y-scrambling test. This model was finally deployed as a Web server, which is freely available to researchers.


## Introduction
  The KEAP1-NRF2 protein–protein interaction (PPI) has emerged as an important molecular target in diseases associated with oxidative stress, including cardiovascular and neurodegenerative disorders. −   Modulation of this pathway through small-molecule inhibition of KEAP1 can enhance NRF2 activity and promote cytoprotective gene expression. −        Despite extensive efforts to identify such modulators, the discovery of potent and selective KEAP1 inhibitors remains challenging due to the shallow and highly dynamic nature of the PPI interface and the structural diversity of reported ligands.

  Several NRF2 activators (Figure  , e.g., dimethyl fumarate) covalently modify KEAP1 cysteine residues through electrophilic attack, thereby disrupting NRF2 degradation. In addition to these covalent NRF2 activators, noncovalent binders such as RA839, compound 2, and ZJ01 prevent protein–protein interactions and activate NRF2 signaling in cellular and animal models. −   Other small-molecule KEAP1 inhibitors (e.g., chlorophyllin sodium salt, 6-bromonaphthalene-1,2-dione, PMI, BiKEA P1, 23, 25, and IR-61) have recently been proposed (Figure  ), but none have reached clinical trials. −

  Computational approaches, particularly quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) modeling and machine learning (ML), have become valuable tools for accelerating small-molecule discovery by enabling in silico prediction of biological activity prior to synthesis. Several studies have applied QSAR, molecular docking, or hybrid modeling strategies to KEAP1 inhibitors and related systems; however, these efforts are often limited by (i) relatively small and chemically narrow data sets, (ii) insufficient treatment of activity cliffs and structural diversity, (iii) reliance on single descriptor sets or feature selection methods, and (iv) limited model validation, particularly the absence of rigorous external validation and y-scrambling to assess robustness. In addition, reproducibility and accessibility remain ongoing challenges, as many reported models are not made available as deployable tools for the broader research community.

  Recent advances in ML algorithms and descriptor generation provide new opportunities to overcome these limitations. −    Ensemble learning methods, gradient boosting algorithms, and deep learning architectures can capture nonlinear structure–activity relationships, while large descriptor spaces derived from multiple cheminformatics toolkits can better represent diverse chemical features. However, integrating such high-dimensional data introduces challenges in feature selection and model generalization, necessitating robust strategies to reduce noise, prevent overfitting, and ensure interpretability.

  In this study, we present a QSAR-based ML framework for predicting the bioactivity of KEAP1 inhibitors that addresses several of the above limitations (Figure  ). A curated data set of KEAP1 inhibitory activity was compiled from ChEMBL and BindingDB and expanded through sequence similarity analysis to improve data coverage. Molecular descriptors and fingerprints were generated using multiple platforms (RDKit, Mordred, and PaDEL), and a composite-score-based feature selection approach was developed to integrate the strengths of multiple selection methods and prioritize robust, chemically relevant features.

  A diverse set of machine learning, boosting, and deep learning models was trained and systematically evaluated using multiple performance metrics, including R 2, RMSE, and MAE, followed by rigorous validation through concordance correlation coefficient (CCC), external validation parameters (Q F1 2, Q F2 2, Q F3 2), K-fold cross-validation, and y-scrambling. ,  The best-performing model was further optimized and deployed as a publicly accessible Web server, enabling rapid prediction of KEAP1 inhibitory activity for novel compounds.

  By combining multisource descriptor generation, composite feature selection, and comprehensive validation, this work provides a robust and reproducible ML-QSAR framework for KEAP1 inhibitor prediction. Importantly, they establish practical design and evaluation principles that can be extended to other challenging PPI targets in drug discovery.


## Experimental Methodology

### Computational Details
  Machine learning studies were conducted in Visual Studio on a workstation with a 12th-gen Intel Core i5–12450H CPU (2.0 GHz base speed, 8P + 4E cores), 24 GB of RAM, and a 4 GB Nvidia GeForce RTX 3050 GPU, running Windows 11. Feature selection was performed at the Texas Christian University’s Advanced Computing Center. All computed values were rounded to four significant figures.


### Sequence Alignment and
Comparison
  To increase the sample size of bioactivity data for KEAP1 inhibitors from ChEMBL, the human KEAP1 protein sequence was compared with sequences from other clinically approved sources of KEAP1 protein. ChEMBL IDs of proteins were selected to retrieve their UniProt IDs and obtain the corresponding FASTA files. Sequences in these FASTA files were then uploaded to the BLAST server (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi), and Q14145 (human) was used as the reference sequence. The server was used to align all the sequences and calculate the length ratio, simple identity %, length similarity %, similarity score, and similarity category. These scores were used to sort similar proteins. Additionally, to study similarity in binding-site residues, the sequences and structures of these proteins were superimposed using the “protein structure alignment”, “multiple sequence viewer/editor”, and “analyze binding sites” modules of Schrodinger 2026–1 software.

## Results and Discussion

### Sequence Analysis
  The development of a QSAR-based ML model for KEAP1:NRF2 inhibition was initiated by procuring bioactivity data from ChEMBL and the BindingDB. ,  The number of molecules tested against the human KEAP1 protein (UniProt ID: Q14145) in the ChEMBL database was limited (381), making the sample size too small for a robust analysis. The following discussion describes other models used to increase the sample size. Sequence analysis using the BLAST server confirmed that the KEAP1 proteins from rat and mouse are homologous to human KEAP1. UniProt IDs were used to retrieve FASTA sequences of rat (Rattus norvegicus, UniProt ID: P57790) and mouse (Mus musculus, UniProt ID: Q9Z2X8), which were compared with the human (Homo sapiens) KEAP1 protein. On completion of the analysis, Q9Z2X8 and P57790 showed >93.5% similarity to human-sourced (Homo sapiens) KEAP1 (Figure  a,b), indicating similarity in binding-site residues as well.

  In addition to this sequence analysis, the NRF2 domain of KEAP1 from rat and mouse species was superimposed onto the human KEAP1 structure using Schrodinger 2026–1, and RMSDs (low values indicate better fit) were calculated. UniProt IDs for these species were used to search the RCSB PDB database for PDB IDs, yielding human KEAP1 (4ZY3 ) and mouse KEAP1 (6ZEY ), but no rat KEAP1 structure. Therefore, we used a computationally generated structure from the AlphaFold database with ID AF–P57790-F1-v6, which has an average pLDDT of 90.06 (very high). On superimposing these protein structures, as shown in Figure  c, the RMSD was found to be less than 0.5 Å, indicating that the rat (Red) and mouse (Purple) KEAP1 structures are similar to human KEAP1.

  To compare binding-site residues, the human KEAP1 protein (4ZY3) was used as a reference, and the cocrystallized ligand (K67) was used to identify binding-site residues. These residues were then compared with those of the other two KEAP1 proteins, and a similarity index was calculated, as shown in Table  : Comparison of binding site residues of the KEAP1 protein sourced from human, rat, and mouse. and Figures S1 and S2. The high similarity in interacting residues and those within 5 Å of the ligand suggests conserved binding mechanisms across species, which is critical for developing accurate predictive models. Therefore, KEAP1 bioactivity results from rat and mouse models were included to increase the sample size and enhance training efficiency.


### Data
Retrieval
  After similarity analysis, the human, rat, and mouse models were selected to retrieve data from the ChEMBL database. Ten ChEMBL IDs (“CHEMBL4296095”, “CHEMBL4523596”, “CHEMBL3562164”, “CHEMBL4296107”, “CHEMBL5465249”, “CHEMBL2069156”, “CHEMBL3038498”, “CHEMBL4106129”, “CHEMBL4296122”, and “CHEMBL4296123”) were identified as having KEAP1-related bioactivities and were used to retrieve molecules with IC50 values. The subsequent ChEMBL database survey yielded 1233 molecules, while a manual search of the BindingDB database yielded 255 molecules (complete list of molecules in the Supporting Excel Sheet SE1).


### Database Screening and
Analysis
  Molecules with missing SMILES (one molecule) and missing IC50 values (69 molecules) were removed to reduce training errors. To prevent data leaks from the training set to the test set, duplicate SMILES (212) were merged by taking the mean pIC50 of duplicate pairs. This screening resulted in 1206 molecules for training. All SMILES were standardized using RDKit33, and IC50 units were converted to nM, followed by conversion of IC50 values to pIC50 values (eq  ), which ranged from 3.00 to 9.85 (Figure  ). This conversion of a wide range of bioactivity values for a larger set of molecules improved the model’s learning and training.

  Before loading the data for fingerprinting, data complexity was evaluated using structural landscape analysis via Tanimoto similarity and activity cliffs analysis (Figure   and Table S1). Tanimoto similarity (eq  ) was computed using Morgan fingerprints (ECFP4, widely used for Tanimoto analysis in QSAR), where a mean value of 0 indicates chemically different molecules and 1 indicates identical molecules. The mean Tanimoto similarity of 0.188 ± 0.045 indicates substantial structural diversity, which can challenge model generalization. To address this, structural outliers with similarity below 0.0989 were identified. A total of 47 molecules were found to be entirely different from the data set, suggesting that linear models may perform poorly and mandating the use of nonlinear models for training. This underscores the importance of nonlinear models for accurately predicting bioactivity across diverse chemical spaces and for ensuring reliable applicability domain coverage.

  Activity cliff analysis helps detect gaps in the SAR landscape and identify structurally similar compounds with widely varying bioactivity. Tanimoto similarity ≥0.70 and |ΔpIC50| ≥ 1.0 were used as criteria to detect the cliffs region, identifying 1540 cliff pairs (0.21% of all the pairs) from ∼1.2 M possible pairs, and also found 547 unique compounds (45.3%) involved in one cliff pair. These cliff pairs suggest that bioactivity may be affected by molecular stereochemistry, making prediction challenging for QSAR models and supporting the need for applicability domain assessment in predicting the bioactivity of novel compounds.

  Finally, to quantify the steepness of the activity cliff, the structure–activity landscape index (SALI) was computed using eq  . After the calculation, the median of SALI was 6.42, with extreme values (greater than 1 M), indicating the presence of identical structures with different stereochemistry, leading to different IC50 values. This indicated that stereochemistry played a critical role in determining IC50 values and suggested incorporating pharmacophoric features to improve the efficiency of the QSAR model training.


### Fingerprinting and Feature
Selection
  The significant impact of stereochemistry led us to include pharmacophoric fingerprints (Avalon fingerprints and Bemis-Murcko scaffold-derived fingerprints (part of RDKit)) alongside the PaDEL, RDKit, and Modred programs. Therefore, after data screening, the SMILES of the finalized molecules were processed, yielding 27,650 fingerprints and descriptors (see the Supporting Information for complete details). Each fingerprint used a specific set of molecular descriptors to generate binary data, mapping chemical structure features to 0 (absence) or 1 (presence).

  Importantly, the molecular descriptors may have a total value greater than 1; this variable magnitude of each descriptor leads to irregularities in the training process. The Standard_Scaler feature was used to scale the entire data set to a range between mean ± std. Later, columns with missing or infinite values exceeding 10% were removed, resulting in the removal of 179 columns. This was followed by the removal of features with zero variance; 10,588 constant columns were removed, leaving 16,883 features. ### Model Deployment and Applicability Domain
  A comparison of the results (Figure  a) indicated that tuning each model led to significant improvements in validation parameters and, consequently, the predictive performance. Among all of these, the tuned CatBoost model showed reliable performance, stable behavior, strong generalization, and high robustness. CatBoost achieved the highest R 2 test score (0.8373), the lowest overfitting score (0.1175), the lowest Test RMSE (0.5527), consistently high K-fold cross-validation score (>0.80), high CCC (0.9106), and high external validation parameter score (>0.80) and performed poorly in the y-scrambling test. These parameters confirm that the tuned CatBoost model dominates with strong pattern-learning capabilities, minimal noise memorization, and high predictive power and accuracy.

  Additionally, permutation importances for each feature were calculated for the CatBoost model (Figure  b), and among the top three, XP-6dv (Modred), Avalon 438 (RDKit), and Avalon 489 (RDKit) were the most crucial. XP-6dv captures atomic connectivity up to six bonds away and is scored on the basis of the presence of valence electrons. The more electrons there are, the higher the score, indicating a more complex or branched structure with electron-rich atoms. By contrast, the Avalon fingerprints are path-based, 1024-bit hashed fingerprints that detect the presence of specific pharmacophoric or fragment-like features, including atom types, bond types, rings, and linear and branched paths.

  To enable CatBoost to predict the IC50 (nM) of the novel molecule, the tuned model was deployed on a Streamlit web server (Figure  c). To improve prediction confidence, a function was added to perform Tanimoto analysis for each molecule (Figure  d). This function categorizes molecules into three broad classes: out-of-domain (<0.40), borderline (≥0.40), and in-domain (≥0.60). These categories add confidence to the model’s predictions. Predictions for molecules within the domain are more trustworthy, and predictions for molecules that are out-of-domain should be treated with caution.


## Conclusion
  Novel molecules that activate the NRF2 pathway are needed to treat CVDs and NDs. However, computational methods to screen large molecular libraries for specificity toward KEAP1 are a key step in drug design and are currently lacking. To address this need, a QSAR-based ML model was developed to predict the potential of novel KEAP1 inhibitors. To develop this model, known KEAP1 inhibitors were retrieved from ChEMBL (1233 molecules) and BindingDB (255 molecules). Their molecular fingerprints (27,650) were generated using PaDEL, Mordred, and RDKit. These fingerprints were screened by using a composite-score-based feature selection method, yielding an optimal set of 194 features. This feature set was used to train a total of 30 models, including classical ML (10), boosting algorithms (4), and deep learning (16). Model performance was rigorously evaluated and ranked according to R 2 > 0.75, RMSE, and MAE. The best model was further validated by CCC, external validation (Q F1 2, Q F2 2, and Q F3 2 > 0.75), K-fold test (>0.75), and y-scrambling test (<0.30). ExtraTrees, CatBoost, and TabTransformer were identified as the best models. The parameters of these three models were then tuned and validated. Among them, CatBoost demonstrated the highest predictive power, with strong R 2 values for the test (0.8373) and training (0.9548) sets and significant improvements in CCC, external validation, cross-validation, and y-scrambling test. These results confirm that the new ML-QSAR model has a high predictive power and was efficiently trained on the available data. As a result, this model is ready for implementation and identification of new molecular targets that activate the NRF2 pathway.
