# Metformin improves chronic rhinosinusitis with depressive-like behavior in mice by targeting TCF4 to inhibit the TLR4/NF-κB pathway


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51321-4.


## Introduction
  Chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) is a chronic inflammatory disease of the upper respiratory tract, with a global prevalence of 5% to 12%1. Characteristic symptoms, including hyposmia, rhinorrhea, nasal obstruction, facial pressure, and recurrent headaches, substantially impair patients’ health-related quality of life, occupational functioning, and psychological well-being, imposing a considerable socioeconomic burden2. Notably, epidemiological evidence indicates that patients with CRS have a 2–3-fold higher risk of developing major depressive disorder (MDD) than healthy individuals, and this association is independent of socioeconomic factors3. At the same time, the severity of depressive symptoms is positively correlated with sinonasal inflammatory burden4,5. This CRS–depression comorbidity exacerbates patient suffering, diminishes treatment responsiveness, and amplifies healthcare utilization and costs6. However, current management remains predominantly symptomatic: intranasal corticosteroids target local inflammation7, whereas conventional antidepressants often face limitations including delayed onset, variable efficacy, and tolerability concerns. Critically, the shared molecular pathways bridging chronic sinonasal inflammation and central neuropsychiatric sequelae remain inadequately elucidated. Consequently, mechanistic insights are urgently needed to inform integrated therapeutic strategies that address both the inflammatory and affective dimensions of this complex comorbidity.

  IIn recent years, the critical role of transcription factors in inflammation and neuropsychiatric disorders has attracted increasing attention. TCF4 (transcription factor 4), an important member of the basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) family, has been implicated in autism spectrum disorder, schizophrenia, and other neuropsychiatric disorders; however, its role in chronic inflammatory diseases and mood regulation remains poorly understood8,9. Previous studies have shown that TCF4 regulates immune cell activation and cytokine secretion by binding to promoter regions of downstream target genes10, providing a theoretical basis for further exploring its regulatory mechanisms in chronic inflammatory responses. Additionally, TCF4 is expressed in brain regions closely associated with emotion regulation and is highly expressed in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex11. Abnormal TCF4 expression may impair neural circuit function and contribute to depressive-like behaviors. Our previous bidirectional Mendelian randomization analysis further confirmed a bidirectional causal relationship between CRS and depression, suggesting that TCF4 may serve as a key mediator in the comorbidity mechanism of these disorders4. However, the specific molecular mechanisms underlying TCF4’s role in CRS–depression comorbidity—and its pharmacological targetability—remain systematically unelucidated.

  Microglia are key immune cells in the central nervous system, and a growing body of evidence suggests that they play a causal role in the onset and progression of depression12,13. Microglia regulate key pathophysiological mechanisms implicated in depression by modulating inflammatory responses, synaptic plasticity, and neural circuit formation12,14. Numerous studies have confirmed that microglia-mediated neuroinflammation is closely associated with major depressive disorder. Moreover, neuroinflammation and the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis are thought to synergistically regulate the stress response, and their dysregulation may collectively contribute to depression onset14. Existing research has shown that many clinically used antidepressants modulate microglial activation and neuroinflammatory levels15. In recent years, pharmacological strategies targeting microglial polarization have emerged as promising interventions for preventing mental disorder recurrence.

  Metformin, a classic hypoglycemic drug, has been found to exhibit significant anti-inflammatory and antidepressant effects in recent years16–18. In inflammatory animal models, metformin has been shown to inhibit inflammatory responses by downregulating the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby reducing neuroinflammation in lung tissue and the hypothalamus19. Other studies have shown that, in a mouse model of allergic rhinitis (AR)–induced depressive-like behavior, metformin alleviates anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors by modulating the TRPV1/NLRP3 or NLRP3/IL-1β signaling pathways20,21. Additionally, this drug suppresses microglial activation by reducing the number of Iba1-positive microglia in the hypothalamus and hippocampal regions22. These findings suggest its potential therapeutic value in nasal inflammation–associated depression. Nevertheless, the specific target genes through which metformin ameliorates local inflammation and central depressive-like behaviors in CRS remain to be systematically investigated.

  Based on the above background, this study aims to investigate whether metformin alleviates CRS-induced depressive-like behaviors by targeting TCF4 to inhibit the TLR4/NF-κB pathway. We hypothesize that microglia-mediated neuroinflammation associated with CRS promotes depressive phenotypes by activating the TCF4/TLR4/NF-κB axis, and that metformin exerts therapeutic effects by regulating TCF4-dependent mechanisms to suppress this pro-inflammatory cascade. This study may provide a novel therapeutic strategy for depression comorbid with CRS.


## Materials and methods

### Animals
  All laboratory mice employed in this study were maintained within a pathogen-free facility at the Animal Research Center of Guizhou Medical University. The animal studies were conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the NIH (NIH publication No. 85 − 23, revised 1996), and were sanctioned by the Ethics Committee of Guizhou Medical University (Ethics Approval No: 2402110), The study was conducted in compliance with National Institutes of Health guidelines and met the Animal Research: Reporting In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines. A total of 54 SPF-grade C57BL/6 mice, weighing between 20 and 30 g, were purchased from Beijing Sibeifu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Certificate No. SCXK (Gui) 2023-0002). The mice were housed in an SPF-grade animal facility at a temperature of 20–26 °C and a humidity of 40% – 70%, with a light cycle from 06:00 to 18:00. They were provided with regular feed and had free access to water.


### Chronic rhinosinusitis with depressive-like behavior model
  The CRS mouse model was based on a previously developed mouse model22. Mice were assigned to four groups (n = 6 per group): Control (Ctrl), CRS-3 month, CRS-4 month, and CRS-5 month. Briefly, mice in the experimental groups were sensitized via intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of ovalbumin (25 mg, 9006-59-1, OVA, MCE, USA) dissolved in 200 µL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing aluminum hydroxide (2 mg) on days 0 and 5. Subsequently, these mice received intranasal (i.n.) challenges with 20 µL of a 3% OVA solution daily for 13 consecutive weeks. During the final 8 weeks of the protocol, mice in the CRS-4 m and CRS-5 m groups (at weeks 12 and 16, respectively) were additionally administered staphylococcal enterotoxin B (210229-94-0, SEB; MCE, USA) together with OVA via intranasal instillation. The Ctrl group received PBS only throughout the study period to serve as a baseline control.


### Stereotactic viral injection and treatments
  CRS-3 m mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital (60 mg/kg) and then fixed on a stereotactic apparatus (SA-100, Yuyan Science, China). The operation was performed according to a previous study23. Briefly, the control group received lentivirus expressing a TCF4 empty vector (sh-NC), or TCF4 knockdown lentivirus (sh-TCF4), injected into the hippocampal region according to the following coordinates: X: 1.0–1.5 mm, Y: −2.0 to − 2.2 mm, Z: −1.8 to − 2.0 mm. A volume of 2 µL of TCF4 virus solution (titer: 1 × 10⁹ TU/mL) was injected into each side of the hippocampus at a rate of 20 nL/min. After injection, the needle was left in place for 10 min to promote viral diffusion and prevent backflow. Finally, the scalp was sutured, and the mice were placed on a 37 °C temperature-controlled pad for recovery. Mice then received intranasal administration of 6% OVA plus SEB for 2 months.

  Before each intranasal administration of 20 µL PBS buffer containing 6% OVA and 10 ng SEB, mice in the CRSDB + metformin group were intraperitoneally injected with metformin solution (ACS3001, Merck–LivingInnovation, China) 30 min earlier; mice in the CRSDB + normal saline group were intraperitoneally injected with an equivalent volume of normal saline 30 min before the same intranasal treatment. Twenty-four hours after the last intranasal administration, the mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and tissue samples were collected simultaneously.


### Behavioral measurements
  The Tail Suspension Test (TST) was carried out as described previously24. Mice were first acclimated to a 1% (w/v) sucrose solution over three consecutive days, with a single bottle of the solution provided daily. Baseline sucrose preference was then established during a 24-hour two-bottle choice test, in which animals had simultaneous access to tap water and the 1% sucrose solution. The formal sucrose preference test was conducted under identical conditions: both fluids were presented in visually identical bottles, and bottle positions were alternated every 6 h to eliminate side bias. Fluid consumption was measured gravimetrically at the start and end of each interval. Sucrose preference was calculated as: [(volume of sucrose solution consumed)/(total fluid intake)] × 100%.

  Morris Water Maze (MWM) experiment: The MWM test was conducted after drug administration in the 3rd, 4th, and 5th months. The training phase of the hidden platform test lasted for five days. In the probe trial, the mice were placed in the water from randomized entry points after the platform was removed. Within 60 s, the time spent in the opposite quadrant, the time spent in the target quadrant, and the number of crossings over the former platform location were recorded.


### Primary microglia culture and treatments
  As previously described, primary microglia were isolated from the hippocampus of control and CRSDB mice. Microglia were isolated from the hippocampus of control mice for the Ctrl group and from CRS-exposed mice for the experimental groups. Magnetic bead–based isolation preserves the in vivo phenotype and functional integrity of microglia, thereby enabling an accurate representation of their involvement in neuroinflammatory processes. The tissues were digested with 0.25% trypsin at 37 °C for 8 min. Homogenates were subjected to a Percoll™ (Thermo Fisher, Massachusetts, USA) gradient to remove myelin and enrich viable glial cells, followed by Percoll gradient separation. Cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 basal medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 0.5 mM glutamine, 25 µM glutamate, and 50 µg/mL penicillin–streptomycin. On the third day after seeding, half the volume of the medium was replaced with fresh, prewarmed complete medium. Cells were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO₂ for 6 days before subsequent experiments.

  In brief, primary microglia were infected with TCF4 knockdown or overexpressing lentivirus (sh-TCF4 or oe-TCF4) or control lentivirus (sh-NC or oe-NC; titer: 1 × 10⁸ TU/mL) and cultured for 3 days. Microglia transduced with lentivirus—or untransduced controls—were treated with metformin at concentrations of 0.02, 0.2, or 2 mmol/L for 24 h prior to subsequent experiments.


### Histopathological analysis
  Tissue samples from mice, including brain and nasal tissues, were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h. Brain tissues were processed through a graded ethanol series (75%, 80%, 95%, 100%) for dehydration, cleared in xylene, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. The sections were dewaxed, rehydrated, and stained with Nissl and hematoxylin-eosin (HE) for structural and cellular assessment. Following staining, the sections were dehydrated, cleared with xylene, and examined under an optical microscope. Nasal tissues, after fixation, underwent decalcification in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) solution for one week, followed by paraffin embedding and sectioning at a thickness of 4 μm. Sections were then stained with HE and periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) to evaluate tissue morphology and mucus-producing cells. Eosinophil and goblet cell numbers were quantified in three randomly selected high-power fields (×400) per sample using light microscopy.


### Western blot analysis
  Protein extracts from mouse hippocampi or cultured microglia were prepared using RIPA lysis buffer (Solarbio) with protease inhibitors. After centrifugation (12,000×g, 15 min, 4 °C), supernatant protein concentrations were quantified by BCA assay (Solarbio). Proteins were resolved on 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Merck Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk before overnight incubation (4 °C) with primary antibodies: TCF4 (22337-1-AP, AB_2879076, 1:5,000; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), TLR4 (19811-1-AP, AB_10638446, 1:5,000; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), p-NF-κB p65 (AF5006, AB_2834847, 1:5,000; Proteintech, Affinity, jiangsu, China), and β-actin (ab6276, AB_2223210,1:10,000; Abcam, Cambridge, UK). HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (111-005-045, AB_2337916, 1:20,000; Jackson ImmunoResearch) was applied before ECL detection (Pizyme Biotech). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH).


### Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis
  Total RNA was extracted from mouse hippocampi or cultured microglia using TRIzol reagent, following the manufacturer’s instructions. The isolated RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using a cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) on an ABI StepOnePlus qPCR system. The relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2^(−ΔΔCt) method and normalized to GAPDH expression. Primer sequences used in the qPCR reactions are listed in Table S1.


### Immunofluorescence analysis
  Hippocampal tissues were blocked with 5% goat serum for 2 h at room temperature and subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Iba1 (ab178846, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), TCF4 (22337-1-AP, 1:200; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), TLR4 (19811-1-AP, 1:200; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), and p-NF-κB p65 (AF5006, 1:200; Affinity, jiangsu, China). Following three washes in PBS, the sections were incubated with the corresponding fluorescent secondary antibody(ActivAb TMGoat Anti-Rabbit IgG/FITC, SF134, Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 1 h at 37 °C in the dark. Nuclear staining was performed using DAPI for 10 min at room temperature. Fluorescent images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope and analyzed with ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD).


### ELISA analysis
  According to the manufacturer’s instructions, the concentrations of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in mouse serum and cell culture supernatants were determined using commercial ELISA kits (Abcam, Cambridge, UK).


### Statistics
  All data are presented as means ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Comparisons between the means of two independent groups were conducted using two-tailed unpaired t-tests. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc test was performed. Statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS 27.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.


## Results

### Clarify the reproducibility and stability of the CRSDB mouse model
  Progressive exacerbation of depression-like behaviors was observed in CRS mice. In the TST, immobility duration was significantly prolonged in 4- and 5-month CRS mice compared to control animals (Fig. 1A). Notably, immobility time in 5-month CRS mice was further increased compared to 3-month CRS mice, indicating a time-dependent worsening of depressive-like symptoms. Concurrently, the SPT revealed a progressive development of anhedonia, with significantly lower sucrose preference observed in 5-month CRS mice compared to their 3-month counterparts (Fig. 1B).

  Cognitive impairment was demonstrated through performance in the MWM. 5-month CRS mice exhibited impaired spatial memory, as evidenced by reduced platform crossings (Fig. 1C), prolonged escape latency during training sessions (Fig. 1E), decreased time spent in the target quadrant, and increased time spent in the opposite quadrant (Fig. 1F-G). Furthermore, the search patterns of CRS mice became increasingly disorganized over time (Fig. 1D).

  Histopathological analysis confirmed a time-dependent progression of mucosal inflammation in CRS mice, characterized by goblet cell hyperplasia (PAS staining, Fig. 1H-I) and inflammatory cell infiltration (HE staining; Fig. 1J).

  This is consistent with the results of the CRSDB model we developed earlier, verifying that the model has good repeatability and stability and can be applied to related research on this comorbidity for a long time.


### The degree of neuroinflammation and neuronal injury induced by CRS increases in a time-dependent manner
  Neuronal damage, microglial activation, and neuroinflammation are key factors in the pathogenesis of depression. H&E staining results showed that, compared with the CRS-3-month and CRS-4-month groups, the CRS-5-month group of mice exhibited obvious neuronal shrinkage and astrocyte activation in the hippocampus (Fig. 1K). Nissl staining revealed that the number of Nissl bodies in the CRS-3-month group was significantly reduced, and hippocampal neuronal staining was lighter; the CRS-5-month group showed a further significant reduction and lighter staining compared with the two earlier groups (Fig. 2A). Notably, CRS induced significant microglial activation, as indicated by a marked increase in Iba1⁺ cell density (Fig. 2B–C), accompanied by upregulation of TCF4, NF-κB p65, and TLR4 at both protein and mRNA levels in hippocampal tissue (Fig. 2G–M). Further studies revealed that peripheral inflammation paralleled central pathological changes, as evidenced by elevated serum levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α (Fig. 2D–F).

  In summary, among all observed indicators, the severity of neuroinflammation and neuronal damage showed a time-dependent worsening trend. Moreover, the CRS-5-month group exhibited more pronounced pathological changes than those in the CRS-3-month and CRS-4-month groups.


### TCF4 deficiency mitigates depression-like behaviors, and neuroinflammation in CRS mice
  To investigate the functional role of TCF4 in CRSDB, we employed stereotaxic delivery of a lentiviral vector encoding a sh-TCF4 into the hippocampus of mice subjected to the CRSDB. This intervention effectively suppressed CRSDB-induced upregulation of hippocampal TCF4 expression (Fig. 3A-C). Behavioral assessments revealed that sh-TCF4-mediated TCF4 knockdown significantly ameliorated multiple depression-related deficits. In the TST, CRSDB mice exhibited prolonged immobility duration—a hallmark indicator of behavioral despair—which was markedly attenuated following TCF4 silencing (Fig. 3A). Similarly, anhedonia, quantified by reduced sucrose preference in the sucrose preference test (SPT), was substantially reversed in shTCF4-treated mice (Fig. 3B).

  Cognitive dysfunction associated with chronic stress was also mitigated by hippocampal TCF4 deficiency. In the Morris water maze, shTCF4 mice demonstrated enhanced spatial learning and memory retention, evidenced by significantly shortened escape latency during acquisition trials (Fig. 3F), increased platform-crossing frequency (Fig. 3C), prolonged dwell time in the target quadrant (Fig. 3E), and reduced time spent in the opposite quadrant (Fig. 3D). Trajectory analyses further corroborated these improvements, reflecting more efficient search strategies and robust spatial memory consolidation (Fig. 3G). Collectively, these findings indicate that hippocampal TCF4 is a critical mediator of CRSDB-induced depressive-like behaviors and cognitive impairments, and its targeted suppression confers resilience against stress-evoked neuropsychiatric deficits.

  Nasal and hippocampal pathological changes were also attenuated by TCF4 knockdown. Histological analysis using PAS staining revealed reduced goblet cell hyperplasia and mucosal thickening in TCF4-deficient CRS mice (Fig. 3H). H&E staining demonstrated decreased inflammatory cell infiltration in both nasal tissues (Fig. 3I) and the hippocampus (Fig. 3J). Nissl staining indicated preserved neuronal morphology in TCF4-deficient CRS mice (Fig. 3K). Notably, microglial activation, assessed by Iba1 fluorescence intensity, was significantly reduced following TCF4 knockdown (Fig. 3L). Additionally, serum levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, were markedly decreased in TCF4-silenced CRS mice (Fig. 3M).

  Collectively, these findings establish TCF4 as a key mediator linking CRS-induced neuroinflammation with depression-like pathology, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target for mood disorders associated with chronic inflammatory conditions.


### TCF4 inhibits microglial activation via a TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway
  To clarify the molecular mechanism by which TCF4 regulates microglial activation in CRSDB, we systematically evaluated the potential association between TCF4 and the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway in both in vivo and in vitro experiments. The results show that, in the CRSDB model, sh-TCF4 intervention significantly reduced the mRNA and protein expression levels of TCF4, TLR4, and p-NF-κB p65 in the hippocampus of mice (Fig. 4A-B), and immunofluorescence verified the weakened fluorescence intensity (Fig. 4C-E). Primary microglial cell experiments confirmed that TCF4 knockdown could simultaneously down-regulate the activity of the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway (Fig. 4G-I, K) and inhibit the secretion of pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α (Fig. 4J). This mechanism reveals that TCF4 slows down the process of neuroinflammation by inhibiting TLR4/NF-κB signal transduction.

### Metformin improves depression-like behaviors, neuron damage, and neuroinflammation in CRS mice
  To investigate the regulatory effects of metformin on depression-like behaviors and neuroinflammation in CRS mouse models, we performed a comprehensive assessment—including behavioral testing, histopathological analysis, and quantification of cellular inflammatory factors—in CRSDB mice following metformin administration. The results showed that metformin intervention significantly reduced the immobility time in the tail suspension test (Fig. 5A), reversed the decreased sucrose preference (Fig. 5B), and effectively improved spatial learning and memory functions: the escape latency was shortened in the Morris water maze training (Fig. 5G), the number of crossings in the platform area increased (Fig. 5C), and the time spent in the target quadrant was prolonged while the time spent in the opposite quadrant was shortened (Fig. 5D, F). Trajectory analysis further confirmed that metformin could optimize the spatial exploration strategy of CRS mice (Fig. 5E). HE staining revealed that metformin could significantly reduce the infiltration of inflammatory cells in the nasal mucosa, and PAS staining showed a reduction in goblet cells (Fig. 5H, J). Meanwhile, HE staining indicated that metformin could activate astrocytes in the hippocampus (Fig. 5I–K), and Nissl staining showed nuclear pyknosis in neurons in the metformin group. The detection of serum inflammatory factors further demonstrated that the levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α were significantly decreased in the metformin group (Fig. 5L), suggesting that it exerts therapeutic effects on the comorbid state through multiple mechanisms, including inhibiting local nasal inflammation, protecting hippocampal neurons, and regulating systemic immune responses.

### Metformin reverses the down-regulation of TCF4 and the inactivation of the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway
  To further clarify the mechanism of metformin in CRSDB mice, we examined the expression changes of related molecules in the hippocampus. Compared with the control group, Western blot, RT-qPCR, and immunofluorescence analyses all showed that metformin intervention significantly downregulated the expression level of TCF4 in the hippocampus (Fig. 6A–C). In addition, metformin treatment also significantly reduced the mRNA expression of TLR4 and NF-κB p65 (Fig. 6D). The Western blot results further indicated that the expression levels of TLR4 protein and phosphorylated NF-κB p65 were significantly decreased (Fig. 6F), and the immunofluorescence staining results were consistent with this, suggesting that metformin can inhibit the activation of the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway(Fig. 6F-I, L). In addition, Microglial activation, indicated by IBA-1 + staining, was significantly attenuated in CRSDB mice following metformin treatment(Fig. 6J-K).

### Metformin targets TCF4 to inhibit the inactivation of the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby attenuating microglia activation
  Molecular docking analysis indicated that metformin had a potential interaction with the TCF4 protein (Fig. 7A). In vitro experiments further verified the targeting relationship between metformin and TCF4. The CCK-8 assay results showed that metformin exerted no significant influence on the viability of primary microglia within the tested concentration range (Fig. 7B). In addition, metformin dose-dependently suppressed TCF4 mRNA and protein expression in primary microglial cells within the concentration range of 0.02–2 mmol/L (Fig. 7C–D). RT-qPCR and Western blot analyses revealed that, compared with the model group, metformin treatment significantly downregulated the mRNA and protein expression levels of TLR4 and NF-κB p65; however, TCF4 overexpression reversed this inhibitory effect (Fig. 7E–F). Additionally, metformin significantly reduced the secretion levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in the microglial cell culture supernatant, and this effect was partially restored under TCF4 overexpression conditions, indicating that TCF4 plays a critical role in mediating metformin’s anti-inflammatory effects (Fig. 7G).

## Discussion
  CRS is a common chronic inflammatory disease, mainly characterized by persistent nasal congestion, facial pain, and olfactory dysfunction, which seriously affects patients’ quality of life25. Epidemiological studies have shown that about 40% of CRS patients also suffer from MDD, highlighting the urgency of in-depth research on this comorbidity phenomenon in clinical practice26–29.

  TCF4 is a key transcriptional regulator in brain development and function, highly expressed in brain regions closely related to emotion regulation such as the hippocampus and Cortical, and plays an important regulatory role in physiological processes such as neural plasticity, learning and memory30–32. Previous genetic and transcriptomic studies have demonstrated that TCF4 plays a crucial role in the plasticity of the central nervous system, synaptic homeostasis, and glial function, and its genetic variations are closely related to the risk of various mental disorders8,33. However, the expression localization and function of TCF4 in the pathophysiological process of inflammation-induced depressive-like symptoms remain unclear. The results of this study show that the expression of TCF4 in the hippocampus of CRSDB mice was significantly upregulated and closely related to microglial activation and the activation of the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway. Notably, TCF4 knockdown could reverse the neuropathological changes and significantly improve nasal inflammation, indicating that TCF4 plays a key regulatory role in the depression-related nasal-brain inflammation axis.

  In the context of CRS and MDD, the “nose–brain inflammation axis” has emerged as a key framework for understanding how peripheral nasal inflammation influences the central nervous system through neuroimmune pathways34,35. The anatomical and pathophysiological basis of this axis lies in the existence of multiple direct and indirect connections between the nasal cavity and the central nervous system, including the neural projections of the olfactory and trigeminal nerves, as well as circumventricular organs where the blood–brain barrier is relatively weak34. Under sustained local inflammation in CRS, these pathways can be activated, leading to the recruitment and activation of central resident immune cells, particularly microglia and astrocytes36. Neuroimaging and animal model studies suggest that key emotion-regulating brain regions, such as the hippocampus and anterior cingulate cortex, are particularly susceptible36. In this study, an increase in nasal mucosal goblet cell count was found to be positively correlated with the severity of depressive-like symptoms, suggesting that local nasal inflammation not only serves as a peripheral concomitant phenomenon but may also act as a functional component of the nose–brain inflammation axis—contributing to the pathogenesis of mood disorders by activating central neuroimmune responses and affecting the structure and function of olfactory pathway-related brain regions.

  The onset of depression is frequently associated with dysregulated inflammatory responses, and certain inflammatory markers may serve as indicators of the severity of depressive symptoms37,38. Neuroinflammation plays a key role in the pathogenesis of depression, and the dynamic spectrum of microglial activation states—ranging from pro-inflammatory to immunoregulatory phenotypes— is closely related to the degree of neuroinflammation39. Pro-inflammatory microglial states mainly releases pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-1β, TNF-α and IL-6, thereby exacerbating neural damage and promoting the development of depressive symptoms. Studies have shown that the levels of inflammatory factors in the serum and brain of patients with depression are significantly elevated. IL-6 and IL-1β, as common pro-inflammatory factors, can induce systemic inflammatory responses; while immunoregulatory microglial states secrete anti-inflammatory factors and neurotrophic factors such as Arg-1, IL-10, CD206 and BDNF, which are conducive to the repair and functional recovery of neurons40–43. Consistent with previous studies, the number of microglia in the hippocampus of CRSDB mice increased, and the expression of pro-inflammatory factors IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α was significantly upregulated, accompanied by neuronal cell damage. Additionally, in primary microglia derived from CRSDB, pro-inflammatory factors IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α were also significantly upregulated, but this change could be effectively reversed by knocking down TCF4. These results suggest that TCF4 modulates the functional states of microglia, thereby alleviating the neuroinflammation.

  The activation of TLR4 in microglia is not only closely related to the occurrence of neuroinflammatory diseases, but also can trigger the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby causing further inflammatory damage44–46. After TLR4 is activated, the NF-κB p65-IκB-α complex dissociates, and IκB-α is phosphorylated and released from the complex. Subsequently, NF-κB p65 is also phosphorylated, promoting the translocation of activated NF-κB into the nucleus, thereby facilitating the expression and release of pro-inflammatory factors47,48. Previous studies have demonstrated that stress provoked enhancement of TLR4/NF-кB pathway, suggesting that TLR4/NF-кB pathway has a significant impact in the neuroinflammation of depression49,50. Studies have shown that regulating the TLR4/NF-κB/BDNF signaling pathway can effectively modulate neuroinflammation and oxidative stress responses, thereby alleviating depression-like behaviors induced by CUMS in mice51. Earlier research have demonstrated that obstructing the TLR4/MyD88 signaling pathway can ameliorate depression in mice52. Another study found that the downregulation of TLR4 expression could produce antidepressant-like effects53. This study found that the activity of the TLR4/NF-κB pathway was significantly enhanced in the CRSDB mouse model, and the down-regulation of TCF4 could inhibit the activation of this signaling axis. In vitro experiments confirmed that TCF4 gene knockout could significantly reduce the expression of TLR4/NF-κB p65 and the secretion of inflammatory factors by inhibiting microglial activation, accompanied by a decrease in the phosphorylation level of NF-κB p65. This regulatory mechanism provides a molecular basis for TCF4-mediated depressive-like behavior by limiting the process of neuroinflammation.

  Metformin, a first-line anti-diabetic drug, has recently gained attention for its neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory properties in mood disorders54. Studies have shown that metformin can down-regulate NLRP3 expression, reduce the number of Iba-1 positive microglia in the hypothalamus, and improve depressive-like behaviors by inhibiting TRPV1 expression and blocking NLRP3 inflammasome activation20–22. The results of this study are consistent with these findings. Metformin can effectively alleviate the symptoms of CRSDB by reducing depressive-like behaviors, improving cognitive function, and reducing inflammation in nasal mucosa tissue and the hippocampus. Notably, metformin can down-regulate the expression of TCF4 and inhibit the activation of the TLR4/NF-κB pathway, providing the first pharmacological evidence of metformin’s targeted regulation of TCF4 and offering a key theoretical basis for its development as a precision antidepressant.

  To verify whether metformin inhibits microglial activation by targeting TCF4, we conducted a rescue experiment. According to literature search, there are no relevant studies reporting this mechanism so far. The results showed that after transfection of oe-TCF4 in primary microglial cells, the sensitivity of cells to metformin was significantly reduced, and the level of inflammatory response was restored accordingly, suggesting that overexpression of TCF4 can antagonize the anti-inflammatory effect of metformin.

  This study has some limitations.First, the precise molecular mechanism underlying the metformin–TCF4 interaction warrants further structural and biochemical validation. Second, although our study focused on microglia, TCF4 is potentially expressed in other neural cell types such as astrocytes and neurons, indicating that cell-type-specific functional roles of TCF4 remain to be elucidated. Additionally, although a decrease in microglial activation levels was observed, the expression changes of microglial-specific markers such as CD68 and Iba1 were not detected, and the molecular evidence chain for the related neuroinflammatory mechanism still needs to be improved. These research gaps provide important directions for our subsequent multi-omics integration analysis, and exploration of the cross-talk mechanism between the nervous and immune systems.


## Conclusion
  In summary, our study demonstrates the stability and reproducibility of the CRSDB model. We elucidate a previously unrecognized proinflammatory signaling pathway in which TCF4 activates neuroinflammation via the TLR4/NF-κB axis. Furthermore, we advance the understanding of metformin’s neuroprotective effects by identifying TCF4 as a key molecular target through which it suppresses microglial activation and alleviates depressive-like pathology. This work not only deepens insight into the pathophysiological mechanisms of depression but also provides a foundation for developing targeted anti-inflammatory therapeutic strategies.
