# Interpretable biophysical neural networks of transcriptional activation domains separate roles of protein abundance and coactivator binding


## Abstract
  Deep neural networks have improved many difficult prediction tasks in biology, but it remains challenging to interpret these networks and learn molecular mechanisms. Here, we address interpretation challenges by building biophysical neural networks for predicting activation domains, the regions within transcription factors that recruit coactivators to drive gene expression. Deep neural networks can now accurately predict acidic activation domains from protein sequence, but these predictors are difficult to interpret. We designed shallow neural networks that incorporated biophysical models and visualized the parameters directly. We found two ways that the arrangement of residues (i.e. sequence grammar) controls function: 1) C-terminal hydrophobic residues increase activation domain strength and decrease protein abundance, and 2) acidic residues at the N-terminus promote coactivator binding, while acidic residues at the C-terminus promote TF abundance. We demonstrate how combining biophysical and deep neural networks maximizes prediction accuracy and interpretability to reveal biological mechanisms across datasets.

## Introduction
  Deep neural networks (NNs) have transformed molecular biology, with applications varying from protein structure prediction1-3 to single-cell transcriptomics4,5. Deep NNs learn complex patterns using millions of parameters, but this complexity also makes it difficult to interpret how and why they make specific predictions6,7. The lack of interpretability surrounding these NNs is of particular concern in biological research, where one of the fundamental goals is not just to make accurate predictions, but also to improve mechanistic understanding of the underlying processes5.

  The field of machine learning interpretability has emerged to shed light on what these deep NNs are learning6-8. There are two common approaches for interpreting biological NNs: either applying post-hoc interpretability methods8,9 or building biological knowledge into the NN architecture10-15. In this work, we demonstrate that both shallow NNs and NNs that incorporate biological models enhance interpretability and further our understanding of the underlying biology.

  We focused on the biological challenge of predicting transcriptional activation domains from protein sequence. Activation domains are located within intrinsically disordered regions of transcription factors (TFs) and interact with coactivator proteins to turn on transcription of a gene16,17. Traditionally, the problem of identifying activation domains from sequence has been difficult because they are disordered, diverse, and poorly conserved18,19. High-throughput screens have uncovered many sequence features of acidic activation domains, including a balance between acidic and aromatic amino acids16,20-30. The large amount of data generated from these screens has also been used to train deep NNs that accurately predict activation domains from protein sequence23,27,30-33.

  We sought to interpret NNs to learn the sequence grammar controlling activation domain function. Sequence grammar is the specific arrangements of amino acids that control function34,35. We do not yet fully understand the grammar of activation domains16. Although amino acid composition is important for function, grammar is also important in many intrinsically disordered regions35,36 and activation domains16,25,27,37. Sequences containing acidic and aromatic amino acids are not always activation domains25,27,28, and predicting activation domains using amino acid composition alone does not achieve the same accuracy as NNs37. The high performance of NNs compared to composition-based predictors suggests that they are learning some grammar. While some NNs for activation domain prediction have been interpreted using post-hoc methods, these interpretations had limited utility and did not reveal any strong grammar signals23,30. We hypothesized that building biological knowledge into NNs would improve interpretability and reveal the grammar of activation domains.

  In this work, we used interpretable NNs to test our understanding of the features that control activation domain function. Our goal was not to outperform the existing predictors, but merely to increase interpretability while achieving similar accuracy. We tried two approaches: 1) designing the shallowest possible NN that maintained prediction accuracy, and 2) incorporating biophysical models into shallow NNs. The shallow NNs have minimal parameters that can be directly visualized. Visualizing parameters allowed direct interpretation, which confirmed known features of activation domains, such as the importance of acidic and aromatic amino acids. The biophysical NNs additionally revealed two grammar signals in activation domains. Acidic amino acids at the N-terminus promote coactivator binding, while acidic amino acids at the C-terminus promote TF abundance. C-terminal hydrophobic amino acids both decrease TF abundance and increase coactivator binding. These BiophysicalNNs also helped us to make sense of the post-hoc interpretations of deeper, more accurate NNs. We apply these BiophysicalNNs to other datasets. Our results highlight the utility of shallow, interpretable NNs for learning molecular mechanisms that govern protein function.


## Results

### The data and neural network architecture
  We trained our NN using data from a published screen measuring two dimensions of activation domain function: TF abundance and activation domain strength19. We define strength as the amount of GFP reporter gene expression in our screen. In this screen, S. cerevisiae cells were transformed with synthetic TFs that contained an mCherry to quantify TF abundance, a DNA-binding domain targeted to a synthetic reporter locus coding for GFP, an estradiol-binding domain for inducible activation, and a variable, C-terminal 40-amino acid sequence that may function as an activation domain (Figure 1A). Two Sort-seq25,38 experiments were performed to measure the amount of mCherry and GFP expression for each variable 40-amino acid sequence (Methods). We measured TF abundance by sorting on mCherry. We measured activation domain strength by sorting on GFP. Our NNs take as input the variable 40-amino acid sequence and predict either 1) activation domain strength (GFP), 2) TF abundance (mCherry), or 3) both of these quantities.

  For all the NN variations, we used the same basic architecture building block (Figure 1B). This block takes as input the one-hot encoded activation domain sequence and applies a convolutional filter, followed by a Leaky ReLU activation function, a fully connected (linear) layer with a single output, and a final Leaky ReLU activation function. This architecture is used in all of our NNs to predict either the total TF abundance (mCherry fluorescence), the activation domain strength (GFP fluorescence), or an intermediate biophysical value (the equilibrium constant between states). We used a single convolutional filter because a single fully connected layer could not as accurately predict the phenotype, and multiple convolutional filters rapidly became uninterpretable. Additionally, in NNs of DNA sequence, convolutional filters excel at detecting TF binding motifs13,15,39. We reasoned that if protein motifs play a large role in this dataset, we would detect them in the convolutional filter. We systematically varied the size of the convolutional filter and the random seed (Methods). The random seed influences the random initialization of NN weights and the order of the training data. The random seed of the NN had a larger impact on performance than the convolutional filter size, and most convolutional filter sizes fell within the noise caused by random seed (Figure S1).


### The shallow neural network for predicting activation domain strength
  First, we trained a NN to predict activation domain strength (the GFP expression) using a single building block (Figure 1B). We started with this shallow NN (ShallowNN) as a baseline so that we could evaluate whether the NNs incorporating biophysical models provided any additional benefit. Although our main goal was to build an interpretable NN, we benchmarked performance of the ShallowNN against existing predictors. We evaluated our best performing ShallowNN (as defined by performance on the validation set) on an unseen test dataset. Our ShallowNN is a slight improvement over linear regression on the one-hot encoding (Pearson correlation of 0.837 for the NN vs 0.801 for regression, Figure S2), likely because of the non-linearities captured by NNs. We also compared the ShallowNN with three published deep NNs, TADA30,31, PADDLE27, and ADPred23, and one residual NN we trained on the same dataset: ADhunter-GFP (Methods). ADhunter-GFP, the ShallowNN, and PADDLE were trained as regressors, while TADA and ADPred were trained as classifiers. We compared the ability of our NN to make quantitative predictions (ADhunter-GFP, PADDLE) and to classify sequences as activation domains (ADhunter-GFP, TADA, PADDLE, and ADPred). The best performing ShallowNN performs slightly worse than ADhunter-GFP and PADDLE on both regression and classification on the test set (Figure 1C, D) while using 700x and ~4500x fewer parameters, respectively (138 for the ShallowNN vs. 96,769 ADhunter and eight ensembled NNs of 80,005 each for PADDLE). The ShallowNN also performs comparably to the other predictors on the classification test (Figure 1C). The ShallowNN generalizes to new datasets29,30 with slightly worse performance (Table S1).

  Next, to determine what features the ShallowNN is learning, we analyzed its parameters (Figure 1E, F). By learning, we mean the aspects of the data that the final trained model deems predictive. The simplicity of the network–it contains only two layers–allows direct visualization of the parameters. For a detailed description of how these parameters operate in the context of the NN, see Methods. Briefly, the convolutional filter identifies important amino acid features in the input sequence. The parameters of the linear layer weigh these features based on where they appear in the input 40 amino acid (aa) sequence. As the convolutional filter size increases, the convolutional filter also begins to pick up positional effects. At the largest convolutional filter size (40aa), the convolutional layer is mathematically equivalent to a linear layer with a single output node.

  Visualizing the convolutional filter weights and linear layer weights for two high-performing NNs with different convolutional filter sizes (Figure 1E and 1F) reveals the relative importance of composition and position on activation strength. These matrices show the effect of each residue at each position in the filter, similar to the matrices from deep mutational scans, which measure the effects on function of mutating each position in a protein to all other amino acids. In deep mutational scans of structured proteins or intrinsically disordered proteins that undergo folding upon binding to another protein, the matrices have vertical streaks at key positions where any substitution disrupts function40-42. The vertical streaks indicate positions that are critical for folding and/or for function. In contrast, in deep mutational scans of other intrinsically disordered proteins, the matrices have horizontal streaks because there are no critical positions, and adding an amino acid has similar effects across many positions in the protein sequence41,43. In our ShallowNN, the matrices have horizontal streaks, indicating that composition is more important than individual positions in these intrinsically disordered activation domains.

  When looking at the convolutional filter parameters, we recover known features of acidic activation domains (Figure 1E, 1F). In both NN’s convolutional filters, basic amino acids lysine and arginine decrease predicted strength, as previously described24,29,32. This pattern can be seen visually in the filters (rows for R and K in Figure 1E, 1F) and is statistically significant via a permutation test44 (adjusted p-values < 0.05, Figure S3, Methods). Additionally, both filters have positive weights for phenylalanine and tryptophan, methionine, leucine, and aspartic acid, increasing the predicted activation domain strength (Figure 1E, 1F, S3, adjusted p-values < 0.002, Permutation test). These signals align with previous observations that acidic, aromatic, and specific hydrophobic amino acids are essential for activation domain function16,20-29.

  Because relying on a single NN can lead to biased interpretations12, we investigated whether these patterns were consistent across hundreds of NNs with various random seeds, filter sizes, and bootstrapped training data. We used NNs trained using 30 random seeds and 200 resamples of the data (bootstraps) to estimate the variability due to the training process and due to the data itself (Figure S4, Methods). The random seed generally impacted NN weight variability more than the training data, which has been recently described45,46 (Figure S5). Individual positions in each filter are highly variable, but the overall sum of the weights applied to each amino acid is similar, consistent with the large role of composition and weak grammar19,37 (Figure S4, S5). The convolutional filters are not picking up on specific protein motifs and instead are picking up composition patterns.

  When we looked at the linear weights, features at the end of the sequence had a slightly greater impact on activation domain strength (Figure 1E, 1F), as has been previously observed29,32. This trend is only statistically significant for the larger convolutional filter size (Figure 1F, S3).

  The ShallowNN identifies known features of activation domains, has similar accuracy as more complex NNs, and has the added benefit of being much more interpretable.


### The shallow neural network for predicting transcription factor abundance
  Next, to identify the sequence features that determine TF abundance, we retrained our ShallowNN to predict the second phenotype in the screen, TF abundance (ShallowNN-abund, Figure 2A). The abundance data was much noisier than the GFP data (replicate Pearson correlation of 0.568), and our best performing NN approached this ceiling (Pearson correlation of 0.555, p-value < 10−300) on unseen test data (Figure 2B). For a comparison to a deep NN, we retrained our deep NN to predict the abundance (ADhunter-Cherry). ADhunter-Cherry once again achieved slightly higher performance with 200x more parameters (Figure 2B).

  By interpreting the ShallowNN-abund, we found that the last few amino acids of the sequence had the largest impact on TF abundance (Figure 2C). As seen visually in the NN convolutional filter parameters, the presence of hydrophobic amino acids in the last few positions decreases the predicted abundance, while charged amino acids increase the predicted abundance (Figure 2C). This pattern is statistically significant: all hydrophobic amino acid weights (M, L, V, A, F, Y, W) in the final position of the convolutional filter are significantly more negative than expected under the null hypothesis of no relationship between sequence and TF abundance (Figure S6, adjusted p-values < 0.05, Permutation test), while all charged amino acids except histidine (D, E, K, R) are significantly more positive than expected (Figure S6, adjusted p-value < 0.05, Permutation test).

  As we did with the ShallowNN for TF strength, we investigated the distribution of NN parameters across hundreds of NNs with various random seeds, filter sizes, and bootstrapped training data. Across all of these NN variations, C-terminal positions in the sequence had the largest impact on predicted abundance (Figure S7). Additionally, in the final position in the convolutional filters, the weights for hydrophobic amino acids were almost always negative, while the weights for charged amino acids were almost always positive (Figure S7).

  When we went back to our experimental data, we found that sequences that ended in a hydrophobic amino acid had lower abundance than those ending in a charged amino acid. By interpreting the NN, we detected a trend in the data that we had previously missed (Figure 2D, S4, p-value=5.05e-39, Welch’s t-test). As the sequences acquired more C-terminal hydrophobic amino acids, they became less abundant (Figure S8). This trend was distinct from the effect of hydrophobic amino acids throughout the sequence, which also decreased TF abundance to a lesser extent (Figure S8). By interpreting our ShallowNN, we were able to uncover strong biological signals that we had missed in our preliminary data exploration.

  Because all synthetic TFs are under control of the same promoter, differences in abundance are most likely due to protein destabilization and degradation. Supporting this hypothesis, a recent paper performing a high-throughput screen of TF abundance found that treatment with a proteasome inhibitor increased the abundance of almost all TFs, indicating that TF abundance in these screens is frequently controlled through protein degradation47. We analyzed the abundance data from two screens in human cells from the same group47,48 and found that sequences ending in hydrophobic amino acids are enriched in low abundance sequences, consistent with the patterns in our data (Figure S9). We also found that C-terminal S. cerevisiae activation domains27 were enriched for acidic residues in the last ten positions (p-value=0.015, Mann–Whitney U test, one-tailed, Figure S10). Together, these analyses suggest that acidic amino acids in C-terminal activation domains could play a role in preventing TF degradation.


### Incorporating Biophysical Models into Neural Network Architecture
  To further increase interpretability, we next designed NNs that incorporated a biophysical model of activation domains (the BiophysicalNNs). These BiophysicalNNs simultaneously predict TF abundance and activation domain strength, unlike the ShallowNNs, where these values were predicted by two separate NNs. We tested two biophysical models, both inspired by our acidic exposure model of activation domain function28,49. In the acidic exposure model, key aromatic and leucine residues make contacts with hydrophobic surfaces of coactivators in the bound state. However, in the unbound state, these same hydrophobic residues interact with one another to drive a collapsed, inactive state. The acidic residues counteract the collapse with electrostatic repulsion and favorable free energy of solvation, promoting an exposed state where the hydrophobic residues are available to bind the coactivator28. We formalized the acidic exposure model as a three-state biophysical model (Figure 4A). As an intermediate, we first studied a two-state binding model where the activation domains exist in two states: unbound or bound to coactivator (Figure 3A). Only activation domains in the second state (bound to coactivator) are able to activate GFP expression50-52.

  We derived an equation relating the equilibrium constants between the states of the biophysical models to the experimental measures of activation domain strength and TF abundance. We made three assumptions: activation domain strength is a function of the amount of activation domain in the bound state, TF abundance is the sum of the amount of TF in each state, and our system is in equilibrium (Figure 3B, 4B, see S11 for full derivation). As in other approaches13,40, we have made the simplifying assumption that the system is in equilibrium because individual biological steps (such as binding or protein abundance) are often at equilibrium despite larger biological processes being out of equilibrium53.

  The BiophysicalNNs predict protein abundance and the equilibrium constants using the basic building blocks described earlier (Figure 1B). The predicted equilibrium constant(s) are used to calculate the amount of TF in the bound state. Finally, a function is applied to predict the total amount of GFP expression from the amount of bound TF. We found that a hill function leads to the highest performance (Methods). The architectures for the two-state and three-state BiophysicalNN are shown in Figure 3C and Figure 4C, respectively.


### Biophysical Neural Networks retain prediction accuracy
  The best performing BiophysicalNN encoding the two-state activation domain model had similar accuracy compared to the ShallowNN (Figure 3D). For larger filter sizes, the two NNs have very similar accuracies (Figure S12). The requirement for larger filter sizes in the two-state BiophysicalNN is likely due to the position-specific grammar signals detected by the two-state BiophysicalNN, which can be captured only by larger convolutional filters that incorporate positional information. These grammar signals will be discussed in the following section.

  The three-state BiophysicalNN also performed comparably to the ShallowNN and the best performing two-state BiophysicalNN (Figure 4D, S12). Like the ShallowNN, both BiophysicalNNs performed similarly to the deeper NNs with many fewer parameters. Both the BiophysicalNNs generalized slightly better than the ShallowNN on new data (Table S1). Overall, all NN variations performed similarly, with individual differences likely coming from differences in random seeds rather than the inherent benefits of one architecture over the other.


### Biophysical Neural Networks reveal activation domain grammar
  Starting with the two-state BiophysicalNN, we investigated what our BiophysicalNNs were learning. We focused on the sequence features that promoted a higher equilibrium constant between the bound and unbound states by looking at the convolution filter and linear layer weights (Figure 3E). The linear layer has a negative weight, so the effect of the convolutional filter weights on the final prediction is reversed (colorbar is reversed). These matrices are once again horizontal, indicating composition is more important than position.

  In the top performing BiophysicalNN, tryptophan, phenylalanine, and methionine residues throughout the sequence increase the predicted strength of coactivator binding, similar to the ShallowNN for predicting activation strength (Figure 3E, S13, all adjusted p < 0.002, Permutation test, Methods). Also, like the ShallowNN, basic residues throughout the sequence decrease the amount of TF in the bound state (Figure S13, adjusted p < 0.002, Permutation test, Methods). These patterns were consistent across hundreds of BiophysicalNNs (Figure S14). Specifically, when we vary both the training data and the randomness in the training process via random seed, tryptophan, phenylalanine, and methionine have the most positive weight across convolutional filter sizes, increasing the predicted equilibrium constant. Lysine and arginine have the most negative weight, decreasing the predicted equilibrium constant (Figure S14).

  Unlike its function in the ShallowNN, in the BiophysicalNN, aspartic acid has two conflicting functions with position-specific dependence, suggesting grammar. Aspartic acid residues at the beginning of the sequence increase predicted coactivator binding (Figure 3E, row for D). Aspartic acid at the end of the sequence does not. Instead, aspartic acid at the end of the sequence increases overall protein abundance (Figure 3F, row for D). This pattern is statistically significant when compared to a null model where the NN was trained using shuffled labels (Figure S13, significantly smaller in N-terminal half: adjusted p-value = 0.009, statistically larger in C-terminal half: adjusted p-value < 0.002, Methods). This pattern was also consistent across hundreds of BiophysicalNNs (Figure S14). Unlike the ShallowNN, the two-state BiophysicalNN can separate the two functions of acidic residues and capture grammar signals.

  Beyond the grammar of acidic residues, we also found other differences between the ShallowNN and the two-state BiophysicalNN for isoleucine, valine, and leucine (Figure 3E). Only valines in the last ~10 positions of the sequence have a negative weight, promoting coactivator binding. Isoleucines and leucines in the second half of the sequence promote coactivator binding (Figure S13, leucine adjusted p-value < 0.002, isoleucine adjusted p-value < 0.002), while those in the first half do not (both adjusted p-value = 0.998). We suspect these signals were messier in the ShallowNN because these same residues also decrease overall TF abundance (Figure 3F). By separating the contributions to coactivator binding and abundance, the BiophysicalNN suggests a dual role for valine, leucine, and isoleucine.

  Next, we evaluated whether the predicted equilibrium constants were capturing the interactions between the 40 amino acid tile and the Med15 coactivator. In the absence of experimental data on the equilibrium constants for our tiles, we instead used FINCHES54 to predict the strength of the interaction between the 40 amino acid tile and the Med15 coactivator ABD1. FINCHES predicts the interactions between intrinsically disordered regions and partner proteins using molecular dynamics force fields and was shown to predict activation domain-Med15 interactions accurately54. We found that the equilibrium constant is correlated more with the FINCHES predicted interaction strength than with the experimental data (Figure S15). In the absence of experiments, we caution against interpreting the equilibrium constants as a true biophysical measure, but the relationship between the predicted interaction strength and the predicted equilibrium constant of binding is intriguing.


### More complicated biophysical models do not increase accuracy or interpretability
  Next, we set up the three-state BiophysicalNN to formalize the acidic exposure model (Figure 4A). In this biophysical model, acidic amino acids promote the exposed state (state 2), and aromatic/hydrophobic amino acids promote the bound state (state 3) and the collapsed state (state 1). We did not enforce this assumption in the NN, however, instead merely designing an NN with three states where only the final state is capable of activating gene expression (Figure 4B, see Figure S11 for full derivation). When we investigated the parameters of the three-state BiophysicalNN, they did not match the expectations of the acidic exposure model (Figure 4E, F). Specifically, acidic amino acids did not promote the exposed state (Figure 4E). Instead, the three-state BiophysicalNN convolutional filter for predicting the equilibrium constant of binding looks very similar to the convolutional filter from the two-state BiophysicalNN (Figure 3E, 4F). The convolutional filter for predicting the equilibrium constant between the exposed and collapsed states is noisy (Figure 4E). Across filter sizes and random seeds, these patterns were not consistent (Figure S16). Because of this variability, we are hesitant to draw any conclusions about sequence features that promote the exposed versus the bound state. This inconsistency also suggests that the three-state BiophysicalNN is underconstrained/overparameterized.

  There are many possible reasons that the parameters of our three-state BiophysicalNN do not match our expectations based on the acidic exposure model. Potentially, the BiophysicalNN is encoding a different three-state model of transcriptional activation domains in which the final state promotes gene expression. For example, another three-state model is a two-step binding mechanism, where binding is initiated by fast, low affinity electrostatic interactions55, then followed by slow, stronger hydrophobic interactions50-52. Alternatively, if the transitions between the exposed and collapsed states are very fast compared to binding56,57, then there is a separation of time scales and the system is better approximated by a two-state model. Estimates of intramolecular reorganization rates in intrinsically disordered regions are faster than the diffusion limit,58 so this separation of time scales is plausible.

  Another explanation, however, is that our NN is not “learning” any three-state biophysical model. NNs are known to build in redundancy, where the same features are picked up by various parts of the network59. In many of our three-state NNs, similar features are picked up by both convolutional filters (Figure 4, S16). More innovative NN design, more complex loss functions, or more experimental measurements of intermediate states may be required to reduce redundancy. Because the three-state BiophysicalNN is underconstrained and inconsistent, we focused the rest of our analysis on the two-state BiophysicalNN.


### Biophysical Neural Networks help to interpret more complicated NNs
  The two-state BiophysicalNN helped us to understand the post-hoc interpretations of the deeper NNs, revealing that the deep NNs had also picked up position-dependent amino acid contributions. We performed a DeepLIFT9 interpretation of ADhunterLite19,33 (Methods), our quantitative predictor trained on data from cells sorted on the GFP:mCherry ratio (the GFP expression normalized by the mCherry signal). Ratio sorting is our most commonly collected and highest resolution data, and ADhunterLite is our workhorse predictor. Note that ADhunterLite is different than the ensemble version of ADhunter33, which was trained on a different dataset. DeepLIFT scores calculate how much each amino acid in the input is contributing to the final prediction9. Averaging all DeepLIFT scores for the Gcn4 dataset indicated that in ADhunterLite, acidic amino acids also have position-dependent effects. Acidic amino acids at the C-terminus decrease the ADhunterLite prediction and amino acids at the N-terminus increase the prediction (Figure 5A). This result contradicts previous analyses that had suggested that acidic residues throughout the sequence increase activation domain strength20-29. This trend aligns, however, with our BiophysicalNN interpretation. Acidic amino acids at the C-terminus increase the total abundance of the TF, counterintuitively leading to a decrease in the GFP to mCherry expression ratio. The ADhunterLite DeepLIFT also finds that aromatic and bulky hydrophobic amino acids have the strongest effect when located near the end of the sequence. The BiophysicalNN also helped us to understand these trends more deeply: C-terminal hydrophobic amino acids both decrease abundance and increase coactivator binding, which both increase the ratio. Finally, the trends in our interpretation of ADhunterLite match the trends in the interpretation of the ensemble version of ADhunter33, suggesting these patterns may result from ratio sorting and not the specific dataset. The BiophysicalNN identified two instances of bidirectional amino acid contributions in ADhunterLite, interpreting the interpreters.

  We also performed an interpretation of another deep NN trained to predict activation domains, PADDLE27. We took fifteen full-length TFs, ran PADDLE on all possible single amino acid mutations, and averaged the effect of each perturbation on the output (Methods). We chose this approach because PADDLE was not amenable to DeepLIFT because the original code used to write and train the NN was not available. Applying this approach to ADhunterLite captures trends similar to those of the DeepLIFT interpretation (Figure S17). Based on this PADDLE interpretation, C-terminal non-aromatic hydrophobic amino acids tend to decrease the predicted activation domain strength (Figure 5B). Initially, this trend was counterintuitive, as previous analyses have found that bulky hydrophobic amino acids positively contribute to activation domain function19,21,23-25,27-30. From our BiophysicalNN, however, we propose that these hydrophobic residues may decrease TF abundance, leading to lower gene expression. In the training data for PADDLE, Sanborn et al.27 attempted to control for TF abundance by gating on a narrow range of mCherry, but we hypothesize that there may have still been variability in TF abundance that contributed to reporter gene expression. In line with this hypothesis, PADDLE predicts the GFP signal in our dataset better than the ratio signal (Spearman correlation of 0.760 for GFP vs. 0.683 for ratio). These two examples demonstrate how BiophysicalNNs can be combined with more sophisticated post-hoc interpretation techniques to enhance our understanding of what deep NNs are learning.


### Deep Neural Networks better predict edge cases
  What is the value of DeepNNs when BiophysicalNNs are far more interpretable and almost as accurate? To answer this question, we compared the errors of the two-state BiophysicalNN and ADhunter-GFP, the deep NN trained on the same data. The errors made by ADhunter-GFP and the BiophysicalNN are correlated (Pearson r = 0.664, p-value < 10−300, Figure 5C). The BiophysicalNN underpredicts more often than the DeepNN (68.8% and 55.6% underpredicted, respectively), suggesting that the DeepNN is better able to handle the imbalance in training data (about 62% of sequences are inactive). Additionally, the residuals of the BiophysicalNN and the ShallowNN are more symmetric than the residuals for the DeepNN (Figure 5D, ADhunter-GFP skew = −1.307, ShallowNN skew = −0.648, BiophysicalNN skew = −0.999), although there is no significant difference in the residual means (p > 0.05, Welch’s t-test).

  We hypothesized that ADhunter-GFP may be better at identifying more complex relationships between sequence and activation domain strength (i.e. it learns more sequence grammar). To test this hypothesis, we trained a linear regression model on composition, or the counts of each amino acid in the sequence (Figure 5E). We took the subset of sequences that were less active than expected based on their composition. Both the two-state BiophysicalNN and ADhunter-GFP tended to overpredict the strength of these sequences, suggesting composition plays a large role in their predictions (Figure 5F). Both were also able to make accurate predictions on a subset of these sequences, indicating that they are able to learn grammar beyond composition alone. The DeepNN generally made smaller errors on these sequences (Figure 5E, p-value = 9.513e-71, Welch’s t-test). ADhunter-GFP also performed better on sequences that were more active than their composition predicted (Figure 5G, p-value = 3.097e-31, Welch’s t-test). On sequences that were correctly predicted by composition, there was not a significant difference between the errors of ADhunter-GFP and the BiophysicalNN (p-value = 0.164, Welch’s t-test). The deep NN, ADhunter-GFP, is better at predicting activation domain strength of sequences where grammar causes large departures from the strength expected from composition alone.


### Generalizing Interpretable BiophysicalNNs to other datasets
  To demonstrate the generalizability of our approach, we retrained the two-state BiophysicalNN on two other datasets. First, we retrained the two-state BiophysicalNN on activation domain data from another screen30. This screen was nearly identical to our original screen (Figure 1A), but the fragments came from plant TFs, and it was performed at another site. We trained many two-state BiophysicalNNs using this data, varying the random seed and convolutional filter size. The main dataset used in the analysis came from the ratio sort (using the ratio of GFP and mCherry to normalize for TF abundance), and the GFP and mCherry Sort-Seq data is of lower quality because far fewer cells were sorted. Despite the lower predictive performance of the NN on this dataset (Figure 6A), it was able to predict the original dataset of Gcn4 orthologs accurately (Figure 6B). Additionally, the parameters of the NN trained using the plant dataset were interpretable and matched the features of the original two-state BiophysicalNN (Figure 6C, correlation = −0.849, p-value=8.055e-223, correlation is negative because the weights in the linear layer have opposite signs). The BiophysicalNN learns the same features from an independent set of activation domains.

  We retrained our two-state BiophysicalNN on data from a structured protein, PSD9540. For single and double mutants of the PDZ3 domain of PSD95 protein, Faure et al.40 measured the abundance and binding to partner protein CRIPT using two yeast growth assays (Figure 6D). We assume a two-state binding model (Figure 6E). Additionally, in order to apply our BiophysicalNN, we make the simplifying assumption that the PDZ3 concentration measured in the abundance assay is equivalent to the PDZ3 concentration in the binding assay (Figure 6F).

  Using the experimental measurements of binding and abundance, we retrained the two-state BiophysicalNN (Figure 6H). The BiophysicalNN makes very accurate predictions (Figure 6G). Our predicted equilibrium constants are highly correlated to experimentally measured equilibrium constants60 (Figure 6I, Spearman ρ=0.917, p-value = 2.717e-12), although the magnitudes are shifted. The authors of the original work also use a shallow NN that incorporates a biophysical model to predict the effect of mutations. Their biophysical model uses three states and predicts ▵G instead of the equilibrium constant of binding. Their predicted ▵G is also very correlated with ▵G calculated from our predicted equilibrium constant (Spearman ρ=0.797, p-value = 3.052e-270). For the PDZ3 domain, the two-state BiophysicalNN is able to predict accurate relative biophysical quantities.

  We interpreted the convolutional filter weights for this PDZ3 BiophysicalNN. The weights of the convolutional filter have vertical streaks, indicating that mutation effects are position dependent as expected for a structured protein (Figure 6H). This result indicates that our modeling framework can appropriately detect vertical streaks in structured proteins or horizontal streaks in disordered proteins. The weights of our NN convolutional filter are similar to the high confidence weights from the original study (Spearman ρ = 0.756, p-value = 1.057e-221).

  Our BiophysicalNN framework is a versatile platform for interpretable machine learning. It can be applied to both high-quality and noisy data. It can interpret both ordered proteins and intrinsically disordered regions. It works well for single and double mutants of a single protein, tiles sampled from a proteome, or on sequences with many substitutions, like the Gcn4 homologs.


## Discussion
  As neural networks (NNs) proliferate in biology and interpretability becomes more important, most interpretation approaches are either more sophisticated algorithms or simple NNs that are designed for direct interpretation. Here, we designed directly interpretable shallow and biophysical NNs that accurately predict transcriptional activation domains from protein sequence. These interpretable NNs revealed dual roles for hydrophobic and acidic amino acids. These dual roles were also present, but not previously appreciated, in the sophisticated interpretations of large NNs. In this way, the interpretable NNs helped us to interpret the interpretations of large NNs.

  We designed our NNs to predict two key functions of activation domains, protein abundance and activation domain strength. These NNs achieve similar accuracy to that of deep NNs with orders of magnitude fewer parameters. The ShallowNNs for predicting activation domain strength detect known features of activation domains, such as the importance of acidic and aromatic amino acids16,20-30, while the ShallowNNs for predicting TF abundance and the BiophysicalNNs highlighted unappreciated signals in our data.

  Previously, the impact of TF abundance in high-throughput activation domain screens had either been ignored22-24, been normalized through ratio sorting25,28,30, or controlled for with gating27. Here, we took an alternative approach and used BiophysicalNNs to model the relationship between abundance and activation domain strength directly, which allows us to deconvolve bifunctional roles of amino acids (Figure 4G). For example, we found that hydrophobic residues, especially in the final C-terminal positions, both increase coactivator binding and decrease TF abundance. This finding aligns with previous studies that have found that activation domains tend to overlap with degrons47,61,62 and that high C-terminal hydrophobic content often decreases protein abundance63,64. These contradictory functions could occur because C-terminal hydrophobic residues are recognized by the unfolded protein response or because activating transcription (via interactions with coactivators) leads to TF degradation, as previously suggested65-68.

  Through our BiophysicalNN, we also identified a grammar signal for acidic amino acids. Acidic amino acids at the N-terminal end of the activation domain increase predicted AD-coactivator binding, while acidic amino acids at the C-terminal end of the activation domain decrease predicted AD-coactivator binding. The effects of C-terminal acidic amino acids on AD-coactivator binding, however, are counteracted because C-terminal acidic amino acids increase TF abundance. Two recent high-throughput screens of protein abundance in humans also found that acidic amino acids promote higher abundance and that acidic residues can counter the degron effect of hydrophobic amino acids in activation domains47,48. We propose that charge at the C-terminus of activation domains is more important for protein abundance than for binding to coactivators.

  The bifunctional role of amino acids that we observed also allows us to reinterpret previous studies. For example, a deep mutational scan study of the CRX TF found that replacing the C-terminal amino acids (WKFQIL) with aliphatic or acidic amino acids increased activation domain strength41, despite the earlier observation that this motif is important for function69. This effect could occur because of the acidic exposure model, as the authors suggested. Alternatively, we suggest that removing aromatic residues at the C-terminus increases TF abundance, which subsequently increases activation domain strength.

  Protein abundance is a necessary precondition for TF function. Many clinical mutations destabilize proteins, prompting efforts to improve the diagnosis of Variants of Uncertain Significance by combining deep mutational scans with measures of protein abundance70,71. In activation domain screens, we and others have tried to control for TF abundance with ratio sorting or gating narrowly on TF abundance. Both TF abundance and the abundance normalization method shape what the NNs learn.

  As has been previously noted, increased interpretability often leads to a decrease in accuracy,6 and this work is no exception. Despite the mild decrease in predictive performance, we find value in building interpretability and biophysical models into NNs. Additionally, we highlight the value of interpreting many NNs in parallel to ensure robust conclusions, as previously demonstrated12,72,73. We trained over 10,000 NNs with varied architecture, random seeds, and data. By interpreting these NNs in parallel, we were able to distinguish robust trends from noise. We also applied permutation testing to evaluate the significance of our conclusions.

  This study complements a growing body of research on NN interpretability. Much work focuses on post-hoc interpretability methods, which can provide useful biological insight. For example, a DeepLIFT/DeepSHAP analysis of NNs trained to predict ATAC-Seq or DNA-Seq data revealed an enzyme bias in the data and enabled the authors to design a NN to correct for this bias74. For activation domains, the deep NN TADA was interpreted using SHAP, which revealed subclasses of activation domains and supported the acidic exposure model30.

  Other research has incorporated biological knowledge into NNs to increase interpretability. For example, an NN that incorporated a biophysical model provided insight into the spatial distributions of protein affecting cell forces73. Other NNs build networks of gene or protein interactions into the architecture, which has also led to biological insight through post-hoc interpretation methods72. For example, DeepLIFT interpretation of an NN trained to predict prostate cancer disease state, P-NET, revealed both known and previously uncharacterized prostate cancer driver genes75. Post-hoc interpretations also have drawbacks, however, and often cannot distinguish biases in the network from real biology12,76, leading to artifacts12. Although techniques have been developed to mitigate artifacts12,76, these challenges highlight the value of directly interpreting NN parameters and applying statistical approaches to evaluate parameter significance.

  This work is most similar to (and inspired by) the recent development of biophysical NNs used to predict genotype-phenotype maps10,11. These biophysical NNs, MoCHI11 and MAVE-NN10, are trained on massively parallel reporter assay data. To map sequence to a biophysical value, MAVE-NN and MoCHI predict intermediate latent phenotypes or biophysical values (such as Gibbs free energy) linearly (i.e. adding the effects of single and double mutations). These NNs are easily interpretable but require data for all single and many double mutants in reference to a single WT sequence and have mostly been applied to structured proteins. In contrast, our approach permits more diverse sequences, can be applied to intrinsically disordered proteins, and has the flexibility to predict non-linear relationships between sequence and biophysical values while still maintaining enough simplicity to interrogate our network directly.

  Another similar biophysical NN, BoltzNet, also uses a single convolutional filter to predict an intermediate biophysical value: the equilibrium constant of a TF binding to DNA13. They showed that their NN correctly infers biophysical parameters and they directly interpreted the convolutional filter weights13. In our BiophysicalNNs, we incorporate features of both BoltzNet13, by using a convolutional filter to predict an equilibrium constant, and MoCHI11, by having our BiophysicalNNs simultaneously predict two dimensions of activation domain function. Combining these approaches is what allows our BiophysicalNN to effectively learn the tradeoffs between acidic and hydrophobic amino acids described above.

  In conclusion, we advocate for a dual-pronged approach incorporating both biophysical NNs and deeper, more accurate NNs. The deep NN can provide the most accurate predictions, while the biophysical NNs can be used to understand the post-hoc interpretations of the deep NN. As NNs become a larger part of biological research, we hope this multi-pronged interpretation will provide another avenue to learn biology.


## Star Methods

### Method Details

#### Dataset preparation
  The dataset used for training and testing is from our previous screen19. The 40-amino acid sequences originate from homologs of the S. cerevisiae Gcn4 TF, a stress-response TF that contains a highly studied acidic activation domain. The full-length Gcn4 homolog sequences are broken into 40 amino acid tiles separated by five amino acids. This tiling strategy resulted in 20,731 designed 40 amino acid tiles. Sort-Seq25,28 was performed to determine the AD strength and mCherry abundance measurements for each tile (Figure 1A). In total, we had mCherry and GFP data for 17,732 tiles. When tiles were present in both of the two replicates, we averaged the score from each replicate to get a single score for each tile.

  Because of the tiling process, many sequences in our dataset were closely related, with some differing by as few as five amino acids. This overlap means that if our NN memorized the training data, it could still perform well on the test dataset, depending on how the sequences were split into train/test splits. To account for this potential bias, we split the data into train, validation, and test sets with minimal overlap. We hierarchically clustered the full-length ortholog sequences, split the sequences into three clusters, and then assigned tiles to each cluster based on the ortholog sequence from which they originated (Figure S18A). In this way, tiles from the same full-length sequence would not be in the training, validation and test split. Additionally, we added all control sequences to the test set (Figure S18A). As a final test, we verified that there was minimal correlation between the similarity of sequences in the test set to the training set and the prediction accuracy (Figure S18B).


#### Neural Network design
  For all the NN variations, we used the same basic building block, illustrated in Figure 1B. This block takes as input the one-hot encoded activation domain sequence and predicts either the total TF abundance (mCherry fluorescence), the activation domain strength (GFP fluorescence), or an intermediate biophysical value (the equilibrium constant between states). The NNs were created using PyTorch77. Additionally, code to load the data was modified from Faure and Lehner11. For the ShallowNN, it was straightforward to use a single block to predict either the activation strength or the TF abundance.

  To predict these measurements using the biophysical models, we first derived an equation relating the biophysical models to the experimental data. This required three assumptions (Figure 3B, 4B). First, the total mCherry fluorescence is equal to the amount of TF in each state. Second, the total GFP fluorescence is related to the amount of TF in the bound state through some function. We compared a linear function and a hill function and found that the hill function led to the best performance (Figure S19). Finally, we assume that the states of the biophysical models are in equilibrium. Using these three assumptions, we derived an equation relating the equilibrium constants to the experimental measurements (Figure S11).


#### Neural Network training
  We trained NNs with six convolutional filter sizes (5, 10, 15, 20, 30, 40). We trained for 200 epochs with early stopping. Early stopping occurred after five epochs with no improvement in the validation loss. We used a learning rate of 0.001, as that led to the highest performance. For the ShallowNN, our loss function was the mean absolute error (MAE) between the predicted and actual values.

  For the BiophysicalNN, we performed the training in two stages because we were predicting two different values. First, we trained the abundance predictor. We froze all the weights not involved in predicting the abundance and used a loss function that included only the abundance values. This loss function also included a term to penalize negative linear weights (this helped in interpretability and did not have a noticeable impact on performance). Specifically, the loss function was:  loss(predictedabundance,experimentalabundance)=MAE(predictedabundance,experimentalabundance)+0.1∗(-sumofnegativeweights)+0.5∗(-sumofnegativeabundancepreds)  After we had trained the abundance module, as defined by the validation loss no longer improving after five epochs, we trained the activation domain strength prediction module. We froze the weights involved in predicting the abundance and used a loss function that included only activation domain strength values. We noticed that the best performing NN often predicted negative equilibrium constants, which does not make sense from a biological perspective. Therefore, we also added a term to the loss function to penalize negative equilibrium constants. Similar to above, we also included a term in the loss function to penalize negative linear weights, which helped with interpretability. The loss function for this stage of training was:  loss(predictedactivation,experimentalactivation)=MAE(predictedactivation,experimentalactivation)+0.1∗(-sumofnegativeweights)+0.5∗(-sumofnegativeequilibriumconstants)


#### Details of how the NN layers work
  In what follows, we describe how each of the layers of the network function. Although this part is likely unnecessary for readers experienced in ML, we include this section because an understanding of how each component of the network functions is essential to interpret the parameters of the network.

  Each NN begins by encoding the input sequence with a one-hot encoding. A one-hot encoding transforms the 40 amino acid sequence into a (20 x 40) matrix where the columns correspond to sequence position and the rows correspond to amino acids. A one in the matrix indicates the specified amino acid (given by the row) is in the given position (given by the column).

  A convolutional filter is then directly applied to the encoding. The convolutional filter is a (20 x k) matrix where the rows correspond to the twenty possible amino acids and the columns correspond to position in the sequence. Starting from the beginning of the sequence, this filter matrix is element-wise multiplied with the portion of the encoded sequence with which it overlaps. If the filter is (20 x 5), it would be multiplied with the first five columns in the one-hot encoding. The result of this multiplication is summed and a bias term is added, resulting in a single value. The filter then slides one position over (i.e. starts at the second amino acid position in the one-hot encoding) and repeats the procedure, adding the same bias term. The filter continues until it reaches the end of the sequence. This results in a 40 − k + 1 length vector. In summary, the convolutional filter is a method of scanning the entire sequence for specific patterns/motifs/important residues without regard for where in the sequence these residues appear.

  The Leaky ReLU activation function transforms each value in the 40 − k + 1 vector that resulted from the convolution. It is a variant of the ReLU activation function where positive values remain unchanged, while negative values are shrunk by some scalar (we used 0.01). Activation functions allow NNs to learn non-linear relationships between sequence and biophysical value. After this activation function is applied, the resulting vector is multiplied by weights with some bias added. These linear weights serve to weigh patterns captured by the convolution filter by their location in the sequence, and then all of these values are summed, capturing positional effects. Finally, a second Leaky ReLU function is applied.


#### ADhunter Implementation
  ADhunterLite is a residual NN implemented in PyTorch77 and trained via PyTorch Lightning78. Its architecture consists of an embedding layer, a convolutional layer, and three residual blocks (batch norm layer, convolutional layer, another batch norm layer), followed by a max pool layer and a fully connected layer. It is trained using the same train/validation/test splits as our ShallowNNs and BiophysicalNNs with mean squared error loss and early stopping. We retrained ADhunterLite on each dataset (GFP, mCherry).


#### Comparison with other NNs
  One difficulty in directly comparing various activation domain predictors is that each predictor takes a different input size: TADA takes 40aa long inputs, PADDLE requires 53aa long inputs, and ADPred takes any input size but produces a predicted value for each residue instead of a single score for the entire sequence. We ran TADA and ADPred on the 40aa tiles in the test set. To get a single value for ADPred, we averaged the predictions for the 40aa tile. To run PADDLE, we also included the thirteen amino acids in the synthetic TF N-terminal to the 40aa tile to create a 53aa tile.


#### Deep NN interpretations
  DeepLIFT9 quantifies the effect of each input feature on the final prediction. Specifically, it calculates how much each input feature contributes to the difference in scores between the NN evaluated with the input and some baseline. For our analysis, we used a null baseline where our one-hot encoded matrix is simply a matrix of zeros. We performed this DeepLIFT analysis on all active sequences in the Gcn4 dataset19 and averaged the result to get a summary of global behavior. More details and the code used to perform the analysis is available on github.

  To interpret PADDLE27, we took fifteen Gcn4 homologs, and we performed all possible single amino acid mutations. To investigate the effect of position within the 53 amino acid input, we centered the mutated amino acid at every possible position within the 53mer. This led to over one million mutated tiles. After running PADDLE on all of these tiles, we calculated the change in activation domain strength between the wildtype and mutant tiles. We then grouped the tiles by mutation position within the tile and the mutated residue identity, calculating the average change in predicted strength for each group.


#### FINCHES predictions
  We used FINCHES54 to predict the interaction between the ABD1 domain of Med15 and all the tiles in our dataset using the folded domain surface interactions method. Specifically, FINCHES identifies solvent accessible patches of the structured protein and predicts interactions between these patches and the intrinsically disordered region. The Calvados force field79 was used. We looked at only the attractive score between the coactivator and activation domain, as the attractive score showed the best correlation across all comparisons.


#### Training on other datasets
  To train on the plant TF dataset30, we got the mCherry and GFP Sort-Seq data from Nick Morffy and Lucia Strader. We used the ADScore column. There were 52,338 total sequences. The data was split into train/test/validation datasets using the same procedure as before (Figure S18). We trained the NNs using the same parameters (early stopping, learning rate) as before.

  For the PDZ dataset, we used binding data from the PSD95-PDZ3_bind_all_mutants.csv file. Abundance data was from the PSD95-PDZ3_abundance_all_mutants.csv file. There were 5,528 sequences that had abundance and binding data. Because the data had such high overlap, we used all of the single mutants and 90% of the double mutants as training data. The rest was used as validation data. We also fixed the convolutional filter size at 84 (the length of the full sequence), because smaller filters did not work given the position-specific effects. We trained the NNs using the same parameters (early stopping, learning rate) as before.


### Quantification and Statistical Analyses

#### Variability of NN parameters
  We wished to explore the variability of our NN parameters both due to the training process and due to the data. To estimate the variability of the NN parameters due to the training process, we varied the random seed. Specifically, for the ShallowNNs, for each convolutional filter size, we trained 30 NNs with different random seeds (180 NNs each). For the two-state and three-state BiophysicalNNs, for each combination of abundance filter size and equilibrium constant filter size, we trained 10 NNs with different random seeds (360 NNs each). A diagram of this approach is shown in Figure S4B.

  To estimate the variability of the NN parameters due to the data itself, we turned to a bootstrapping approach. We randomly sampled from our training data with replacement 200 times (maintaining the sequence, abundance, strength relationship) and then retrained the NN, fixing the seed to be the same as that of our best performing NN. A diagram of this approach is shown in Figure S4A.

  We quantified the similarity between the weights of the NN by calculating the cosine similarity between weight vectors.


#### Statistical tests of NN parameters
  To test the statistical significance of our best performing NN parameters, we used a permutation approach, as proposed by Esser-Skala and Fortelny12 and Olden and Jackson44. For the permutation testing, we fixed the seed to be the same as that of our best performing NN, as suggested by Olden and Jackson, so that our permutation test would capture seed biases. We also shuffled data labels together (strength and abundance) within each data set (train/validation/test) to preserve biases within our data splits. A diagram of this approach is shown in Figure S3A.

  To perform the permutation test, we:

  Permutation tests rely on a defined test statistic. For our test statistic, we most commonly used the sum of the weights for each amino acid (i.e. horizontal sums of weights in the convolutional filter). Specifically, we tested the hypothesis that, for a specific amino acid, the sum of the weights applied to this amino acid is more positive/negative than expected if there is no relationship between sequence and strength/abundance.

  We also tested if the individual weights were more extreme than expected. Specifically, we tested if, for a specific parameter (i.e. position in convolutional filter, weight in linear layer), the parameter is more negative/positive than expected if there is no relationship between sequence and strength/abundance. Note that this test statistic assumes independence between all the parameters, which is not necessarily the case.

  Since we were testing each amino acid/parameters, we corrected for multiple hypothesis testing across amino acids/parameters using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure80.
