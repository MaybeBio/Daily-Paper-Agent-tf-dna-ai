# Integrating network toxicology and transcriptomics to identify CXCL8, EGFR, PTPRC, MYC, and AKT1 as key targets of microplastic-induced miscarriage


## Abstract
  Miscarriage, a prevalent adverse pregnancy outcome linked to maternal-fetal immune dysregulation, is increasingly suspected to be influenced by environmental microplastics, although specific molecular mechanisms remain elusive. This study integrated network toxicology, transcriptomics, and molecular dynamics simulations to elucidate the pathogenic role of microplastics in miscarriage. Initially, network toxicology identified 396 microplastics-related targets, yielding 38 intersection genes with miscarriage-associated targets from GeneCards. Functional enrichment revealed significant involvement in chemokine signaling and cytokine–cytokine receptor interactions. Analysis of the GSE183555 transcriptome dataset pinpointed 5 hub genes: CXCL8, EGFR, PTPRC, MYC, and AKT1. Immune infiltration analysis demonstrated that these hubs correlated significantly with altered immune cell populations, particularly CD8+ Tem cells, while gene set enrichment analysis and gene set variation analysis highlighted their enrichment in interleukin-17, nuclear factor kappa-B, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition pathways. Furthermore, regulatory networks involving specific transcription factors and miRNAs were constructed. Molecular docking confirmed stable binding affinities between hub proteins and potential ligands (e.g., Afatinib for EGFR, Eupalinin A for AKT1), which was further validated by 100-ns molecular dynamics simulations showing consistent root mean square deviation, radius of gyration, and hydrogen bond stability. In conclusion, this multi-omics approach uncovers a critical regulatory axis where microplastics may disrupt pregnancy maintenance by modulating key immune-inflammatory pathways via CXCL8, EGFR, PTPRC, MYC, and AKT1. These findings provide novel mechanistic insights into microplastics-induced reproductive toxicity and identify promising therapeutic targets for preventing environmentally triggered miscarriage, warranting further experimental validation in clinical and animal models.


## 1. Introduction
  Miscarriage, defined as the spontaneous termination of pregnancy before 20 weeks of gestation, represents one of the most common adverse outcomes in human reproduction, with an estimated incidence rate of 10% to 20% among clinically recognized pregnancies.[1,2] The maternal-fetal interface plays a crucial role in determining pregnancy outcomes, and imbalances in immune cell populations and their functions may lead to miscarriage. Natural killer cells and macrophages are the most abundant immune cells at this interface, participating in trophoblast invasion, uterine vascular adaptation, and fetal growth.[3,4] However, excessive activation of cytotoxic natural killer cells and pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages in early pregnancy may result in miscarriage.[5–8]

  Microplastics, widespread emerging pollutants, have attracted global attention due to potential hazards to ecosystems and human health. They can enter and accumulate in the human body via diet, respiration, and skin contact.[9,10] While research on their direct link to miscarriage is exploratory, basic studies suggest microplastics may affect pregnancy outcomes through multiple pathways: acting as endocrine disruptors to interfere with maternal sex hormone balance,[11–13] inducing inflammation and impairing placental function, and triggering oxidative stress to damage embryonic DNA and increase apoptosis risk.[14] However, the specific molecular mechanisms remain unclear, requiring more high-quality epidemiological and targeted laboratory studies for verification.

  To address these gaps, this study integrated network toxicology and transcriptome sequencing to investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying microplastic-induced miscarriage. First, network toxicology was used to predict microplastic targets and screen targets overlapping with miscarriage. Then, using the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) miscarriage transcriptome dataset (GSE183555), key genes were identified via immune infiltration, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)/gene set variation analysis (GSVA), and protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis. Finally, molecular docking and dynamics simulations verified the binding stability of key genes with potential ligands, aiming to clarify the regulatory pathways and provide potential targets for further research.


## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1. Network toxicology analysis of microplastics
  Microplastic SMILES sequences were retrieved from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and input into ADMETlab3.0 (Computer-Aided Drug Design & Bioinformatics (CBDD), Xiangya School of Pharmaceutical Sciences, Central South University, https://admetmesh.scbdd.com/service/evaluation/cal) and ProTox 3.0 (Structural Bioinformatics Group, Institute of Physiology, Charité – Universitätsmedizin Berlin, https://tox.charite.de/protox3/) for toxicity prediction.


### 2.2. Prediction of microplastic targets and construction of disease targets
  Microplastic names and molecular formulas were verified via PubChem, and potential targets were retrieved from ChEMBL (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/chembl/), with downloaded targets designated as microplastic targets. Miscarriage-related targets were searched using “miscarriage” in GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/) and OMIM (https://www.omim.org/). Targets were merged and de-duplicated to form the final disease-target library (note: OMIM webpage parsing failed; target data were mainly derived from GeneCards with existing valid entries).


### 2.3. Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes functional annotation
  R package “ClusterProfiler” (YuLab, School of Basic Medical Sciences, Southern Medical University) was used for functional annotation of intersection genes. Gene ontology/Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathways with P < .05 and q < 0.05 were considered significant.


### 2.4. PPI network
  PPI interactions of intersection genes were retrieved from STRING (https://string-db.org/) with Homo sapiens as the species and interaction score ≥0.4 and visualized via Cytoscape.


### 2.5. Data download
  GSE183555 dataset (GPL21697 annotation) was downloaded from GEO (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/info/datasets.html), including 5 miscarriage cases and 5 controls.


### 2.6. Immune infiltration
  xCell algorithm was used to infer relative abundances of 64 immune cell subsets and analyze correlations between key genes and immune cell contents.


### 2.7. GSEA and GSVA analysis
  GSEA (MsigDB v7.0 background; Broad Institute, Inc.) and GSVA were used to analyze pathway enrichment differences between high/low key gene expression groups, with adjusted P < .05 as significant. The R package “RcisTarget” predicted transcription factors based on motifs. miRNAs related to key genes were obtained from mircode, and networks were visualized via Cytoscape.


### 2.8. Transcription factor regulatory network
  Key gene protein structures were obtained from AlphaFold (https://alphafold.com/), and related drugs were predicted via DGIdb (https://www.dgidb.org/), with drug structures from ChemSpider (https://www.chemspider.com/). AutoDock was used for docking, and Gromacs2023 for 100-ns MD simulation (GAFF/AMBER14SB force fields, TIP3P water model), with stability analyzed via root mean square deviation (RMSD)/radius of gyration (Rg)/root mean square fluctuation (RMSF)/Buried solvent accessible surface area (SASA).


### 2.9. Non-coding RNA network related to key genes
  All analyses used R 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing), with two-sided tests and P < .05 as statistically significant.


### 2.10. Molecular docking
  Three-dimensional structures of key gene proteins were retrieved from AlphaFold. Potential drugs for these genes were identified via DGIdb, with drug structures obtained from ChemSpider. Molecular docking (9 runs) was performed using AutoDock. The lowest binding energy pose (excluding the first reference conformation) was selected and visualized in PyMOL to show protein–small molecule binding sites.


### 2.11. Molecular dynamics simulation
  Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation was performed using Gromacs2023. Small molecules, proteins, and water were described by GAFF, AMBER14SB, and TIP3P force fields, respectively. The complex system was simulated under isothermal–isobaric conditions with periodic boundary conditions. Hydrogen bonds were constrained by the LINCS algorithm with a 2-fs time step. Electrostatic and non-bonded interactions were treated with the PME method (1.2 nm cutoff) and a 10 Å cutoff, respectively. Temperature (298 K) and pressure (1 bar) were controlled by V-rescale and Berendsen coupling. After 100 ps NVT/NPT equilibration, a 100-ns production run was conducted. Trajectories were analyzed by VMD (Theoretical and Computational Biophysics Group) and PyMOL (Schrödinger, LLC), and stability was evaluated using RMSD, Rg, RMSF, and buried SASA.


### 2.12. Statistical analysis
  All analyses were performed using R language (version 4.3.0). All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < .05 was considered statistically significant.


## 3. Results

### 3.1. Construction of compound-target network and disease-target prediction
  PubChem data were used to search for microplastics, retrieve the Canonical SMILES of their molecular structures, and then use the ChEMBL database to retrieve the Canonical SMILES of the molecular structures. Finally, 396 effective targets of microplastics were obtained, and the interaction between microplastics and targets was displayed in the form of a network diagram using Cytoscape (Fig. 1A). Next, we extracted disease-related targets with a relevance score >1 from the GeneCards database, obtaining a total of 1396 targets, and 409 targets from the OMIM database. After merging and removing duplicates, a total of 1143 disease targets were obtained. Then, the intersection of miscarriage targets and the previous 396 microplastic targets was taken, resulting in 38 intersection target genes (Fig. 1B).


### 3.2. Enrichment analysis and PPI network
  Gene ontology results showed that the genes were mainly enriched in pathways such as viral process, chemokine-mediated signaling pathway, and response to chemokine (Fig. 1C). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes results showed that the genes were mainly enriched in the Chemokine signaling pathway, Cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, JAK-STAT signaling pathway, and so on (Fig. 1D). To further identify key genes affecting miscarriage, we constructed a PPI network of common targets through the STRING database (http://cn.string-db.org/) for the obtained intersection targets and visualized it using Cytoscape (Fig. 1E). Through degree analysis, we obtained the top 5 genes with the highest correlation: CXCL8, EGFR, PTPRC, MYC, and AKT1. These 5 genes are the key genes for our subsequent research.


### 3.3. Immune infiltration analysis
  This study showed the proportion of immune cell content in each patient and the correlation between immune cells in different forms (Fig. 2A and B). At the same time, this study found that CD8+_Tem_xCell was significantly different between the 2 groups (Fig. 2C). We further explored the relationship between key genes and immune cells and found that AKT1 was significantly positively correlated with Myocytes_xCell and Erythrocytes_xCell, and significantly negatively correlated with Basophils_xCell and CD4+_T.cells_xCell. CXCL8 was significantly positively correlated with Erythrocytes_xCell and pDC_xCell, and significantly negatively correlated with Skeletal_muscle_xCell. EGFR was significantly positively correlated with Erythrocytes_xCell, and significantly negatively correlated with NKT_xCell and Osteoblast_xCell. MYC was significantly positively correlated with Astrocytes_xCell and Endothelial_cells_xCell, and significantly negatively correlated with CD4+_memory_T.cells_xCell and Macrophages_M2_xCell. PTPRC was significantly positively correlated with HSC_xCell and significantly negatively correlated with Platelets_xCell (Fig. 2D). In addition, we also analyzed the correlation between key genes and different immune factors, including immunosuppressive factors, immunostimulatory factors, chemokines, and receptors. These analyses suggest that key genes are closely related to immune cell infiltration levels and play an important role in the immune microenvironment (Fig. 2E–I).


### 3.4. GSEA and GSVA analysis
  GSEA results showed that CXCL8 was enriched in signaling pathways such as the interleukin-17 signaling pathway, p53 signaling pathway, and apoptosis (Fig. 3A). EGFR was enriched in the nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) signaling pathway, tumor necrosis factor signaling pathway, gonadotropin-releasing hormone signaling pathway, and so on (Fig. 3B). PTPRC was enriched in the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, proteasome, cGMP-PKG signaling pathway, and so on (Fig. 3C). MYC was enriched in the ribosome, peroxisome, p53 signaling pathway, and so on (Fig. 3D). AKT1 was enriched in the cell cycle, mineral absorption, lysine degradation, and so on (Fig. 3E). GSVA analysis showed that CXCL8 was enriched in pathways such as EPITHELIAL_MESENCHYMAL_TRANSITION and TNFA_SIGNALING_VIA_NFKB (Fig. 3F). EGFR was enriched in MITOTIC_SPINDLE, G2M_CHECKPOINT, and so on (Fig. 3G). PTPRC was enriched in ANGIOGENESIS, ALLOGRAFT_REJECTION, and so on (Fig. 3H). MYC was enriched in EPITHELIAL_MESENCHYMAL_TRANSITION, ANGIOGENESIS, and so on (Fig. 3I). AKT1 was enriched in G2M_CHECKPOINT, E2F_TARGETS, and so on (Fig. 3J). This suggests that key genes may affect the progression of miscarriage through these pathways.


### 3.5. Correlation analysis of key genes
  In this study, genome-wide co-expression association analysis of key genes was performed using expression profiles. We set strict screening criteria (cor > 0.7, Pval < .05) to identify co-expressed genes with significant statistical significance. Subsequently, we selected the top 10 and bottom 10 genes most correlated with key genes for visualization and displayed them through a correlation heatmap (Fig. 4A–E).


### 3.6. Construction of transcription factor regulatory network and miRNA network of key genes
  We took key genes as the gene set for this analysis and found that they are regulated by multiple transcription factors and other common mechanisms. Therefore, enrichment analysis of these transcription factors was performed using cumulative recovery curves. Motif-TF annotation and selection analysis of important genes showed that the motif with the highest Normalized Enrichment Score (NES: 5.31) was cisbp__M3633. All motifs enriched in key genes and corresponding transcription factors were displayed (Fig. 5A and B). Then, we reverse-predicted key genes through the mircode database, obtained 81 miRNAs, totaling 188 mRNA–miRNA pairs, and visualized them using Cytoscape (Fig. 5C).


### 3.7. Molecular docking
  Molecular docking was performed on key genes. The selected proteins and compounds for key genes were the following: CXCL8: P10145-Yangonin, EGFR: P00533-Afatinib, PTPRC: P08575-Benzocatechol, MYC: P01106-Aminopterin, AKT1: P31749-Eupalinin A. The docking binding energies were presented in tabular form, and the docking results were visualized. Molecular docking results showed that the binding energy of CXCL8: P10145-Yangonin was −6.1 kcal/mol (Fig. 5D); the binding energy of EGFR: P00533-Afatinib was −6.8 kcal/mol (Fig. 5E); the binding energy of PTPRC: P08575-Benzocatechol was −5.9 kcal/mol (Fig. 5F); the binding energy of MYC: P01106-Aminopterin was −5.4 kcal/mol (Fig. 5G); and the binding energy of AKT1: P31749-Eupalinin A was −7.6 kcal/mol (Fig. 5H).

## 4. Discussion
  As a common reproductive health problem, the incidence of miscarriage is as high as 10% to 20%, with a prominent risk in early pregnancy. This condition not only poses significant risks to maternal physical health but also has profound psychological consequences, including anxiety and depression.[1,15] The maternal-fetal interface is a dynamic immunological environment where a complex interplay of immune cells, including natural killer cells and macrophages, is critical for maintaining pregnancy. A disruption in this delicate immune balance, particularly an overactivation of cytotoxic and pro-inflammatory cells, is a well-established contributor to miscarriage. While genetic, endocrine, and immune factors are known to be involved,[16–18] the potential role of emerging environmental pollutants, such as microplastics, is an increasingly important area of research. Microplastics, pervasive environmental contaminants, can enter the human body through ingestion and inhalation, and preliminary evidence suggests they may act as endocrine disruptors and induce inflammatory responses that could jeopardize pregnancy.[14,19] However, the specific molecular mechanisms through which microplastics contribute to miscarriage remain largely unexplored.

  In this study, we employed an integrated approach combining network toxicology and transcriptomic sequencing to systematically investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying microplastic-induced miscarriage. We initially predicted 396 potential targets of microplastics using public databases and identified 38 common targets that overlapped with miscarriage-related genes. Through PPI network analysis, we pinpointed 5 key genes – CXCL8, EGFR, PTPRC, MYC, and AKT1 – as central players in this interaction. Subsequently, we utilized the GEO miscarriage transcriptome dataset (GSE183555) to perform immune infiltration analysis and enrichment studies, revealing that these key genes are predominantly involved in inflammatory and immune-regulatory pathways, such as the chemokine signaling pathway and the JAK-STAT signaling pathway. Our findings not only elucidate a potential regulatory network through which microplastics may disrupt the immune microenvironment at the maternal-fetal interface but also provide a theoretical foundation for future targeted interventions.

  Our integrated network toxicology and transcriptomic analysis identified 5 key genes – CXCL8, EGFR, PTPRC, MYC, and AKT1 – as critical mediators of microplastic-induced miscarriage. The enrichment of these genes in chemokine signaling, JAK-STAT, and cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction pathways underscores the central role of immune dysregulation in this pathology.[20–22] This is further supported by our immune infiltration analysis, which revealed significant alterations in CD8+ Tem cells and correlations between these key genes and various immune cell subsets, including a negative association between MYC and M2 macrophages. These findings align with the known importance of a balanced immune microenvironment at the maternal-fetal interface, where excessive pro-inflammatory responses can disrupt trophoblast invasion and lead to pregnancy loss.[23–27] The identified pathways, particularly the interleukin-17 and NF-κB signaling cascades, provide a mechanistic link between microplastic exposure and the induction of a cytotoxic inflammatory milieu, which is consistent with prior studies linking environmental pollutants to adverse pregnancy outcomes through inflammatory pathways.[28]

  The molecular docking and dynamics simulations validated the stable binding of microplastic-associated compounds to our key proteins, particularly AKT1 (Eupalinin A, −7.6 kcal/mol) and EGFR (Afatinib, −6.8 kcal/mol). The stability of these complexes, as evidenced by consistent RMSD, Rg, and hydrogen bond profiles over 100 ns simulations, suggests that these interactions are not merely transient but may lead to sustained functional perturbations. Specifically, the stable binding of ligands to AKT1, a core node in the PI3K-Akt pathway, could disrupt cell survival and proliferation signals critical for trophoblast function.[29–31] Similarly, the interaction with EGFR, a master regulator of cell growth and differentiation, may impair placental development. Our network toxicology approach, which integrates multiple databases to predict toxicological mechanisms, offers a robust framework for identifying the multi-target effects of environmental pollutants, consistent with recent methodological advances in the field.[32] The convergence of these computational predictions with clinical transcriptomic data from the GSE183555 dataset provides a compelling rationale for targeting these specific pathways in future therapeutic interventions.

  The identification of specific regulatory networks, including transcription factors and miRNAs, further elucidates the complex molecular architecture of microplastic-induced miscarriage. The most enriched motif, cisbp__M3633, suggests a master transcriptional regulator may orchestrate the expression of these key genes. Moreover, the 188 mRNA–miRNA pairs we identified indicate a post-transcriptional layer of regulation that could be exploited for biomarker development or therapeutic targeting. While our study provides a comprehensive in silico framework, it is limited by its reliance on computational predictions that require experimental validation. Future studies should focus on in vitro models using trophoblast cell lines or in vivo animal models to confirm the functional impact of these key genes and their interactions with microplastics. Furthermore, the specific microplastic compounds used in docking, while based on database predictions, should be tested in controlled exposure experiments to assess their real-world relevance. The transferability of these findings to other environmental pollutants remains an open question, warranting comparative toxicological studies.

  Despite the comprehensive integration of network toxicology, transcriptomics, and MD simulations, several limitations warrant acknowledgments. First, the microplastic targets were primarily predicted via computational databases rather than validated through experimental exposure models, which may not fully capture the complex biotransformation and bioaccumulation of diverse microplastic polymers in vivo. Second, the transcriptomic analysis relied on a relatively small GEO dataset (GSE183555) with a limited sample size, potentially affecting the statistical power and generalizability of the identified immune infiltration patterns. Furthermore, while molecular docking and dynamics simulations suggested stable binding affinities between key genes and potential ligands, these findings remain theoretical without subsequent in vitro or in vivo verification to confirm their functional regulatory roles in miscarriage pathogenesis. Future studies employing larger clinical cohorts and experimental validation are essential to substantiate these mechanistic insights.

  In conclusion, this study elucidates the potential molecular mechanisms linking microplastic exposure to miscarriage by identifying a core regulatory axis involving CXCL8, EGFR, PTPRC, MYC, and AKT1. Our multi-omics approach reveals that microplastics may disrupt pregnancy maintenance primarily by modulating immune microenvironment homeostasis, specifically through chemokine signaling, inflammatory responses, and altered immune cell infiltration at the maternal-fetal interface. The identified pathways, including JAK-STAT, NF-κB, and apoptosis signaling, highlight critical nodes where environmental pollutants could exacerbate immune dysregulation leading to adverse pregnancy outcomes. Moreover, the predicted small-molecule interactions provide a foundational framework for developing targeted therapeutic strategies or diagnostic biomarkers. Ultimately, these findings bridge the gap between environmental toxicology and reproductive immunology, offering novel insights into how emerging pollutants contribute to miscarriage and paving the way for precise interventions to mitigate environmental risks in prenatal care.
