# A conserved basic motif mediates membrane anchoring and TRAF2 recruitment across costimulatory TNFRs


## Abstract
  Costimulatory receptors of the TNFR superfamily are key components in synthetic immunoreceptors, yet how their intracellular domains coordinate membrane anchoring with adaptor recruitment remains unresolved. Here, using solution-state NMR and membrane-reconstituted FRET assays, we identify a conserved basic-residue-rich sequence (BRS) in the juxtamembrane region of OX40 that binds anionic phospholipids and adopts a membrane-inserted conformation. This membrane engagement enables a dual-site interaction with TRAF2, combining canonical PxQxx binding with a secondary membrane-proximal interface, to promote robust NF-κB activation. Disruption of the BRS impairs adaptor docking without reducing receptor surface expression or ligand-induced clustering. This regulatory architecture is further supported in 4-1BB and CD40, indicating broader relevance across TNFR costimulatory receptors. Our findings refine current models of TNFR signaling and offer a generalizable framework for receptor design. Incorporating membrane-anchoring motifs into synthetic constructs may enhance spatial control of adaptor recruitment, thereby improving signal fidelity, sensitivity, and performance in CAR-T applications.


## Introduction
  Members of the tumor necrosis factor receptor (TNFR) superfamily are essential regulators of T cell activation and function (Chen and Flies, 2013; Croft, 2003; Morris and Allen, 2012). Their cytoplasmic domains (CTD), which engage adaptor proteins such as TRAFs to initiate downstream signaling, have been widely incorporated into chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) designs to enhance CAR-T cell persistence, expansion, and cytotoxicity (Jaeger-Ruckstuhl et al, 2024; Larson and Maus, 2021; Mackensen et al, 2022; Wehrli and Maus, 2021). Among these, OX40 (CD134) has emerged as a key costimulatory module, with its CTD shown to promote cytokine production and long-term memory formation (Katsarou et al, 2021; Tan et al, 2022; Zhang et al, 2021; Zhang et al, 2025). However, despite its growing application, the spatial and regulatory mechanisms underlying OX40 mediated signaling remain poorly defined, limiting its rational optimization in CAR engineering (Edner et al, 2020; Renand et al, 2020; Wang et al, 2024).

  The cytoplasmic domain (CTD) of OX40 transduces signals primarily through the recruitment of TRAF adaptor proteins (Kawamata et al, 1998; Thapa et al, 2024; Xiao et al, 2012). Structural analyses of several TNFR costimulatory receptors have established that a conserved PxQxx motif, located at the C-terminus of their CTD, mediates binding to TRAF2, TRAF3, and TRAF5 (Compaan and Hymowitz, 2006; Croft, 2009, 2010). While this canonical motif in the ~40 amino acid CTD of OX40 has been extensively characterized and integrated into CAR-T designs, the functional significance of other regions, particularly the membrane-proximal segment, remains unresolved.

  Sequence alignment revealed that the juxtamembrane region of OX40 is enriched in conserved basic residues, including R241, R242, R245, and K252, forming a basic residue rich sequence (BRS) immediately downstream of the transmembrane domain. This motif suggests a potential regulatory role. Previous studies have shown that similar basic clusters mediate membrane association and conformational regulation in other immune receptors. In CD28 and CD3ε, such residues facilitate membrane insertion of ITAM elements, modulating activation thresholds (Jiang et al, 2025; Xu et al, 2008; Yang et al, 2017). In PD-L1, basic residues contribute to membrane retention by shielding ubiquitination sites, thereby enhancing surface stability (Wen et al, 2021). These observations indicate that BRS motifs may serve as structural elements critical for spatial organization and signal integration of membrane receptors (Harayama and Riezman, 2018; Wang et al, 2014).

  Building on the above analyses, this study systematically investigates the functional role of the OX40 intracellular domain, with particular focus on the membrane-proximal basic residue rich sequence (BRS) and its contribution to membrane anchoring and signal transduction. Structural and functional analyses revealed that the conserved arginine residues R241 and R242 mediate specific recognition of negatively charged phospholipids on the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane, stabilizing a membrane anchored conformation of residues 236–242. This BRS motif contributes to formation of a membrane-proximal interface with the C-terminal domain of TRAF2, enabling its membrane localized recruitment and promoting formation of a membrane-proximal OX40-TRAF2 complex essential for NF-κB activation. Mutagenesis of R241 and R242 to neutral residues markedly impaired phospholipid binding and TRAF2 association, resulting in an almost complete loss of downstream signaling. Extending this mechanism to other TNFR family costimulatory receptors, including 4-1BB and CD40, revealed that conserved basic residues within their juxtamembrane regions similarly support TRAF-mediated NF-κB activation, indicating that BRS dependent membrane anchoring represents a broadly conserved signaling strategy within the TNFR superfamily.

  This work delineates a previously unrecognized mechanism by which the OX40 BRS coordinates membrane lipid engagement and TRAF2 docking to facilitate signal initiation (Ye et al, 1999). These findings identify the BRS as a second, functionally distinct signaling interface that cooperates with the canonical C-terminal PxQxx TRAF-binding motif, thereby expanding the current model of TNFR signal integration (Ceccarelli et al, 2017). This conceptual advance provides a mechanistic framework for the rational design of OX40 targeting biologics and CAR-T constructs, emphasizing the necessity of incorporating both canonical and membrane anchoring motifs for effective immune modulation.


## Results

### OX40 juxtamembrane domain interactions with anionic phospholipids
  Cellular membranes exhibit distinct phospholipid compositions, with intracellular membranes being particularly enriched in anionic phospholipids such as phosphatidylserine (PS), phosphatidylinositol (PI), and phosphatidylglycerol (PG) (Harayama and Riezman, 2018). These negatively charged lipids play pivotal roles in stabilizing membrane receptors and orchestrating signal transduction cascades (Singh et al, 2024; Skotland and Sandvig, 2019; Wang et al, 2014; Yen et al, 2018). Sequence analysis revealed that the intracellular juxtamembrane domain of OX40 contains evolutionarily conserved basic residue arginine (R241/R242) and a polar tyrosine (Y238) with elevated pKa (Appendix Fig. S1A). To investigate potential lipid-protein interactions, we expressed recombinant peptides spanning OX40 residues 236–277, encompassing both juxtamembrane and cytosolic regions, purified in E.coli to >95% homogeneity, and validated by MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry (Appendix Fig. S1B–D).

  Uniformly 15N/13C-labeled OX40236-277 samples were analyzed using solution-state NMR spectroscopy, yielding complete backbone assignments, except for four proline residues (P247, P248, P253, P254) (Fig. 1A). To simulate T cell membrane environments, bicelle systems (q = 0.8) containing either zwitterionic DMPC or anionic DMPG were titrated into the NMR samples. Notably, significant chemical shift perturbations (CSPs) were observed exclusively upon titration with DMPG bicelles, whereas no CSPs were detected with DMPC bicelles (Fig. 1B; Appendix Fig. S1E). Mapping of the DMPG-induced CSPs revealed a critical interaction hotspot spanning residues 236–242, a structurally conserved linker that bridges the transmembrane helix and the cytoplasmic signaling loops. This region, which includes three polar residues (Y238, R241, R242), is likely involved in mediating electrostatic interactions. To dissect residue specific contributions, alanine or serine substitutions were introduced: Y238A (Y-A), R241S/R242S (2R-S), and the triple mutant Y238A/R241S/R242S. CSP analysis of the remaining residues (A236, L237, L239, L240) during DMPG titration revealed that the R241/R242 mutations significantly attenuated lipid-induced perturbations, while Y238A resulted in moderate effects.   We then conducted paramagnetic relaxation enhancement (PRE) experiments using 16-doxylstearic acid (16-DSA) to provide direct evidence of membrane partitioning. OX40236-277 exhibited pronounced PRE effects at residues 236–242 upon DMPG incorporation, such as Y238/L240, indicating deep insertion of the N-terminal juxtamembrane segment into the lipid bilayer. In contrast, minimal PRE amplitudes at the C-terminus confirmed aqueous exposure, such as T261/L274, establishing a clear boundary between the membrane embedded and cytosolic domains (Fig. 1D; Appendix Fig. S2E). Overall, we conclude that the juxtamembrane domain of OX40 specifically interacts with negatively charged phospholipids, primarily mediated by two conserved arginine residues.


### Membrane embedded dynamics of the OX40 236–242 motif
  To corroborate the membrane partitioning mechanism, we performed deuterium decoupled nuclear Overhauser effect (NOE) spectroscopy on perdeuterated OX40236-277 samples reconstituted in DMPG bicelles (q = 0.8). Strategic deuteration of both the protein amide and alkyl groups or DH6PC eliminated spurious background signals, enabling unambiguous detection of lipid-protein interactions. Striking NOE correlations (< 5 Å) were observed between the acyl chains of DMPG and six consecutive residues (A236-R241), supporting insertion into, or close proximity to, the lipid acyl chain region (Fig. 2A).   To complement NMR findings, we established a fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) assay probing real time membrane insertion dynamics. NHS-rhodamine labeled OX40236-277 (randomly conjugated to primary amines and lysine side chains) was reconstituted in DMPG liposome containing ~10% NBD-PE as FRET donor. Upon 463 nm excitation, wild-type (WT) samples exhibited strong emission at 593 nm (FRET efficiency = 0.55 ± 0.05), indicative of close proximity between the 236 and 242 motif and lipid acyl chains (Fig. 2B,C). Mutation of juxtamembrane arginine residues (R241S/R242S) reduced FRET efficiency by 25%, whereas Y238A showed no significant attenuation. This dichotomy suggests R241/R242 mediate electrostatic membrane docking. Introducing additional mutations (Y238A/R241S/R242S/R245S) shows no FRET signals difference with R241S/R242S, confirming cooperative engagement of the entire 236–242 cassette in membrane partitioning.

  Complementary relaxation analyses (T1, T2, heteronuclear NOE) revealed profound dynamic restructuring upon lipid binding. The 236–242 motif exhibited a fivefold increase in R2 (20.6 s−1 in DMPG bicelle vs. 3.2 s−1 in solution) and elevated heteronuclear NOE values (0.52 vs. 0.12), indicating transition to a conformationally rigid state characteristic of membrane-anchored domains (Fig. 2D). These collective data establish that anionic phospholipids stabilize the juxtamembrane region through both electrostatic attraction and hydrophobic burial.

  By integrating NMR spectroscopy with in vitro FRET assays, we identified R241 and R242 as critical determinants of membrane partitioning within the OX40 juxtamembrane domain. These evolutionarily conserved arginine residues facilitate dual stabilization through two mechanisms: electrostatic steering via coordination with anionic phospholipid headgroups, and hydrophobic insertion mediated by adjacent aliphatic residues (A236, L237, L239, L240).


### R241/R242 driven membrane anchoring enhances NF-κB signaling without reducing receptor surface expression or ligand-induced clustering
  Juxtamembrane basic residue rich sequences (BRS) have been shown to play critical roles in various membrane proteins, contributing either to receptor stability, as observed in PD-L1 (Wen et al, 2021), or to downstream signaling, as in CD3ε (Xu et al, 2008). Given the importance of arginine residues R241 and R242 in anchoring the 236–242 motif of OX40 to the membrane, we further investigated whether this localization affects receptor turnover or signaling efficacy in a cellular context.

  To evaluate receptor turnover, we performed cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays in two independent T-cell-derived systems. WT OX40 or the R241S, R242S and R241S/R242S double mutant (2R-S) were stably expressed in Jurkat cells, which have low basal OX40 expression, and in HuT78 sgOX40 cells, in which endogenous OX40 was depleted and reconstituted with the corresponding WT or mutant receptors (Appendix Fig. S3A–C). After blockade of protein synthesis with CHX, Flag-OX40 levels were monitored over time by immunoblotting and normalized to tubulin (Fig. 3A–D). In both Jurkat and HuT78 sgOX40 reconstituted cells, the BRS mutants showed slower loss of Flag-OX40 than WT OX40, with the 2R-S mutant displaying the most delayed turnover. Thus, mutation of R241/R242 does not destabilize OX40 or accelerate receptor degradation.   We next examined whether the mutations affect OX40 oligomerization at the plasma membrane under ligand bound conditions. A bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay was established by fusing the N- and C-terminal fragments of Venus to the cytoplasmic tail of OX40, enabling fluorescence reconstitution upon receptor self-association (Fig. 3E). Because the assay requires paired constructs, BiFC was performed for WT and the double mutant 2R-S, the variant that exhibited the strongest functional defect. Upon stimulation with the naturally occurring trimeric OX40L ectodomain (OX40L), both WT and 2R-S showed robust Venus reconstitution, with comparable fluorescence intensity (Fig. 3F), indicating that R241/R242 are not required for ligand-induced receptor clustering. These results indicate that substitution of R241/R242 does not impair the ability of OX40 to cluster at the plasma membrane.

  Having excluded reduced effects on receptor stability or clustering, we next evaluated signaling output. As a member of the TNFR superfamily, OX40 requires ligand-induced receptor multimerization to recruit TRAF adaptors and activate the canonical NF-κB pathway. To standardize receptor activation in cells, we stimulated HeLa NF-κB reporter lines expressing WT or mutant OX40 with TNC-scOX40L, a tenascin-C-fused single-chain OX40L agonist designed to promote multivalent receptor clustering (Appendix Fig. S3I–K). Similar tenascin-C-fused single-chain TNF ligand formats have previously been used to enhance TNFR family receptor activation, including in studies of TNFR2 signaling (Rauert et al, 2011; Rauert et al, 2010). Dose response analyses revealed that the single mutations R241S and R242S markedly reduced ligand-induced NF-κB activation. In contrast, the double mutant 2R-S completely failed to activate NF-κB across all ligand concentrations tested (Fig. 3H,I). ### Membrane anchoring by the OX40 basic residue rich sequence promotes efficient canonical NF-κB and PI3K-AKT signaling
  In the preceding section, we showed that mutations within the juxtamembrane basic residue rich sequence (BRS) of OX40 markedly attenuate downstream signaling. To define which signaling nodes are specifically affected, we systematically examined key molecular markers within OX40 mediated pathways. Upon ligand engagement, OX40 recruits TRAF2 to activate the IKK complex, leading to phosphorylation and degradation of IκBα, subsequent NF-κB activation, and promotion of T-cell proliferation and survival. In addition to this canonical cascade, previous studies have reported that OX40 signaling can also modulate the PI3K-AKT phosphorylation cascade (So et al, 2011; Song et al, 2005; Zhang et al, 2021) (Appendix Fig. S4A). To validate the functional readouts observed in the NF-κB luciferase reporter assay, we therefore assessed the phosphorylation status of critical downstream effectors by immunoblotting.

  We first analyzed Jurkat T cells stably expressing wild-type (WT) OX40. To determine an appropriate stimulation window, cells were stimulated with TNC-scOX40L for 0, 5, 10, 15, 30, 60 and 120 min, followed by immunoblotting for p-AKT, total AKT, p-IκBα, total IκBα, p-p65 and total p65. OX40-WT induced rapid phosphorylation of AKT, IκBα and p65, with the strongest or near-strongest activation observed at approximately 10 min after stimulation (Fig. 4A–D). Based on this kinetic profile, we selected the 10-min time point for direct comparison between WT and BRS mutant receptors. Under this condition, substitution of either R241 or R242 markedly reduced phosphorylation of AKT, IκBα and p65 compared with WT, and the double mutant R241S/R242S (2R-S) showed the strongest overall impairment (Fig. 4E–H).   To determine whether this effect was also observed in the HeLa reconstitution system used for the reporter assays, we performed the same kinetic and mutant comparison analyses in HeLa cells stably expressing OX40 (Appendix Fig. S4B,C). Similar to Jurkat cells, OX40-WT induced time dependent phosphorylation of AKT, IκBα and p65 after TNC-scOX40L stimulation, with robust activation detected around 10 min (Fig. 4I–L). Direct comparison at this time point showed that R241S, R242S and 2R-S all reduced phosphorylation of these downstream markers relative to WT (Fig. 4M–P). Thus, the signaling defects caused by BRS mutation are reproducible in both T-cell-derived and reconstituted cell systems.

  Taken together, these data show that the juxtamembrane arginines R241 and R242 are required for efficient early activation of canonical NF-κB and PI3K-AKT signaling. Combined with the preserved receptor surface expression, delayed rather than accelerated receptor turnover, and intact ligand-induced clustering described above, these results support a model in which BRS mediated membrane anchoring regulates productive OX40 signal initiation downstream of receptor engagement.


### Disruption of the juxtamembrane basic motif impairs membrane-proximal OX40-TRAF2 engagement
  Given that substitution of R241 and R242 markedly attenuates OX40 signaling despite preserved receptor surface expression, intact ligand-induced clustering, and delayed rather than accelerated receptor turnover, we next asked whether the juxtamembrane basic residue rich sequence (BRS) impairs downstream signaling by disrupting TRAF2 engagement. To visualize receptor–adaptor organization at the plasma membrane, we generated stable HeLa cell lines co-expressing OX40-GFP and TRAF2-mCherry. Flow cytometry confirmed comparable OX40-GFP and TRAF2-mCherry expression across WT and BRS mutant cell lines (Appendix Fig. S5A).

  Confocal imaging was then performed in the absence and presence of TNC-scOX40L stimulation. These results indicate that mutation of R241/R242 impairs membrane-proximal OX40-TRAF2 organization both before and after ligand stimulation.

  To determine whether the reduced colocalization reflects impaired receptor–adaptor complex formation, we performed co-immunoprecipitation assays using FLAG-tagged OX40 variants under both unstimulated and TNC-scOX40L stimulated conditions. Thus, the juxtamembrane BRS is best interpreted as a membrane-proximal regulatory element that promotes efficient and spatially constrained TRAF2 engagement, rather than as the sole TRAF2-binding determinant. Collectively, the imaging and co-immunoprecipitation data support a model in which R241/R242 mediated membrane anchoring facilitates productive OX40-TRAF2 complex assembly, providing a mechanistic explanation for the reduced NF-κB and PI3K-AKT signaling observed in BRS mutants.


### Membrane anchoring by the juxtamembrane basic motif creates a secondary TRAF2 contact site adjacent to the canonical PxQxx motif
  OX40 activates the NF-κB signaling cascade through engagement of its conserved C-terminal motif “PIQEE” with TRAF2, a mechanism broadly conserved among TNFR superfamily costimulatory receptors (Matsuzawa et al, 2008; Ye and Wu, 2000). In OX40, however, this motif is spatially separated from the membrane-proximal basic residues R241 and R242 by a ~ 30 residue flexible loop. Our cellular analyses showed that disrupting these basic residues severely impairs TRAF2 recruitment and complex formation despite an intact PIQEE motif, suggesting that membrane anchoring by the BRS contributes to TRAF2 engagement beyond the canonical interface.

  To dissect this mechanism at residue resolution, we recombinantly purified the TRAF2 C-terminal domain (TRAF2-C), known to specifically recognize the PIQEE motif, and established a membrane mimetic system in which the cytoplasmic tail of OX40 (OX40-CTD) was anchored to phosphatidylglycerol (PG) lipid nanodisc. Direct overlay of free OX40-CTD with PG-NDC associated OX40-CTD further confirmed chemical shift perturbations around the BRS region upon nanodisc association (Appendix Fig. S6B). In contrast, when OX40-CTD was lipid nanodisc anchored, TRAF2-C binding induced additional signal broadening in residues surrounding the BRS, indicating the presence of a secondary TRAF2 engaging region in the membrane-proximal region (Fig. 6B; Appendix Fig. S6C).Figure 6Membrane anchored R241/R242 residues potentiate TRAF2 recruitment in cooperation with the canonical PxQxx motif.(A, B) Residue-resolved 1H-15N HSQC peak intensity ratios (I/I0) of 15N-labeled OX40 cytosolic tail (residues 236–277) upon titration with TRAF2 in the absence (A) or presence (B) of phosphatidylglycerol nanodiscs (PG-NDC).   We further assessed this interaction by incrementally titrating TRAF2-C into the PG-nanodisc bound OX40-CTD. Peaks corresponding to both the canonical TRAF2 interface (E265) and the BRS-proximal region (Y238) exhibited concentration dependent broadening and intensity loss, whereas residues outside these sites (G257, I277) showed only minimal changes (Fig. 6C). These observations suggest that membrane bound OX40-CTD promotes TRAF2 membrane targeting through cooperative interactions at two spatially distinct regions (Fig. 6D). Consistent with this model, substitution of R241 and R242 abolished signal perturbations in the juxtamembrane region, confirming that basic-residue mediated membrane anchoring is essential for formation of the proximal TRAF2 interface (Appendix Fig. S6D–H).

  To directly quantify this effect on binding affinity, we performed bio-layer interferometry using PG-nanodisc-reconstituted OX40-CTD. TRAF2 bound OX40 WT with a dissociation constant of KD = 1.27 μM, whereas the double mutant OX40 2R-S (R241S/R242S) displayed a dramatically weakened affinity (KD = 117.1 μM) (Fig. 6E,F). To further distinguish the contribution of the BRS from that of the canonical PIQEE motif, we analyzed an OX40-CTD mutant in which the PIQEE motif was disrupted. Mutation of the PIQEE motif reduced but did not abolish TRAF2 binding, with an apparent KD of 39.98 μM, whereas the combined OX40-2R-S+3Mut construct showed little detectable TRAF2 binding (Appendix Fig. S6I,J). These data indicate that the membrane-proximal BRS provides a PIQEE independent contribution to TRAF2 engagement, while both the BRS and the canonical PIQEE motif are required for maximal OX40-TRAF2 binding.

  The physiological relevance of this mechanism was assessed using fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) measurements in cells. Full length TRAF2 fused to monomeric teal fluorescent protein (mTFP) was stably expressed in cells expressing OX40-WT, R241S, R242S, or the double mutant 2R-S, and plasma membranes were labeled with the lipophilic dye R18. FRET efficiencies measured by donor dequenching revealed a marked reduction in membrane-proximal TRAF2 localization in all three BRS mutants, with the double mutant exhibiting the strongest loss of FRET relative to WT (Fig. 6G,H). These results indicate that R241 and R242 are required to maintain TRAF2 proximity to the plasma membrane, a prerequisite for efficient downstream NF-κB activation.

  Together, these findings delineate a noncanonical mechanism in which the membrane-proximal BRS functions not only as a lipid anchoring module but also as a spatial organizer that positions TRAF2 in close proximity to its primary PIQEE binding site, thereby enhancing the apparent affinity and productive assembly of the receptor–adaptor complex. This secondary membrane dependent interface provides a PIQEE independent contribution but cooperates with, rather than replaces, the classical PxQxx motif, revealing an additional regulatory layer governing TNFR signal transduction.

Besides OX40, additional costimulatory receptors, including 4-1BB, CD27, TNFR2, and CD40, also activate NF-κB signaling through TRAF engagement, typically via a conserved intracellular PxQxx motif.   Building on our findings in OX40, we considered whether membrane-proximal BRS elements might similarly support NF-κB activation across other costimulatory TNFRs. A sequence alignment of representative family members revealed that these juxtamembrane regions are consistently enriched in basic residues, primarily arginine and lysine, positioned immediately adjacent to the transmembrane domain. To explore their functional significance, we established NF-κB luciferase reporter HeLa cell lines expressing mutant forms of 4-1BB and CD40 (Appendix Fig. S7). Upon stimulation with their respective ligands, WT 4-1BB and CD40 each exhibited robust, dose-dependent canonical NF-κB activation (Fig. 7B,C). Sigmoidal response curves were observed across a broad concentration range, and EC50 values were determined for each receptor, confirming that all constructs were functionally competent and responsive to ligand stimulation in this system. Flow cytometry confirmed comparable cell surface Flag binding among WT and BRS-mutant 4-1BB and CD40 constructs (Appendix Fig. S7), indicating that reduced reporter activation was not caused by reduced receptor surface expression.

  We first examined 4-1BB, a costimulatory TNFR that, like OX40, contains conserved juxtamembrane arginine residues immediately downstream of the transmembrane domain. Mutation of either R215 or R217 markedly reduced ligand-induced NF-κB activation, and combined mutation of both residues nearly abolished the reporter response (Fig. 7D). We next tested whether a similar principle applies to CD40, whose juxtamembrane BRS is enriched in lysine rather than arginine residues. Mutation of four conserved lysines in this region strongly impaired CD40L-induced NF-κB activation (Fig. 7E). Together, these results indicate that membrane-proximal basic residues, whether arginine- or lysine-enriched, support efficient ligand-induced NF-κB activation across distinct costimulatory TNFRs. Although further receptor specific studies will be required to define the precise adaptor recruitment mechanism for each receptor, these data support a broader role for juxtamembrane basic motifs in TNFR signal regulation. This principle may operate alongside canonical TRAF binding motifs and should be considered when evaluating TNFR signaling or designing synthetic immunoreceptors.


## Discussion
  This study identifies a membrane anchoring mechanism within the juxtamembrane region of OX40 that is indispensable for downstream signaling. Integrated NMR spectroscopy, FRET analysis, and functional assays demonstrate that two evolutionarily conserved arginines, R241 and R242, drive specific interactions with anionic phospholipids and neighboring aliphatic residues, stabilizing the 236–242 segment in a membrane-inserted conformation. This lipid engagement imposes conformational rigidity on the cytoplasmic tail without reducing receptor surface expression or impairing ligand-induced oligomerization. Disrupting this anchoring mechanism by point mutations markedly attenuates NF-κB and PI3K-AKT activation in HeLa and Jurkat cell contexts, establishing membrane-proximal basic residues as essential determinants of productive OX40 signaling.

  A key mechanistic insight emerging from this work is that the juxtamembrane basic residue rich sequence (BRS) forms an auxiliary, membrane dependent TRAF2 contact surface that operates in cooperation with the canonical C-terminal PxQxx motif. Residue resolved perturbation mapping of membrane-reconstituted OX40-CTD shows selective broadening within 236–242 upon TRAF2-C binding, consistent with the emergence of a secondary interaction region that is absent in solution. BLI measurements further indicate that disruption of R241 and R242 reduces TRAF2 affinity by approximately two orders of magnitude, and live cell FRET reveals impaired membrane-proximal TRAF2 localization in BRS mutants. Together, these data support a cooperative dual-site model in which membrane anchoring not only stabilizes receptor topology but also helps position the distal PIQEE motif for efficient engagement with the canonical TRAF2-C binding groove, thereby enhancing binding avidity and spatial organization at the membrane interface (Fig. 8). In this model, the BRS first engages negatively charged phospholipids at the inner leaflet and creates a membrane-proximal environment that favors TRAF2-C engagement, while the PIQEE motif remains the primary canonical TRAF2-binding element. Notably, BRS mutations only modestly reduce bulk TRAF2 association detected by co-immunoprecipitation, consistent with retention of the canonical PIQEE motif. However, proximity-sensitive assays reveal impaired membrane-proximal engagement, indicating that the BRS enhances spatially constrained adaptor assembly rather than serving as the primary binding interface.   The broader significance of this membrane dependent regulatory module is reinforced by its conservation across multiple TNFR family costimulatory receptors. Systematic mutagenesis in 4-1BB and CD40 reveals that mutation of juxtamembrane basic residues diminishes NF-κB activation, phenocopying the OX40 BRS mutant phenotype and supporting a role for these motifs in efficient ligand-induced NF-κB activation. Notably, these basic clusters include both arginine- and lysine-rich motifs, suggesting that enrichment of positively charged residues is a common feature associated with membrane anchoring and adaptor positioning, although the detailed contribution of individual side chains remains to be determined. Together, these features support a conserved dual-site regulatory model within the TNFR superfamily, operating alongside the canonical PxQxx motif to fine-tune signaling strength.

  Our findings refine current models of TNFR signal integration by establishing the BRS as a cooperative, membrane anchored signaling element. This conceptual advance underscore the importance of membrane composition and lipid engagement in shaping receptor architecture and adaptor recruitment. Beyond mechanistic insight, these results have direct translational implications. Current CAR-T constructs incorporating TNFR-derived intracellular domains predominantly emphasize the PxQxx motif as the principal driver of signal transduction. The present study suggests that failure to recapitulate native membrane anchoring features may limit TRAF docking efficiency and downstream signaling output. Rational incorporation of conserved BRS elements into synthetic receptors could enhance activation thresholds, modify tonic signaling, improve T cell persistence, and increase cytotoxicity in adoptive cell therapy. Furthermore, the membrane dependent dual-site mechanism may provide a generalizable framework for tuning receptor sensitivity or creating microenvironment selective signaling responses, especially in tissues with heterogeneous anionic lipid distribution.

  While these data define the BRS as a membrane anchored module that couple lipid engagement to TRAF2 recruitment, the atomic determinants of OX40-TRAF2 recognition remain unresolved. In particular, the present data do not determine whether TRAF2-C uses two fully separate binding surfaces to recognize the BRS and PIQEE motifs, or whether membrane anchoring promotes a composite or dynamic interface. Future structural studies, such as cryo-EM analysis of the OX40L-OX40-TRAF2 complex, together with crosslinking mass spectrometry or molecular dynamics simulations, will be essential to delineate the geometry of this dual-site arrangement and to reveal how receptor clustering and lipid heterogeneity influence higher order signaling assemblies. Such efforts will further clarify how membrane-anchoring modules integrate with canonical motifs to shape adaptor organization and diversify signaling outcomes across TNFR family receptors.

### Assembly of bicelle based membrane mimetic systems
  Long chain lipids 1,2-dimyristoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DMPC; Avanti, #850345) and 1,2-dimyristoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoglycerol (DMPG; Avanti, #840445) were each mixed with the short chain lipid 1,2-dihexanoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DH6PC; 6:0 PC; Avanti, #850305) at a 1:1.25 molar ratio after dissolution in chloroform. The mixtures were dried under a nitrogen stream to form thin films and lyophilized overnight. Lipid films were rehydrated in phosphate buffer (25 mM sodium phosphate, 100 mM NaCl, pH 6.5) to generate bicelles. OX40-CTD was incorporated at a 2:1 protein to bicelle molar ratio and incubated overnight. The bicelle size parameter q = [long chain lipid]/[DH6PC] was adjusted, when necessary, by adding DH6PC or lowering its concentration through dialysis. NMR confirmed q ≈ 0.8, which yields bilayer like topology with a planar region radius of ~34 Å.

For sequence specific backbone resonance assignments of the OX40-CTD with and without bicelle, a series of TROSY enhanced triple resonance experiments were employed. These included HNCA, HN(CO)CA, HN(CA)CO, HNCO, HNCACB, and HN(CO)CACB (Kay et al, 2011; Salzmann et al, 1999). All spectra were recorded on a uniformly labeled (15N, 13C, 85% 2H) OX40-CTD sample at 30 °C and a proton Larmor frequency of 600 MHz.

### Intermolecular NOE mapping of lipid interactions
  To determine the position of dynamic OX40-CTD in the lipid bilayer, protein-lipid distance constraints were obtained from 3D 15N-edited NOESY-TROSY-HSQC spectra recorded using 15N-labelled protein samples in bilayer liposomes composed of conventional DMPG and deuterated DH6PC (q = 0.8) (τNOE = 200 ms). This spectrum enables the measurement of exclusive protein-lipid NOE between the peptide chain main chain amide protons and DMPG. 2H-labelled protein samples were recorded in a bilayer lipid bilayer composed of conventional DMPG and deuterated DH6PC (q = 0.8), enabling the measurement of exclusive protein-lipid NOE between the peptide chain main chain amide protons and the aliphatic protons of DMPG. Subsequently, protein-lipid NOEs were grouped into three categories based on the position of the protein relative to the lipid. To define the position of OX40-CTD within the lipid bilayer, protein-lipid distance restraints were derived from three-dimensional 15N-edited NOESY TROSY-HSQC spectra (τNOE = 200 ms) acquired with perdeuterated 15N-labeled OX40-CTD reconstituted into bicelles composed of conventional DMPG and deuterated DH6PC (q = 0.8). ### Preparation and reconstitution of PG nanodiscs
  1,2-Dimyristoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoglycerol (DMPG) was dissolved in chloroform, dried under a stream of nitrogen to form a thin lipid film, and further vacuum-dried to remove residual solvent. The lipid film was rehydrated and solubilized in buffer containing sodium deoxycholate. Solubilized DMPG was mixed with membrane scaffold protein MSP1D1 at a molar ratio of 80:1 (DMPG:MSP1D1) and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Detergent removal was achieved by dialysis at room temperature against buffer identical to that used for protein experiments. Following dialysis, samples were clarified by high-speed centrifugation and purified by size-exclusion chromatography using FPLC. Nanodisc-containing fractions were collected and quantified.

  For reconstitution, purified OX40-CTD was mixed with PG nanodiscs and incubated at room temperature for 15 min prior to NMR measurements.


### Statistical analysis
  Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software. Data are presented as mean ± SD unless otherwise indicated in the figure legends. The number of independent biological replicates, independent protein preparations, or analyzed cells is indicated in the corresponding figure legends. For comparisons between two groups, independent samples t tests, also referred to as unpaired two-tailed Student’s t tests were used. For comparisons among more than two groups with a single control group, ordinary one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test was used. For experiments involving two independent variables, including CHX chase assays, ordinary two-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test was used to compare each experimental group with the corresponding WT or control group across the indicated time points. For dose–response reporter assays, EC50 values were generated by nonlinear regression using a four-parameter logistic model; statistical comparisons between WT and mutant receptors were performed at the indicated ligand concentrations using the tests specified in the corresponding figure legends. Exact or adjusted P values are provided in the corresponding figure legends. P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Significance was denoted as follows: ns, not significant; *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001.
