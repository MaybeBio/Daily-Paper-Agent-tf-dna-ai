# Multi-omics integration identifies macrophage senescence driven by the RUNX1-P53 axis as a key mechanism in diabetic foot ulcer


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10142-026-01946-5.


## Introduction
  A diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) is a major chronic complication of diabetes mellitus (DM). It is primarily characterized by foot ulcers, infection, and deep tissue destruction, which can lead to non-traumatic lower limb amputation in severe cases, making DFU one of the principal causes of hospitalization and mortality among patients with diabetes (Bandyk 2018, Pena et al. 2020). Notably, epidemiological data on DFU are closely associated with age and diabetes duration. DFU is more common in elderly patients, and the risk of developing DFU and undergoing amputation increases significantly with age (Astasio-Picado et al. 2022). A systematic review observed major amputation prevalence rates of 1.6% for ages 18–44, 3.4% for ages 45–64, and 3.6% for those over 65 years (Edmonds et al. 2021). As the life expectancy of patients with diabetes increases, they are exposed to hyperglycemia and other metabolic abnormalities for longer periods. These factors act persistently over time, accelerating DFU onset and progression (Singh et al. 2005).

  The diabetic foot pathophysiology is extremely complex, involving a process driven by the synergistic interaction of multiple factors. Core pathogenic elements include diabetic neuropathy, peripheral arterial disease, infections, and a chronic inflammatory state (Bus et al. 2024). These factors contribute to severe impairment of wound healing. Within this complex pathological network, chronic inflammation is a consequence and a key driver of disease progression. Aging significantly increases the risk of chronic diseases, including diabetes (Li et al. 2024). With advancing age, the function of the immune system gradually declines, a process called immunosenescence (Liu et al. 2023). A key aspect of immunosenescence is “inflammaging”, a persistent, low-level, non-infectious inflammation associated with the development of various age-related diseases, including DM (Liu et al. 2023). Pathogen clearance is reduced by 30%–50% in elderly diabetic patients compared to their younger counterparts (Pawelec 2018). Macrophages are key effector cells in the innate immune system that are essential for initiating, progressing, and resolving inflammation and facilitating tissue repair (Lloberas et al. 2019). However, under pathological conditions, including aging and diabetes, macrophages can enter a dysfunctional senescent state. Senescent macrophages exhibit multiple functional impairments, including significantly reduced phagocytic, migratory, and chemotactic capacities, rendering them unable to perform effective clearance and repair functions (Moss et al. 2024). Reduced macrophage phagocytosis hinders biofilm matrix penetration, causing persistent infections in up to 75% of DFU in individuals ˃ 75 years (Supardy and Kumar 2025). In the diabetic foot’s unique microenvironment, marked by hyperglycemia, hyperlipidemia, and oxidative stress, macrophage senescence is significantly accelerated and intensified (Wang et al. 2021, Basu Mallik et al. 2018). Studies have revealed that hyperglycemia can drive inflammatory aging and senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) secretion in macrophages by glucose transporter 1-mediated reprogramming of glucose metabolism, activating the mechanistic target of rapamycin kinase (mTOR) and nuclear factor kappa B subunit 1 (NF-κB) signaling pathways(Wang et al. 2021).

  We performed a multi-omics integrative analysis, incorporating transcriptomics and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), to study the role and mechanisms of cellular senescence in DFU pathogenesis. Our results initially demonstrated a significant upregulation of senescence in the DFU tissues. Further single-cell analysis revealed that macrophages harbored the highest senescent cell proportion, and these senescent macrophages exhibited enhanced intercellular communication, particularly through the CXCL signaling axis. By integrating genome-wide association study (GWAS) data with transcriptomic profiles, we constructed a novel macrophage senescence score (MSS) that proved an effective predictor of DFU. Through machine learning modeling, the transcription factor RUNX1 was identified as a key feature in the MSS. Subsequent in vitro functional experiments confirmed that RUNX1 promotes high glucose-induced macrophage senescence. Mechanistically, RUNX1 and enhanced P53 acetylation, leading to increased P53 protein stability and the subsequent activation of the downstream senescence program.

### Bulk RNA sequence integration and preprocessing
  Data from five DFU datasets, comprising 36 DFU and 42 NDFU samples from GSE134431, GSE143735, GSE144441, GSE154556, and GSE199939 (Table 1 for details), were integrated by using the sva package in R software to eliminate the batch effect. Data normalization and differential expression analyses were performed using the DEseq2 package. To ensure that downstream co-expression modules and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) reflected genuine biological variations rather than sample-specific noise, a two-step quantitative quality control was implemented to eliminate sample outliers.First, unsupervised hierarchical clustering was performed, and a static height cutoff of H = 20,000 was applied to the dendrogram to remove visually isolated branches (sFig. 2 A). Second, to mathematically validate this filtration, we calculated the standardized sample connectivity Z.k. Samples with a Z.k < -2.5 were identified as significant technical outliers and excluded from downstream analysis (sFig. 2B), ensuring high homogeneity and reliability of the co-expression networ. Adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |logFC| ≥ 1 were defined as the thresholds for the DEGs.


### Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) and gene set variation analysis (GSVA)
  We performed GSEA and GSVA to better understand the biological processes associated with DFU. The GSVA package was used to perform GSVA and single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA). The clusterProfiler package was used to conduct GSEA. The senescence-related gene set was obtained from the studies by Wyles et al. (Wyles et al. 2025) (SenSkin) and Saul et al. (Saul et al. 2022) (SenMayo).


### Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)
  WGCNA was performed to identify modules of highly correlated genes, summarize the interconnections between modules and associations with external sample traits, and identify candidate biomarkers. To ensure a scale-free topology network R2 = 0.851, a soft-thresholding power of β = 18 was determined using the pickSoftThreshold function. The adjacency matrix was then transformed into a Topological Overlap Matrix (TOM). Co-expression modules were constructed using hierarchical average linkage clustering with a minimum module size (minModuleSize) set to 100. To merge modules with highly similar expression profiles, a dynamic tree cut algorithm was employed with a merge cut height (mergeCutHeight) threshold of 0.25, corresponding to a module correlation of 0.75. Using the WGCNA package, we constructed a WGCNA to identify modules most relevant to senescence in patients with sencense. We preprocessed the sample data and removed the outliers. The “MEturquoise” modules, which exhibited the highest correlation with senescence, were selected as key modules for further analysis.


### Enrichment analyses of DEGs
  We used the clusterProfiler package to analyze gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathways, to elucidate the main biological functions of selected genes.


### Analysis of scRNA-seq
  Three public scRNA-seq datasets (GSE165816, GSE245703, and GSE268834; details in Table 1) were used to identify senescent cells in patients with DFU. The SCP package was used to process scRNA-seq data. The “RunCellQC” function was used for cell quality control. Specifically, we retained cells with detected gene numbers (nFeature_RNA) exceed 1000, and unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts (nCount_RNA) greater than 5000. Cells with a mitochondrial gene expression fraction exceeding 20% were excluded. Double cell was removed by DoubletFinder. After removing low-quality cells, 916,686 cells were included in the subsequent analysis. “Integration_SCP” function was applied to integration, normalize, scale, dimension reduction, identified clusters, and cluster visualization for all the cells. To eliminate potential batch effects across different datasets, we applied the Harmony algorithm. The specific marker genes for each cell type were used for cluster annotation. Cluster annotation was performed using specific marker genes for each cell type. The AUCell package was applied to quantify the senescence score for all cell types based on the corresponding SenMayo and SenSkin gene sets. Given the inherent limitations of capturing classical senescence markers via high-throughput single-cell RNA sequencing, identifying true senescent populations computationally requires comprehensive transcriptomic signatures. Therefore, we employed the AUCell algorithm with the well-established SenMayo and SenSkin gene sets. This pathway-level scoring strategy mitigates the dropout effect of individual genes and provides a highly robust, biologically grounded approximation of the senescence phenotype in complex tissue microenvironments.The merger of SenMayo-positive and SenSkin-positive cells was defined as a senescent cell. The “CellChat” package was used to analyze and depict cell-cell interactions and signaling pathways based on scRNA-seq data. Statistical significance of the inferred intercellular communications was evaluated using a permutation test with 1,000 permutations. Only ligand-receptor interactions with a P-value < 0.01 were considered statistically significant and retained for downstream prioritization. Macrophages were selected for subsequent analyses. The ‘RunDEtest’ function from the SCP package was used to identify DEG between aging macrophages and macrophages. The “RunSlingshot” function from the SCP package was used to conduct a single-cell trajectory analysis. The “RunDynamicFeatures” function was used to calculate dynamic features for each lineage in the macrophage subset.


### Senescence-relevant genes identification in scRNA-seq
  We used single-cell disease relevance score (scDRS) analysis to identify genes associated with macrophage senescence (Zhang et al. 2022). This computational framework assesses disease risk at the single-cell level by integrating GWAS data with transcriptomic profiles. Senescence risk genes are protein-coding loci situated within ± 10 kb of identified risk variants from each GWAS dataset (mvAge, PhenoAge, GrimAge, Hannu, and DFU-UKBB).


### Predictive model establishment for DFU
  To evaluate the performance of MSS in predicting DFU, a meta dataset was created by combining five DFU datasets (GSE134431, GSE143735, GSE144441, GSE154556, and GSE199939) after removing the batch effect using the sva package. Patients in the meta-dataset were divided into training set (NDF = 28, DF = 27) and validation sets (NDF = 7, DF = 6) in an 8:2 ratio, with the other four datasets (GSE68183, GSE269483, GSE272918, and GSE271701) serving as testing sets (NDF = 3, DF = 19). To construct a robust predictive model for DFU, we evaluated five classical machine learning algorithms: K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Naive Bayes, Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Decision Tree (DT). These algorithms were strategically selected to represent a diverse array of foundational learning paradigms (instance-based, probabilistic, ensemble, margin-based, and rule-based). Such classical approaches are widely recognized as highly suitable for transcriptomic datasets, as they effectively manage high-dimensional feature spaces while mitigating the overfitting risks commonly encountered when applying deep learning to biomedical cohorts with limited sample sizes. Based on the MSS, this ML algorithms were used for model training. Five-fold cross-validation (CV) resampling was used to ensure robustness. The Mlr3 package facilitated the training and prediction of all ML algorithms. The final MSS model was selected based on its top performance through testing. For the RF algorithm, the depth of trees (max.depth) was evaluated across [3 to 10], and the optimal max_features was determined between ‘sqrt’ and ‘log2’. For the SVM, grid search optimized the penalty parameter C within [0.1, 1, 10, 100] and the kernel function [“polynomial”, “radial” and “sigmoid”]. For KNN, the number of neighbors (n_neighbors) was tuned among [3 to 20]. Model performance was systematically assessed using Accuracy, Sensitivity, Specificity, and the F1-score. Where applicable, 95% Confidence Intervals (CIs) for the area under the curve (AUC), Accuracy, Sensitivity, Specificity, and the F1-score were calculated using the DeLong method to provide a robust measure of predictive certainty.


### Complex predictive model explanation
  The DALEX package was used to improve the interpretability of our final model. The DALEX package offers multiple explainers to elucidate the connection between input variables and model outputs. The DALEX package facilitates the comprehension of variable significance, explores variable-outcome relationships, and evaluates the impact of each variable on individual predictions.


### Cell culture and transfection
  Raw 264.7 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 ℃ in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator. For transfection, the cells were transfected with specified expression plasmid combinations using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen). For stable transfections, the suitable lentivirus was introduced into the supernatant, and the medium was altered within 6–8 h. Cells were treated with 2 µg/mL puromycin to select for those with the resistance gene, confirming successful transfection.


### Senescence-associated β‐galactosidase (SA‐β‐gal) assay
  The SA-β‐gal activity was assessed using a senescence detection kit (C0602, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) following the protocol provided by the manufacturer. SA-β-gal-positive cells were quantified, and their percentages were calculated.


### Co-immunoprecipitation
  Equal amounts of cell lysates were pre-absorbed with protein G-Sepharose beads (Amersham Biosciences) at 4 ℃ for 1 h, and the precleared lysates were incubated with the indicated antibodies at 4 ℃ for 2 h, followed by incubation with protein G-Sepharose beads for an additional 1 h at 4 ℃. The immune complexes were then washed extensively with lysis buffer, eluted by boiling in sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) sample buffer for 5 min, and subjected to immunoblot analysis.


### Western blotting
  Proteins from cell lysates were separated on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Merck Millipore, USA). The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with primary antibodies and then treated with secondary antibodies. The antibodies used are listed in Table 2.

### Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
  SASP concentrations in the culture medium were measured using an ELISA Kit (interleukin [IL]-1β: EK201B, IL-6: EK206HS, MCP-1: EK287, MULTI SCIENCES, Zhejiang, China) following the manufacturer’s guidelines.


### Immunofluorescence staining
  Cells were seeded onto 8-well chamber slides (SAINING, #1093000, China) and allowed to adhere overnight. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with Triton X-100, and blocked with a bovine serum albumin solution. The cells were incubated with primary and fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies. The cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI-containing mounting medium. Fluorescent images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica, Germany).


### Statistical analysis
  All in vitro experiments including Western blotting and ELISAwere performed independently at least three biological triplicates, with technical replicates included in each independent experiment to ensure reproducibility. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2). Statistical analyses of Western blotting and SA-β-gal results were normalized by the control group. The differences between the two groups were assessed using Student’s t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. P-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) procedure to control the False Discovery Rate (FDR). A p or adjust p ˂ 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all comparisons.


## Results

### Senescence-related pathways were upregulated in DFU
  The workflow of this study is presented in sFig. 1A. To clarify the role of senescence in DFU pathogenesis, we initially assessed the level of SASP markers, including CDKN1A, CDKN2A, CXCL8, MMP3, TGFB1, and IL1B in GSE134431 and GSE199939 datasets. The normalized count of CDKN1A (effect size = 0.77, p.adjust = 0.00044), CXCL8 (effect size = 0.664, p.adjust = 0.0027), TGFB1 (effect size = 0.553, p.adjust = 0.012), and IL1B (effect size = 0.727, p.adjust = 0.00098) was significantly increased in GSE134431, while CXCL8 (effect size = 0.630, p.adjust = 0.0043), MMP3 (effect size = 0.753, p.adjust = 0.00064), TGFB1 (effect size = 0.845, p.adjust = 0.00012), and IL1B (effect size = 0.676, p.adjust = 0.0022) were upregulated in GSE199939 (Fig. 1A). The result indicated an upregulating senescence level in DFU. Consequently, we performed differential gene expression analysis. A meta-cohort was integrated into five datasets obtained from the GEO (GSE134431, GSE143735, GSE144441, GSE154556, and GSE199939, Table 1). Following cluster analysis and assessment of the average gene expression per sample, ten outliers were excluded (sFig. 2 A and B). Batch effects across samples were corrected using the sva package (Fig. 1B), and differential analysis was conducted using the DESeq2 package, identifying 704 upregulated and 3,269 downregulated genes (Fig. 1C). To achieve a robust consensus, three distinct algorithms GSEA (sFig. 2 C), GSVA (sFig. 2D), and ssGSEA (sFig. 2D), were applied to evaluate the enrichment of the SenMayo and SenSkin signatures. A unified assessment demonstrated that both senescence signatures were consistently and significantly enriched across all independent methodologies (Fig. 1D).

### Identification of a senescence-associated gene co-expression module
  Next, WGCNA was used to analyze the meta-cohort. The study constructed an adjacent matrix for an unsigned network using β = 18 (R2 = 0.851) while maintaining high connectivity based on gene distribution (Fig. 2A). Subsequently, 19 co-expression modules were screened (Fig. 2B). To identify the core functional modules driving DFU progression and senescence, we evaluated the correlation between the identified co-expression modules and DFU-related and senescence phenotypes. Notably, the MEred module showed prominent associations with specific individual features, particularly SenMayo score (R = 0.82 P = 6e−7for ssGSEA of SenMayo, R = 0.77 P = 1e− 12 for GSVA, Fig. 2C). However, the MEturquoise module demonstrated the most statistically significant correlation across DFU (R = 0.57, P = 2e− 6, Fig. 2C) and senescence phenotypes (R = 0.74 P = 1e− 11 for GSVA of SenMayo, R = 0.79 P = 5e− 14 for ssGSEA of SenMayo, R = 0.75 P = 5e− 14 for ssGSEA of SenSkin, R = 0.71 P = 2e− 10 for GSVA of SenSkin, Fig. 2C) collectively, achieving the highest overall average absolute correlation coefficient (average R2 = 0.75, P < 0.05 across all traits). The global trait-wide consistency of MEturquoise suggests it represents the most comprehensively dysregulated gene co-expression network in DFU macrophages, warranting its selection for downstream hub gene analysis. A notable correlation was observed between gene significance (GS) for senescence score and module membership (MM) in the MEturquoise module, suggesting that its hub genes are strongly associated with senescence (R2 = 0.65 for GSVA of SenMayo, R2 = 0.7 for ssGSEA of SenMayo, R2 = 0.62 for ssGSEA of SenSkin, R2 = 0.56 for GSVA of SenSkin, Fig. 2D). The module comprised 2084 genes (Table S1) was selected for further analysis. KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that these genes were enriched in pathways associated with senescence, including the cell cycle, DNA replication, cellular senescence, and P53 pathways (Fig. 2E).

### Senescence level was increased in macrophages in scRNA-seq
  To delineate the senescence levels of distinct cellular subpopulations in DFU, we performed additional analyses of the scRNA-seq data. A meta-cohort was integrated by three datasets obtained from the GEO (GSE165816, GSE245703, and GSE268834, Table 1). Following the clustering of high-quality cells, nine distinct cell types were identified and annotated in the UMAP plot (Fig. 3A). The cells were marked, including KRT1, KRT5, KRT10, and KRT14 for keratinocytes (Kera), ATAC2, and TAGLN for smooth muscle cells (SMCs), MZB1 and IGKC for plasma, CD3D and CD3E for T lymphocyte and NK cell (T/NK), CD79A and MS4A1 for B lymphocyte (B lym), CD68 and AIF1 for macrophage (Macro), MLANA and MITF for melanocytes (Melan), VWF and ACKR1 for endothelial cells (Endo), DCN and CFD for fibroblasts (Fibro) as previously established (Fig. 3B). The AUcell package was used to assess cellular senescence activity, using a curated gene set from SenMayo and SenSkin databases as a metric. Kera, SMCs, Fibro, Endo, and Macro exhibited positive levels of cellular senescence across all the clusters (Fig. 3C). while we also assess cellular senescence by using the function “AddModuleScore”, the results were consistent with AUcell (sFig 4 A). The SenMayo signature captures systemic, pan-tissue senescence, the SenSkin signature specifically reflects the local transcriptomic context of cutaneous tissue. Given the importance of SenMayo and SenSkin genesets, cells common to both datasets were defined as senescent cells. By defining senescent macrophages based on the intersection (dual-positive) of both signatures, we applied a highly stringent filter to minimize false positives driven by transient general inflammation, thereby isolating a high-confidence senescent population in the DFU microenvironment. Consistent with the previous results of bulk RNA-seq, we observed that both the number (1041 versus 356) and proportion (2% versus 0.9%) of senescent cells were higher in the DFU group compared to the CTRL group (Fig. 3D, sFig 4B). Furthermore, we observed that Marco-positive cells exhibited the highest senescent cell proportion among all clusters (Fig. 3E, sFig 4 C, p < 0.01). Subsequently, we compared the cell cycle distributions between senescent and non-senescent cells. We observed a reduction in the G2M phase score among senescent cells, including SMCs, Fibro, Endo, and Macro, indicating cell cycle arrest (Fig. 3F). Additionally, we examined the expression of SASP-related genes and observed elevated MMP1, CXCL1, CCL2, and IL1B levels in senescent cells (Fig. 3G). To further confirm that the senescent cells reflect a true senescent state rather than merely transient inflammatory activation, we assessed the expression of canonical senescence regulators. Notably, senescent macrophages exhibited significantly higher expression of the canonical senescence marker CDKN1A compared to non-senescent macrophages. While TP53 and CDKN2A transcript levels were not significantly elevated (sFig 4D).

### Cell-cell communications of senescent macrophage
  Senescent cells exhibit a distinct senescence-associated secretory phenotype, enabling them to secrete bioactive factors that mediate communication with neighboring cells. Given that macrophages contained the highest senescent cell proportion, we focused subsequent analyses on macrophage senescence. After classifying macrophages into senescent (Aging_Macro) and non-senescent (Macro) macrophages, we performed cell-cell communication analysis using the “CellChat” package. The results indicated that both cluster of macrophages interact strongly with endothelial cells, with Aging_Macro exhibiting higher interaction strength than their non-senescent counterparts (sFig. 3 A). We further studied the specific signaling pathways between these macrophages and endothelial cells, and identified the CXCL pathway as the most differentially active pathway between Aging_Macro and non-senescent macrophages (sFig. 3B). In these interactions, both macrophage clusters act as senders, while endothelial cells serve as receivers (sFig. 3 C). Ligand-receptor analysis revealed that multiple CXCL signaling pathways significantly contributed to macrophage-endothelial communication (sFig. 3D). Specifically, compared to non-senescent macrophages, Aging_Macro revealed enhanced signaling through the CXCL8 ligand to the ACKR1 receptor on endothelial cells (sFig. 3D). We then examined the alterations in macrophage-endothelial interactions under DFU conditions. Our analysis revealed that DFU enhanced the communication strength between macrophages and endothelial cells (sFig. 3E). By comparing global information flow between cohorts, the CXCL signaling pathway exhibited one of the most dramatic upregulations in the DFU microenvironment compared to the Control (sFig. 3 F). Within the enriched pathways, we specifically interrogated interactions originating from Aging_macro to Endo. As shown in the updated bubble plots, the CXCL8-ACKR1 pair demonstrated the highest communication probability and strict statistical significance (P < 0.01) exclusively in the DFU condition (sFig. 3G). These findings suggest that senescent macrophages in DFU may amplify CXCL secretion, potentially activating downstream pathways in endothelial cells (sFig. 3G). These in silico predictions require further experimental validation, for example by assessing ACKR1+ endothelial senescence and the therapeutic potential of CXCL8 inhibition in DFU models. Previous studies have identified ACKR1 as a vital chemokine scavenger on endothelial cells, which plays a critical role in modulating local chemokine gradients and regulating inflammatory responses and vascular dysfunction(Guo et al. 2023, Raslan et al. 2024).


### Cellular heterogeneity of senescent macrophage in DFU
  Our transcriptomic analysis specifically prioritized macrophages due to their indispensable role in orchestrating both the inflammatory and tissue-remodeling phases of wound healing. In diabetic foot pathology, the aberrant accumulation of senescent macrophages directly impairs this delicate balance. By persistently secreting SASP factors, these cells sustain a chronic inflammatory microenvironment that prevents ulcer resolution, making them a highly relevant target for our integrated omics investigation. Given that macrophages contain the highest senescent cell proportion and play a critical role in wound healing in diabetic foot conditions, we next performed unsupervised clustering of macrophages to study their heterogeneity further. As demonstrated in a previous study (Liu et al. ), we identified six clusters in macrophage, including four macrophage clusters: Mac_inf (PCLAF, MZB1, and IGKC), Mac1 (IL1B, MMP19, and EREG), Mac2 (DAB2 and C1QA/B), and Mono_like (FCN1, CD14, and S100A10), two dendritic cell clusters: Plasmacytoid DC (pDC, CLEC9A, IDO1, and XCR1), cDC2 (CD1C, FCER1A, and CLEC10A, Figs. 4A and B). Subsequently, we quantified the senescent cell proportion across each samples and each Macro subpopulations (Fig. 4C sFig. 5 A). Except for pDCs, all major cell subtypes contained detectable senescent cells, with Mac1 exhibiting the highest senescence ratio (Fig. 4D and sFig. 5B). We then performed differential gene expression analysis between senescent and non-senescent macrophages and identified 182 DEGs (Fig. 4E). KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that these DEGs were predominantly associated with inflammatory pathways, including TNF, NF-κB, and IL-17 signaling (Fig. 4D). After excluding dendritic cells, we conducted pseudotime trajectory analysis on macrophages using the “Slingshot” package. The trajectory inference identified two distinct lineages. They all originated from Mac_inf and Mono_like, while culminating in Mac1 (Lineage2) and Mac2 (Lineage1), respectively (Fig. 4E). Senescent cells were predominantly located in the mid-to-late stages of both lineages. Consistent with this spatial distribution, SenMayo and SenSkin scores increased gradually along pseudotime, peaking in the intermediate phases before declining toward the terminal states (Fig. 4G). The expression dynamics of representative genes (IL1B, CXCL3, MMP19) illustrates how these specific drivers dictate the trajectory toward the senescent state (sFig. 5 C). We further characterized the dynamic gene expression patterns along each lineage. Lineage 1 was marked by signature genes enriched in biological processes such as apoptosis, antigen presentation, and the complement system (Fig. 4H). Conversely, lineage 2 exhibited dynamic genes strongly associated with NF-κB, TNF, and TLR signaling pathways (Fig. 4H).

### Establishment of an MSS via scRNA-seq analysis, WGCNA, and GWAS
  Previous studies have revealed transcriptomic characteristics of cellular senescence and its alterations in intercellular communication. Notably, these processes are profoundly influenced by epigenetic regulation. To gain a more comprehensive understanding of how genetic risks impact cellular senescence and DFU pathology through epigenetic mechanisms, we introduced scDRS to integrate GWAS risk signals with transcriptomic data at single-cell resolution, aiming to identify core senescence-associated genes that drive disease progression. We performed scDRS scoring using GWAS data associated with senescence (GrimAge, Hannum, PhenoAge, and mvAge) and DFU. The results revealed enrichments of senescence-related and diabetic foot diseases across all macrophage clusters, with a significantly higher scDRS-positive cell proportion for DFU compared to senescence-related traits (Fig. 5A). We further evaluated the disease relevance of each cell subtype and observed that Mac2 exhibited the strongest association with senescence-related diseases (Fig. 5B). Finally, we assessed the correlation between DFU and aging, identifying a positive linear relationship wherein the senescence disease score increased concomitantly with the DFU score (Fig. 5C). To ensure transparency and reproducibility, the derivation of MSS gene set was conducted through a rigorous, stepwise intersection of three distinct analytical modules (MSS.X, MSS.Y, and MSS.Z), ultimately resulting in a highly robust 73-gene signature: Step 1 (MSS.X Definition): MSS.X comprises the bulk transcriptomic co-expression network associated with the senescence score. Through WGCNA, the functionally dominant turquoise module was extracted, and was performed Spearman correlation analysis using key module genes identified to examine the relationship between gene expression levels in macrophages and the senescence score. We finally got a subset of 1023 genes strongly correlated with the senescence score (Spearman R > 0.5 and FDR < 0.05, Fig. 5D, gene listed in Table. S2). Step 2 (MSS.Y Definition): MSS.Y represents the senescence-associated DEGs at the single-cell level. By comparing AUCell-defined senescent macrophages with non-senescent macrophages, we identifie 1409 upregulated genes based on strict thresholds of log2FC > 0.25 and adjusted p < 0.05 (Fig. 5D, gene listed in Table S3). Step 3 (MSS.Z Definition): MSS.Z denotes the genetically driven candidate genes mapped from positively correlated genes from four GWAS-related traits (Gene listed in Table S4 for GrimAge, Table S5 for Hannum, Table S6 for mvAge, and Table S7 for PhenoAge). Utilizing the intersection of four genesets, we successfully identifying 2821 potential risk genes (Fig. 5D). Step 4 (Final Intersection): To establish a deeply integrated signature supported by single-cell regulation, bulk transcriptomic networks, and genetic susceptibility, we intersected the three subsets. The final MSS gene set consists of the 73 genes present in the overlapping region of MSS.X, MSS.Y, and MSS.Z, Fig. 5D, Table S8). To distill a highly robust and biologically actionable MSS, we applied a stringent multi-dimensional intersection strategy. We hypothesized that true key drivers of macrophage senescence should be detectable across multiple analytical layers. Therefore, we intersected genes derived from bulk co-expression networks (MSS.X), single-cell transcriptomic profiling (MSS.Y), and genetic/clinical trait relevance mapping via scDRS (MSS.Z). Rather than introducing redundancy, this three-way intersection serves as an orthogonal validation step, effectively filtering out modality-specific technical noise. This stringent cross-validation successfully narrowed the candidates down to a core set of 73 genes, defining the final MSS utilized for downstream functional and clinical evaluations.Using KEGG and GO enrichment analyses, we preliminarily identified biological processes associated with MSS. These pathways were significantly enriched in apoptosis, senescence, and inflammation-related processes (Fig. 5E). To further validate the statistical robustness of the MSS, we conducted a sensitivity analysis on the core parameters used for its construction. During the screening of DEGs, we systematically adjusted the log2FC threshold (ranging from 0.25 to 1) to derive a series of alternative MSS candidate gene sets. Subsequently, we re-evaluated the diagnostic performance of these reconstructed scores for DFU status in the validation set using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The results demonstrated that despite variations in the log2FC, the AUC remained highly stable, consistently ranging between 0.79 and 0.85 (sFig 6 A). This finding strongly indicates that the MSS classifier possesses robust generalization capabilities and that its predictive efficacy is not contingent upon a single, arbitrary statistical threshold. To validate the utility of MSS, we scored macrophage senescence in single-cell data using the MSS gene set. We observed a higher senescent macrophage proportion in the DFU group compared to the CTRL group (Fig. 5F). We further evaluated the MSS score in bulk RNA datasets using GSVA and ssGSEA and identified that the DFU group had significantly higher scores than the CTRL group (Fig. 5G). Additionally, differential gene expression analysis followed by GSEA confirmed that the MSS gene set was significantly positively enriched (Fig. 5H).

### DFU prediction based on MSS
  To evaluate the potential of MSS in predicting DFU, we constructed a prediction model using a meta-cohort. After removing batch effects, the dataset was divided into 80% training and 20% validation sets. Using the MLR3 package, we trained five machine learning models: KNN, Naive Bayes, RF, SVM, and DT, with 5-fold cross-validation repeated multiple times (Fig. 6A). We then evaluated these models using the validation set by calculating the AUC, Accuracy, Sensitivity, Specificity, and the F1-score, and classification error. The findings demonstrated that RF and KNN achieved high AUC values and classification accuracy (Figs. 6B and C; Table 3). These two models were further validated in an independent test cohort comprising the GSE68183, GSE272918, GSE269483, and GSE271701 datasets. The KNN model achieved a higher AUC than RF (0.737 versus 0.684, Fig. 6D). We also built KNN models based on SenMayo and SenSkin gene sets for comparison. The AUC values of SenMayo (0.614) and SenSkin (0.526) were lower than that of the MSS-based KNN model (Fig. 6E). To interpret the model, we used the DALEX package, which revealed that RUNX1 had the highest feature importance (Fig. 6F). Partial dependence plots suggested a U-shaped relationship between RUNX1 expression and its contribution to the model prediction (Fig. 6G). A breakdown plot for sample (GSM394690) demonstrated the contribution of each feature to the prediction of the model (Fig. 6H).

### RUNX1 downregulation alleviated cellular senescence in macrophages
  To study the RUNX1 role in macrophage senescence, we first established a high glucose-induced senescence model in RAW264.7 cells, based on established methods from the literature (Diwan et al. 2024). Cells were collected on day 4 (Fig. 7A), and SA-β-Gal staining revealed that high glucose significantly exacerbated senescence in RAW264.7 cells (Figs. 7B and C). Western blotting further revealed a marked increase in the protein levels of the senescence markers P21 and P16 (Fig. 7D). ELISA analysis of the culture medium indicated that high glucose increased the secretion of SASP components, including IL-1β, IL-6, and MCP-1 (Fig. 7E). These results confirmed the successful establishment of the cellular senescence model. Having validated the model, we identified that high glucose promoted RUNX1 upregulation (Fig. 7D). To determine whether RUNX1 influences macrophage senescence, we constructed RAW264.7 cells with RUNX1 knockdown using three distinct shRNAs, which effectively reduced RUNX1 expression (Fig. 7F). shRNA1, which exhibited the highest knockdown efficiency, was selected for subsequent experiments. Following SA-β-Gal staining, we observed that RUNX1 knockdown reduced cellular senescence (Figs. 7G and H). Correspondingly, Western blotting results indicated that, compared to the scramble control group, RUNX1 knockdown significantly decreased the P21 and P16 levels (Fig. 7I). Similarly, ELISA results revealed that efficient RUNX1 knockdown decreased the secretion of SASP factors, including IL-1β, IL-6, and MCP-1 (Fig. 7J). In summary, our findings indicate that Runx1 promotes macrophage senescence.

### RUNX1 promoted macrophage senescence by enhancing P53 acetylation
  Having demonstrated the RUNX1 role in macrophage senescence, further study is required to elucidate how RUNX1 influences macrophage senescence. Considering the significant P21 and P16 upregulation in our experiments, both downstream effectors of P53, this suggests potential alterations in P53 activity. Given the crucial role of P53 in senescence, we hypothesized that RUNX1 may affect macrophage senescence by modulating P53. Western blotting revealed a marked increase in P53 levels in RAW264.7 cells under high-glucose-induced senescence (Fig. 8A). Subsequently, we constructed RUNX1 overexpressing cells using a RUNX1 overexpression plasmid (Fig. 8E). SA-β-gal staining indicated that RUNX1 overexpression exacerbated cellular senescence (Fig. 8B). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that RUNX1 and P53 were expressed in the nucleus, with partial co-localization observed between the two proteins (Fig. 8C). Further co-immunoprecipitation assays in RAW264.7 cells confirmed an interaction between P53 and RUNX1. After co-overexpressing P53 (pcDNA3.1-P53) and RUNX1 (pcDNA3.1-RUNX1), immunoprecipitation with a P53 antibody followed by detection with a RUNX1 antibody demonstrated a direct protein-protein interaction (Fig. 8D). Previous studies have reported that RUNX1 can influence P53 acetylation (Wu et al. 2013). Therefore, we examined the acetylation status of P53. Western blotting revealed increased P53 acetylation during high-glucose-induced senescence. Moreover, transfection with increasing amounts of the RUNX1 overexpression plasmid (low-OE: 2 µg, High-OE: 4 µg) led to a dose-dependent increase in P53 acetylation and total P53 protein levels (Fig. 8E). Based on these findings, we propose that RUNX1 promotes macrophage senescence and is associated with enhanced P53 acetylation, which might stabilizes P53 and increases its protein abundance, thereby driving the senescence.

## Discussion
  This study presents the first comprehensive analysis delineating the central role of cellular senescence, specifically within macrophages, in DFU pathogenesis. Through a multi-omics integrative analysis combining transcriptomics, scRNA-seq, and GWAS data, we systematically demonstrated a significant upregulation of senescence in DFU. At the single-cell level, macrophages were identified as the predominant cell population harboring senescent cells. These senescent macrophages exhibited enhanced pro-inflammatory communication, particularly via the CXCL signaling axis. By integrating these findings, we constructed a novel MSS that proved to be an effective predictor of DFU. Further machine learning and functional validation highlighted the transcription factor RUNX1 as a key driver of this process. Mechanistically, RUNX1 promotes high-glucose-induced macrophage senescence and is associated wth enhancing P53 acetylation, might stabilizing the P53 protein and activating its downstream senescence program (Fig. 8F). Collectively, these findings reveal an axis associating macrophage senescence with DFU pathology and propose novel biomarkers and potential therapeutic targets for this debilitating complication.

  Our integrative analysis firmly positions cellular senescence, particularly in macrophages, as a pivotal pathological feature of DFU. Wound infections, elevated glucose levels, and advanced glycosylation end products can influence macrophage phenotypes and functions (Liu et al. 1999, Rungratanawanich et al. 2021). The macrophage function dysregulation, such as the reduced ability to phagocytose pathogens and apoptotic cells, hinders the transition to the reparative M2 phenotype (Hesketh et al. 2017, Mirza and Koh 2011). This leads to a higher recruitment of macrophages to the wound, where they are activated into M1 macrophages (Khanna et al. 2010). A scarcity of M2 macrophages can delay wound healing and often result in additional tissue damage (Mirza et al. 2013). Our single-cell heterogeneity analysis also provided this option. The M1 phenotype, which is labeled as Mac1 subpopulation, exhibits the highest senescence ratio and expresses pro-inflammatory markers (IL1B and MMP19) and appears to be a key dysfunctional subset. Its pseudotemporal positioning in the mid-to-late stages of pseudotime, alongside elevated senescence scores and a signature enriched in NF-κB and TNF signaling, suggests that senescence may prevent the macrophages from switching to the M2 phenotype and lock these macrophages into a chronically activated, non-resolving state. This is further corroborated by their cell cycle arrest (reduced G2/M score) and heightened expression of SASP factors (MMP1, CCL2, and IL1B), which can directly degrade matrix, recruit more immune cells, and induce senescence in neighboring cells, thereby creating a self-amplifying, pro-inflammatory vicious cycle that stalls the wound healing. Similar experiments with macrophages sourced from mice and individuals with diabetes exhibit that these macrophages are hyperresponsive to inflammatory stimulants and therefore secrete more pro-inflammatory cytokines, have difficulties switching to the more reparative M2-like phenotypes, and, when looked at within the wound setting, prolong the inflammatory phase(Miao et al. 2012, Huang et al. 2021, Geng et al. 2023). Another key mechanistic insight from our study is the altered intercellular communication orchestrated by senescent macrophages. The markedly intensified CXCL signaling axis, specifically the CXCL8-ACKR1 pathway from senescent macrophages to endothelial cells, offers a credible connection between cellular senescence and the clinical features of DFU. CXCL8 is a potent chemoattractant for neutrophils and a modulator of angiogenesis (Matsushima et al. 2022). Continuous CXCL8 secretion from senescent macrophages may maintain neutrophil-dominated chronic inflammation, hindering the shift to the healing proliferative phase (Rai et al. 2022, Yang et al. 2023). Concurrently, signaling by ACKR1, a non-signaling interceptor receptor, may reduce angiogenesis by inducing senescence in endothelial and adhesion and emigration of leucocytes (Xu et al. 2007, Pruenster and Rot 2006). This aberrant crosstalk may explain, in part, the concurrent presence of excessive inflammation, impaired angiogenesis, and persistent infection observed in non-healing DFUs.

  Building upon the phenotypic characterization of senescent macrophages, our study constructs a novel and disease-relevant metric, the MSS. The predictive power of MSS outperforms generic senescence gene sets (SenSkin and MayoSen), highlighting its specificity. This enhanced performance stems from its unique construction: It integrates genetic risk signals from bulk transcriptomic of DFU, aging GWAS, and single-cell transcriptomics of DFU. Consequently, MSS captures a state of cellular aging and a pathology-embedded senescence program active in macrophages in the diabetic wound context. It represents a promising translational tool, moving from descriptive omics toward a quantifiable biomarker with potential for risk stratification in patients with DFU.

  Central to this senescence program is the transcription factor RUNX1, identified as the top-ranking feature by our machine learning model. This computational prediction was robustly validated by functional assays. While RUNX1 is well-established in hematopoiesis and leukemogenesis, its role in senescence remains poorly characterized (Lin 2022, Gonzales et al. 2021, Na et al. 2020). RUNX1 induces senescence-like growth arrest, and knocking down RUNX1 reduces the quiescence-associated transcription factor expression (Wolyniec et al. 2009, Wong et al. 2015). This result may suggest RUNX1 is involved in senescence. Our findings expand its repertoire to regulate stress-induced senescence in terminally differentiated macrophages. We delineate a clear mechanistic pathway: high-glucose stress upregulates RUNX1, which interacts with and is associated with the P53 acetylation. Similar results are identified in a previous study (Wu et al. 2013, Zhang et al. 2021). This post-translational modification is critical for stabilizing P53 protein, leading to its accumulation and the subsequent transcriptional activation of canonical senescence effectors, including p21 (CDKN1A) and p16 (CDKN2A) (Nagasaka et al. 2022). Therefore, RUNX1 acts as a molecular linchpin, transducing the persistent metabolic insult (hyperglycemia) into an epigenetic mark (P53 acetylation) that locks the cell into a senescent fate. This axis provides a novel and targetable node connecting the diabetic microenvironment to the dysfunctional cellular state driving impaired healing. Notably, our results demonstrated a concurrent upregulation of both acetylated and total P53 levels. Consistent with established regulatory mechanisms, this accumulation of total P53 is most likely driven by post-translational stabilization rather than de novo transcriptional induction. Hyperacetylation of P53 prevents its interaction with MDM2, thereby blocking ubiquitin-dependent proteasomal degradation and leading to its intracellular accumulation. Future studies utilizing cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays or transcriptomic validations could further isolate the exact kinetics of this stabilization process.

  While our study provides compelling multi-level evidence, several limitations must be addressed to contextualize the findings and guide future research. First, the retrospective nature of bulk and single-cell RNA-seq datasets, predominantly from public repositories, may introduce population bias and limit the generalizability of our conclusions across diverse ethnicities. The transcriptomic signatures of senescence, such as SenMayo and SenSkin, serve as approximations. Given the substantial biological overlap between the SASP and general immune responses, cells achieving high scores based on these signatures may partially reflect a state of profound inflammatory activation alongside true cellular senescence. Future studies employing multi-omics approaches are warranted to distinguish between these closely intertwined cellular states. Second, scRNA-seq analysis, while revealing, is constrained by sample size and lacks spatial context. Spatial transcriptomics or multiplex immunohistochemistry on human DFU tissue sections is essential to validate the precise anatomical localization of senescent macrophages and their physical proximity to endothelial cells, confirming the inferred cellular crosstalk. Third, our mechanistic dissection, despite being substantiated in vitro, requires in vivo validation. Generating myeloid-specific Runx1 knockout mice in diabetic wound models will be crucial to confirm its non-redundant role in driving senescence and impairing healing in a complex organismal setting. Furthermore, the specific acetyltransferase involved and the exact lysine residue on P53 modified by RUNX1 activity remain to be mapped. Fourth, in integrating genetic and transcriptomic data, we employed a standard fixed linear window of ± 10 kb upstream and downstream of the gene body to map GWAS variants to their target genes. This heuristic strategy is widely used as a standard empirical parameter in genomic enrichment frameworks, as it effectively captures proximal cis-regulatory elements while minimizing the background noise introduced by irrelevant distal linkage disequilibrium loci. However, we acknowledge the inherent biological limitations of this one-dimensional physical mapping approach. Eukaryotic transcriptional regulation relies on complex three-dimensional chromatin architecture, this linear window fails to capture distal enhancers or super-enhancers that may be linearly distant but spatially adjacent, which typically exert regulatory effects via physical contacts like chromatin loops. Meanwhile, this mapping method does not incorporate tissue-specific expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) evidence at the molecular functional level. Therefore, future studies should integrate high-resolution 3D genomic data and eQTL mapping specific to foot skin to achieve a more precise delineation of the transcriptional regulation governed by DFU genetic susceptibility variants. Finally, it is important to note that the MSS is currently an in silico transcriptomic classifier. While it demonstrates diagnostic performance, its translation into a practical, point-of-care clinical assay remains a future objective. Such a transition will necessitate the reduction of the current 73-gene panel to a more streamlined and cost-effective set of biomarkers, followed by rigorous prospective clinical validation to confirm its predictive value and safety across diverse patient populations.


## Conclusion
  In conclusion, through a synergistic combination of multi-omics bioinformatics, machine learning, and experimental validation, this study establishes macrophage senescence as a central pathological mechanism in DFU. We demonstrate that it is not a passive bystander but an active driver of disease. Senescent macrophages exhibit a distinctive pro-inflammatory SASP, engage in aberrant communication via the CXCL-ACKR1 axis to disrupt the microenvironment, and are propelled by a defined molecular cascade involving RUNX1 and P53 acetylation. The developed MSS translate these mechanistic insights into concrete tools for potential risk prediction and targeted intervention.
