# Discovery of a novel STING-binding peptide associated with reduced cGAMP-induced inflammatory gene expression


## Abstract
  cGAMP-induced STING activation contributes to inflammatory and interferon-related signalling, making STING a relevant target for inhibitor development. In this study, a 59,319-sequence peptide library was screened against STING by molecular docking, and four top-ranked peptides were selected for evaluation. MST analysis demonstrated that Peptides 1–4 bound to recombinant STING, with Peptide-1 showing the highest affinity (Kd = 0.15 ± 0.01 μM). Docking and simulation analyses suggested that binding was mediated by hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic contacts. Molecular dynamics, MM/PBSA, and free energy landscape analyses suggested stable binding with favourable calculated energetics. Peptide-1 showed no apparent cytotoxicity up to 10 μM in RAW264.7 macrophages and primary BMDMs, while dose-dependently reducing cGAMP-induced IFN-β and IL-6 expression at both protein and mRNA levels. This inhibitory effect was accompanied by reduced STING and IRF3 phosphorylation. Collectively, these findings suggest that Peptide-1 may bind STING and attenuate cGAMP-induced IFN-β and IL-6 expression.


## Introduction
  Inflammation represents a fundamental host defense mechanism against pathogen invasion and is essential for tissue repair and physiological homeostasis1. However, excessive activation or persistent dysregulation of inflammation can lead to the aberrant release of pro-inflammatory mediators, thereby contributing to the initiation and progression of various inflammatory diseases2,3. Given the increasing global burden of inflammatory diseases, the exploration of key regulatory targets has emerged as a major research priority in this field4. Accumulating evidence indicates that STING (Stimulator of Interferon Genes) is an important regulator of inflammatory responses5,6. In vivo studies have further shown that genetic ablation of STING can alleviate pathological inflammatory injury in certain disease settings7. Therefore, the development of STING inhibitors may represent a promising strategy for inflammatory diseases.

  STING is a key adaptor protein localised to the endoplasmic reticulum membrane and plays a central role in innate immunity and inflammatory disease pathogenesis8. Structurally, STING comprises an N-terminal transmembrane domain, a cytosolic ligand-binding domain (LBD), and a C-terminal tail9. Binding of the intracellular second messenger cGAMP (2′3′-cyclic GMP–AMP) activates STING10. STING activation is associated with increased IRF3 phosphorylation and the induction of interferon- and inflammation-related mediators, including IFN-β and IL-611. Therefore, STING is an important regulator of inflammatory responses, and its aberrant activation is closely associated with inflammatory diseases12. Given the critical role of the LBD pocket in ligand recognition, targeting this site may offer a feasible strategy for modulating STING activation and reducing expression of IFN-β and IL-6.

  The development of STING inhibitors has garnered considerable interest, particularly for strategies that directly engage the ligand-binding region. Mechanistically, reported STING inhibitors are commonly classified as covalent inhibitors or competitive ligands, although additional targeting modalities have also been explored13,14. Reported covalent small-molecule STING inhibitors, represented by CHN105, H-151, and C-178, suppress STING activation by forming covalent conjugates with the critical cysteine residue Cys91 or nearby cysteine residues involved in STING activation15–17. Nevertheless, such covalent agents may be associated with several challenges, including cysteine-dependent reactivity, potential selectivity liabilities, and reduced efficacy when target-site engagement is compromised18,19. Furthermore, the reported small-molecule competitive inhibitors mainly include Compound 1820, SN-01121, and HY-222. Previous studies have indicated that peptides represent a potential modality for STING-targeted regulation23. Compared with conventional small molecules, peptides can provide a broader interaction surface and better shape complementarity to the binding pocket24. Previous studies have shown that the cyclic peptide Astin C exerts anti-inflammatory effects by targeting STING and thus serves as a reference peptide in this study25. Although Astin C has provided preliminary proof of concept for the design of STING-targeting cyclic peptides, further optimisation is still needed to improve its potency and developability26. Therefore, the development of cyclic peptides targeting the STING LBD with high binding affinity and enhanced inhibitory potency remains an area of considerable interest.

  Molecular docking is a widely used virtual screening approach in early-stage discovery owing to its efficiency, cost-effectiveness, and applicability to large-scale candidate screening27,28. By leveraging the structural features of target binding sites, it enables rapid assessment of ligand binding modes and key interactions, thereby facilitating candidate identification28,29. Our previous studies have established a solid methodological basis for the present work through the identification of multiple small-molecule and peptide candidates from databases using molecular docking30–33. Integrating molecular dynamics simulations with MM/PBSA (molecular mechanics/Poisson–Boltzmann surface area) binding free-energy calculations and free energy landscape analysis provides complementary in silico evidence for evaluating the stability, energetic favorability, and conformational behaviour of peptide–target complexes, thereby supporting the rational ptimizeionn of inhibitors34,35. Here, a rigid-receptor/flexible-ligand docking protocol was employed to screen a cyclic peptide library, leading to the identification of Peptide-1 as a STING-binding peptide associated with reduced expression of IFN-β and IL-6.


## Materials and methods

### Reagents and materials

The purity of each peptide was determined by analytical high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), with all peptides showing a purity of >98%. Their molecular weights and chemical identities were further confirmed by liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS). The manufacturer-provided analytical HPLC chromatograms and LC–MS spectra are presented in Supplementary Figure S1. Recombinant human STING protein (cat. No. ab268713) was purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, United Kingdom). The 2nd Generation RED-NHS Protein Labelling Kit (cat. No. MO-L011) and Monolith NT.115 capillaries (cat. No. MO-K022) were purchased from NanoTemper Technologies (Munich, Germany). The primer sequences for IL-6 and IFN-β were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). TRIzol Reagent (cat. No. 15596026) was purchased from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA, USA). Thermo Fisher High-Capacity Cdna Reverse Transcription Kit (cat. No. 4374966) was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Human IL-6 ELISA Kit (cat. No. ab178013), Human Interferon beta ELISA Kit (cat. No. ab278127), and Phospho-IRF3 (S386) and Total IRF3 ELISA Kit (cat. No. ab279834) were all purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, United Kingdom).


### Cell culture
  RAW264.7 murine macrophages (cat. No. TIB-71) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). Cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated foetal bovine serum, 100 U/Ml penicillin, and 100 μg/Ml streptomycin. Cryopreserved primary bone marrow macrophages (BMDMs) derived from C57BL/6 mice (cat. No. C57-6030) were purchased from Cyagen Biosciences (Suzhou, China). Cells were maintained in complete RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with the indicated additives. Basal culture medium and penicillin–streptomycin mixture were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Heat-inactivated foetal bovine serum (FBS) was purchased from Gibco (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). All cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2, and those at logarithmic growth phase were harvested for subsequent functional assays.


### Peptide library construction
  The cyclic pentapeptide library was constructed in silico using the QuaSAR-CombiGen module of the Molecular Operating Environment (MOE) software30. Two dipeptide fragment databases and one single-amino-acid fragment database were used as the building blocks for combinatorial enumeration. In the library construction, predefined attachment points were assigned to each fragment to guide fragment connection and cyclisation. Each cyclic pentapeptide was generated by sequentially combining one dipeptide fragment from the first dipeptide database, one single-amino-acid fragment, and one dipeptide fragment from the second dipeptide database, thereby producing a five-residue cyclic peptide scaffold. Through exhaustive enumeration of all possible fragment combinations, a peptide library containing 59,319 cyclic pentapeptides was constructed. All generated peptides were collected into a single MOE database. Energy minimization was then performed in MOE to optimise the peptide geometries and convert the initial two-dimensional structures into three-dimensional conformations for subsequent molecular docking.


#### Molecular docking-based screening
  The crystal structure of human STING in complex with its ligand (PDB ID: 4F5D) was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB). The structure was determined at a resolution of 3.00 Å, with an R-free value of 0.242, indicating good structural quality and reliable refinement. Subsequently, the original ligand was removed, and the retrieved STING protein structure was preprocessed using the QuickPrep module in MOE. During protein preparation, hydrogen atoms were added, protonation states were assigned using Protonate 3D, atomic charges were calculated, water molecules located more than 4.5 Å from the ligand-binding site were removed, and the structure was energy-minimized to an RMS gradient of 0.1 kcal/mol/Å33. The active pocket was defined based on the original ligand-binding site. The established library containing 59,319 cyclic peptides was docked into the active pocket of STING using the Dock module in MOE. During the docking procedure, the receptor was kept rigid while all cyclic peptide ligands were defined as flexible. Docking was carried out using the Triangle Matcher placement method with the Rigid Receptor protocol, and poses were evaluated by London dG followed by GBVI/WSA dG rescoring32. For each peptide, multiple docking poses were generated, ranked by docking score, and the best-scoring pose was retained for subsequent analysis. The binding score of the positive control peptide Astin C was also calculated under the same docking conditions for reference. The top-ranked peptides were subsequently selected for further in vitro evaluation.


### Microscale thermophoresis assays
  Human recombinant STING protein was fluorescently labelled using the 2nd Generation RED-NHS Protein Labelling Kit following the manufacturer’s protocol, and the labelled protein was stored in the dark to prevent fluorescence quenching30. The concentration of fluorescently labelled STING protein was kept constant at 50 nM. Additionally, the tested peptides were prepared in PBST buffer [phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.05% (v/v) Tween-20] and subjected to twofold serial dilution to generate 11 concentration points ranging from 0.02 to 16 μM. The labelled STING protein was mixed with each serially diluted peptide sample, incubated for 5 min in the dark, loaded into standard capillaries, and analysed using the Monolith NT.115 instrument (NanoTemper Technologies GmbH, Munich, Germany). Measurements were carried out at 26 °C with 100% LED power, 20% MST power, and a data acquisition time of 30 s36. Binding curves were fitted using the built-in 1:1 binding model, and the equilibrium dissociation constant (Kd) was determined by nonlinear regression of concentration-dependent fluorescence response curves.


#### Surface plasmon resonance
  The interaction between the candidate peptides and recombinant human STING was quantified by surface plasmon resonance (SPR). All measurements were performed on an SPR molecular interaction analyser at 25 °C using HBS as the running buffer. The CM5 sensor chip was preconditioned with 50 mM NaOH, 1 M NaCl, 0.1% SDS, and 10 mM HCl, followed by chip normalisation with 70% glycerol37. Recombinant human STING was diluted to 50 nM in HBS and immobilised onto the detection flow cell. Peptides were prepared in HBS and serially diluted to generate 12 concentrations, including a blank control, over a range of 0.02–16 μM. Samples were injected sequentially from low to high concentrations at a flow rate of 60 μL/min, and association and dissociation phases were recorded in real time38. After each analytical cycle, the next cycle was initiated only after full baseline recovery. Raw sensorgrams were processed by reference subtraction and baseline correction. The processed sensorgrams were fitted using a 1:1 Langmuir kinetic model to obtain the association and dissociation rate constants and calculate the equilibrium dissociation constant for each peptide–STING interaction.


### Molecular dynamics simulations
  MD simulations were performed to characterise the binding features and dynamic interaction mechanisms of the STING–Peptide-1 complex. The optimal complex conformation obtained from molecular docking was defined as the initial structure for the MD simulation. The topological parameters of STING and Peptide-1 were assigned using the AMBER99SB-ILDN and GAFF force fields, respectively39. Furthermore, the Mg2+ ion was retained, parameterised as a divalent magnesium ion, and included in the simulation topology. The STING–Peptide-1 complex was solvated in a cubic simulation box with TIP3P water molecules, maintaining a minimum distance of ≥10 Å between the solute and box boundaries. Counterions were added to neutralise the system charge, followed by 5000 steps of energy minimisation using the steepest descent algorithm. The system was equilibrated sequentially for 1 ns under NVT conditions at 300 K and then for 1 ns under NPT conditions at 1.0 bar before the production MD simulation. Following pre-equilibration, the molecular dynamics simulations were performed using GROMACS 2024.3, with trajectory snapshots recorded at an interval of 10 ps. Finally, the structural stability of the complex was systematically evaluated by root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (Rg), solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), hydrogen bond distribution, and secondary structure analysis.


### Molecular mechanics/Poisson–Boltzmann surface area calculations
  MM/PBSA analysis was performed using equilibrated conformations from the MD trajectory. To comprehensively capture the time-dependent variation in binding free energy across the full MD simulation timescale, representative snapshots were uniformly sampled from the entire trajectory and subjected to subsequent free-energy calculations40. The binding free energy was calculated according to ΔGbind = Gcomplex − Gprotein − Gpeptide, where Gcomplex, Gprotein, and Gpeptide represent the free energies of the complex, receptor protein, and peptide, respectively. The total binding free energy was composed of van der Waals, electrostatic, polar solvation, and nonpolar solvation contributions. Polar solvation energy was calculated using the Poisson–Boltzmann model, whereas the nonpolar solvation term was estimated from the SASA41. The final binding free energy was reported as the average value across all extracted snapshots. Per-residue energy decomposition was further performed to evaluate the relative contribution of interfacial residues to STING–Peptide-1 complex formation and stabilisation, thereby identifying potential hotspot residues involved in peptide recognition.


### Essential dynamics
  Using trajectories from the equilibrium molecular dynamics simulation, the free energy landscape of the STING–Peptide-1 complex was constructed using RMSD and Rg as reaction coordinates to analyse conformational energetics and characterise the intrinsic dynamic behaviour of the system. Initially, the probability distribution of conformational states in the simulation trajectories was statistically analysed, and the corresponding free energy was calculated using the Boltzmann equation42. Finally, two-dimensional and three-dimensional free energy profiles were generated to characterise the distribution and stability of the dominant low-energy conformational basins throughout the simulation.


### MTT assay for cell cytotoxicity detection
  RAW264.7 cells in logarithmic growth phase and fully differentiated BMDMs were seeded separately into 96-well culture plates at a density of 5 × 10³ cells per well and incubated overnight to allow adherence. Blank control, vehicle control, and Peptide-1 treatment groups were established. After Peptide-1 administration, all groups were incubated for an additional 24 h. Subsequently, 20 μL of MTT working solution (5 mg/mL) was added to each well under light-shielded conditions, followed by a further 3 h incubation. Then, 150 μL dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added to dissolve formazan crystals. The absorbance of each well at 570 nm was determined using a multifunctional microplate reader (Varioskan LUX, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA)32. Relative cell viability of each group was calculated after subtracting the background absorbance from blank wells. Dose–response curves were fitted by nonlinear regression with peptide concentration as the x axis and cell viability as the y axis to calculate the cytotoxic IC50 values of Peptide-1 in the two cell types. The maximum tested concentration of Peptide-1 in this assay was 10 μM. Therefore, a fitted IC50 value greater than 10 μM indicated negligible cytotoxicity of Peptide-1 across the tested concentration range.


### Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays
  RAW264.7 macrophages and primary BMDMs were seeded in 6-well culture plates and cultured until reaching 70% confluence. Cells were divided into the following groups: blank control group, 2′,3′-cGAMP-only stimulation group, groups treated with 2′,3′-cGAMP plus 2, 4, or 8 μM Peptide-1, and 2′,3′-cGAMP combined with 8 μM Astin C positive inhibitor group. Cells were preincubated with Peptide-1 or Astin C for 6 h, while control groups were treated with an equal volume of vehicle solvent. After preincubation, all groups except the blank control were stimulated with 1 μM 2′,3′-cGAMP for an additional 6 h. Cell culture supernatants were harvested after stimulation to detect the secretion levels of IFN-β and IL-621. Mouse-specific SimpleStep ELISA kits for the detection of murine IFN-β (cat. no. ab252363) and IL-6 (cat. no. ab222503) were obtained from Abcam (Cambridge, United Kingdom).

  After removing culture supernatants, cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 5 min. Cell pellets were resuspended in ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer (strong, cat. no. P0013B, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) supplemented with 1× protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (cat. no. P1048, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), and incubated on ice for 30 min. The lysate mixture was centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 15 min, and clear soluble supernatants were collected. Equal aliquots of lysates were analysed using the corresponding ELISA kits to detect the expression levels of phosphorylated and total proteins. The Phospho-IRF3 (S386) and Total IRF3 ELISA Kit (cat. no. ab279834) and Mouse STING SimpleStep ELISA Kit (cat. no. ab315058) were obtained from Abcam (Cambridge, United Kingdom). The FastScan™ Phospho-STING (Ser366) ELISA Kit (cat. no. 82083) was purchased from Cell Signalling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA). The p-STING/total STING and p-IRF3/total IRF3 ratios were calculated to normalise phosphorylation signals to the corresponding total protein levels. All ELISA measurements were performed according to the manufacturers’ protocols.


### Quantitative real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction
  RAW264.7 macrophages and primary BMDMs were seeded at 4 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates and cultured overnight for full adherence. Four treatment groups were established: blank control, 2′,3′-cGAMP alone, 2′,3′-cGAMP plus 2/4/8 μM Peptide-1, and 2′,3′-cGAMP combined with 8 μM Astin C. Cells were pre-treated with Peptide-1 or Astin C for 6 h, with vehicle administered to control wells. Afterward, cells were challenged with 1 μM 2′,3′-cGAMP for 6 h43. After treatment, total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, and RNA samples with acceptable A260/A280 ratios of 1.8–2.0 were used for reverse transcription. Reverse transcription was performed with the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to generate complementary DNA. Quantitative real-time PCR was then performed using SYBR Green-based detection on an Applied Biosystems 7500 Real-Time PCR System. Each reaction was prepared with cDNA template, gene-specific forward and reverse primers, SYBR Green qPCR master mix, and nuclease-free water. The amplification program consisted of initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s44. The mRNA expression levels of IFN-β and IL-6 were quantified, with GAPDH used as the internal reference gene. Relative mRNA expression was calculated using the 2–ΔΔCT method. All primer sequences are listed in Table S1.


### Statistical analysis
  Data processing and graphical visualisation were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). All experiments were conducted in biological triplicate, with results presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Data were analysed using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple-comparisons test to compare each treatment group with the cGAMP-stimulated group. Differences with P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.


## Results and discussion

### Identification of STING-targeting peptides by molecular docking
  To systematically identify potential STING-targeting inhibitory peptides, a docking screening workflow was established (Figure 1). Briefly, the peptide library containing 59,319 sequences was first constructed, and structure-based molecular docking against STING was then performed. Based on docking scores and binding-conformation analysis, the top four peptides were selected as candidate molecules. Chemical structures of Peptides 1–4 are presented in Figure 2. The docking scores of Peptides 1–4 and the positive control Astin C are shown in Figure 3. Lower docking scores generally indicate greater predicted binding stability. Astin C showed a docking score of −7.78 ± 0.34 kcal/mol, whereas all four candidate peptides exhibited lower scores. Among them, Peptide-1 showed the most favourable predicted docking score, with the lowest docking score of −12.93 ± 0.47 kcal/mol. Taken together, these results supported the selection of Peptides 1–4 for further in vitro validation.


### MST and SPR analyses define the binding affinity of the STING–peptide interaction
  To validate the reliability of the docking results, the binding affinities of peptides 1–4 and Astin C towards STING were further quantified by MST. The concentration–response curves are presented in Figure S2, and the derived Kd values are summarised in Table 1. MST results showed that Peptides 1–4 all bound to recombinant human STING in vitro, and their binding affinities were quantified using Kd values. Lower Kd values indicate stronger binding affinity. Astin C exhibited a Kd of 0.96 ± 0.08 μM, whereas all four candidate peptides showed lower Kd values, indicating stronger binding to STING than the positive control. Among them, Peptide-1 showed the strongest binding, with the lowest Kd value (0.15 ± 0.01 μM).

  To further characterise the dynamic binding profiles of Peptides 1–4 and Astin C to STING and independently verify the affinity results obtained by MST, SPR was performed for real-time kinetic binding analysis. The Kd values derived from SPR kinetic fitting are summarised in Table S2. Consistent with the MST results, SPR measurements confirmed that Peptides 1–4 could bind to STING with higher affinities than the positive control Astin C. Among them, Peptide-1 showed the lowest SPR-derived Kd among the tested peptides, with a Kd value of 0.32 ± 0.04 μM, indicative of highly stable STING–peptide complexes. Collectively, SPR kinetic data and MST affinity data showed consistent trends. The two independent in vitro binding assays mutually validated the specific molecular interaction between peptides 1–4 and STING, further demonstrating that Peptide-1 possessed the strongest STING-binding ability among all tested peptides.

  To further assess the consistency between the docking results and the experimentally determined binding affinities, linear regression analysis was performed between the predicted binding free energies and the MST-derived Kd values, as shown in Figure 4. An apparent positive association was observed between predicted binding free energy and the MST-derived Kd values, with an R2 value of 0.8424. Overall, lower predicted binding free energies were associated with smaller Kd values, indicating that peptides with greater predicted binding stability generally exhibited stronger binding affinity in vitro. These findings suggest that docking-derived binding free energies may, to some extent, correlate with the experimentally observed binding affinities of peptides towards STING. These results support the use of docking as a preliminary screening strategy for peptide selection.


### Structure–activity relationship analysis
  To elucidate the interaction patterns and structure–activity relationships of Peptides 1–4, their predicted binding conformations in complex with STING were systematically analysed (Figure 5), and the key intermolecular interactions are summarised in Table 2. All four peptides were predicted to occupy the ligand-binding pocket of STING and form non-covalent interactions with key residues. However, the stability and integrity of their interaction networks differed markedly. Peptide-1 formed two hydrogen bonds with Arg238 via its carbonyl group (2.74 Å) and one hydrogen bond with Thr267 through its hydroxyl group (2.97 Å). In addition, it engaged in hydrophobic interactions with Tyr163, Tyr167, Tyr240, Tyr263, and Pro264, while Mg2+ within the binding pocket further stabilised the predicted complex through coordination effects. These interaction features were consistent with the low docking score and high binding affinity of Peptide-1, supporting the relatively strong binding propensity of Peptide-1 towards STING. The absence of hydroxyl groups in Peptides 2–4 precluded hydrogen bonding with Thr267. In addition, structural differences among these peptides resulted in weaker hydrophobic interactions with Tyr163, Tyr167, Tyr240, Tyr263, and Pro264. The general agreement between the docking-based ranking and MST-derived binding affinities suggested that the differential binding of Peptides 1–4 may be related, at least in part, to their distinct interaction patterns within the binding pocket. These results also suggested that spatial complementarity, together with possible Mg2+-associated interactions, may contribute to peptide binding. The more favourable electrostatic and hydrophobic complementarity observed for Peptide-1 may further help explain its relatively stronger binding affinity.


### Conformational dynamics and structural stability of the STING–Peptide-1 complex
  Given the strong binding affinity of Peptide-1 for STING and its well-defined interaction network, MD simulations were performed to evaluate the structural stability and dynamic behaviour of the complex. The results are presented in Figure 6. RMSD was used to evaluate the overall structural stability of the system, as shown in Figure 6(A) and 6(B). The complex RMSD increased rapidly within the initial 20 ns and then reached a stable plateau of 0.35–0.45 nm, with only a modest rise at the late stage of the simulation, suggesting the absence of global conformational collapse or large-scale dissociation. STING displayed relatively smaller fluctuations, predominantly within 0.25–0.35 nm, suggesting that the receptor retained a stable conformation upon Peptide-1 binding. In contrast, Peptide-1 maintained a consistently low RMSD of 0.10–0.18 nm, reflecting strong conformational restriction of the peptide in the bound state. The RMSD frequency distribution further corroborated these findings. The complex, STING, and Peptide-1 all exhibited well-defined and relatively concentrated distribution peaks, with Peptide-1 showing the narrowest profile. This indicates restricted conformational fluctuations of the peptide, likely reflecting stable interactions at the STING binding interface. Rg was used to assess the compactness of STING during the simulation, as shown in Figure 6(C). The Rg of STING in the STING–Peptide-1 system remained largely stable at 1.74–1.76 nm throughout the simulation, with no sustained increasing or decreasing trend, indicating that the overall folding state and spatial compactness of STING were well preserved. Consistently, the Rg distribution (Figure 6D) exhibited a single dominant peak, further supporting the conformational stability of the complex. Consistent with the RMSD analysis, the stable Rg profile suggests that Peptide-1 binding did not induce unfavourable large-scale conformational perturbations in STING, but instead supported complex stabilisation through local conformational accommodation.

  RMSF analysis was performed to evaluate residue-level flexibility during the simulation (Figure 6E and 6F). Most residues exhibited relatively low fluctuations, with higher RMSF peaks observed only in a few loop or terminal regions, indicating that the overall STING structure remained stable. Notably, residues surrounding the binding pocket showed generally low RMSF values, suggesting that Peptide-1 binding contributed to local structural stabilisation. This limited local flexibility may facilitate conformational accommodation at the binding interface, which may be consistent with improved structural complementarity between STING and Peptide-1. Further secondary-structure analysis was performed to monitor the time-dependent conformational behaviour of the protein (Figure 6G). The major secondary-structure elements of STING remained largely stable throughout the simulation, indicating that Peptide-1 binding did not cause substantial perturbation of the overall protein conformation. Intermolecular hydrogen-bond analysis (Figure 6H) showed that STING and Peptide-1 maintained a dynamic hydrogen-bonding network throughout the simulation. Although the number of hydrogen bonds fluctuated over time, 1–4 hydrogen bonds were observed at most time points. These interactions were not rigidly fixed but instead formed a dynamically stable interaction network, which may represent a key contributor to the structural stability of the complex.

  In addition, SASA analysis showed that the surface area of the complex was largely maintained within 110–118 nm2, with the distribution peak centred at 114–115 nm2. This indicates that the complex did not undergo pronounced increases in surface exposure or abnormal exposure of hydrophobic regions during the simulation, further supporting its conformational stability. The stable SASA profile also suggests effective solvent shielding at the Peptide-1–bound interface, which may reduce water-mediated disruption and thereby enhance the stability of interfacial interactions. Collectively, multiple dynamic descriptors, including RMSD, Rg, RMSF, secondary structure, hydrogen bonding, and SASA, consistently support the structural stability of the Peptide-1–STING complex during the simulations. Peptide-1 binding did not induce substantial perturbation of the STING global conformation; instead, complex stability was maintained through limited local flexibility, a stable intermolecular hydrogen-bonding network, and preserved structural compactness. Notably, the slight increase in complex RMSD observed at the late stage of the simulation may reflect local conformational re-equilibration at the binding interface or flexible regions. However, this change was not accompanied by marked alterations in Rg, secondary structure, or SASA, indicating that it more likely represents local conformational fine-tuning under equilibrium conditions rather than complex destabilisation. Overall, the molecular dynamics results suggest that Peptide-1 can form a stable complex with STING and maintain favourable conformational compatibility and interfacial stability within the simulated timescale.


### Energetic basis of STING–Peptide-1 binding revealed by MM/PBSA analysis
  To further elucidate the binding of Peptide-1 to STING from a thermodynamic perspective, MM/PBSA analysis was performed to evaluate the binding free energy and its energetic components (Figure 7). Residue-wise energy decomposition showed that Tyr163, Tyr167, Arg232, Arg238, Tyr240, and Thr263 made favourable contributions to peptide recognition, with Arg238 and Thr263 exhibiting the strongest stabilising effects (Figure 7A). Component-level analysis further indicated that favourable binding was primarily driven by van der Waals and electrostatic interactions, whereas polar solvation made an opposing contribution (Figure 7B). As shown in Figure 7(C), the gas-phase interaction energy was strongly negative (−63.00 ± 0.20 kcal/mol), indicating a substantial intermolecular driving force between the peptide and the protein. In contrast, the solvation free energy was positive (36.00 ± 0.01 kcal/mol), thereby opposing binding. Nevertheless, the total binding free energy remained negative (−27.00 ± 0.20 kcal/mol), suggesting that the binding of Peptide-1 to STING was calculated to be thermodynamically favourable under the simulation conditions.

  The MST-derived dissociation constant was converted into an apparent experimental standard binding free energy using the thermodynamic relationship ΔGexp = RTIn (Kd/1M), where R = 1.987 × 1 0 –3 kcal/mol/K, and T = 298.15 K. Based on the MST-derived dissociation constant for the STING–Peptide-1 interaction (Kd = 0.15 μM), the corresponding apparent experimental standard binding free energy was calculated to be ΔGexp = −9.31 kcal/mol. Compared with the MM/PBSA-derived binding free energy of −27.00 kcal/mol, the MST-derived ΔGexp was substantially less favourable, differing by approximately 17.69 kcal/mol. This discrepancy suggests that MM/PBSA may overestimate the absolute binding strength. Thus, its results should be interpreted primarily as a qualitative assessment of interfacial energetic contributions rather than an exact quantitative reproduction of the experimental binding free energy.

  This discrepancy may arise from inherent differences in the methodological framework and system conditions between MM/PBSA calculations and MST experiments. First, the MM/PBSA approach used here primarily evaluates enthalpic contributions, including van der Waals, electrostatic, and solvation terms, but does not explicitly account for the conformational, rotational, and translational entropy losses associated with peptide binding. Such entropic penalties can be substantial for flexible peptide ligands and may lead to overestimation of the absolute binding strength45. Second, the ionic and solvent environments are not fully matched between the MD simulation and the MST assay. MST was performed in a salt-containing experimental buffer, whereas the simulation system included only counterions required for charge neutralization46. Finally, MM/PBSA estimates binding energetics from conformations sampled within a finite MD trajectory and may preferentially represent stable, tightly bound states. In contrast, MST reports an ensemble-averaged apparent affinity contributed by multiple conformational states in solution47.


### Free energy landscape analysis
  To further characterise the stability and dominant conformational distribution of the STING–Peptide-1 complex, a free energy landscape (FEL) was constructed from the MD trajectory using RMSD and Rg as reaction coordinates (Figure 8). The lowest-energy region corresponded primarily to conformations with RMSD values of approximately 0.35–0.45 nm and Rg values of approximately 1.73–1.75 nm, indicating that this region represented the dominant stable conformational state of the complex (Figure 8A). The corresponding three-dimensional FEL (Figure 8B) illustrated more clearly the energy distribution of the system and revealed a distinct dominant low-energy basin surrounded by relatively uniform high-energy regions. These results suggest that the complex was thermodynamically favoured to remain in this low-energy conformational state. Notably, the low-energy region was not confined to a single, sharply defined basin but displayed moderate extension and local ruggedness, suggesting limited interfacial conformational adjustments or transitions among microscopic substates. This observation is consistent with the preceding RMSD, Rg, RMSF, hydrogen-bonding, and SASA analyses, indicating that Peptide-1 binding to STING is not governed by rigid locking but is instead stabilised through local conformational accommodation and a dynamic interaction network. Overall, the free energy landscape analysis further supports the favourable conformational and thermodynamic stability of the STING–Peptide-1 complex.


### Peptide-1 cytotoxicity and cytokine responses in RAW264.7 cells and BMDMs
  To evaluate the potential cytotoxicity of Peptide-1, cell viability was assessed by the MTT assay in RAW264.7 macrophages and primary BMDMs. Peptide-1 showed no apparent cytotoxicity in either cell type at the highest tested concentration of 10 μM, with IC50 values exceeding 10 μM (Table S3), indicating favourable cellular safety within the concentration range used for subsequent functional assays. Thus, the regulatory effects of Peptide-1 on cytokine production are unlikely to result from non-specific cell damage or cell death.

  Subsequently, ELISA was performed to quantify IFN-β and IL-6 secreted protein levels in the culture supernatants and assess the effect of Peptide-1 on cGAMP-induced inflammatory responses (Figure 9). cGAMP stimulation markedly increased the secretion of IFN-β and IL-6 in RAW264.7 cells and BMDMs. Compared with cGAMP treatment alone, Peptide-1 significantly reduced the supernatant levels of both cytokines in a concentration-dependent manner, with the strongest inhibition observed at 8 μM. These results indicate that Peptide-1 reduced cGAMP-induced IFN-β and IL-6 release, supporting its regulatory effect on STING-mediated inflammatory responses at the protein level.

  Furthermore, qPCR was performed to quantify the mRNA levels of IFN-β and IL-6 and to determine whether Peptide-1 regulates inflammatory cytokine expression at the transcriptional level (Figure S3). Consistent with the ELISA results, cGAMP markedly upregulated IFN-β and IL-6 mRNA expression in RAW264.7 cells and BMDMs, whereas Peptide-1 significantly suppressed the transcriptional activation of both cytokines in a dose-dependent manner. Notably, Peptide-1 exerted the strongest inhibitory effect at 8 μM, exceeding that of the positive control Astin C. Collectively, Peptide-1 exhibited no apparent cytotoxicity under the tested conditions in RAW264.7 cells and primary BMDMs and effectively inhibited cGAMP-induced IFN-β and IL-6 expression at both the secreted protein and mRNA levels. These findings provide cellular evidence supporting Peptide-1 as a potential STING- modulating peptide.


### Peptide-1 attenuates cGAMP-induced phosphorylation of STING and IRF3
  To further assess the effect of Peptide-1 on cGAMP-induced STING-related signalling, ELISA kits were used to quantify the levels of phosphorylated and total STING and IRF3. cGAMP treatment markedly increased the ELISA-derived ratios of p-STING/t-STING and p-IRF3/t-IRF3 in both RAW264.7 macrophages and primary BMDMs, whereas Peptide-1 pre-treatment significantly reduced these phosphorylation ratios in a dose-dependent manner, with the strongest inhibition observed at 8 μM (Figure 10). Similar trends were observed in both macrophage models, suggesting a consistent regulatory effect of Peptide-1 on cGAMP-induced STING and IRF3 phosphorylation. Together with the cytokine ELISA and RT-qPCR results, these findings indicate that the suppression of IFN-β and IL-6 expression by Peptide-1 is associated with reduced STING and IRF3 phosphorylation, providing cellular-level evidence for its STING-inhibitory activity.


## Conclusions
  Using a structure-guided virtual screening strategy, this study identified potential STING-binding peptides from a cyclic peptide library containing 59,319 sequences. By integrating MST, SPR, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, MM/PBSA binding free-energy calculations, and free energy landscape analysis, the binding affinity, interaction profile, and complex stability of the candidate peptides were systematically evaluated. Among the tested peptides, Peptide-1 exhibited the strongest STING-binding profile, with interactions mainly involving hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic contacts, and key interfacial residues. Computational analyses further supported the conformational stability and favourable energetic features of the STING–Peptide-1 complex within the simulated timescale. At the cellular level, Peptide-1 showed no apparent cytotoxicity up to 10 μM in RAW264.7 macrophages and primary BMDMs, while dose-dependently reducing cGAMP-induced IFN-β and IL-6 secretion and mRNA expression. This effect was accompanied by decreased phosphorylation of STING and IRF3, suggesting that Peptide-1 modulates cGAMP-induced STING-related inflammatory and interferon responses. Overall, these findings identify Peptide-1 as a candidate STING-binding peptide with the ability to attenuate cGAMP-induced IFN-β and IL-6 expression, providing a molecular basis for the further development of STING-targeted regulatory peptides.

  This study has several limitations. First, the MM/PBSA and molecular dynamics results primarily reflect conformational and energetic features under specific simulation conditions and should not be interpreted as direct equivalents of absolute experimental binding free energies. In addition, the current findings are based mainly on in vitro binding assays, computational analyses, and cell-based models. Therefore, the in vivo anti-inflammatory activity and pharmacokinetic properties of Peptide-1 require further validation. Overall, Peptide-1 represents a promising STING-binding peptide lead, providing a foundation for the further optimisation and functional investigation of STING-targeted peptide inhibitors.
