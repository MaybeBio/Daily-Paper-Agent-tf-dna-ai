# Development and Usability Testing of an Information Needs‐Based Chatbot for Individuals With Pathogenic Variants in 
BRCA
 Genes in Korea: A Methodological Study


## ABSTRACT
  Korean individuals with BRCA pathogenic/likely pathogenic (P/LP) variants have limited access to genetic counseling due to shortages of genetic professionals and lack of reimbursement. Despite the growing interest in digital health interventions, few digital counseling support tools have been designed to address the diverse information needs of this population. This study aimed to develop a user information needs–based chatbot for providing individualized information for individuals with BRCA pathogenic variants and to evaluate its usability. The chatbot, All about BRCA, was developed using a commercial chatbot platform through three stages from October 2021 to September 2022: identifying information needs via a systematic review and web‐crawling analysis of patient‐generative questions from online communities; developing a prototype chatbot validated by seven healthcare professionals and implementing it as rule‐based system; conducting usability testing with individuals with P/LP variants in BRCA genes, using the modified mHealth App Usability Questionnaire and daily usage diaries. The modified mHealth App Usability Questionnaire scores were analyzed using descriptive statistics, and narrative diary data were analyzed using content analysis. Following iterative rounds of reviews and usability testing, the final chatbot comprised 10 information categories with 62 predefined question‐scenario‐responses. Nineteen participants evaluated the chatbot, yielding a mean modified mHealth App Usability Questionnaire score of 5.7 ± 0.8 out of 7. Scoring on all subscales, that is, ease of use, interface/satisfaction, and usefulness, exceeded 5.5. Responses on narrative diary data were predominantly positive, with users valuing systematic, detailed, and practical information. Key limitations of the chatbot included restricted question recognition and limited information depth. Despite limitations in question recognition, the chatbot demonstrated acceptable usability as a complementary tool to support individuals with BRCA pathogenic variants. It may help address constraints of limited consultation time and genetic expert shortages. Future development should strengthen conversational capabilities.


## Introduction
  Pathogenic/likely pathogenic (P/LP) variants in genes associated with hereditary breast ovarian cancer syndrome (HBOC), such as BRCA1 and BRCA2 (hereafter BRCA), substantially increase the risk of breast, ovarian, and other cancers (Moultrie et al. 2025; Park et al. 2015). Individuals with P/LP variants in BRCA genes often experience anxiety about cancer development and recurrence (Kwiatkowski et al. 2016; Moultrie et al. 2025), decisional conflicts regarding risk‐reducing surgeries and surveillance (Al‐Hilli et al. 2023; Hartmann and Lindor 2016; Yuen et al. 2020), and difficulties disclosing genetic test results to family members (Morgan et al. 2025; Yuen et al. 2020). To address these challenges, genetic counseling has been recommended by multiple professional guidelines (American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists 2017; Korean Breast Cancer Society 2021b; Manahan et al. 2019; National Comprehensive Cancer Network 2026; Pujol et al. 2021) and has been shown to improve genetic knowledge and support informed decision‐making (Siglen et al. 2025). However, the proportion of individuals with P/LP variants in BRCA genes who receive genetic counseling ranges from 18% to 100%, with substantial variability in content and quality (Biesecker et al. 2021; Hoskins and Gotlieb 2017; Vogel et al. 2018). In the Republic of Korea (hereafter Korea), genetic counseling is not reimbursed by the National Health Insurance system and is further constrained by a shortage of trained workforce with genetic expertise, resulting in limited access to genetic care services (Kim, Kong, et al. 2020). Consequently, depending on institutional resources and workforce capacity, various healthcare professionals, such as physicians and nurses, provide genetic counseling (Choi et al. 2025).

  Among individuals with P/LP variants in BRCA genes, information needs constitute the most frequently reported unmet need (Park et al. 2023). Even after receiving counseling, there are persistent misconceptions about the mode of inheritance (Pedrazzani et al. 2021), information gaps, and inaccurate recall due to information delivered verbally (Augestad et al. 2017; Cragun et al. 2026), and misalignment between the information provided during counseling and their individualized needs (Vogel et al. 2018). Patients consistently express a desire for personalized information tailored to their life stage, age, family cancer history, and personal cancer experience to support cancer risk management (Park et al. 2023; Sung et al. 2026; Caiata‐Zufferey et al. 2025). However, the complexity of genetic information and decisions regarding risk‐reducing surgery, combined with limited staffing, time, and infrastructural resources in clinical settings, pose significant challenges to delivering tailored information (Augestad et al. 2017; Vogel et al. 2018). Efficient, patient‐centered information delivery strategies that complement counseling, grounded in patients' specific information needs, may help address these challenges (Moultrie et al. 2025; Vogel et al. 2018; Yuen et al. 2020).

  Chatbots represent a digital conversational agent for delivering standardized, accessible, and potentially personalized genetic information (Schmidlen et al. 2019; Siglen et al. 2019). By enabling interactive communication and providing 24/7, low‐cost access to on‐demand information, chatbots can complement traditional counseling models (Chaix et al. 2019; Divya et al. 2018; Ivanovic and Semnic 2018). Previous studies have demonstrated moderate to high user satisfaction and feasibility of chatbots in hereditary cancer care (Al‐Hilli et al. 2023; Kaphingst et al. 2024; Nadarzynski et al. 2019; Siglen et al. 2021). Chatbots have focused on high‐risk groups screening (e.g., “Gia” and LINE‐based chatbots (Al‐Hilli et al. 2023; Moultrie et al. 2025; Nazareth et al. 2021; Sato et al. 2024; Sato et al. 2021)) or providing genetic testing information (e.g., “Rosa”) (Siglen et al. 2019; Siglen et al. 2021). Some support pre‐visit genetic counseling, including informed consent, genetic testing result delivery, family information sharing, and follow‐up scheduling (e.g., “MyCode”) (Morgan et al. 2025; Schmidlen et al. 2022; Schmidlen et al. 2019). However, their development and use in the genetic care context of different countries remain limited. This study describes the development and initial usability testing of a user information needs–based chatbot for individuals with P/LP variants in BRCA genes in Korea, designed for use across both pre‐ and post‐genetic counseling phases to address unmet information needs.


## Materials and Methods
  The chatbot was developed through three stages from October 2021 to September 2022: identifying user information needs, developing a prototype chatbot, and conducting usability testing with individuals with P/LP variants in BRCA genes (Table 1). Given the limited methodological guidance and rigorous evaluation of healthcare chatbots (Siglen et al. 2021), this study adopted a structured, stepwise development and evaluation process.


### Identifying User Information Needs
  As the first step in chatbot development, we identified the information needs of individuals with P/LP in BRCA genes, by employing a two‐step approach: establishing an analytic framework for information needs and analyzing patient‐generated questions from online communities.


#### Establishing an Analytic Framework for Information Needs
  We conducted a systematic review to capture the breadth and depth of information needs of the target population through a comprehensive search of five databases (MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL, Cochrane, and PsycINFO) and a manual search conducted up to October 6, 2021. The detailed methods and results of this review have been reported previously (Park et al. 2023). The review identified nine categories of information needs among families harboring P/LP variants in BRCA genes. After excluding three categories outside the chatbot's intended scope (i.e., psychological issues, decision‐making support for risk‐reducing options, and cancer treatment and prognosis), the remaining six categories were subsequently reorganized to align with the chatbot's informational scope. Specifically, broad categories were sub‐divided into more specific informational domains. For example, the category of cancer risk‐reducing strategies was divided into the cancer prevention strategies, risk‐reducing salpingo‐oophorectomy, and risk‐reducing mastectomy. Similarly, the category of personalized cancer risk was divided into non‐surgical risk‐reducing options and disclosure of genetic information to family members. This reorganization resulted in a total of 9 categories, which served as the initial analytic framework for the subsequent analysis of patient‐generated online questions.


#### Analysis of Patient‐Generated Online Questions
  Given that individuals with P/LP variants in BRCA genes frequently seek information through online sources (Yuen et al. 2020), we collected and analyzed patient‐generated questions from online communities using web‐crawling methods (Kumar et al. 2017). Web‐crawling is an automated data retrieval process that systematically collects vast amounts of data from the internet by following hyperlinks to assist in the creation of a search engine's index (Kumar et al. 2017). Using Listly (https://www.listly.io/), we automatically retrieved questions containing the keyword “BRCA” from NAVER online communities, the most widely used search engine platform in Korea, over a five‐year period (April 1, 2017, to April 1, 2022).

  These patient‐generated questions were categorized using a refined analytic framework based on findings of the systematic review. Within each category, patient‐generated questions addressing similar information needs were grouped and synthesized into predefined questions. For example, within the category of non‐surgical risk‐reducing options, questions related to cancer surveillance methods for women and men and pharmacological prevention strategies were organized into separate predefined questions. This process resulted in an initial set of 32 predefined questions derived from the analytic framework. For example, the patients' question, “When can I resume daily activities after oophorectomy?” was classified to the predefined question “Risk‐reducing bilateral salpingo‐oophorectomy” based on the analytic framework; and the predefined question “What is the cost of BRCA genetic testing?” was categorized under “Genetic testing.” For patient‐generated questions that were not adequately addressed by the existing predefined questions (e.g., preparation for surgery and eligibility for private insurance enrollment), 14 additional predefined questions were developed (Table 1, Table 2). Consequently, at this stage, the chatbot content comprised 46 predefined questions organized into 10 information‐needs categories (Table 1, Table 2).


### Development of the Prototype Chatbot

#### Building the Chatbot Content Database
  Based on the identified information needs, a content database was developed, comprising entity keywords; predefined questions; and predefined responses (Table S1); and scenarios that structured conversation flow by matching user queries to entity keywords and triggering the corresponding predefined question and response, or a fallback response when user queries failed to be recognized or matched with entity keywords or predefined questions (Figure S1).

  To ensure the reliability of the information provided to patients by the chatbot, scenarios and responses were developed based on national and international clinical guidelines and manuals, including the 2nd Breast Genetic Counseling Manual (Korean Breast Cancer Society 2021a), the 9th Korean Breast Cancer Treatment Guidelines (Korean Breast Cancer Society 2021b), NCCN Hereditary/Familial High‐Risk Assessment Guidelines (National Comprehensive Cancer Network 2021), the European Cancer Society Clinical Guidelines for BRCA Testing (Pujol et al. 2021), and peer‐reviewed literature. For geographically specific information (e.g., costs of surgery and hospitals providing genetic testing services in Korea), data were independently retrieved from official government and hospital websites.


#### Validating the Chatbot Content Database
  To evaluate the appropriateness of the chatbot content database, seven healthcare professionals (two physicians specializing in HBOC, one researcher specializing in hereditary cancer, and four genetic counseling nurses) reviewed the chatbot components between June 12 and July 17, 2022. The components included 46 predefined questions, responses, and scenarios, which were rated on a 4‐point Likert scale (1 = not appropriate at all, 4 = very appropriate). The Item Content Validity Index (I‐CVI), defined as the proportion of healthcare professionals rating each item as 3 or 4, was calculated to assess adequacy. Items achieving an I‐CVI ≥ 0.78 were considered acceptable for inclusion in the chatbot content (Polit and Beck 2006).


#### Prototype Development
  Using the validated content database, a prototype chatbot was developed on a commercially available platform in Korea. The Kakao i Open Builder was selected because KakaoTalk is the major platform accounting for approximately 96% of the smartphone messenger market in Korea (Kim, Lee, and Chang 2020). The platform operates as a rule‐based system that employs natural language processing to match entity keywords in user‐generated questions with predefined responses (Kim, Lee, and Chang 2020). The chatbot was developed to be device agnostic, that is, accessible across multiple devices, including smartphones, tablets, and computers.

  The chatbot interface consisted of a text input field for user questions; a persistent menu (i.e., always‐accessible interface) displaying all 10 information categories; and related topic buttons below each response (Figure 1). To address the limited information capacity of the chatbot, users could access a dedicated online community (https://cafe.naver.com/oncologygenenurse) through a “more information” button. Chatbot responses were delivered in three formats: text (≤ 1000 characters), images (≤ 3 MB), and cards (image–text combinations, ≤ 400 characters) with web links (Figure 1).


### Usability Testing

#### Participants and Recruitment
  Usability testing was conducted from July 25 to August 6, 2022. Individuals with P/LP variants in BRCA genes were recruited using convenience sampling through advertisements posted in an online cancer community (Breast Cancer Story on the NAVER platform) and through snowball sampling. Eligibility criteria included: 19–65 years old; confirmed carrier of a P/LP variant in one of the BRCA genes; with or without a BRCA‐related cancer diagnosis; using KakaoTalk. Participants received a token of appreciation (mobile coupon worth KRW 50,000; approximately USD 34) after completing usability testing.


#### Data Collection and Analysis
  Participants were able to use the chatbot for three consecutive days. They were instructed to ask at least three questions per day and to complete usage diaries daily after each use. On day three, they were asked to complete a usability questionnaire.

  To evaluate usability testing, the mHealth App Usability Questionnaire (MAUQ) was used (Zhou et al. 2019). The questionnaire was independently translated into Korean by two bilingual researchers. Disagreements were reconciled through consensus, as described in the literature (Presser et al. 2004). The MAUQ originally consists of 18 items across 3 subscales (Zhou et al. 2019). We removed item “I could use the app even when the Internet connection was poor or not available” as not applicable, since the chatbot requires an internet connection to function. The final 17‐item questionnaire assessed ease of use (5 items), interface and satisfaction (7 items), and usefulness (5 items) using a 7‐point Likert scale (1 = not at all, 7 = very much), with higher scores indicating greater usability (Zhou et al. 2019). Descriptive statistics (means and standard deviations) were calculated using SPSS software (version 30; IBM Corp., NY, USA).

  Daily usage diaries documenting overall chatbot evaluation, information‐seeking topics, useful information obtained, user expectations regarding chatbot, and chatbot improvement needs were analyzed. Diary data (narrative data) was analyzed with content analysis (Elo and Kyngäs 2008). Responses were iteratively coded and categorized using MAXQDA software (version 2022; VERBI Software GmbH, Berlin, Germany), and frequencies were calculated to summarize patient‐reported issues.


### Ethical Consideration
  This study was approved by the institutional review board of Yonsei University Health System, Severance Hospital (IRB No. 4‐2021‐1709). All study participants, including healthcare professionals and patients, provided electronic informed consent.


## Results

### Information Needs of Individuals With 
BRCA PVs
  Through web crawling of NAVER online communities, k = 6024 questions were identified. From these questions, k = 2562 questions irrelevant to BRCA‐related health issues and k = 642 duplicate entries were excluded, yielding k = 2820 unique questions for subsequent analysis and chatbot content development (Figure S2). Analysis of these questions led to the refinement of the initial framework, resulting in 10 categories of information needs and 46 predefined questions (Table 2). Based on the refined framework, the chatbot content database was constructed. The final categories included: BRCA genes; genetic testing; cancer prevention strategies; risk‐reducing salpingo‐oophorectomy; risk‐reducing mastectomy; non‐surgical risk‐reducing options; disclosure of genetic information to family members; cancer risk for family members; legal and ethical issues; and sources of reliable information (Table 2).


### Development of the Prototype Chatbot
  I‐CVI scores of the healthcare professionals who reviewed the initial chatbot content database (k = 46 predefined questions‐responses sets) ranged from 0.86 to 1.00, indicating acceptable content validity (Table 2). Based on their feedback and observed high fallback response rates during prototype construction, iterative revisions (more than 4 rounds) were conducted to enhance clarity, comprehensiveness, accuracy, and question recognition capability. These revisions expanded the database from the initial k = 46 to k = 60 predefined questions and responses, and from 496 to 852 entity keywords.


### Usability Testing

#### Characteristics of Participants
  Of the 21 patients recruited and enrolled in this study, two participants dropped out due to inability to complete the three‐day chatbot usage or the usability questionnaire. Consequently, 19 female participants with P/LP variants in BRCA genes completed usability evaluation and were included in this analysis. The age of the 19 participants ranged from 30 to 56 years (Table 3). Most participants had a college education or higher (n = 17), were married (n = 16), and had either breast (n = 15) or ovarian cancer (n = 4). Although individuals without a cancer diagnosis were eligible for participation, no unaffected BRCA carriers (previvors) were recruited. Of those with breast cancer, 11 had undergone risk‐reducing mastectomy, and 9 had undergone or were planning to undergo risk‐reducing salpingo‐oophorectomy (RRSO). None of the participants with ovarian cancer had undergone or were planning to undergo risk‐reducing surgery, as all had already undergone salpingo‐oophorectomy as part of their ovarian cancer treatment, and none were considering prophylactic mastectomy. The mean time from receipt of genetic test results to participation was 36.0 ± 25.0 months (range: 0–99).


#### Usability Evaluation
  Table 4 presents the item‐level results of the usability evaluation using the MAUQ. The overall mean MAUQ score was 5.7 ± 0.8 (possible range: 1–7), and all three subscale means exceeded 5.5: ease of use (5.9 ± 1.4), usefulness (5.7 ± 1.3), and interface and satisfaction (5.5 ± 1.4). Across subscales, items such as ease of use, learnability, and support for treatment‐related decision‐making received the highest ratings (≥ 6.1), whereas finding information when questions were entered incorrectly and the chatbot's ability to understand user questions scored comparatively lower (4.0–4.6), suggesting areas for further refinement.


#### Feedback From Daily Use Diaries and Identified Issues
  Table 5 presents the results of the content analysis of the narratives collected through the daily usage diaries. Frequencies reported (f) represent the number of similar comments in diaries across all participants. The overall evaluation was predominantly positive (f = 27), with participants noting systematic and comprehensive information that would be useful for newly diagnosed patients. However, eight comments reported limited usefulness due to lack of novel information and question recognition limitations.

  The most frequently asked questions concerned cancer prevention methods (f = 57), family cancer risk and management (f = 30), and BRCA‐related knowledge (f = 26). Participants particularly valued specific, practical information not readily available elsewhere, including screening limitations (e.g., transvaginal ultrasound has limitations for detecting ovarian cancer), surgical costs and outcomes (e.g., endometrial cancer possibility after RRSO), nationwide genetic testing hospitals, family inheritance probability (e.g., 50% chance of passing the BRCA P/LP variants to offspring), prevalence of BRCA pathogenic variants (e.g., 1 in 300–800 individuals), and employment discrimination protection (e.g., legal protection against insurance and employment discrimination based on BRCA status).

  Participants wished for more detailed professional information (f = 27), including a list of physicians specializing in hereditary cancer, targeted preventive medications (e.g., tamoxifen), and survival data. The most frequently mentioned improvement need was enhanced question recognition capability (f = 21), followed by increased information depth and scope expansion (f = 18), and user experience improvements (f = 8) (e.g., text and figure alignment, slow loading speed when connecting to external web site, and lack of suggested questions upon input).


### Distributing the Chatbot
  To address fallback issues identified during the usability evaluation, the chatbot content was further refined and expanded. The final version comprised k = 62 predefined questions and responses up from 60 in the usability‐tested version and 1069 entity keywords. The final version of the chatbot was made public under the name “All about BRCA” in September 2022.


## Discussion
  This study developed and evaluated “All about BRCA,” a Korean‐language, user information needs–based chatbot designed to support individuals with BRCA P/LP variants across both pre‐ and post‐genetic counseling phases. The chatbot, comprising 10 information categories and 62 predefined question–scenario–response sets, demonstrated acceptable usability, with users valuing its systematic and practical information delivery, although limited question recognition remains a challenge.

  The chatbot developed in this study has distinctive features compared to existing chatbots developed for hereditary cancer. In contrast to other chatbots that primarily focus on pre‐test genetic education (Kaphingst et al. 2024; Yi et al. 2025), this chatbot comprehensively addresses real‐world information needs of individuals with P/LP variants in BRCA genes across both pre‐ and post‐genetic testing phases, covering not only commonly reported topics, such as genetic testing and cancer risk management, but also practical concerns including communication of test results to children, insurance, and legal and ethical issues (e.g., private insurance enrollment and employment discrimination according to personal data protection). A recent systematic review (Webster et al. 2023) reported that among seven studies that used five distinct chatbots for genetic risk assessment and counseling, only one was available in non‐English (Spanish). Thus, our study contributes to the literature by offering a chatbot available in Korean and grounded in the needs of Korean patients regarding hereditary cancer.

  This has important implications for the Korean healthcare context. In Korea, genetic counseling is not reimbursed by the National Health Insurance, and consultation times are typically limited to 3–5 min (Kim, Lee, and Chang 2020; Park et al. 2025), restricting access to genetic counseling and comprehensive and tailored information delivery. Individuals with P/LP variants in BRCA genes often make decisions on risk‐reducing surgeries and genetic testing with insufficient information (Park et al. 2024; Park et al. 2025). The chatbot developed in this study, consistent with previous research findings (Siglen et al. 2021; Siglen et al. 2025), provides information without time or location constraints, enabling patients to prepare for counseling and engage in more efficient discussions within limited consultation time, while also meeting their information needs even after genetic counseling (Siglen et al. 2021). Furthermore, standardized information delivery through the chatbot may help alleviate the workload of genetic counseling workforces (Siglen et al. 2021; Siglen et al. 2025).

  Acceptable MAUQ scores and positive feedback from daily usage diaries indicate that the chatbot can also support the information needs as a complement to genetic counseling. High scores on items such as “helped with treatment decisions” and “was appropriate for obtaining BRCA‐related information” suggest that the chatbot has the potential to function as a decision‐support tool, rather than merely an information delivery tool. These findings are consistent with previous research showing that web‐ and/or chatbot‐based education may enhance patient understanding and support decision‐making in genetic counseling contexts (Al‐Hilli et al. 2023; Morgan et al. 2025; Yi et al. 2025; Katapodi et al. 2018; Baroutsou et al. 2023).

  Narrative data collected from diaries indicated that participants valued practical information relevant to the Korean healthcare milieu, such as costs of risk‐reducing surgeries and genetic testing for their family, availability of genetic testing hospitals, insurance coverage, and employment discrimination protection. These findings support prior studies highlighting the importance of integrating patients' real‐life and institutional contexts into hereditary cancer information tools (Morgan et al. 2025; Moultrie et al. 2025; Siglen et al. 2025). Furthermore, this study suggests that information provision for individuals with P/LP variants associated with cancer predisposition should move beyond basic medical facts to include context‐specific localized and actionable information that patients can apply in their daily lives.

  Narrative data also showed that while participants were satisfied with the systematic and in‐depth information provided by the chatbot, they simultaneously expressed ongoing needs for “more in‐depth and specialized information” and “psychological counseling”, which revealed a gap between information depth and emotional needs. These technical and emotional limitations suggest that chatbots have value as tools that provide standardized information and can complement genetic counseling, which requires nuanced, real‐time responses to complex and dynamic patient concerns (Morgan et al. 2025; Siglen et al. 2025). At the same time, the findings indicate that chatbots should not be considered a replacement for formal genetic counseling, particularly for individuals experiencing psychological distress, decisional uncertainty, or psychosocial concerns that require individualized assessment and support from trained healthcare professionals. Nevertheless, the chatbot may be valuable as an informational support tool. Considering that participants in the usability evaluation had already lived with a BRCA diagnosis for some time, the perceived usefulness of the chatbot may be even greater among individuals newly diagnosed with P/LP variants, suggesting its potential applicability as a supportive intervention in the early phase following diagnosis.

  Taken together, this study provides several implications for clinical practice, particularly in genetic counseling for individuals with P/LP variants in BRCA genes. First, integrating information needs–based chatbots into genetic counseling pathways can support patients in continuously accessing reliable, evidence‐based information, particularly in settings where consultation time is limited. Second, this study demonstrates that structuring chatbot content based on patient‐generated questions, rather than relying solely on expert‐driven content organization, can enhance the relevance of digital health interventions. This patient‐centered development methodology may be applicable to other clinical contexts beyond hereditary cancer. Third, for broader clinical application, technical advancement, including improved natural language processing performance, is needed to develop a system that can respond more flexibly to diverse forms of patient questions.

  Despite these strengths, the chatbot exhibited technical limitations. Fallback issues persisted despite continuous expansion of entity keywords and responses, likely due to the rule‐based architecture's difficulty in handling typographical errors, lengthy sentences, spacing variations, and ambiguous expressions. Prior studies have also reported similar challenges, such as fallback rates of approximately 43% in rule‐based chatbots (Siglen et al. 2021). To enhance conversational flexibility and accuracy, future chatbots could consider hybrid approaches that integrate large language models with retrieval‐augmented generation while maintaining the reliability of medical information (Maryamah et al. 2024). In addition, the relatively small number of only female participants (n = 19) that evaluated the usability of the chatbot limits the generalizability of findings. As all participants had a personal cancer history, usability among previvors, who may have distinct informational needs, remains untested. Future studies should evaluate the chatbot's usability and effectiveness among previvors, as well as other underrepresented groups such as males or groups with low digital literacy who are not familiar with smartphones or digital platforms, through large‐scale randomized controlled trials. The study lacks comparative data on nonuser characteristics, a limitation that has been reported previously (Webster et al. 2023), although evaluating the effectiveness of the chatbot compared to existing counseling methods (usual care) was beyond the scope of this methodological study. Finally, as evaluation was limited to short‐term usability, the effects on long‐term usage experience, behavioral changes, and patient health outcomes were not confirmed. Future studies should examine real‐world utilization and user interaction patterns following chatbot implementation and evaluate the long‐term impact of chatbot use on clinical outcomes, such as actual genetic testing uptake or preventive surgery decisions.


## Conclusions
  This study developed a user information needs–based chatbot for individuals with P/LP variants in BRCA genes, which demonstrated acceptable usability despite limitations in question recognition inherent to the rule‐based system. The findings suggest that the chatbot has the potential to function not only as an information delivery tool but also as a decision‐support tool in healthcare settings with limited access to genetic counseling and genetic expertise. Future studies should examine long‐term effects on clinical outcomes in larger and more diverse populations.
