# Uncovering the mechanism of DCLK3 in colorectal cancer progression through WGCNA and machine learning


## Background
  Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a prevalent malignant tumor of the digestive tract with high morbidity and mortality rates. Although doublecortin like kinase 3 (DCLK3) is upregulated in CRC and promotes disease progression, its specific mechanisms remain unclear.


## Methods
  Core factors of CRC were identified through bioinformatics analysis, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), and machine learning algorithms. The expression level of DCLK3 and ELK1 were evaluated using bioinformatics databases (GSE142279, The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)) and experimental validation (quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR), Western blot). Functional assays (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay, flow cytometry, Transwell assay, wound healing assay, sphere formation assay, tube formation assay, in vivo model, hematoxylin and eosin staining (HE), and immunohistochemistry (IHC)) and mechanism studies (prediction via Genecards and JASPAR databases, dual-luciferase reporter assay, and chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)) were performed to validate and explore the molecular mechanism of DCLK3 in CRC.


## Results
  Bioinformatics analysis combined with WGCNA and machine learning algorithms identified DCLK3 as a core gene. DCLK3 was significantly upregulated in CRC, and its high expression was associated with poorer overall survival in patients. Knockdown of DCLK3 inhibited the proliferation of CRC cells, promoted apoptosis, and suppressed cell invasion, migration, cancer stemness, and angiogenesis. ETS like-1 protein (ELK1) promoted malignant phenotypes in CRC cells by transcriptionally activating DCLK3. Also, in vivo studies demonstrated that ELK1 activated DCLK3 to facilitate CRC tumor growth.


## Conclusion
  DCLK3, identified through bioinformatic analysis, promotes CRC progression via its transcriptional activation by the transcription factor ELK1, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target.

## Introduction
  Colorectal cancer (CRC), commonly referred to as bowel cancer, is a malignancy originating in the colon or rectum [1]. It ranks among the leading causes of cancer-related incidence and mortality worldwide [2]. The risk of developing CRC increases with age, and the disease is often characterized by an asymptomatic early phase and slow progression [3]. Prognosis is highly dependent on the stage at diagnosis [4]. Therefore, elucidating the underlying mechanisms of CRC pathogenesis and identifying novel molecular targets are of paramount importance [5].

  Doublecortin like kinase 3 (DCLK3) is a serine/threonine protein kinase that plays critical roles in various physiological processes [6]. The structure and function of DCLK3 make it particularly important in neuronal development, as it can influence neuronal migration, differentiation, and synaptic plasticity [7, 8]. In recent years, DCLK3 has been widely recognized as an oncogene, as it is highly expressed in multiple malignant tumors and associated with poor prognosis. For example, DCLK3 was linked to poor prognosis in gastric cancer patients by regulating ferroptosis and mitochondrial function [9]. Additionally, a study reported that DCLK3 was upregulated in CRC and promoted disease progression [10], though its specific mechanisms of action remained unclear.

  As a member of the ETS transcription factor family, ETS like-1 protein (ELK1) participates in key cellular processes including proliferation, apoptosis, differentiation, and cell cycle regulation [11]. Dysregulation of ELK1 is closely associated with cancer development, where it promotes tumor growth [12], enhances cancer cell invasion and migration [13], and induces therapy resistance [14]. Studies reported that elevated ELK1 expression accelerated breast cancer cell growth and was linked to poor patient prognosis [15]. In pancreatic cancer, ELK1 drove malignant progression by inducing aberrant expression of cyclin-dependent kinases regulatory subunit 2 [16]. Furthermore, in endometrial cancer, ELK1 transcriptionally activated glutathione peroxidase 4 to suppress ferroptosis, thereby promoting disease progression [17]. In this study, website-based predictions indicated the presence of binding sites for ELK1 in the DCLK3 promoter, and ELK1 was shown to induce the activation and transcription of DCLK3. However, it remains unclear whether ELK1 exerts its role in the development of CRC by mediating DCLK3.

  This study seeks to elucidate the regulatory mechanism of DCLK3 in the malignant progression of CRC through in vitro and in vivo experiments, thereby providing new therapeutic targets for CRC treatment.


## Materials and methods

### Database analysis
  Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were screened from the GSE142279 dataset in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) under the criteria of P < 0.05 and |log₂ fold change| > 1. For differential expression analysis, the raw count matrix was evaluated using the DESeq2 R package, which incorporates size factor normalization (median-of-ratios method) to correct for differences in sequencing depth. Expression of DCLK3 in CRC tissues was evaluated using the The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov). Potential binding sites for the transcription factor ELK1 were predicted using the GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org) and JASPAR (https://jaspar.elixir.no) databases.


### Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)
  To systematically investigate gene co-expression patterns and identify functional modules significantly associated with traits, this study performed WGCNA based on the expression profiles of 6,000 preselected genes across 20 samples. First, by evaluating the scale-free topology fit index (R²) and mean connectivity under different soft-thresholding powers (β), the parameters satisfying scale-free topology (R² = 0.858, β = 22) were selected to construct an unsigned adjacency matrix. Subsequently, the adjacency matrix was converted into a Topological Overlap Matrix (TOM) to more robustly characterize the connection similarities between genes. Hierarchical clustering was performed using TOM-based dissimilarity, and the dynamic tree-cutting algorithm was applied to identify co-expression modules, with key parameters including minimum module size (minModuleSize = 30), deepSplit control (deepSplit = 2), PAM stage (pamStage = TRUE), and merge cut height for modules (mergeCutHeight = 0.25), ultimately yielding 23 modules. After calculating the module eigengene (ME) for each module, modules with P < 0.05 and |correlation coefficient| > 0.3 were defined as significantly correlated modules. For the key modules, hub genes with high module membership (MM/kME > 0.8) and gene significance (GS > 0.2) were further screened for subsequent analyses.


### Machine learning
  The R package glmnet (version 4.1-8) was employed to perform least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) logistic regression on the candidate genes. Feature selection was performed through 5-fold cross-validation (k = 5), guided by the optimal lambda.min value. The Support Vector Machine-Recursive Feature Elimination (SVM-RFE) algorithm was executed with 5-fold cross-validation (k = 5) and halve.above parameter configured to 100, utilizing the R package e1071 (version 1.7–16). Genes yielding the highest accuracy or smallest error were selected for subsequent analysis. The random forest algorithm was executed using the R package randomForest (version 4.7.1.2). Random forest models were trained using 5-fold cross-validation for each tree number configuration, with test set error rates averaged across folds. The optimal tree number was identified by minimizing this average error rate, enabling screening of significant genes from the optimized model.


### Human samples
  Tumor tissues (n = 39) and matched adjacent normal tissues (n = 39) were collected from 39 CRC patients (53 ± 11.2 years) at The First Affiliated Hospital of Henan University of Medicine. Ethical approval was obtained from the The First Affiliated Hospital of Henan University of Medicine Ethics Committee, with all participants providing written informed consent.


### Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)
  Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Solarbio, Beijing, China) and reverse transcribed into cDNA with the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Shiga, Japan). After the forward and reverse primers, SYBR Green qPCR Mix (Takara), and template cDNA were mixed, amplification was performed according to the qRT-PCR protocol. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) served as the internal control, and the 2-ΔΔCt method was utilized to calculate gene expression. The required primers are as follows:DCLK3 Forward: CAGGACCCGAGGAGAAGAGA.DCLK3 Reverse: GTCTGCACTCCTTCACGACA.GAPDH Forward: AAGCCTGCCGGTGACTAAC.GAPDH Reverse: GCATCACCCGGAGGAGAAAT.


### Western blot
  After thorough lysis with radio-immunoprecipitation assay buffer (Yeasen, Shanghai, China), the lysate was mixed with loading buffer (Yeasen) and boiled to complete protein denaturation. Following electrophoresis, proteins were transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). After blocking for 1 h, membranes were incubated with anti-DCLK3 antibody (1:500, PA5-75969, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), anti-ELK1 antibody (1:1000, RAB02304, Abnova, Taiwan, China), and anti-GAPDH antibody (1:5000, ab8245, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), followed by incubation with goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:10000, 31460, Invitrogen) or goat anti-mouse IgG (1:5000, 31430, Invitrogen). Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescent (ECL) developing solution (Beyotime).


### Cell culture and treatment
  CRC cell lines (SW620, HCT-116, SW480, and LoVo) and normal human colonic epithelial cells (NCM460) were provided by Sunncell (Wuhan, China). NCM460 cells were maintained in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 Medium (RPMI-1640; Gibco, Rockville, MD, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Beyotime). SW620, HCT-116, SW480, and LoVo cells were cultured in their respective cell-specific media (Sunncell). Culture conditions were maintained at 37℃ with 5% CO₂. Cells were treated under different conditions as required by the experiments.


### Small interfering RNA (siRNA) and overexpression vector establishment
  The siRNAs targeting DCLK3 (si-DCLK3) and ELK1 (si-ELK1), the overexpression vectors of DCLK3, and the corresponding negative controls (si-NC) were constructed and synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) was used to carry out transfection following the manufacturer’s specifications.


### (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay
  Cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 103 cells per well in a 96-well plate. Once the cells adhered, they were treated accordingly. MTT solution (Beyotime) was added to the cells and the plates were incubated at 37℃ in the dark for 4 h. Subsequently, Formazan solubilization solution (Beyotime) was dispensed, and incubation was continued. After confirming the complete dissolution of formazan crystals by observation, the optical density (OD) was recorded at a wavelength of 490 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA).


### Apoptosis assay
  Apoptosis was assessed using an Apoptosis Assay Kit (Beyotime). After trypsinization, cells were centrifuged and the supernatant was discarded. The cell pellet was then resuspended in the Annexin V-fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) binding buffer provided with the kit, followed by staining with propidium iodide (PI; Beyotime) and Annexin V-FITC. The mixture was gently vortexed and incubated at room temperature for 15 min in the dark. Following incubation, apoptosis was analyzed using flow cytometry (BD, Franklin, New Jersey, USA).


### Transwell assay
  For invasion assays, matrigel (Corning, Corning, NY, USA) was applied to coat the upper Transwell chamber (Corning), with the lower chamber filled with complete medium (Gibco). After matrigel had fully solidified, the cell suspension was added to the upper chamber. After 24 h of culture, cells were first fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime), then rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Beyotime), and finally stained with crystal violet (Beyotime) for 10 min. Following air-drying, the slides were examined under a microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).


### Wound healing assay
  Using a 200 µL sterile pipette tip, two to three straight scratch lines were created on the SW480 and LoVo cells monolayer in a pretreated 6-well plate. To remove detached cells, the cultures were washed with PBS and then maintained in serum-free or low-serum medium (Gibco). Scratch images were immediately captured under an inverted microscope (Olympus). After 24 h of culture, the same field of view was re-photographed to document cell migration.


### Sphere formation assay
  A single-cell suspension was prepared and seeded into ultra-low attachment 6-well plates. The plates were then incubated for 2 weeks. Beginning on day 7 of culture, spheroid formation was observed daily under a microscope (Olympus). On day 14, the number of spheroids in each well was counted.


### Tube formation assay
  After seeding in 6-well plates, SW480 and LoVo cells were cultured in serum-free medium (Gibco) for 24 h when they reached 70%-80% confluence. The supernatant was collected and used to prepare tumor-conditioned medium. Pre-chilled 96-well plates were coated with 50 µL of Matrigel (Corning) per well and incubated at 37℃ for 30 min to polymerize. Once the gel solidified, HUVEC (Sunncell) single-cell suspension and tumor-conditioned medium were added. The plates were then incubated for 12 h. Photographs were taken using a microscope (Olympus) at an appropriate field of view.


### Dual-luciferase reporter assay
  The pGL3 vector (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) was used to construct both the mutant luciferase reporter plasmid (MUT-DCLK3) and the wild-type reporter plasmid (WT-DCLK3), containing the DCLK3 promoter region. These reporter plasmids were co-transfected into cells with either the siRNA targeting ELK1 (si-ELK1) or the empty vector control (si-NC). After 48 h of culture, the Dual-Lucy Assay Kit (Solarbio) was employed to determine luciferase activity.


### Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)
  A commercial ChIP Assay Kit (Beyotime) was employed for ChIP experiments. The cell pellet was collected and lysed in 500 µL of ice-cold cell lysis buffer. Nuclei were pelleted by centrifugation and subsequently lysed in 300 µL of nuclear lysis buffer. The chromatin was sheared into fragments ranging from 200 to 1000 base pairs using sonication. The lysate was then incubated overnight with either an anti-ELK1 antibody (1:20, RAB02304, Abnova) or a control IgG (1:500, MAB14943, Abnova) antibody. Antibody-chromatin complexes were incubated with Protein A/G agarose beads for 2 h. Following washes, the beads were eluted, and the cross-links were reversed by adding NaCl. Proteins were then degraded, and the DNA was purified for subsequent PCR validation.


### Establishment of a stable ELK1 knockdown LoVo cell line
  The short hairpin RNA targeting human ELK1 (sh-ELK1), the overexpression vector (OE-DCLK3), and the non-targeting negative control shRNA (sh-NC) were synthesized and cloned into the pLKO.1-puro lentiviral vector (GenePharma). Recombinant lentiviral plasmids were co-transfected into 293T cells with the packaging plasmids psPAX2 and pMD2.G using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen). At 48 h post-transfection, the lentivirus-containing supernatant was collected, centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 min, and filtered through a 0.45 μm cellulose acetate membrane (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, USA) to remove cell debris. LoVo cells were then seeded into 6-well plates and cultured until they reached 50–60% confluence. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with the collected lentiviral supernatant in the presence of 8 µg/mL polybrene (Sigma-Aldrich) to enhance transduction efficiency. After 24 h of incubation, the medium was replaced with fresh complete growth medium. At 48 h post-transduction, the medium was replaced with selection medium containing 2 µg/mL puromycin (Sigma-Aldrich). The selection medium was replaced every 2 days for 10 days to eliminate non-transduced cells and establish stable clones.


### Mouse models
  BALB/c nude mice (male, 4–6 weeks old, weighing 18–22 g) were obtained from SPF Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The mice were housed under standard controlled environmental conditions (22 ± 2 ℃, 50–60% humidity, and a 12 h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to sterilized food and water. Following a one-week acclimation period, the animals were randomly assigned into three experimental groups (n = 5 per group). The stringent protocols for animal care, systematic randomization, and in vivo outcome evaluations were optimized in accordance with established preclinical in vivo methodologies [18, 19].For the subcutaneous xenograft model, LoVo stable cell lines were generated using lentiviral vectors. Cells stably expressing sh-NC, sh-ELK1, or sh-ELK1 together with oe-DCLK3 were harvested during the logarithmic growth phase and resuspended in sterile PBS. Subsequently, 100 µL of the prepared cell suspension (1 × 10⁶ cells per injection) was subcutaneously inoculated into the right inguinal region of each mouse. Tumor dimensions were closely monitored using a digital vernier caliper starting from day 8 post-injection and subsequently at regular 5-day intervals. Tumor volume in mice was measured starting from day 8 post-injection and subsequently at regular 5-day intervals. At 28 days post-injection, mice were euthanized to minimize suffering in accordance with animal welfare guidelines. The animals were placed in a euthanasia chamber and euthanized via CO2 inhalation (displacement rate of 10–30% chamber volume/minute). After confirming deep unconsciousness and cessation of breathing and heartbeat, cervical dislocation was performed as a secondary physical method to ensure death. All procedures were performed in compliance with the national guidelines for the welfare and ethical review of laboratory animals (GB/T 35892 − 2018). All animal procedures were performed following approval from the The First Affiliated Hospital of Henan University of Medicine Ethics Committee.


### Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining
  Tumor tissue was fixed with formaldehyde (Merck, Shanghai, China) and embedded in paraffin (Solarbio), then sectioned into 5-micron-thick slides. Subsequently, sections underwent dewaxing with xylene (Merck) and rehydration with a gradient ethanol solution (Merck). Following hematoxylin and eosin (Beyotime) staining, sections were dehydrated and cleared for observation.


### Immunohistochemistry (IHC)
  After tumor tissue was sectioned, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies against Ki-67 (1:200, ab16667, Abcam), matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9; 1:1000, ab76003, Abcam), and DCLK3 (1:5000, PA5-83509, Invitrogen). Subsequently, a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (1:1000, ab6721, Abcam) was applied to the sections. Signal detection was performed using 3,3’-dihydrochloride of diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride (DAB; Gene Tech, Shanghai, China), followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin (Beyotime) for 2 min. Finally, sections were mounted and examined under an optical microscope (Olympus).


### Statistical approach
  GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) was employed for the visualization of experimental data. For comparisons between two independent groups, the Student’s t-test was used, whereas one-way analysis of variance was employed for comparisons involving three or more independent groups. For the survival analysis, patients were dichotomized into high- and low-expression groups based on the median expression value of DCLK3. The Log-Rank test was subsequently used to compare survival differences between the groups. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. As this survival analysis was exclusively focused on validating the prognostic value of a single predefined target gene, no multiple testing correction was conducted.


## Results

### Bioinformatic screening for DEGs in CRC
  The GSE142279 dataset, which comprises high-throughput mRNA sequencing profiles from 20 CRC tissues and their matched controls, was analyzed. A total of 4,160 DEGs were identified. Volcano plots and heatmaps were generated to visualize the up- and down-regulated DEGs (Fig. 1A-B). Additionally, a distinct heatmap was constructed to highlight the DEGs with the most pronounced fold changes (Fig. 1C).

### Identification of co-expression modules and gene selection using WGCNA
  To identify co-expression modules and pivotal genes in CRC, WGCNA was applied to the GSE142279 dataset. A soft thresholding power of 22 was determined for the 10 CRC tissues and their matched normal controls (Fig. 2A). Hierarchical clustering was performed using TOM dissimilarity, and the dynamic tree cut algorithm was employed to identify co-expression modules, resulting in 23 distinct modules (Fig. 2B). A heatmap of module-trait relationships revealed that the red, blue, and turquoise modules were significantly associated with the tumor phenotype (Fig. 2C). Based on the analysis of module membership and gene significance in the red module, 83 hub genes were identified (Fig. 2D). Intersecting these hub genes with the previously identified DEGs from GSE142279 yielded 14 overlapping genes (Fig. 2E), providing candidate molecules for further investigation in CRC.

D Scatter plot of gene significance versus module membership in the red module. E Overlap between WGCNA-identified genes and DEGs from GSE142279


### Machine learning-based screening of key genes in CRC
  The 14 pre-selected genes underwent machine learning analysis. LASSO regression identified 8 genes through feature selection: dual specificity tyrosine phosphorylation regulated kinase 3 (DYRK3), phospholipid phosphatase 4 (PLPP4), ST6 N-acetylgalactosaminide alpha 2,6-sialyltransferase 5 (ST6GALNAC5), fibroblast growth factor 14 (FGF14), transforming growth factor beta 2 (TGFB2), docking protein 5 (DOK5), brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), and DCLK3 (Fig. 3A). The SVM-RFE algorithm evaluated the relationship between the number of features and model performance, ultimately selecting 3 genes: PLPP4, COL22A1, and DCLK3 (Fig. 3B). In the random forest analysis, the optimal number of trees was set to 10 based on cross-validation results, leading to the selection of 10 important genes: DCLK3, parathyroid hormone like hormone (PTHLH), PLPP4, DYRK3, DOK5, proline rich 16 (PRR16), COL22A1, FGF14, HECT, C2 and WW domain containing E3 ubiquitin protein ligase 2 (HECW2), and TGFB2 (Fig. 3C). The intersection of genes identified by the three algorithms yielded two candidate key genes, PLPP4 and DCLK3 (Fig. 3D), which were selected for further investigation in CRC.

### DCLK3 is highly expressed in CRC
  To evaluate the expression patterns of these two candidate genes, the GSE142279 dataset was analyzed. The results showed that the mRNA expression levels of both PLPP4 and DCLK3 were significantly elevated in CRC tissues compared with normal tissues (Fig. 4A-B). Analysis of TCGA data further confirmed a marked increase in DCLK3 mRNA levels in CRC samples (Fig. 4C). Consistent with this, DCLK3 mRNA levels were significantly higher in CRC tissues compared to normal controls (Fig. 4D). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis of the clinical cohort (high DCLK3 expression, N = 20; low DCLK3 expression, N = 19) demonstrated that elevated DCLK3 expression was significantly associated with a shorter overall survival in CRC patients (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, DCLK3 protein expression was markedly elevated in tumor samples compared to matched normal samples (Fig. 4F), and was also significantly higher in CRC cell lines than in normal cell lines (Fig. 4G). In summary, DCLK3 is significantly upregulated in CRC.

### Knockdown of DCLK3 suppresses malignant phenotypes in CRC cells
  To investigate the impact of DCLK3 on CRC, siRNA-mediated knockdown of DCLK3 was performed to assess functional alterations in cancer cells. Western blot analysis confirmed that si-DCLK3 effectively reduced DCLK3 protein expression in both SW480 and LoVo cells (Fig. 5A). Silencing DCLK3 in these cell lines significantly suppressed cell proliferation, promoted apoptosis, and inhibited cell invasion and migration capabilities (Fig. 5B-E). Furthermore, DCLK3 knockdown impaired the stem-like sphere-forming ability and angiogenic capacity of SW480 and LoVo cells (Fig. 5F-G). Overall, silencing DCLK3 markedly suppresses malignant biological behaviors in CRC cells.

### ELK1 transcriptionally activates DCLK3 expression
  To explore the role and underlying mechanism of ELK1 in CRC, ELK1 expression was examined in clinical specimens. The results demonstrated that ELK1 expression was markedly upregulated in CRC tissues compared with adjacent normal tissues (Supplementary Figure A). Moreover, ELK1 expression showed a significant positive correlation with DCLK3 expression (Supplementary Figure B). To further elucidate the regulatory relationship between ELK1 and DCLK3, bioinformatic predictions using the GeneCards and JASPAR databases revealed that the transcription factor ELK1 can bind to the promoter region of DCLK3, suggesting that ELK1 may be involved in the transcriptional activation of DCLK3 (Fig. 6A-B). Western blot analysis confirmed that transfection with si-ELK1 significantly reduced ELK1 protein levels in SW480 and LoVo cells (Fig. 6C). Subsequent dual-luciferase reporter assays demonstrated that ELK1 knockdown markedly decreased the luciferase activity of the WT DCLK3 promoter, while the MUT promoter activity remained unchanged (Fig. 6D-E). ChIP assays revealed a significant enrichment of the DCLK3 promoter fragment in the ELK1 antibody group compared to the IgG control, confirming the direct binding of ELK1 to this regulatory region (Fig. 6F). Furthermore, silencing ELK1 led to a significant downregulation at both the mRNA and protein levels of DCLK3 (Fig. 6G-H). Collectively, these results indicate that the transcription factor ELK1 directly binds to the DCLK3 promoter and acts as a positive regulator of its expression.

### ELK1 promotes malignant phenotypes in CRC cells by regulating DCLK3
  To validate the functional role of the ELK1-DCLK3 axis in CRC, a rescue experiment was performed by overexpressing DCLK3 in SW480 and LoVo cells following ELK1 knockdown. Western blot analysis confirmed that DCLK3 protein expression was significantly decreased in the si-ELK1 group but restored in the si-ELK1 + OE-DCLK3 group (Fig. 7A). Functional assays demonstrated that ELK1 silencing suppressed cell proliferation, promoted apoptosis, and inhibited invasion and migration; these effects were reversed by subsequent DCLK3 overexpression (Fig. 7B-E). Furthermore, tumor sphere formation and tube formation assays revealed that the impaired sphere-forming ability and angiogenic capacity induced by si-ELK1 was also markedly rescued by DCLK3 overexpression (Fig. 7F-G). Taken together, ELK1 promotes malignant phenotypes in CRC cells by upregulating DCLK3 expression.

### In vivo validation of the ELK1-DCLK3 axis on CRC tumor growth
  To elucidate the oncogenic role of the ELK1-DCLK3 axis in CRC, a xenograft model was established. First, the knockdown efficiency of ELK1 was verified. Compared with the sh-NC control group, the ELK1 protein level in the sh-ELK1 group was significantly decreased (Fig. 8A). Both the tumor volume and weight were significantly reduced after ELK1 knockdown. This tumor-suppressive effect was reversed by DCLK3 overexpression (Fig. 8B-C). Western blot results showed that ELK1 knockdown significantly reduced the protein expression of ELK1 and DCLK3 in tumor tissues, whereas DCLK3 overexpression reversed the protein expression of both ELK1 and DCLK3 (Fig. 8D-E). Furthermore, HE and IHC staining demonstrated that ELK1 knockdown led to reduced cell density, a decreased proportion of Ki67-positive cells, and diminished expression of MMP9 and DCLK3 in tumors. All these inhibitory effects were significantly reversed following DCLK3 overexpression (Fig. 8F). In conclusion, the in vivo data confirm that ELK1 promotes tumor growth, proliferation, and invasion in CRC by upregulating DCLK3.

## Discussion
  CRC is a malignant tumor originating from the distal part of the digestive tract. It is closely associated with modern lifestyles and dietary habits [20, 21]. While early-stage detection often leads to high cure rates, advanced disease is correlated with poor prognosis [22, 23]. Therefore, it is critically important to investigate the pathogenesis and progression of CRC and to identify novel molecular targets. Through bioinformatic analysis, DCLK3 was identified as a candidate gene associated with CRC. Further analysis of the GSE142279 dataset revealed a significant upregulation of DCLK3 expression in CRC, suggesting its potential role as a biomarker for this disease.

  DCLK3 is a serine/threonine protein kinase with adenosine triphosphate-binding and catalytic activity, functioning in intracellular signal transduction and peptidyl-serine phosphorylation [7]. While research on DCLK3 in oncology remains limited, it is widely recognized as an oncogene. Previous studies have demonstrated that the A allele of the non-coding variant rs1800734 disrupts AP-4 transcription factor binding, thereby elevating DCLK3 expression through long-range chromosomal interactions and promoting CRC progression [10]. The present investigation further demonstrated that DCLK3 was consistently upregulated in clinical CRC specimens and cell lines. Functional assays confirmed that DCLK3 knockdown potently inhibited malignant phenotypes such as proliferation, invasion and migration, stemness, and angiogenesis, concomitantly inducing apoptotic cell death.

  ELK1 is a critical transcription factor that plays an important role in tumors [24]. In breast cancer, ELK1 promoted cell cycle progression by activating kinesin family member 26B transcription [25]. Additionally, ELK1 regulated glutathione metabolism through mediation of kinesin family member C1, thereby enhancing breast cancer cell proliferation [26]. In glioblastoma, ELK1 promoted glutamine metabolism by activating glutamate dehydrogenase 1 transcription [27]. In lung adenocarcinoma, ELK1 facilitated epithelial-mesenchymal transition and tumor progression through upregulation of B7-H3 [28]. These studies collectively demonstrated the oncogenic function of ELK1 as a transcription factor. This study confirmed that ELK1 directly bound to the DCLK3 promoter region and promoted malignant phenotypes in CRC cells by activating DCLK3 expression. Further validation through in vivo animal models confirmed that the ELK1-DCLK3 axis promoted tumor growth, proliferation, and invasion.

  Despite the robust demonstration of upstream transcriptional regulation of DCLK3 by ELK1 and their functional synergy through extensive in vitro and in vivo rescue experiments in the present study, the precise downstream signaling pathways responsible for the tumorigenic effects of DCLK3 in CRC remain to be directly validated via in vitro protein assays. However, a recent study indicated that DCLK3 drives tumor progression by upregulating TCF4 and its downstream target genes c-Myc and Cyclin D1 [9]. The activation of these classical proliferation-associated proteins is highly consistent with the enhanced cell proliferation and increased Ki67 expression observed in our ELK1-DCLK3 axis model. Therefore, further studies will be conducted to identify the downstream phosphorylation cascades, aiming to comprehensively elucidate the mechanism of the ELK1-DCLK3 axis in CRC.

  In addition to the direct regulation of intrinsic pathways within tumor cells, the tumor microenvironment (TME) plays a central role in modulating immune responses. The TME is a highly dynamic ecosystem composed of various immune cells, cytokines, and tumor-associated factors, all of which play important roles in regulating immune responses and determining therapeutic efficacy. For instance, studies have shown that overactivated signaling cascades remodel the TME by altering cytokine profiles and recruiting immunosuppressive immune cells, thereby limiting the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors [29]. Similarly, key drivers of cancer stemness may negatively correlate with anti-tumor immune infiltration, actively shaping the immune microenvironment [30]. Furthermore, recent studies have demonstrated that advanced targeted therapies, such as antibody-drug conjugates, can induce immunogenic cell death and successfully convert the colorectal cancer microenvironment into an inflammatory, immune-responsive state [31]. The present study shows that DCLK3 promotes angiogenesis and tumor invasion, processes that are closely associated with TME remodeling. Aberrant activation of the ELK1-DCLK3 axis likely also contributes to the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment, which may limit the efficacy of immunotherapy. Therefore, future research should not only focus on intracellular signaling but also strive to elucidate how DCLK3 regulates the cytokine secretion profile and immune cell infiltration within the tumor microenvironment.

  In conclusion, through bioinformatic analysis, this study identifies DCLK3 as a key regulatory factor in CRC and demonstrates that the transcription factor ELK1 promotes malignant phenotypes by inducing DCLK3 expression. These findings confirm that the ELK1-DCLK3 regulatory axis represents a molecular marker for CRC development and progression, providing a theoretical basis for clinical diagnosis and treatment.
