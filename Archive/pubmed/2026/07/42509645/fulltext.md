# ATF4, ZIK1, and PNMA3 Transcriptomic Signatures Predict Chemoradiotherapy Response in Cervical Cancer


## Background
  Cervical cancer (CC) is a main malignancy affecting women globally, with a high mortality rate. Chemoradiotherapy resistance is a significant factor in determining treatment outcomes, and prognostic biomarkers are essential for the appropriate selection of therapeutic strategies. This study aimed to identify gene expression signatures that may allow for follow‐up treatment response in CC patients.


## Methods
  Samples of non‐stem CC cells selected by Fluorescence‐activated Cell Sorting from 21 Responders and 10 Non‐responder patients, who attended the Mario Penna Institute (Belo Horizonte—Brazil) from August 2017 to May 2018, and submitted to Next‐generation RNA sequencing, were analyzed to evaluate differential gene expression profiles between both groups. Machine Learning and statistical analyses were performed to identify a signature related to clinical outcomes.


## Results
  2670 Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with log2FoldChange ≤ −1 or ≥ 1 and adjusted p‐value ≤ 0.05 were found. Of these, 1591 were protein‐coding, but only 8 were considered relevant based on machine‐learning and statistical analyses for classifying treatment response. However, only PNMA3 and ZIK1 were markedly upregulated in non‐responder patients. Among the genes upregulated in Responder patients, only ATF4 and ATP1B3 were identified by machine‐learning analysis. On the other hand, only ATF4, PNMA3, and ZIK1 were associated in network interactions, more specifically to apoptosis pathways, and the differential expression of ATF4 and PNMA3 occurred exclusively in cervical cancer non‐stem cells, in the presence of ZIK1.


## Conclusions
  We propose that upregulation of PNMA3 and downregulation of ATF4, specifically in cervical cancer non‐stem cells, is associated with upregulation of ZIK1 and with resistance to apoptosis and chemoradiotherapy. Therefore, suggesting that these genes may serve as potential signatures for CC prognosis.


## Introduction
  Cervical Cancer (CC) is considered the fourth most common female malignancy and the fourth cause of cancer death among women worldwide. In 2022, an estimated 662,301 new cases occurred, along with approximately 348,874 deaths. The disease significantly impacts women's health, with a forecast of a 14.8% increase in new cases by 2030, based on 2022 rates [1]. The distribution of CC differs worldwide, highlighting the significant disparities in incidence and mortality between developed and developing countries [2]. The morbidity and mortality rates in high‐income countries have declined, primarily due to adequate infrastructure for prevention, diagnosis, follow‐up, and treatment [3].

  The most common histological subtypes of CC are squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) and adenocarcinoma (AC), accounting for approximately 80% and 20% of invasive CC, respectively [4]. Early diagnosis increases the likelihood of successful treatment and directly impacts patient survival rates. When detected early, the 5‐year survival rate for patients with invasive CC is 92%. At the stage where CC has already spread to surrounding tissues or organs and regional lymph nodes, the 5‐year survival rate is 58%, and for distant metastases, it is only 17% [5]. Improving survival remains the main aim of treatment. Therefore, it varies according to the stage of cancer, the presence of metastases, or recurrence [6]. One of the obstacles to reaching this is, in part, cisplatin resistance (CPR), which can be either intrinsic or acquired. Additionally, studies have demonstrated that the molecular mechanisms underlying the development of CPR are complex and may be associated with specific characteristics, including increased DNA damage repair, activation of the epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT), and inhibition of apoptosis [7]. Furthermore, despite advances in treatment, the recurrence rate for this cancer remains high, at around 15% for FIGO stages IB–IIA and over 25% for more advanced stages. Therefore, there remains a need for prognostic methods with greater specificity for this cancer, in line with the WHO's 90‐70‐90 strategy for eliminating this disease in the coming years [8, 9].

  In this context, this study aimed to explore Differentially Expressed Genes (DEGs) involved in the response to chemoradiotherapy in cervical cancer non‐stem cells (CCNSCs) to identify new prognostic signatures for this cancer, thereby contributing to appropriate drug repositioning and the choice of therapeutic strategy.


## Material and Methods

### Patient Recruitment and Samples Selection
  Samples from 31 women, with an average age of 52.3 ± 16.8 years (range 24–82), diagnosed with CC at the Mário Penna Institute (Belo Horizonte, Brazil), were obtained from August 2017 to May 2019. To be included in this study, the patients should fulfill the following criteria: (a) histopathological diagnosis of adenocarcinoma or squamous cell carcinoma; (b) no history of cancer or any immune diseases; (c) no prior treatment history; (d) provided written informed consent. The patients underwent chemoradiotherapy first diagnosis and biopsy, and after the subsequent diagnosis and biopsy; histopathological and clinical data were collected (Table 1). The following criteria were used to classify patients as responders (R) or non‐responders (NR) to chemoradiotherapy: (1) Patients were classified as R if no cervical neoplastic lesions were observed up to 8 months after the end of treatment. (2) Conversely, they were classified as NR when any neoplastic lesions were detected within 8 months of treatment completion, which included patients with partial response (PR), progression disease (PD) or stable disease within this group. Clinical exams (vaginal, pelvic, and abdominal), pathological analyses, and imaging exams were considered to confirm the patient's treatment response. The study was approved by the local Institutional Review Board (CAAE: 82703418.8.0000.5121).


### Fluorescence‐Activated Cell Sorting (FACS) and Sequencing Library Preparation
  Fluorescence‐activated cell sorting (FACS) was used to isolate cervical cancer stem‐like cells (CCSCs) and CCNSCs from a complex mixture of tumor cells based on their light‐scatter and fluorescent staining profiles. Selected cells were processed with the SMART‐Seq v4 Ultra‐low Input RNA Sequencing Kit (Takara Bio USA, CA) according to the manufacturer's instructions for cDNA synthesis. For RNA sequencing library preparation, the Nextera XT Library Prep Kit (Illumina) and Nextera XT Index Kit V2 Set A (Illumina) were used according to the manufacturer's instructions. These processes were described in detail by our group [10]. The sequences are available in the SRA database (PRJNA812529).


### Morphometric Analysis
  Initially fixed in 10% buffered formalin, the cervical biopsy specimens were processed into paraffin blocks and stored at room temperature. The histopathology reports of cervical biopsies were issued by the same pathologist. Twenty‐three patients were included in this histological evaluation (Figure S1). To ensure a comprehensive evaluation of the entire tumor area, histological sections stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) were viewed using a 40× objective on a trinocular Nikon Eclipse microscope (Nikon), and 20 images were scanned with a Nikon micro‐Camera DS‐Ri2 (Tokyo, Japan). The freeware ImageJ v1.33 and the counter plug‐in downloaded from the NIH website (http://rsb.info.nih.gov/ij) were used for image analysis [11]. Lymphocytes and plasma cells in the intra‐tumoral and stromal‐tumoral regions were manually counted. Each picture was 4908 × 3264 pixels (365.18 μm × 242.86 μm). The profile of Tumor‐infiltrating Lymphocytes (TIL) was evaluated intra‐tumoral (iTIL) and stromal region (sTIL) separately and together (tTILs). For eosinophils counting (Tumor‐associated tissue eosinophilia—TATE), the cells that showed bright‐red granules within their cytoplasm and a nucleus with one or two lobes in 20 high‐power fields (HPF) were considered. These analyses were executed by a researcher blinded to the groups examined. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 9 (GraphPad Software, United States) [12]. Differences were considered statistically significant when p < 0.05.


### Statistical Analysis

#### Pre‐Processing of Transcriptome Data
  Our group previously analyzed CCSCs [10]. For the CCNSC analysis, the quality of the sequence was assessed using the FastQC tool [13]. Low‐quality reads (Phred score ≤ 25) in the 3′ end and adapters were filtered using the tool Trimmomatic with parameters TRAILING:25 and ILLUMINACLIP:2:30:10:adapter_fasta_file, respectively [14]. After trimming, the remaining sequences were reanalyzed with FastQC to confirm the required quality score. Lastly, they were mapped and aligned to the human genome ( Homo sapiens  GRCh38) using the STAR software (version 2.7.10b) [15] and the GTF file (version 113) for the reference human genome GRCh38 obtained from the ENSEMBL website (https://www.ensembl.org/Homo_sapiens). The alignment parameters “outFilterMultimapNmax 1” and “quantMode GeneCounts” were included to generate uniquely mapped reads and count the number of reads mapped to each specific gene. The raw read counts were filtered with HTSFilter [16] and then normalized with DESeq2 [17], both with default settings.


#### Differential Gene Expression (DGE) Analyses
  Differential gene expression (DGE) analyses were performed using DESeq2 with maximum‐likelihood estimates (MLE) and the Wald test. Only genes with log2 fold change (Log2FC) ≥ 1 or ≤ −1 and adjusted p‐value (padj) ≤ 0.05 were considered differentially expressed comparing 21 R to 10 NR patients to chemoradiotherapy. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were visualized as heatmaps using Pheatmap (version 1.0.13) in R [18]. For this purpose, the default settings were used, with hierarchical clustering of rows (genes) and columns (patients/samples) based on Euclidean distance. In addition, the data were internally scaled by rows‐genes (scale = “row”), and the z‐score values for each gene in each sample (patient) were plotted on the heatmap according to the following formula: z=x−μσ  z—z score value; x—normalized gene expression count; 𝞵—mean of the normalized counts for a given gene across the samples; 𝞼—standard deviation of the normalized counts for a given gene across the samples. Genes are upregulated when z‐score ≥ 0 and downregulated when z‐score ≤ 0.


#### Selection of the Candidate Gene Expression Signatures
  To identify genes as potential signatures for predicting resistance or sensitivity to chemoradiotherapy in CCNSCs, four different methods and strategies were employed: Treating all protein‐coding genes as attributes and all 21 R and 10 NR samples as instances, a decision tree analysis was performed to identify the DEGs that best classify the patients as Responder and Non‐responder to chemoradiotherapy. The Leave‐one‐out cross‐validation (LOOCV) was applied to estimate the classification accuracy and test the model's generalizability, and the Full training approach was also evaluated. The sensitivity and specificity values for this model, with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), were calculated using the confusion matrix values obtained from the decision tree; these values are: a—number of true positives, b—number of false negatives, c—number of false positives, and d—number of true negatives;The 50 DEGs most predictive for correctly classifying patients into a target class (R or NR) were also selected based on the highest values for average merit and average rank metrics, which measure the information gain and the ranking position of the attributes (genes) based on this merit score, respectively. As with decision tree analyses, LOOCV was applied to this model to evaluate it;Based on the hierarchical clustering observed in the heatmaps generated previously, and considering the clusters that best grouped patients into R or NR, the DEGs that were upregulated (z‐score ≥ 0) or downregulated (z‐score ≤ 0) in at least 70% of the patients in the evaluated cluster and had opposite expression profiles in at least 70% of all other patients were used in new decision tree analyses. The aim was to select, with greater specificity, those genes that best classify patients into R or NR. Only protein‐coding DEGs were selected for this assessment;To identify those genes that were clearly upregulated or downregulated in R or NR patients, only those DEGs with values of z‐score ≥ 1 or ≤ −1 in at least 50% of the patients belonging to one of these groups were selected.

  For all previously mentioned analyses, the normalized counts from DESeq2 were used. Decision Tree (DT) and Information Gain (IF) analyses were performed using the WEKA software (Waikato Environment for Knowledge Analysis, version 3.6.11, University of Waikato, New Zealand) with parameters: J48 ‐C 0.25 ‐M 2 (default) for DT and ‐N50 (top 50 DEGs) for IG, respectively [19]. Sensitivity, specificity, and 95% confidence intervals were calculated using the diagnostic test feature of MedCalc Software Ltd. (Version 23.2.1) [20], based on values obtained from the confusion matrix generated by WEKA.


#### Interaction Networks and Survival Analyses of Candidate Signature Genes
  The software QIAGEN Ingenuity Pathway Analysis [21] was used to identify interaction networks related to the regulation of cancer hallmarks for those genes selected as potential factors in predicting treatment response. In addition, the tools from the KmPlotter Pan‐cancer RNA‐seq database were used to estimate overall survival (OS) and recurrence‐free survival (RFS) using data from cervical squamous cell carcinoma (CESC), comprising 304 samples [22, 23]. For this purpose, the previously selected DEGs were used as input, and parameters were estimated for a 5‐year survival threshold, with no restrictions on FIGO stage, histological grade, or any other factors. Results were considered statistically significant where the log‐rank p‐value was ≤ 0.05, and there was a clear separation between the survival curves.


#### Validation in External Cohorts
  To validate our differential gene expression results and the machine learning‐based selection methods, a set of samples from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database was evaluated (Table 2). Clinical data for the patients were filtered to retain only samples with treatment outcome information (Supporting Information). The raw gene counts were subjected to the same filtering and normalization analyses as our data, using the HTSFilter and DESeq2 tools, respectively (Supporting Information). Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed using z‐scores calculated from the normalized counts of all remaining genes after removing those with non‐significant counts. Four different PCAs were performed. The first and second evaluated the samples from the TCGA‐CESC and CGCI‐HTMCP‐CC projects separately regarding their classification as R (complete response) or NR (partial response or progressive disease). The third evaluated the TCGA samples collectively, and finally, the joint evaluation of our samples with the external cohort.

  To validate the results from the PCA analyses and potential gene signatures, DGE analyses were performed on samples from the TCGA and our cohort. Samples identified as complete response (CR) were classified as responders (R), whilst those with partial response (PR) or progressive disease (PD) were classified as non‐responders (NR). Samples subject to chemoradiotherapy were identified as CCR, and when FIGO stage IIIC or higher samples were excluded, they were classified as IIIC‐IV. Comparisons were made considering both all genes present in either sample set (mergeT—union set) and only those genes common to both (mergeF—intersection set).

  To confirm any differences between our samples and those in the external database, DGE analyses were carried out. A total of nine comparisons were carried out: one comparing only NR samples from the CGCI‐HTMCP‐CC project with our NR samples; a second comparing the R groups; and a third measuring the difference between the sample groups, plus the effect of response or non‐response to chemoradiotherapy on the data sets. The same strategies were applied to both the TCGA‐CESC data alone and the combined data from both projects.

  All these analyses were carried out separately, from gene filtering through to machine learning‐based selection methods, taking into account the differences between the sample sets used for each comparison and the number of genes evaluated in each. All TCGA samples with missing or inconsistent data were removed from the analysis. All possibilities of differential gene expression were evaluated considering our samples and the external cohort. The machine learning methods were validated only for comparisons in which the genes previously selected as potential signatures for response to chemoradiotherapy were differentially expressed.


## Results

### Individuals
  The general characteristics of patients were previously described in Table 1 and by Zuccherato et al. [10].


### Preprocessing of Transcriptome Data
  The total number of raw reads obtained from RNA‐Seq, the trimmed reads after removing low‐quality sequences and adapters, as well as sequences uniquely mapped to a single gene, were quantified (Table S1). Similarly, the raw counts for all genes in the GTF annotation file (version 113) to which reads mapped, as well as the normalized counts following the removal of genes with negligible counts, were provided (Tables S2 and S3). A total of 78,932 genes were quantified, and 72,733 remained after filtering and normalization.


### DEGs in Cervical Cancer Non‐Stem Cells (CCNSCs) Populations
  In the DGE analysis, 2670 DEGs were identified when comparing 21 R patients with 10 NR patients (Figure 1A and Table S4). Among all DEGs, there were 1591 (~59.59%) protein‐coding genes, 125 (~4.68%) lncRNAs, and 954 (~35.73%) other ncRNAs and pseudogenes (Figure 1B). Most protein‐coding genes were upregulated, while lncRNAs, pseudogenes, and other ncRNAs were downregulated. The z‐scores were calculated for all genes remaining after normalization (Table S5).


### Selection of the Candidate Gene Expression Signatures
  In the decision tree analysis using all 1591 protein‐coding DEGs found as input, the genes ATF4 and ATP1B3 correctly classified all samples as R and NR in Full Training mode. However, only around half of the samples were correctly classified using LOOCV mode, with considerable sensitivity but low specificity (Tables S6 and S7). Among the 50 genes with the highest information gain for correctly classifying patients as R or NR, ATF4 was identified as the second‐best classifier, whilst ATP1B3 was ranked seventh (Table S8). Based on hierarchical clustering of DEGs and samples, three distinct patient groups with distinct gene expression profiles were identified (Figure 1A). The first group comprised 6 NR patients (063, 121, 103, 104, 048, and 109) with a set of genes that were upregulated in this cluster but downregulated in all other samples. The second group comprised 7 R patients (045, 135, 055, 034, 010, 117, and 037), also exhibiting a profile of gene overexpression among themselves, but with low expression in all other samples. Finally, a third group comprising all 18 remaining patients (4 NR and 14 R) showed clear downregulation of virtually all DEGs.

  When selecting upregulated DEGs (z‐score ≥ 0) in at least 70% of patients classified as Group 1, but downregulated in all others, only 34 protein‐coding genes were identified. The decision tree results for this set classified the majority of patients correctly in Full training mode, but only about half using LOOCV, with low specificity (Tables S6 and S9). Meanwhile, for group 2, which best clustered R patients, 961 protein‐coding genes were identified as upregulated using the same selection criteria. The decision tree again identified ATF4 and ATP1B3 as the best predictors of whether patients belonged to the R or NR group, achieving the highest accuracy with LOOCV and the highest sensitivity and specificity (Figure 2 and Tables S6 and S10).

  Finally, to identify genes that were clearly differentially expressed between the majority of R patients and NR patients, DEGs with a z‐score of ≥ 1 or ≤ −1 in at least 50% of the samples belonging to each of these groups were selected. Of these, only 6 out of the 29 identified were protein‐coding genes (Table S11). A heatmap of all DEGs selected by the four described strategies was generated (Figure 3).


### Interaction Networks Between the Selected Genes and Their Impact on Patient Survival
  IPA results demonstrated that the protein ATF4 is involved in regulating apoptosis across various cell types, including cervical cancer cells. Direct activation of PRKN by ATF4 was found to directly inhibit PNMA3. Furthermore, several miRNAs that target these genes in common, as well as ZIK1, were identified (Figure 4). In addition, PNMA3 and ZIK1, which were predominantly upregulated in NR patients in group 1 and downregulated in all other groups, have been shown to play roles in the regulation of EMT, cell proliferation and migration, immune cell infiltration, and metastasis. However, their roles in these pathways still need to be characterized experimentally (Figure 5).

  Among the genes selected as potential predictors of response to chemoradiotherapy in CCNSCs, ATF4 did not show a significant impact on OS or RFS in an external cohort of 304 patients, as assessed using tools from the Kmplotter database. However, at high expression levels, this gene was characterized as a protective factor (hazard ratio [HR] < 1), increasing the probability of survival (Figure 6A,B). Conversely, PNMA3 significantly increased the probability of overall survival (Figure 6C) at high expression levels (HR < 1 and log‐rank p ≤ 0.05), although it had no impact on RFS (Figure 6D). Just like PNMA3, ZIK1 significantly affected OS (Figure 6E) at high expression levels, acting as a protective factor (HR < 1 and log‐rank p ≤ 0.05), but not the RFS (Figure 6F).


### PCA Results for External Cohort Validation
  The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) explained the largest proportion of variance among samples from the CGCI‐HTMCP‐CC project. However, PC1 and PC2 accounted for only a small proportion of the total variance, precluding the identification of distinct clusters of responder and non‐responder patients to the treatment (Figure S2 and Supporting Information). Although a better clustering of patients into R or NR groups and a higher proportion of total variance accounted for by these components were observed compared with the TCGA‐CESC data (Figure S3 and Supporting Information). The PCA using samples from both TCGA‐CESC and CGCI‐HTMCP‐CC yielded similar results, but the majority from CGCI‐HTMCP‐CC were concentrated within the range −100 to −25 along the PC1. On the other hand, samples from TCGA‐CESC were predominantly located between 0 and 50 along the same axis (Figure S4). In contrast, a clear distinction was observed between all TCGA samples and those in our cohort, with PC1 explaining a significantly higher proportion of variance (Figure S5).


### Differential Gene Expression (DGE) for External Cohort Validation
  A total of 72 comparisons were made between R and NR samples, considering only data from the CGCI‐HTMCP‐CC, or only samples from the TCGA‐CESC separately, as well as between these two sample sets and between all these external cohort samples and our cohort (Table 3 and Supporting Information). The gene PNMA2, a member of the Paraneoplastic Ma Family (PNMA), was identified as a DEG in six of the comparisons evaluated: four of these involved only samples from the CGCI‐HTMCP‐CC project, and two involved only samples from the TCGA‐CESC. On the other hand, PNMA5 was recognized in 13 DGE analyses, six involving only TCGA‐CESC samples and seven incorporating data from this project as well as that from the CGCI‐HTMCP‐CC. Nonetheless, PNMA3 was differentially expressed in 14 comparisons involving our samples and all samples in the TCGA database, and in each case ZIK1 was also detected as a DEG. Moreover, ZIK1 was identified as a DEG alongside ATF7‐NPFF and PNMA8A in four analyses, whilst it was detected without any members of the PNMA family or of the Activating Transcription Factor (ATF) gene family on three other occasions, and only once in the presence of the ATF3 and ATF4 genes. Finally, ATF4 was found in four comparisons involving all our samples and those from the TCGA, all of which had undergone chemoradiotherapy. In three of these, both ZIK1 and PNMA3 were present, and in one, only ZIK1 and the gene ATF3 were present. In a single comparison that also included our cohort, the pseudogene ATF4P4 was identified alongside ZIK1 and PNMA3.


### Decision Tree and Information Gain Analyses for External Cohort Validation
  In only three analyses of differential gene expression based on TCGA data were the genes ATF4, ZIK1, and PNMA3 identified, and PNMA3 was simultaneously identified as a DEG, and our cohort was included in all of them (Table 3 and Supporting Information). Furthermore, all samples had also undergone chemoradiotherapy. For the first comparison, 74 R patients and 15 NR patients were evaluated, and all patients in the external cohort had a partial response (PR) to chemoradiotherapy (Table 3). In this context, only the genes found in both our samples and the external cohort were evaluated. A total of 1375 protein‐coding DEGs were identified and used as features for DT and IF analyses. The decision tree results indicated that ATF4 and ZIK1, along with three other genes, were the best classifiers of patients into R and NR groups (Figure S6A and Table S12). Similarly to the approach applied to our data, by analyzing only the DEGs in common with the 961 previously identified in our results, a total of 847 genes were used as features in a new DT analysis, increasing the number of correct predictions using the LOOCV method, as well as the sensitivity and specificity of the results, in the same way as occurred when using only our sample set (Figure S6B and Table S12). ATF4 and ATP1B3 were again selected as the best classifiers for R and NR patients, but in the presence of two other genes. When using the same samples but excluding those at FIGO stage III‐C or higher, whilst retaining all genes annotated and quantified either in the TCGA data or within our own samples, ATF4 was again selected as the best attribute by the decision trees (Figure S6C,D, and Table S12). Finally, considering only the genes shared (intersection) between our samples and the external cohort, when 1375 DEGs were used as attributes, ATF4 and four other genes were selected (Figure S6E and Table S12). Similarly, when only 847 protein‐coding genes were evaluated, the same selection occurred, including ATF4, but with ARID5B and TMPRSS11A replaced by NCBP2 and ATP11B (Figure S6F and Table S12). The first Information Gain (IF) analysis used the same 89 samples and 1375 genes as the first decision tree shown above (Figure S6A) and identified ATF4 as the third‐ or fourth‐best classifier for patients. Meanwhile, ZIK1 was ranked between 8th and 10th (Table S13). When all FIGO stage III‐C‐IV samples were removed, as shown in the third decision tree (Figure S6C), ATF4 ranked between fourth and fifth, whilst ZIK1 ranked between 10th and 15th (Table S13). At last, when all 1591 protein‐coding genes identified as DEGs in our data were used as attributes for the same set of samples, ATF4 and ZIK1 occupied the same positions (Table S13).


### Differential Gene Expression Between Our Samples and the External Cohort
  To clarify the high diversity and distinctiveness between our samples and those from the TCGA, as indicated by the PCA results for PC1 (Figure S5), our DGE analysis results comparing our cohort with the external database showed that, out of approximately 75,000 genes assessed, nearly 70,000 were differentially expressed (Supporting Information).


## Discussion
  Despite major advances in vaccination against the various types of oncogenic HPV, which protect the global female population against approximately 90% of CC cases, as guaranteed by Gardasil 9. Furthermore, if clinical screening is carried out at least twice in a woman's lifetime, the number of cases of this cancer would be reduced by 97% by 2100 [24]. Even so, according to a 2022 review, there is a global mortality rate of 50% among women with this cancer, with approximately 90% of these deaths occurring in low‐ and middle‐income countries, as based on 2018 data [25]. Recently, HPV‐independent cervical cancers have been characterized by subdividing CESC and adenocarcinomas into two new categories. Furthermore, distinct molecular pathways have been linked to these CC subtypes, as well as to a higher prevalence of metastasis and treatment resistance [26]. In this scenario, the hypermethylation of genes such as RASSF1A has been linked to HPV‐negative cervical cancer, and more specifically to adenosquamous carcinoma. The protein complex consisting of MOAP‐1 (PNMA4), RASSF1A, and BAX induced apoptosis and chemosensitization in breast cancer cell lines and animal models [27, 28]. Similarly, various studies have demonstrated pro‐ or anti‐apoptotic roles mediated by PNMA family members interacting with RASSF family members, depending on the cancer type and the protein complex formed by these molecules. To perform their functions, these proteins depend on members of the BCL‐2 family or cytokines such as TNF‐alpha, which are among the main regulators of the intrinsic and extrinsic apoptotic pathways, respectively [29]. Interestingly, PNMA1 has recently been identified as a new biomarker for the prognosis of hepatocellular carcinoma; for this cancer, it is an oncoprotein that suppresses the immune system within the tumor microenvironment, thereby promoting the proliferation and migration of cancer cells. Furthermore, this protein has been identified as a potential target for tyrosine kinase inhibitors [30]. In this context, our results have demonstrated for the first time the differential gene expression of PNMA3 and its potential role in cancer, more specifically in CCNSCs, as well as its potential contribution to resistance to chemoradiotherapy and a possible role in apoptotic pathways (Figures 3, 4, 5). Interestingly, among 2670 DEGs, 1591 were protein‐coding genes, but only 6 genes (KRT26, MCF2, NUMBL, PNMA3, SDR42E2, and ZIK1) were found with a ∣z‐score ∣ ≥ 1, for at least half of the patients R or NR (Figures 1 and 3 and Tables S4, S5, and S11). Specifically, for PNMA3, these z‐score values were close to two standard deviations (SD) above the mean for Group 1 of NR patients and one SD below the mean for several R patients. Protein‐coding genes were preferentially identified as upregulated (log2FC > = 1) in R patients compared to NR patients, with most patients having expression values close to the mean. Nevertheless, PNMA3 was one of the only DEGs associated with non‐response and, therefore, resistance to chemoradiotherapy, and exhibited high relative expression between R and NR groups. Machine learning models confirmed that ATF4 and ATP1B3 effectively classified patients, particularly when focusing on the subset of genes upregulated in Responder patients, achieving high sensitivity and specificity. This is due to the high heterogeneity of cancer, caused by environmental factors, different types of tumor cells, and other tumor‐associated cells, as well as differences at the molecular and pathogenic levels. The individuality of this disease has become an increasingly frequent observation, as can be seen in the oncogenic role of ATF4 in a wide variety of cancers, but as a tumor suppressor in medullary thyroid cancer [31, 32, 33]. Additionally, less invasive treatments with greater specificity and reduced cytotoxic effects on non‐tumor cells have been increasingly explored, including various immunotherapeutic strategies [34]. Faced with this challenge, the lack of specificity in the treatment of CC, primarily through chemotherapy with platinum‐based compounds, combined or not with radiotherapy, may contribute to increased mortality and recurrence rates associated with the disease. Therefore, ATF4 was selected as the best classifier for classifying patients as R or NR in CCNSCs based on the decision tree results, as well as among those with the highest information gain scores. Besides the fact that this was upregulated in the R group and downregulated in the NR, it may be considered a potential candidate for a favorable prognosis, possibly contributing to sensitivity to chemoradiotherapy. Regarding ZIK1, this gene was selected in both the IF and ∣z‐score ∣ ≥ 1 strategies, with an expression profile very similar to that of PNMA3. On the other hand, the results of the external validation confirmed substantial differences not only of technical nature, but also biological, among the datasets from projects CGCI‐HTMCP‐CC and TCGA‐CESC. PNMA2 and ATF7IP were particularly shown as DEGs when samples from CGCI‐HTMCP‐CC were considered, while PNMA5 was found in the presence of samples from TCGA‐CESC. ATF7‐NPFF and PNMA8A were exclusively associated with chemoradiotherapy response in the external cohort, but they were not identified as DEGs between patients submitted only chemotherapy or radiotherapy. Finally, ZIK1 was differentially expressed only with chemoradiotherapy, which is particularly relevant to our data and is also evident in external comparisons. Curiously, ATF4 was recognized only when our data and external samples submitted to chemoradiotherapy were evaluated, and in all cases where ZIK1 was present. Likewise, PNMA3 was detected only in the presence of ZIK1. These robust co‐expression patterns suggest a potential regulatory network between PNMA3, ATF4, and ZIK1, although this relationship requires further functional validation. Supporting our findings, OS analysis using the Kaplan–Meier plotter demonstrated that high expression of PNMA3 and ZIK1 was significantly associated with an increased survival probability in CC patients. Although our results showed opposite expression patterns, these genes were associated with the 5‐year survival rate (Figure 6C,E). Finally, to determine if ATF4, PNMA3, and ZIK1 expression could influence tumor‐associated microenvironment (TME) and the phenotype observed in our cohort, we evaluated iTIL, sTIL, and tTILs between both patient groups. Although no statistically significant difference in total TIL count was observed between the R and NR groups, a slightly higher number of iTILs was observed in R patients compared to NR patients. For the NR patients, a higher infiltrate was observed in the stromal region, suggesting that R displayed an immunologically more active TME (Figure S7), as previously observed by our group [35]. Consequently, our data suggest that evaluation of ATF4, PNMA3, and ZIK1 gene expression can provide a characteristic signature of the response to chemoradiotherapy, specifically in CCNSCs. Furthermore, a higher number of iTILs and TATE in the R group before chemoradiotherapy may make the TME more immunologically active and more responsible for therapy susceptibility in CC, although further studies involving a larger cohort are needed. Despite robust in silico validation using external cohorts (TCGA and CGCI), we acknowledge certain limitations in our study. First, the retrospective design of our internal cohort and its relatively small sample size warrant caution. Second, the regulatory relationships and resistance mechanisms proposed here are based on transcriptomic and computational predictions. Definitive confirmation of these mechanisms requires further functional assays, both in vitro and in vivo.
