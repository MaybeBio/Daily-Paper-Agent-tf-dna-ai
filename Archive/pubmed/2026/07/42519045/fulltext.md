# Hierarchy of grammar rules for the language of transcriptional activation domains


## Summary
  Transcriptional activation domains (ADs) of eukaryotic gene activators have remained enigmatic for decades as short, consensus-less, extremely variable amino acid sequences that lack a specific structure and interact fuzzily with an uncertain number of targets. Understanding AD sequence grammar is critical for solving the enigma. Using rational design of AD sequences and high-throughput in vivo experimentation combined with bioinformatic analysis and machine learning, we refined grammar rules for AD sequences, calculated the relative importance of each rule to define a rule hierarchy, and linked each rule to the biochemical features essential for biological function. The key dominating and common features—redundant representation in the sequence of aromatic residues and the obligatory net negative charge of the sequence are consistent with the novel idea that ADs function as acidic-hydrophobic surfactants, which is crucial for understanding eukaryotic gene regulation and the function of intrinsically disordered protein regions.


## Introduction
  The long-standing enigma of transcriptional activation domains (ADs) of gene-specific activators1 stems from the paradox of ADs driving the critically vital function of eukaryotic gene expression by short, seemingly consensus-less, and immensely variable amino acid sequences that do not form a specific structure, while interacting fuzzily with an uncertain number of targets. The recent exponential accumulation of high-throughput experimental data2,3,4,5,6,7,8 increasingly exposes the incompleteness of the long-standing coactivator recruitment model for AD function and demands flexibility of thinking and potentially a new mechanistic model.1

  ADs are often categorized by composition into acidic, proline-, glutamine-, and serine-rich classes.9 The function of by far the most abundant class, acidic ADs, is often explained by the acidic exposure model10,11 whereby the hydrophobic residues, which are critically important for AD function, are exposed in an amphipathic fashion by hydrophilic acidic residues, thus enabling hydrophobic interactions with coactivators. In this model, the acidic residues play a facilitating role by solvent-exposing hydrophobics, which are the key residues for recruiting coactivators to gene promoters. Recruitment, however, demands a relatively high level of specificity of sequence, structure, and interaction, which ADs lack.

  The extremely low specificity and high variability of AD sequences are often ignored or underestimated. The results of multiple independent screens for functional AD sequences2,7,12,13,14 consistently show that approximately 1% of all possible random sequences can function as ADs in vivo in the context of different gene promoters. Considering a sufficient length of 20 amino acids,2,5 1% functional combinations corresponds to a staggering >1024 sequences (see Erkine et al.1 for calculations), while for a 30 aa stretch2 it is even more enormous, amounting to >1037 sequences. Within this vast continuum, sequences, however, follow identifiable rules: (1) the absence of basic amino acid residues or at least a net negative charge,4,5 (2) redundant and balanced representation of acidic and hydrophobic, preferably aromatic, residues,4,10 (3) with aromatic residues dominating in AD composition over aliphatic residues,4,5,8 (4) with acidic and hydrophobic residues preferably not forming significant acidic or hydrophobic clusters,4,5,6,10,11 (5) and with hydrophobic residues preferably situated closer to the molecule terminus and acidic residues situated more internally.4,5 (6) Additionally, for sequences following the above rules, the functionality increases with an increased number of proline residues.5,15 The relative importance of the above rules to each other has not been established.

  In this study, we have identified a measurable hierarchy of rules suggesting that the net negative charge and the redundant representation of aromatic residues are the dominating requirements, while other rules have significantly lower importance. In addition, we determined that although the presence of proline residues usually facilitates functionality, a mere exposure of hydrophobic residues achievable by intermixing aromatic and proline residues is not sufficient for functionality, suggesting that the “acidic exposure” model is incomplete. The combinatorial requirement of acidic and hydrophobic (primarily aromatic) residues informs us about the hydrophobic and basic nature of the potential AD targets.


## Results

### Experimental setup
  We used the high-throughput approach to compare thousands of designed sequences for functionality in vivo (see STAR Methods and Cooper et al.5). Briefly, thousands of individual nucleotide sequences were parallel-synthesized and cloned by in-frame fusion to the DNA-binding domain of the Gal4 gene activator within the yeast centromeric shuttle vector.15 The resulting plasmid library was transformed into the yeast Y2HGold strain, where individual Gal4 hybrids bearing distinct library sequences were expressed and screened for functionality as ADs driving the expression of the Gal-Aureobasidin antibiotic resistance reporter gene. The library DNA for different growth time points was isolated and sequenced to determine the number of reads for each individual sequence at each time point. The growth slope for each sequence, normalized to time 0, was calculated and served as a measure of AD functionality relative to internal positive and negative controls (see STAR Methods and previous publications5,15). Contrary to similar screens performed in the past, and based on cell sorting,2,3,6,8 which results in a binary scale (functional/non-functional sequences), our screen produces a continuous AD functionality scale, which allowed us to refine grammar rules and to calculate relative importance for rules, thus defining the rule hierarchy.

  Considering the huge potential variability of AD sequences (>1024), which is impossible to investigate in its entirety, we studied the most common, abundant, and dominant class of acidic ADs.1,9 We designed a set of sequences around six basic rules (Figure 1), which are consistently identified in multiple publications: presence of aromatic and acidic residues,4,5,10 redundancy of these residues in the sequence,4,5,10 absence of basic residues,4,5 balance of aromatic and acidic residues,4,5,10 C-terminal preference of aromatic residues,4,5 and avoidance of aromatic clusters.4,5 To further decrease the enormous variability of AD sequences and make combinatorial space closer to the limit dictated by current massive parallel oligonucleotide synthesis technology, we designed sequences using the three amino acids found to be most influential and sufficient for AD functionality2,4,8: W, D, and R. We added G as a neutral “placeholder,”4,5 which, later in our experiments, we replaced with a variety of amino acids to test their effect on functionality. We designed 32 individual sets, each representing one of the possible combinations of rules formulated above.   Although other hydrophobic residues and proline were shown to affect the AD functionality2,3,4,5,8 we did not include these residues within the rule combination sets because these amino acids appear in ADs significantly less frequently,2,3,4,5,8 and because it was shown previously that the presence of only aromatic and acidic residues is not only sufficient for functionality but also creates an abundance of highly active ADs.5,7 For instance, 32% of all possible combinations of W and D, and 33% of all combinations of F and D, for ten positions in the amino acid sequence, are functional in vivo,5 with remaining nonfunctional sequences often simply not following one or more of the above rules. Additionally, we had to limit the number of sequences in the library due to restrictions of the massive parallel synthesis protocol and the ability to handle safely without losses during the in vivo screening. Nevertheless, the effects of proline and all hydrophobic amino acids were also analyzed separately in this study (Figures 4 and 6).


### Importance of grammar rules relative to each other
  To determine the importance of each rule for the prediction of AD functionality for all 32 sets together, we calculated linear regression coefficients, which show how much a sequence’s functionality is influenced by following each rule separately. Figure 1A shows that the two dominant rules are the redundant representation of aromatic and acidic residues and the absence of positively charged residues, with coefficients of 3.51 and 2.85, respectively. The regression coefficients for the balance between aromatic and acidic residues, the C-terminal position of aromatic residues, and clustering are significantly lower, thus contributing substantially less to sequence functionality.

  The relative importance of each rule becomes clearer from the analysis of individual sets (Figure 1B). First, set #1, which has sequences that follow all rules, is 100% functional, and the functional score for the majority of sequences is among the highest in the library. These observations suggest that formulated rules sufficiently describe AD functionality, at least within the designed library. Set #2 and #3, which follow all rules except either avoiding aromatic clusters or not following the C-terminal preference of aromatics, are similarly functional to set #1 sequences while sequences in sets #4 through #8 which break the aromatic acidic balance rule as well as different combinations of clustering and C-terminal preference rules have a decrease median function score relative to set #1, while still remaining predominantly functional. Non-functional outliers in sets #4 through #8 have sequence features that greatly deviate from the corresponding rules (large clusters, significant imbalance between W and D residues, and/or drastic N-terminal enrichment of W residues) (see Figure 1B insets and Figure S1). Note that because library sequences were selected from the random pool for 20 amino acid positions using G, W, D, R amino acids, the number is 420 which is > 1012 of possible combinations, and because of the limited set size due to library size limitations, the deviation from the rule(s) can vary significantly, from mild to a strong deviation from the rule. Potentially, we could design sequences with substantial deviation from or strict adherence to rule(s); however, it would introduce designer bias, which we wanted to avoid when selecting sequences from the pool of random sequences.

  The extension of the analysis toward sets 9–32 reveals an abrupt shift in functionality as soon as the rules for redundancy of aromatic and acidic residues and/or the absence of basic residues are broken (Figure 1B sets 9–32). This outcome is consistent with the high values of the regression coefficients for these two rules.


### Redundancy of hydrophobic residues and net negative charge are defining features of ADs
  To further explore the most significant feature of redundancy of acidic and aromatic residues, we looked at all sequences that have variations in the number of W and D residues while lacking R residues, including additional sequences that contain only one or two W and D residues not present in any of the initial 32 sets (Figure 2A). The corresponding heatmap shows that sequences that contained only one, two, or three Ws were non-functional, while for D the redundancy requirement is less strict showing that moderate functionality is possible even with two or three Ds but not with a single D.   We then wanted to check if the total charge is a more critical parameter. Indeed, for all sequences in our library, generally and consistent with the redundancy rule, the functionality becomes obvious when the total charge is ≤ −2 even in the presence of ≥4 Rs (Figure 3B). These data strongly suggest that the critical parameter for AD functionality is the total negative charge of AD, even in the presence of positive charges in the sequence.

  According to the acidic exposure model,9,10,16 which attempts to explain the AD composition and functionality, the major reason for acidic residues in the AD sequence is the exhibition of hydrophobic residues for interactions with coactivators. Following this logic and also considering that prolines facilitate AD function, as evidenced by the allotment of the proline-rich class of ADs,9,17,18 we wanted to test if replacing acidic residues in WD-rich sequences while exposing hydrophobic residues would maintain AD functionality. Figures 4A and 4B show that PWPW or PWPWPW sequences cannot satisfy the functionality requirements without the presence of additional D residues.   While analyzing individual sequences in the proline-rich subset, we noticed that AD functionality is affected by the distance between hydrophobic and acidic regions, showing a marked decrease in functionality with the increased separation of acidic and hydrophobic regions (Figure 4C). That observation again suggests that both hydrophobic and acidic regions are necessary and cooperate.


### Extreme deviations from hydrophobic/acidic balance, C-terminal preference, and avoidance of hydrophobic clusters significantly affect AD functionality

For this, we assessed how the deviations from these rules affect AD functionality (Figure 5).   Similarly, deviation of the average position of W residues away from the C-terminus progressively diminishes the AD functionality (Figure 5B). The number of nonfunctional sequences starts to significantly accumulate with the average W position deviation of >3 residues. The hydrophobic clusters avoidance rule is more complex (Figure 5C). It shows a strong dependence of this feature on whether Ds flank the largest hydrophobic cluster. With D flanking, sequences were not significantly affected by the size of the hydrophobic cluster. In contrast, without flanking, the trend is clear: The increased size of the hydrophobic cluster began to diminish AD functionality for cluster sizes greater than 4. These data are consistent with previous observations of the flanking effect by acidic residues.15


### Expanding the amino acid repertoire beyond acidic and aromatic residues modulates AD functionality
  So far, we have investigated rules and rule hierarchy mostly in the context of W and D combinations, which are two compositionally dominant amino acids in ADs.3,4,6,8 Although it substantially decreases the enormous number of possible combinations, thus allowing us to see rules, their nuances, and relationships more clearly, this limitation of composition deviates from the much richer composition of natural AD sequences, which, although they are significantly enriched in Ws and Ds, also contain E, other aromatic amino acids, and sometimes Ls.2,3,4,5,8 To test how other amino acids might affect AD functionality, we used set #1, which follows all rules, and replaced all or half of Ws with F, Y, or Ls or all or half of Ds with Es (Figure 6A). We see that the full or partial replacement of D for E did not affect AD functionality significantly. Full or partial replacement of W for F generally affected the AD functionality mildly. The effect was stronger for full replacement of Ws to Ls and even stronger for Y replacement, creating mostly non-functional sequences for full W to Y replacements, while only slightly affecting functionality for partial W to Y replacements.   To test a further expanded repertoire of AD compositions we created sequences where the WD positions remained the same as in set #1 while half of the Gs (“place holders”) were substituted with aliphatic (A, V, I, L, M), or other amino acids (S, T, N, Q, P, C) occasionally appearing in ADs2,3,4,5,8 (Figure 6B). While the functionality of modified set #1 sequences was generally only mildly affected, a remarkable number of non-functional sequences appeared for V and I amino acids. This is surprising considering that both V and I are structurally and chemically not significantly different from L, which in this context did not have such a negative effect.

  An essential factor is that replacing G with any hydrophobic residue in set #1 shifts the balance between acidic and hydrophobic residues. To test how this affects AD functionality, we checked dependence of the functionality score on the number of specific amino acids added, replacing Gs (Figure 6C). We see that the progressive increase of the hydrophobic residues generally decreases AD functionality with the effect not observed for A, which has the lowest hydrophobicity, and most visible for the increasing numbers of L, I, or V, which have highest hydrophobicity. The strongest effect was observed for isoleucine, while for leucine, which is isomeric to isoleucine, the effect was lower.

  Similarly, to affect the balance rule, the addition of hydrophobic residues might affect hydrophobic cluster formation, especially when the substitutions extend or combine existing clusters of W residues. Indeed, we see (Figure 6D) that while generally hydrophobic clustering is associated with lower functionality, the effects for individual amino acids are I > V > L > M > A.


### Refined grammar rules for ADs
  Realizing how deviations from the initially formulated rules are affecting AD functionality, we refined rules (Figure 7), recalculated linear regression coefficients for each rule, and used the refined rules to estimate the accuracy of prediction using our designed library dataset, an unbiased random library dataset,2 and a natural AD sequences dataset.8 We see that in all three datasets, the prediction accuracy increased, suggesting that the refined rules better reflect the AD functionality in vivo. The poor prediction accuracy in the natural sequences’ dataset3 might be related to differences in the library designs5 and even more to the extremely small size of both designed and natural sequence libraries in comparison to the whole space of possible active AD sequences (see limitations of the study section later in discussion).Figure 7Redefined rules predict AD functionality more accurately(A) Effect of each rule on activity. For each library, a model was trained on a subset of sequences and then tested on the remaining sequences from that library.   The following are refined grammar/composition rules that define the functionality of acidic ADs. (1) Functional acidic ADs should contain both acidic and hydrophobic (preferably aromatic) residues. (2) Increasing the redundancy of hydrophobic (aromatic) and acidic residues increases AD functionality. At least 3 of each acidic and aromatic residues are required for functionality. (3) Decreasing net charge (more negative charge) increases AD functionality. A net negative charge of at least −3 is required for functionality. (4) Decreasing the difference in the number of hydrophobic (aromatic) (>3) and acidic (>3) residues increases AD functionality. A difference in the number of hydrophobic (aromatic) and acidic residues of no more than 7 is required for functionality. (5) Shifting the average position of hydrophobic (aromatic) residues toward the C-terminus increases AD functionality. An average position of hydrophobic (aromatic) residues within the last 15 residues of the 20 amino acid sequence is required for functionality. (6) Large hydrophobic (aromatic) clusters, unless flanked by acidic residues, decrease AD functionality. The last three rules are minor and influence the AD function only if they are significantly deviated from. In addition, in this study (Figure 4C), we observed a trend that the increasing distance between the hydrophobic and acidic regions of the sequence decreases AD functionality.

  Given the close, and at times partially overlapping, relationship among the defined rules, we evaluated multicollinearity (high correlation) between the rule features as initially defined (“Old”) and after redefinition (“New”). Highly correlated rule features would impair the interpretation of individual regression coefficients as the influence of that rule on the functionality of a sequence. Multicollinearity was assessed by estimating variance inflation factors (VIFs) for the rule features (Table S2). VIFs measure how much an estimated regression coefficient’s variance is increased due to collinearity, with a value of 1 corresponding to no multicollinearity and a value > 5 corresponding to a significant amount of multicollinearity.19 Initial (“Old”) binary rules were defined to minimize multicollinearity, as evident from Table S2. The redefined (“New”) continuous rules do show slight multicollinearity with rule 2 (redundancy) having the largest VIF value at 2.17, which corresponds to an R2 value of 0.54. Alongside modifications to the other rules, a modification to rule 3 (charge) to include the number of acidic residues likely was a large contributing factor to this increase in collinearity, now that redundancy and charge rules both incorporate a count of acidic residues in calculating the score for each rule feature.

  Sequences that follow the most important rules 2 and 3 demonstrate clearly positive influences on functionality. The influence of the minor rules may be more apparent if evaluated in combination with other rules. We conducted a pairwise interaction analysis to determine the influence of each rule alongside each of the other rules (Table S3). Prior to the linear regression, the rule feature values were centered to reduce multicollinearity and improve the interpretation of coefficients.

  The main effects of rule 2 (redundancy), rule 3 (negative charge), and rule 5 (position) are maintained (or strengthened) in the interaction analysis, while the main effect of rule 4 (balance) is eliminated (Table S3). This means that if all other rules are at an average value, balance has no stand-alone effect. Several strong interactions are detected in this analysis (Figure S1). Balance is a strong modulator in combination with charge and redundancy, in opposing directions. If the charge score is high (more negatively charged), increasing the balance strongly increases the slope, while if the redundancy score is high, increasing the balance decreases the slope. There is also a strong synergistic interaction between redundancy and position. Higher scores for positions amplify the effect of higher redundancy scores in producing highly functional sequences. There is also a positive interaction between redundancy and cluster rules. This corresponds to the additional benefit of avoiding clusters in sequences with high redundancy. Below, we analyze and discuss the connections between calculated rules and biochemical features, and their implications for the mechanism of AD function.


## Discussion
  We have analyzed 7327 individual sequences enriched in W, D, R, and G residues, with other amino acids present at lesser frequencies. The set was designed using six rules for ADs.5 The presence of aromatic and acidic residues was considered a foundational rule, based on the abundance of W and D amino acids in ADs revealed by unbiased random-sequence screens2,4,7 and on the sufficiency of these amino acids to create thousands of functional ADs when the other rules are followed.5 The foundational rule regression coefficient was not calculated; thus, by design, the set of sequences analyzed in this study reflects and represents only the most common, abundant, and dominant class of acidic ADs1,9 and is not directly extendable to other classes of ADs, such as glutamine- and serine-rich.1,9 A subset of sequences #1 (Figure 1B) in which all sequences followed these rules showed 100% functionality, suggesting that at least for this set, the rule definitions generally correctly reflect in vivo AD function. While quantitatively characterizing the relative importance of these rules (Figures 1 and 7), we see that the most critical rules are a redundant representation of hydrophobic (W > F > Y > L) and acidic (D, E) residues, and the absence of basic residues. That is consistent with previous compositional analyses of fungal, plant, and human ADs2,6,7,8 and with earlier observations of a sharp drop in the probability of sequence functionality when both of these rules are broken together (Figure 1 and Broyles et al.,4 Cooper et al.5). Other rules, such as the balance between acidic and hydrophobic residues, the external exposure of hydrophobic residues, and the avoidance of hydrophobic clusters, appear to be less significant. Each rule, even the most important, is not strict and can be deviated from; however, increasing deviation from each rule progressively decreases the probability that the sequence is functional and its level of activity as AD.

  Analyzing each of the above rules, we refined each rule definition and now better understand each rule, which is reflected in higher accuracy of prediction of sequences as AD using refined rule definitions (Figure 7B). Below, we analyze each rule and attempt to interpret each of them in the context of the biological function.

  Dissecting each rule, we see that the increasing number of hydrophobic residues, especially Ws, generally increases the probability of functionality as AD, which is consistent with our previous observations,4 and the minimum requirement for at least three and for some sequences two W residues (Figure 2), which is consistent with previous calculations1 based on the extremely high (∼1%) functional AD sequence selection from a random sequence pool. This minimal requirement for functionality, extreme sequence variability, and absence of a consensus sequence, in turn, suggests extremely low affinity and the transient nature of AD interaction with targets.

  Analysis of the requirement for the absence of positively charged residues shows that the rule is not absolute: R and K residues occasionally occur in functional ADs (Figures 1 and 3, Erijman et al.,2 Morffy et al.,6 Ravarani et al.,7 DelRosso et al.8). Importantly, sequences can remain functional even in the presence of positively charged residues, provided the net charge of the sequence remains below −2 (Figure 3 and Sanborn et al.3). This finding, together with the high relative importance of this rule, underscores that a negative charge is a core feature of AD functionality, on par with the requirement for redundant hydrophobic residues.

  Although negatively charged residues can promote solvent exposure of hydrophobic residues, this aspect of the function is less decisive than the acidic exposure model proposes.10,11 Substituting acidic residues with proline—which preserves the hydrophobic residues' exposure—abolishes AD function (Figure 4), thus suggesting that the negative charge per se is critical. Nevertheless, proline likely plays a facilitating role, as it makes sequences with PWPWPW functional (Figure 4B) with even two Ds, which compositionally is outside of the aromatic/acidic minimum requirements (W ≥ 4, D ≥ 3, see Figure 2A). This observation is consistent with previous observations indicating that proline greatly enhances the AD activity for sequences containing acidic and aromatic residues.5,15 In this context, which is similar for proline-rich AD of CTF1,17,20 and proline-containing ADs of P53,18 where proline likely prevents intramolecular hydrophobic aggregation, ensuring that hydrophobic residues remain solvent-exposed. Even so, acidic residues remain indispensable (Figure 4), consistent with the idea that the functional target is positively charged.

  An interesting observation is that the increasing distance between the acidic and hydrophobic regions negatively affects AD functionality (Figure 4C). This new trend discovered in this study suggests that spatial cooperation between AD extremities, similar to general surfactants,21 affects its properties. For instance, considering the initial interactions of the nucleosome surfactant model are hydrophobic,4,22 the negatively charged acidic head of AD, if spatially separated, can be more efficiently repelled from the DNA/histone interface by DNA phosphates, thus reducing AD functionality.

  The balance between acidic and hydrophobic residues was highlighted as an essential AD feature on several occasions.4,11 The relatively low importance of this feature, compared with the above rules, again underscores the significance of the dual acidic-hydrophobic AD composition. We demonstrate that the deviation from this rule is tolerable for the AD functionality if the minimal requirement for the acidic-hydrophobic redundancy is satisfied (Figure 3 and Broyles et al.,4 Cooper e al.,5 Staller et al.11). Extreme deviations kill functionality, likely due to the increased probability of multiple hydrophobic residues' internal aggregation, or, if the surfactant model is considered, due to increased repulsion from the DNA.

  The C-terminal preference of hydrophobic residues over acidic ones might reflect the spatial aspect of AD function and indicate that initial hydrophobic interactions are preferable over the electrostatic ones. Considering the relatively low importance of this rule, it likely reflects a facilitation of the main acidic-hydrophobic rule and, as such, becomes critical only during extreme deviations (Figure 5B). Although natural ADs are often present either at the C- or N-terminus of the gene activator,18,23,24,25 the intramolecular localization of ADs was also observed.26,27,28 Thus, the data of these publications also suggest that the terminal preference rule is not critical and likely plays a minor role in modulating AD activity levels. In the context of our experimental setup, it might also reflect a C-terminal bias of the Gal4 screening system.

  Similarly, the relatively low importance of the cluster avoidance rule might reflect its role as a facilitator rather than a prominent feature. A larger hydrophobic cluster likely increases the likelihood of internal hydrophobic collapse, thereby decreasing AD functionality. Importantly, this rule is apparent only if acidic residues do not flank the hydrophobic cluster (Figure 5C). Arguably, the flanking configuration decreases the probability of hydrophobic aggregate formation by the electrostatic repulsion of the flanking acidic residues, thereby minimizing the effect of internal hydrophobic interactions.

  The addition of varying hydrophobic amino acids to the already functional ADs does not significantly affect functionality if the number of added residues does not greatly shift the acidic-hydrophobic balance (Figure 6). Interestingly, the most negative effect is seen for the addition of V and I, but not so much for L. The difference between I and L is fascinating, as these amino acids are isomeric. The explanation might be connected to the fact that L might form an abundance of functional combinations with acidic residues5 and was demonstrated previously as frequently present in ADs,2,3,4,8 while it was not critically necessary for ADs.4 The structural difference is that both methyl groups of the L side chain are in the γ position, whereas in I, they are split between the β and γ positions. The different effects of L and I might provide clues to the structural nature of AD-target interactions and warrant further investigation.

  Overall, the analysis of AD features and the relative importance of compositional rules provides essential clues for solving the long-standing enigma of ADs.1 The astronomic variability of interchangeable AD sequences, which vary by composition and length, the absence of an AD consensus sequence, and even of specific AD mini-motifs,4,5 the intrinsic structural disorder, all complicate the explanation of AD function via recruitment of coactivators, which is central for the acidic exposure model. The fact that ADs with extremely different compositions, such as containing only W and D, or only F and D, or only L and D, were shown to be equally functional in vivo in identical experimental conditions,5 further complicates the utilization of the recruitment model. However, the hugely variable and numerous functional AD sequences analyzed in this and other studies2,3,4,5,6,7,10,11 are all unified by a common and dominant feature highlighted by this study—a redundant representation of acidic and hydrophobic residues. This, in turn, suggests a unifying universal mechanism. Unlike in the case of the recruitment model, observations and rules discussed in this study fit well with the recently introduced amphiphilic acidic-hydrophobic model of AD function1,5,22 and warrant its future investigation.


### Limitations of the study
  Due to the extremely high number of possible AD sequences (>1024, see the introduction section), any in vivo analysis, including the current study, even in the high-throughput format, cannot even come close to covering and evaluating all sequence variants. This restriction suggests several implications. (1) The acceptance of the presence of hydrophobic and acidic residues as a foundational rule suggests that the rules analyzed in this study and the rule hierarchy apply only to the most common, abundant, and dominant class of acidic ADs. (2) Any sequence rules, including those formulated in this study, need to be considered as approximations and, as such, are subject to future refining. (3) The extremely high variability of AD sequences also suggests that rules cannot be strict, are likely prone to deviations and exceptions (Figure 5 and Cooper et al.5), and likely can be affected by intramolecular and external contexts. (4) The values of the regression coefficients also need to be considered as approximations, as they depend on the dataset size used for the calculation and the specific formulation of the rules. That is especially true for the clustering rule, which overlaps with the redundancy rule (bigger clusters tend to correlate with higher redundancy).

  Even though the interpretations of the rules are consistent with the surfactant hypothesis, the in vivo design of the current study limits interpretations and requires future in vitro validation of the hypothesis. The extremely low specificity of ADs at the sequence-structure-interactions levels makes the task not trivial and requires new models and assay development.1

### Experimental model and study participant details

### Method details

#### Library construction, cloning, and screening
  Library construction and cloning were conducted as described previously.5 The parental library plasmid was constructed by cloning the fragment containing the ADH1 promoter and the Gal4(1-147) DBD cassette from the pGBKT7 vector into the SacI and KpnI sites of the centromeric yeast shuttle vector pRS314.

  The design library contained 12,400 individual nucleotide sequences totaling 80 nucleotides with ∼60 amino acid coding nucleotides, a stop codon, and a unique ∼20 nucleotide barcode. The design library sequences were synthesized at the GenScript commercial facility, amplified by PCR five times (each time appending a unique Biological Replicate (BioRep) barcode), quantitated for the DNA content, and mixed in equal proportions into a single pool. For description of more detailed steps, see Figure S3 of Cooper et al.5 The pool was cloned into the NcoI and SalI restriction sites of the parental library plasmid. The library complexity was estimated by individual colony counts after transformation for a fraction of the total transformation mix, then multiplying by the fraction factor. Total complexity was estimated to be ∼10ˆ6. The total content of individual sequences within the library was determined by NGS at GenScript. The NGS also confirmed the in-frame fusion of AD sequences to the Gal4 DBD region.

  After the bacterial cloning and verification, the plasmid library was isolated and transformed into the yeast strain Y2HGold (Clontech/Takara). The maintenance of the library complexity was determined by the individual colony count for a fraction of a transformation mix as described above for the bacterial transformation. The number of individual yeast transformants for entire library was estimated to be ∼10∧6. After transformation, the whole-library cell culture was transferred into synthetic yeast growth medium lacking tryptophan and containing 150 or 200 μg/mL of aureobasidin. The cultures were grown for three days with a daily 1/100 dilution to maintain the culture in the mid-log phase. Cell culture samples were taken at 0, 0.5, 1, 2, and 3 days. DNA was isolated using a Thermo Scientific Pierce Yeast DNA Extraction Reagent Kit. The library component was isolated by PCR using the Invitrogen AccuPrime SuperMix I kit with primers containing Illumina adapters and barcodes unique for each DNA sample. DNA samples were controlled for purity, repeatedly quantitated for DNA content, and sequenced at the NovoGene commercial facility.


### Quantification and statistical analysis

#### Sequence processing
  Processing of the design library sequencing data was conducted as described previously.5 For each sample, cutadapt29 was used to demultiplex by BioReps, remove adapter barcodes, and remove sequence reads shorter than 73 residues. Reads were mapped using STAR30 to a general feature format file pseudo-genome of 12,400 sequences. The pseudo-genome was built using the 12,400 designed sequences, with their unique barcodes, each represented by its own “gene” assigned to its own “chromosome.” Successful mapping required a perfect match (allowing no mismatches). For each sample, the output read (gene) count file was used as the input for further data processing.


#### Estimation of sequence growth rates
  Data processing and analyses were conducted in R version 4.4.1 (R Core Team, 2023). Correlations among the read counts of biological replicates (BioReps) were calculated to ensure reasonable consistency. For each sample, counts for each sequence were converted to log-transformed counts per million. The counts were then normalized to the average count for each sequence at the initial time-point. Sequences with 0 or only 1 BioRep with any counts at the initial timepoint were removed (n = 1020 sequences).

  For the estimations of growth rates over time, the data was split into two groups based on aureobasidin treatment concentration during the experiment (150 or 200 μg/mL). Robust linear regression (implemented in the MASS package in R) was used to estimate the growth slope of each sequence over time; this was our final estimate for the functionality of each sequence. Sequences where counts dropped to 0 at the first growth timepoint (n = 4312 (150 μg/mL treatment group); n = 3468 (200 μg/mL treatment group)) could not have accurate growth slopes calculated. Growth slopes were calculated for the remaining sequences (n = 7068 (150 μg/mL treatment group); n = 7912 (200 μg/mL treatment group)) using all BioReps at the initial timepoint and first growth timepoint. The intercept was not defined, though expected to be ∼0 based on normalization.

  For further processing and analysis, the growth slopes for the 200 μg/mL aureobasidin treatment group were used. A functionality threshold was determined as the mean slope + 2 standard deviations for the non-functional stop-codon controls, where growth slopes could be calculated (n = 105). The functionality threshold was subtracted from all slopes to set the threshold to 0. Sequences with growth slopes greater than zero are considered functional.


#### Sequence sets
  The library contained several control sequence sets: sequences comprised solely of a stop-codon (n = 153), 4 replicates of 24 natural AD sequences (n = 96), and 4 replicates of 6 previously tested sequences containing W and D residues (n = 24). In addition to the controls, the library was comprised of series of sequence sets designed to test specific hypotheses.

  The first series of sets were each comprised of 100 sequences containing combinations of W, D, G, and R residues. All of these sequences contained at least a single W residue and a single D residue. These 32 sets (n = 3200) represented all combinations of whether five rules on sequence composition were or were not followed (25 = 32 combinations). The five rules, defined in more detail below, dictate that functional activation domain sequences contain; multiple W and D residues (redundancy), equal numbers of W and D residues (balance), no basic residues (charge), C-terminal enrichment of W residues (position), minimal consecutive W residues (clustering). The following metrics define sequences that follow each rule. Redundancy: ≥3 W and ≥3 D residues; Balance: number of W residues = number of D residues; Charge: 0 R residues; Position: average position of W residues is past position 12 (of 20); Clustering: <50% of residues adjacent to W residues are W residues. An additional 300 sequences containing unconstrained compositions of W, D, G, and R residues were included (n = 300).

  The second series of sets consisted of balanced combinations of 1 or 2 W and D residues and 1 or 2 F and D residues. For the 1W1D set (n = 380) and the 1F1D set (n = 380) the entire combinatorial space for two residues within a total sequence length of 20 residues was used. In addition to the single W/F and single D residues, the remaining 18 residues in each sequence were G residues. For the 2W2D set (n = 380) and the 2F2D set (n = 380) only a subset of the combinatorial space for four residues within a total sequence length of 20 residues was randomly sampled. The remaining 16 residues in each sequence were G residues.

  The third series of sets were derivatives of the 100-sequence set that followed all rules where the W, D, or G residues were substituted with other residues. For the W residue substitutions, all or half of the W residues in each sequence were substituted with F, Y, or L (n = 300 (half substituted); n = 300 (all substituted)). In the case of an odd number of W residues, for half substitutions, the number of substituted residues was rounded up to the nearest whole number. The residues randomly selected for substitution in each sequence were the same residues replaced with F, Y, or L. For the D residue substitutions, all or half of the D residues in each sequence were substituted with E (n = 100 (half substituted); n = 100 (all substituted)). For the G residue substitutions, half of the G residues in each sequence were substituted with A, V, I, L, M, S, T, N, Q, P, or C (n = 1100). For rounding and position conservation of substituted residues, the same methods used for W substitutions were used for D and G substitutions.

  The fourth series of sets consisted of two templates of W and P residues (n = 407): template 1) PWPW and template 2) PWPWPW. This template was present at the C-terminal end of the sequence, with the remaining residues consisting of G residues to obtain a total sequence length of 20. In addition to sequences that only consisted of W, P, and G residues, the sets contained derivatives where 1-6 G residues were substituted with D residues. These sequences represented the entire combinatorial space for the position of D residues as directly adjacent residues or evenly spaced by 1-5 intervening G residues.


#### Sequence features analyses
  Among each set of sequences, the effects of following sequence features on function were determined for aromatic, basic, and acidic residues: absolute and relative number of residues, position of residues, and clustering of residues. The absolute number of aromatic and acidic residues was used to determine redundancy. The relative amount of aromatic and acidic residues was used to determine balance. Deviation from balance corresponds to the absolute difference in the number of aromatic and acidic residues. The relative amount of acidic and basic residues was used to determine charge. The average position of aromatic residues was determined by assigning each position in the 20 residue sequences a value from 1-20 and then averaging the values corresponding to positions with aromatic residues. Deviation from C-terminal preference corresponds to the number of positions lower (rounded to a whole position) the average position of aromatic residues is from an average of 12 where higher average positions are assigned a value of 0. The clustering score was calculated as the average proportion of aromatic residues among the two residues adjacent to each aromatic residue, where a score of 0 corresponds to no adjacent aromatics and a score approaching 1 corresponds to a sequence with all aromatics adjacent to other aromatics aside from the aromatics at the end of the sequence. The largest cluster was determined as the longest stretch of consecutive aromatic residues. Flanking corresponds to the presence of acidic residues directly adjacent on both sides of the largest cluster.


#### Rules analysis and refinement
  The resulting distributions of function and sequence compositions for sequences following different rules were used to empirically refine each rule. For each refined rule, cutoff values were replaced with continuous ranges that separate the lowest and highest probabilities of function. The following metrics define sequences that follow or break each rule. The range of values between these two values defines sequences with intermediate probabilities of the function. Redundancy: minimum of number of aromatic (W or F) or acidic residues >5 (rule followed) and <3 (rule broken); Balance: absolute difference in the number of W and D residues <3 (rule followed) and >5 (rule broken); Charge: difference in the number of acidic and basic residues (net charge) <-5 (rule followed) and >-3 (rule broken); Position: distance of average position of aromatic (W or F) residues from C-terminus <12 (rule followed) and >15 (rule broken); Clustering: average proportion of adjacent aromatic/non-aromatic residues <0.5 or aromatic clusters flanked by acidic residues (rule followed) and >0.5 and aromatic clusters not flanked by acidic residues (rule broken).


#### Regression models
  Linear regression (conducted using the stats package in R) was performed on the entire first series of sets corresponding to rules (rules sets) to determine the effect on growth slope, both of initial (old) and of refined (new) formulations of the five rule features. For each old rule feature, a value of 1 was assigned if the requirements of the rule were met, and a value of 0 was assigned if the rule was not followed. For each new rule feature, values of 1 and 0 were assigned if the rule was followed or broken, respectively, and intermediate values between 0 and 1 were assigned based on the intermediate score between the rule cutoffs. For example, for redundancy, a sequence with 5 W and 5 D residues has a redundancy score of 5 and a feature value of 0.75. Linear regression coefficient estimates were obtained for each feature as an indicator of how following each rule influences functionality.

  Lasso logistic regression (conducted using the caret package in R) was performed on the rules sets to develop and test models trained to predict AD functionality based on both the initial (old) and refined (new) formulations of the five rule features. The sequences were split into five even groups, four used for training and one used as the testing set. The training/testing process was repeated five times using a different hold-out test set each time. Area under the ROC (receiver operating characteristic) curve (AUC) was computed on the test set after each training round and averaged across the five rounds to find the final reported AUROC. For random sequences2 and natural sequences,3 the libraries were each split 80/20 into training and test sets. Values for the five features were determined as described for the designed library, with modifications to account for the greater variety of residues and for a different sequence length. To calculate feature scores, aromatics corresponded to W and F residues, acidics corresponded to D and E residues, and basics corresponded to K and R.


#### Feature collinearity and interaction analysis
  The collinearity of the five rule features before and after redefining was analyzed by calculating variance inflation factors (VIFs) for each rule feature used in the linear regression models. VIFs were calculated using the car package in R. Pairwise interactions between the redefined rule features were determined through linear regression (conducted using the stats package in R) of the effect on growth slope of each of the five individual rule features and ten paired rule features. Prior to the linear regression, the five rule features were centered by subtracting the mean of each feature from each sequence’s values for each feature (calculated using the scale function in base R).
