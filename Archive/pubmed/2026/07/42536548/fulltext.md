# The role of cellular senescence in sepsis and its mechanism in copper death: An analysis based on machine learning


## Abstract
  Sepsis is characterized by potentially fatal organ failure resulting from the host’s abnormal response to infection. Due to the complex and rapid progression of sepsis, timely diagnosis and intervention are required to improve patient prognosis. Copper-dependent cell death, known as “cuproptosis,” is a newly discovered mode of cell death that relies on copper. Senescence refers to a state of irreversible cessation of cell division. Both processes play significant roles in various diseases. However, the roles of genes related to cuproptosis and senescence in the pathogenesis of sepsis remain insufficiently understood. In this study, we utilized bioinformatics techniques to explore the involvement of copper-dependent cell death and its connections to sepsis and cellular senescence. We obtained 3 sepsis datasets (GSE28750, GSE54514, and GSE131761) from the Gene Expression Omnibus database and classified the raw data using R packages (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Copper death- and aging-related genes were manually screened, and differentially expressed cuproptosis and cellular senescence-related differentially expressed genes associated with sepsis were identified. Subsequently, enrichment analysis was applied, and key genes were screened using machine learning techniques for the construction and validation of a sepsis diagnostic model. We then constructed mRNA–miRNA and mRNA–transcription factors interaction networks for the key genes, followed by differential gene analysis, immune infiltration, and enrichment analysis. We identified 17 cuproptosis and cellular senescence-related differentially expressed genes and performed gene enrichment analysis. Subsequently, using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression analysis and random forest algorithm, we identified sepsis-related cuproptosis and senescence-associated differentially expressed genes. After taking the intersection, we obtained 11 key genes. Next, through immune infiltration analysis, we found a positive correlation between pyruvate dehydrogenase E1 subunit beta (PDHB) and central memory cluster of differentiation 4 (CD4) T cells, glutaminase and activated CD8 T cells, as well as prion protein, PDHB, and monocytic lineage. There was a negative correlation between PDHB and type 17 T helper cells, amyloid beta precursor protein and activated CD8 T cells, PDHB and neutrophils, and CD274 and B lineage cells. These results suggest that cuproptosis may promote the development of sepsis by affecting the immune system and metabolic functions, providing new insights into the potential pathogenic mechanisms and therapeutic targets of sepsis.


## 1. Introduction
  A serious worldwide health concern, sepsis is defined by potentially fatal organ failure brought on by an aberrant host reaction to an infection. According to an important study, sepsis was diagnosed in around 48.9 million persons globally, leading to over 11 million fatalities. This is responsible for around 20% of all fatalities worldwide.[1] Traditionally, septic patients have been predominantly managed in intensive care units. However, despite this, some high-risk septicemia patients are treated outside these specialized units due to limitations in resources or advanced disease stages at presentation.[2] This practice introduces additional challenges to sepsis management. Thus, the early detection of sepsis in general wards and the application of comprehensive treatments, including aggressive antimicrobial therapy, could potentially enhance clinical outcomes. Achieving such early detection and intervention requires a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying sepsis development and progression.

  Although immune imbalance and metabolic abnormalities in sepsis have been extensively studied in recent years, research indicates that early in sepsis, systemic or local acidosis can trigger the release of copper ions from copper-binding proteins such as ceruloplasmin,[3] leading to elevated plasma copper levels. Abnormal serum copper concentrations may reflect the severity of sepsis. Given the involvement of copper ions in cellular metabolism and oxidative stress, their dysregulation may be closely linked to emerging cell death mechanisms. However, the recently identified copper-dependent cell death pathway, known as cuproptosis, has not been systematically investigated in the context of sepsis, especially regarding its relationship with cellular senescence.[4] Currently, no specific molecular markers accurately reflect immune–metabolic imbalance or enable the early identification of sepsis risk. Excessive immune activation and metabolic dysregulation are key drivers of organ dysfunction and disease progression. However, the molecular mechanisms linking these metabolic and immune disturbances remain unclear, prompting the current investigation.

  Based on this background, the present study hypothesizes that cuproptosis- and senescence-related genes play critical regulatory roles in the onset and progression of sepsis and that their expression profiles can be used to construct a diagnostic molecular model. Disruption of copper homeostasis may cause mitochondrial metabolic dysfunction and immune cell impairment,[5] whereas activation of senescence-associated signaling pathways may amplify inflammation and aggravate tissue injury.[6] To this end, multiple Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) transcriptome datasets were integrated, and machine learning algorithms (least absolute shrinkage and selection operator [LASSO] regression and random forest [RF]) were applied to identify key genes, construct and validate a diagnostic model for sepsis, and investigate the crosstalk between cuproptosis and senescence-related signaling pathways. Collectively, this approach elucidates the molecular basis of immune dysregulation and identifies potential therapeutic targets to enhance patient recovery.[7] Furthermore, it broadens the theoretical understanding of cuproptosis-mediated immune regulation in infectious diseases and provides novel insights for early diagnosis, immunomodulatory therapy, and personalized treatment of sepsis.


## 2. Methods

### 2.1. Integrating and normalizing sepsis datasets
  The database analysis was conducted from January to May 2024. Qi Ma was responsible for data organization, analysis, validation, visualization, and drafting the initial manuscript. Hai-Rong Yang performed formal analysis, research implementation, and methodological design. Jian-Zhong Wang assisted in data organization and contributed to project management. Xiao-Ya Zhang supported data organization, participated in formal analysis, and supervised manuscript preparation. Yao-Lin Zhang contributed to data organization, supervision, validation, and visualization. Wen-Jie Zhou oversaw the overall conceptualization, assisted with data organization and project management, secured funding, and reviewed and revised the manuscript. Collectively, the team ensured the accuracy, consistency, and integrity of the entire analytical process.

  This study utilized sepsis-related datasets obtained from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/),[8,9]retrieved using the R package “GEOquery.”[10] Three datasets – GSE28750,[11] GSE54514, and GSE131761[12] – ere included in the analysis.

  Specifically, GSE28750 contained 10 sepsis samples and 20 normal samples, GSE54514 included 127 sepsis and 36 normal samples, and GSE131761 consisted of 81 sepsis and 33 normal samples. All samples were obtained from Homo sapiens (Table 1). To ensure data comparability, the Bioconductor R package “sva”[13] was employed to correct batch effects and generate a unified GEO dataset containing 218 sepsis and 89 normal samples. The integrated dataset was then standardized and normalized using the “limma”[14] R package. Finally, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the expression matrices before and after batch-effect correction to assess the effectiveness of the normalization process.[15]


### 2.2. Identification and analysis of copper death and cellular senescence-related genes
  A search using the term “Cuproptosis” in the GeneCards database[16] (https://www.genecards.org/) identified 24 cuproptosis-related genes (CRGs), all labeled as “Protein Coding.” Similarly, a PubMed search with “Cuproptosis” yielded 57 CRGs.[17] After merging and eliminating duplicates, 72 unique CRGs were obtained. Furthermore, searches using “Cellular Senescence” in both GeneCards and PubMed resulted in 3584 cellular senescence-related genes (CSRGs). The intersection of these 72 CRGs and 3584 CSRGs revealed 29 genes associated with both cuproptosis and cellular senescence-related genes (CCSRGs).


### 2.3. Identification of copper death and cellular senescence-related differentially expressed genes (CCSRDEGs)
  Using the R package “limma,” differential gene expression analysis was carried out to identify DEGs, adopting thresholds of |logFC| > 0 and P value <.05. To pinpoint CCSRDEGs, we visualized the intersection of DEGs from the GEO dataset (|logFC| > 0 and P value <.05) and CCSRGs using a Venn diagram. For data visualization, volcano plots and differential sorting plots were generated with the ggplot2 R package, clearly depicting the differentially expressed genes. Moreover, gene ontology (GO)[18] and Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG)[19] pathway enrichment studies were carried out using the clusterProfiler R package[20] in order to comprehend the biological importance of CCSRDEGs. The significance criteria were established at P value <.05 and false discovery rate (FDR) (q value) <0.05.


### 2.4. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)
  We obtained the gene dataset “c2.cp.all.v2022.1.Hs.symbols.gmt” from molecular signatures database. GSEA was then applied to the samples obtained from the GEO. A P value of <.05 and an FDR of <0.25 were the parameters for the GSEA.[21]


### 2.5. Gene set variation analysis (GSVA)
  Using the reference gene set “h.all.v7.4.symbols.gmt,” which was acquired from the molecular signatures database, the integrated GEO dataset (combined datasets) was examined for differences in gene function across groups. For GSVA,[22] a criterion of P value <.05 was employed to ascertain the significance of enrichment. The input for GSVA was derived from the expression matrix, and the enriched pathways discerned in the GSVA outcomes were subsequently computed.


### 2.6. Construction and validation of sepsis diagnostic model
  To identify key genes from the intersection of CCSRDEGs for further analysis, we utilized 2 advanced machine learning techniques. Several studies have demonstrated the great efficacy of machine learning in the biomedical domain, including the identification of disease-related biomarkers, precise clinical diagnosis, and enhanced therapeutic approaches. For our analysis, we applied logistic regression models and RF algorithms using the “glmnet”[23] and “randomForest” packages, respectively. These techniques enabled us to systematically select and evaluate the most significant genes from the dataset, ensuring a robust and comprehensive approach to our subsequent analyses.[24] LASSO has shown excellent adaptability and generalization across various datasets, while RF enhances model accuracy and stability. The intersection of these 2 sepsis diagnostic algorithms yielded the final candidate key genes for further analysis. Afterwards, the R program ggdecision curve analysis (DCA) was utilized to generate DCA plots and nomograms for important genes, respectively, and the R package rms was employed to assess the precision and discriminatory ability of the logistic regression model. The diagnostic efficacy of the linear predictors for sepsis in the logistic regression model was evaluated using the “pROC” R program.


### 2.7. Constructing mRNA–miRNA and mRNA–transcription factor (TF) interaction networks using Cytoscape
  The encyclopedia of RNA interactomes database (version 3.0),[25] an enhanced version of the starBase database, was employed to predict miRNA interactions with key genes (mRNAs). We applied a pancancerNum threshold >10 to filter the mRNA–miRNA interactions. Cytoscape (Cytoscape Consortium) was used to show the final regulatory network. Additionally, TFs that regulate key genes at the posttranscriptional level were identified from the ChIPBase database.[26] The relationships between these TFs and important genes were examined, and Cytoscape software was used to show the mRNA–TF regulatory network.


### 2.8. Analysis of differential gene expression in key genes Mann–Whitney U tests
  Mann–Whitney U tests (Wilcoxon rank sum test) were conducted to assess the disparities in gene expression between sepsis and normal specimens within the GEO dataset (combined datasets). Following the analysis of expression discrepancies, significant genes were identified, and their receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were evaluated in the combined GEO dataset (combined datasets).[27]


### 2.9. Immune infiltration analysis
  Single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA)[28] method was utilized to compute enrichment scores, indicating the proportion of immune cells in both sepsis and control samples. Samples were selected based on a P value <.05, and differences were visualized using grouping comparison plots. Furthermore, the association between immune cells and differentially expressed genes linked to CCSRDEGs in sepsis was illustrated by correlation heatmaps made using the R pheatmap package. Subsequently, MCPCounter[29] was used to assess the infiltration levels of immune and stromal cells using expression data. In addition to 2 nonimmune stromal cell types, fibroblasts and endothelial cells, this tool estimates the presence of 8 different types of immune cells: dendritic cells, neutrophils, B lymphocytes, cluster of differentiation 4+ (CD4+) T cells, CD8+ T cells, natural killer (NK) cells, monocytes, and cytotoxic lymphocytes (which include CD8+ T cells and cytotoxic innate lymphoid NK cells). With a significant criterion of P value <.05, a Spearman correlation analysis based on MCPCounter estimations and antigen gene expression was carried out.


### 2.10. Functional similarity analysis
  A reliable quantitative technique for assessing the similarity of genes and genomes is provided by GO annotation. In this work, we measured the GO semantic similarity of important genes using the GOSemSim program. We calculated the geometric mean of these genes across 3 GO categories: cellular component, molecular function, and biological process (BP), to derive a comprehensive similarity score. The ggplot program was used to depict the functional similarity analysis results, allowing for a clear and comprehensive data representation.


### 2.11. Statistical analysis
  The R program (version 4.2.2) was used to analyze and examine the information. Independent Student t tests were utilized for normally distributed variables and the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for non-normally distributed data in order to assess the statistical significance of continuous variables in 2 groups. When comparing 3 or more groups, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used. Correlation coefficients between different molecules were determined using Spearman correlation analysis, with a P value <.05 designated as the level of statistical significance.


### 2.12. Ethical approval
  This study was exempted from ethical review, as it used publicly accessible and anonymized data that the ethics committee determined posed no risk of disclosing participant privacy.


## 3. Results

### 3.1. Data collection and correction
  The research design is outlined in Figure 1. We applied the R package sva to remove batch effects from the datasets GSE28750, GSE54514, and GSE131761, resulting in an integrated GEO dataset. First, we compared expression value differences before and after batch effect removal using box plots (Fig. 2A–B). Next, we used PCA (Fig. 2C–D) to compare the low-dimensional feature distributions of the datasets before and after batch effect removal. The results from the box plots and PCA indicate that the batch effects in the samples were effectively eliminated after processing.


### 3.2. Sepsis-related differential expression genes associated with copper death and cellular senescence
  The GEO dataset, when integrated (combined datasets), was categorized into sepsis and normal groups. Using the R package “limma” for differential expression analysis, 4201 DEGs were found. Within this set, 2133 genes showed up-regulation (log FC > 0 and P value <.05), while 2068 genes exhibited down-regulation (log FC < 0 and P value <.05), as depicted in the volcano plot of the analysis (Fig. 3A). To pinpoint CCSRDEGs, we initially overlapped 72 CCRGS and 3584 CSRGs, visually represented through a Venn diagram (Fig. 3B), resulting in 29 CCSRGs. Subsequently, we intersected all DEGs with the 29 CCSRGs to extract 17 sepsis-related CCSRDEGs (Fig. 3C), displayed through a Venn diagram. Ultimately, the expression variances of sepsis-related CCSRDEGs were scrutinized, and a heatmap was used to map the differential ranking (Fig. 3D).


### 3.3. Gene ontology (GO) and pathway enrichment analysis (KEGG)
  We utilized the R programming language to carry out GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses on 17 sepsis-associated CCSRDEGs. Detailed results can be found in Table 2. The GO enrichment analysis revealed significant biological responses involving copper ions, cellular responses to copper ion presence, metal ion binding, and reactions to inorganic substances. The tricarboxylic acid cycle complex, early endoplasmic reticulum, oxidoreductase complex, and mitochondrial matrix were shown to have significant enrichment. Molecular function analysis revealed notable associations with ubiquitin-protein ligase binding, ubiquitin-like protein ligase binding, S-acyltransferase activity, and lipopolysaccharide binding. The tricarboxylic acid cycle, carbon metabolism, neurological illnesses, cartilage disease, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, Alzheimer disease, and cancer-related central carbon metabolism were among the pathways in which DEGs were found to be enriched, according to KEGG pathway analysis (Fig. 4A–B). Furthermore, we constructed networks representing BP, cellular component, molecular function, and KEGG pathways derived from the GO and KEGG enrichment analyses (Fig. 4C–D). Larger nodes in these networks indicate entries containing more connected molecules, while lines in these networks show links between molecules and their annotations.


### 3.4. Gene set enrichment analysis and gene set variation analysis
  The influence of gene expression on sepsis and the BPs implicated in the consolidated GEO dataset were assessed through GSEA, as presented in Figure 5A. Significant enrichment within biological pathways, including the Tcr pathway (Fig. 5B), Ctl pathway (Fig. 5C), CTLA4 pathway (Fig. 5D), IL-17 pathway (Fig. 5E), and the CD28 dependent Pi3k Akt signaling (Fig. 5F), was highlighted in Table 3. To further investigate the variances across different groups in the h.all.v7.4.symbols.gmt gene set within the integrated GEO dataset, GSVA was conducted. Detailed results are provided in Table 4, revealing statistically significant differences (P value <.05) in pathways such as mitotic spindle, glycolysis, G2M checkpoint, cholesterol homeostasis, spermatogenesis, xenobiotic metabolism, UV response up, E2F targets, estrogen response late, and apical junction. Among the groups categorized as normal versus sepsis within the integrated GEO dataset. The GSVA study results depicting differential expression between these groups are presented in the heatmap below (Fig. 5G).


### 3.5. Identification of diagnostic genes through machine learning
  After intersecting the 4201 DEGs with the 29 genes related to copper death and cellular senescence (CCSR), we discovered 17 CCSRDEGs in sepsis. Logistic regression analysis was performed on these 17 CCSRDEGs. The forest plot (Fig. 6A) highlighted the expression grouping of CCSRDEGs within the logistic regression model, indicating that 16 CCSRDEGs (amyloid beta precursor protein [APP], beta-secretase 1 [BACE1], beclin 1 [BECN1], cluster of differentiation 274 [CD274], death domain associated protein [DAXX], dihydrolipoamide branched chain transacylase E2 [DBT], dihydrolipoamide S-acetyltransferase [DLAT], dihydrolipoamide S-succinyltransferase [DLST], glutaminase [GLS], glutathione peroxidase 4, high-mobility group box 1, metallothionein 1X, pyruvate dehydrogenase E1 subunit beta [PDHB], prion protein [PRNP], solute carrier family 25 member 3, and alpha-synuclein) displayed statistically significant differences (P value <.05). Subsequently, a diagnostic model for sepsis was created using the LASSO regression technique, illustrated in the LASSO regression model plot (Fig. 6B) and the LASSO variable trace plot (Fig. 6C). The RF algorithm was then employed to compare the expression levels of these 16 significant CCSRDEGs between normal groups and sepsis, resulting in a decision tree error curve plot (Fig. 6D). This analysis revealed that 11 CCSRDEGs (APP, BACE1, BECN1, CD274, DAXX, DBT, DLAT, DLST, GLS, PDHB, and PRNP) had a significant impact on sepsis diagnosis (Fig. 6E). Finally, by intersecting the CCSRDEGs identified through both the LASSO regression model and the RF algorithm, we identified 11 key genes: APP, BACE1, BECN1, CD274, DAXX, DBT, DLAT, DLST, GLS, PDHB, and PRNP. These key genes were further visualized with a Venn diagram (Fig. 6F) for comprehensive investigation.


### 3.6. Validation of sepsis diagnostic model
  In order to verify the effectiveness of the sepsis diagnostic model, a nomogram was developed to show the connections among important genes (model genes) in the combined GEO dataset (integrated datasets) (Fig. 7A). The results revealed that BECN1’s expression level was notably more effective in the sepsis diagnostic model than other factors. We created a calibration plot (Fig. 7B) to evaluate the model’s ability to predict actual outcomes. It is clear that the calibration line, shown as a dashed line, slightly deviates from the diagonal of the ideal model but closely matches it. Additionally, DCA displayed the clinical usefulness of the sepsis diagnostic model (Fig. 7C). The ROC curve from the logistic regression model also demonstrated a strong diagnostic performance (Fig. 7D).


### 3.7. Construction of mRNA–miRNA and mRNA–TF interaction networks
  Investigating the interaction networks between mRNAs and miRNAs, as well as mRNAs and TFs, is essential for gaining insights into gene regulatory mechanisms, the molecular underpinnings of diseases, and for the discovery of novel therapeutic targets and biomarkers. By leveraging the encyclopedia of RNA interactomes database, we predicted the miRNAs that interact with 11 key genes (Fig. 8A), which yielded interaction data for 10 key genes and 87 miRNAs, culminating in the formation of 121 mRNA–miRNA interaction pairs. Using the ChIPBase database, we identified TFs interacting with 11 key genes. This search provided data for 65 TFs associated with 9 of these key genes, enabling us to create the mRNA–TF regulatory network (Fig. 8B).


### 3.8. Differential expression analysis of key genes in sepsis and normal groups in combined datasets
  Recognizing the pivotal involvement of immune and inflammatory mechanisms in sepsis pathogenesis, we performed an analysis of immune cell infiltration to explore the regulation of immune responses in sepsis. The differences in expression for 11 key genes between sepsis and normal groups within the GEO dataset were presented. The findings indicated that 8 key genes (BECN1, CD274, DAXX, PDHB, PRNP, APP, DBT, and GLS) exhibited statistically significant differences in expression between the groups in the combined datasets (P value <.001). Moreover, a correlation analysis of the full expression matrix of the 11 key genes from the GEO dataset showed positive correlations between DLAT and PRNP, and PRNP and DLAT, while negative correlations were noted between PRNP and DAXX, and DLAT and DAXX (Fig. 9A–B). Further, the functional similarity analysis and visualization of the 11 key genes indicated that BECN1 had the highest functional similarity with other key genes (Fig. 9C). Lastly, ROC curve analysis for the prediction of sepsis using 7 genes revealed that CD274 and PRNP had higher area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.669 and 0.668, respectively. The AUC values for the other 5 genes (DAXX, PDHB, BECN1, GLS, and DBT) were 0.661, 0.647, 0.631, 0.612, and 0.609, respectively (Fig. 9D–J). These results suggest that all 7 gene features possess significant diagnostic value.


### 3.9. Immune infiltration analysis (ssGSEA and MCPCounter)
  ssGSEA and MCPCounter techniques were used to conduct a correlation analysis in order to investigate the relationship between these diagnostic genes and immune cell infiltration. This investigation aimed to provide a more thorough understanding of the role played by genes associated with central copper apoptosis in immunological infiltration. The findings from the ssGSEA analysis revealed that PDHB is positively correlated with central memory CD4 T cells and that GLS is positively correlated with activated CD8 T cells. Conversely, APP showed a negative association with activated CD8 T cells, and PDHB showed a negative correlation with type 17 T helper cells (Fig. 10A–B). The MCPCounter analysis revealed that PRNP and PDHB both had positive correlations with monocytic lineage immune cells. Additionally, negative correlations were noted between PDHB and neutrophils, as well as between CD274 and B lineage immune cells (Fig. 10C).


## 4. Discussion
  Sepsis is a tricky syndrome to understand as there is no single reason for sepsis, and the mechanisms aren’t fully defined yet. The treatment of sepsis has become more focused, thanks to the advancement of medical technology.[30] The majority of sepsis patients receive treatment in a timely fashion in the emergency department and intensive care units.[31,32] The need for early identification and risk stratification has focused disproportionate attention on a wide variety of prognostic markers. Although these markers assist medical practitioners in adopting more timely and efficient disease management techniques,[33–36]they remain nonspecific and diagnostic, and results return too late for practical use. This argues for more investigation into the mechanisms that define and drive the syndrome of sepsis.

  This study thoroughly examines the molecular interactions of cuproptosis,[37] cellular senescence, and immunological dysregulation in the pathogenesis and progression of sepsis. By integrating multicenter transcriptomic data and employing a multi-algorithmic approach, we identified 17 differentially expressed genes linked to copper-induced cell death and cellular senescence (CCSRDEGs). We also found 11 important diagnostic genes (APP, BACE1, BECN1, CD274, DAXX, DBT, DLAT, DLST, GLS, PDHB, and PRNP) and created a diagnostic model that works well. These findings highlight the crucial importance of copper-dependent cellular apoptosis and mitochondrial metabolic dysfunction in sepsis-related immune metabolic disorders, providing innovative theoretical insights into the systemic immune metabolic dysregulation observed in sepsis.[38,39]

  The findings show that certain genes linked to copper and cell stress are upregulated in key energy pathways like the tricarboxylic acid cycle, glycolysis, and oxidative phosphorylation. This points to how disrupted copper levels can exacerbate mitochondrial problems, starving cells of energy and pushing septic tissues toward premature aging or senescence. BECN1 stands out as the top player in our diagnostic model, hinting that glitches in autophagy – the cell’s cleanup process – might be a major culprit behind ongoing inflammation and tissue harm in sepsis. This fits with earlier research showing that faulty autophagy ramps up inflammatory damage.[40–42]

  On top of that, the elevated levels of the immune checkpoint protein CD274 (also known as PD-L1) explain why the immune system often goes into a paralyzed state during sepsis.[43] Significant differences in APP and PRNP expression suggest that protein misfolding and stress in the endoplasmic reticulum could be fueling the body’s widespread inflammatory storm.[44] Then there are genes like PDHB, DBT, DLAT, and DLST, which serve as central hubs in mitochondria. They seem to drive shifts in metabolism and interfere with immune cells by altering how cells use oxygen-based fuels and convert energy. Changes in PDHB and GLS appear to be the body’s attempt to patch up energy shortages amid all the inflammation, which ties into the hot topic of “immunometabolic coupling.” Recent studies highlight how mitochondrial handling of pyruvate is crucial for firing up macrophages and dialing in inflammatory responses, backing up what we’re seeing with PDHB and GLS here.[45]

  By analyzing the data with techniques like LASSO regression and RF analysis, we pinpointed 11 crucial genes that link sepsis to cuproptosis – a type of cell death associated with copper overload. Among them, changes in PDHB and GLS could disrupt the activity of pyruvate dehydrogenase, an enzyme key to energy production, resulting in a buildup of harmful reactive oxygen species and inflammation driven by the senescence-associated secretory phenotype,[46] which is essentially cells aging prematurely and releasing inflammatory signals.

  This lines up nicely with our GSEA, showing ramped-up activity in pathways like PI3K-Akt and IL-17 signaling, which are big players in cell survival and immune responses.[47] Likewise, shifts in CD274 point to the immune system putting the brakes on T cells through checkpoint mechanisms, potentially weakening the fight against infection.[48] Meanwhile, PRNP and APP might disrupt how proteins fold and maintain balance inside cells, making cellular aging worse and drawing in more immune cells to the chaos.[49] This is backed by our ssGSEA results, which show strong links to CD4+ T cells and neutrophils – key immune responders.[50]

  All in all, these insights support our main idea: the interplay between cuproptosis and cellular senescence is a driving force behind organ failure in sepsis, mainly by throwing off the body’s redox balance (how it handles oxidative stress) and messing with immune control.

  Our analysis of immune cell infiltration, using tools like ssGSEA and MCPCounter, revealed some major shifts in the immune profiles of sepsis patients. This backs up the idea that a messed-up immune response is a classic feature of sepsis.[51] We spotted tight connections between important metabolic genes and specific immune cell groups, like Th17 cells, CD4+ central memory T cells, CD8+ T cells, and NK cells. This goes further than earlier studies that mostly zeroed in on inflammatory markers,[52] giving us richer details on the underlying mechanisms and a bigger-picture view of the whole system.

  Wrapping it up, our work shines a light on how central the immune system is in sepsis, and it points to how cuproptosis (copper-related cell death) and cellular senescence (premature aging) might be tweaking immune cell actions. These insights deepen what we know about how sepsis develops and pave the way for fresh approaches to diagnosing and treating it.

  This research packs a real punch in both theory and real-world applications. At its core, genes like PDHB, GLS, and CD274 act as a vital link connecting how our bodies handle energy, maintain balance against oxidative stress, and regulate the immune system.[53] This gives us a fresh way to grasp why the immune system and metabolism go haywire in sepsis – a life-threatening condition where the body’s response to infection spirals out of control. Diving deeper, the strong ties between PDHB, GLS, and how mitochondria (our cells’ powerhouses) manage energy hint that tweaking metabolic pathways might reduce organ damage. Meanwhile, weird changes in CD274 open the door to using immune checkpoint therapies – think of them as brakes on the immune system – to treat sepsis more effectively. On top of that, shifts in these genes’ activity could become handy biomarkers for spotting sepsis early and predicting how it might progress.

  In the clinic, weaving these markers into prediction tools could make a huge difference in catching sepsis sooner and customizing treatments for each person. Overall, this work beefs up our understanding of how immunity and metabolism interplay in critical illnesses while pointing to exciting new targets for diagnosing and treating sepsis in the age of personalized medicine.

  Bioinformatics were employed to examine the associations among sepsis, copper-induced cytotoxicity, cellular senescence, and their respective genes. But there are some things that need to be made clear. The GEO data for this study came from public transcriptome databases. Even after accounting for batch effects, these datasets may not accurately reflect the temporal progression of the disease or its differential impact on various demographic groups. Second, bioinformatics predictions were the main reason for most of the connections between genes and the immune system. This is a big problem because they haven’t been tested in a lab. Scientists need to test the genes and pathways they discovered in the lab to confirm their involvement in sepsis development. Third, the diagnostic model made predictions about what would happen, but it needs more clinical data to be useful in other places and in the real world. The suggested diagnostic framework and biomarkers do not have clinical validation, which is necessary to determine their usefulness.

  Even if steps were taken to reduce batch effects, combining several datasets may still leave behind certain batch effects that could change how we understand results from differential expression and pathway analysis. Future research should concentrate on 3 principal domains: validating the expression profiles of key genes and their relationship with disease severity and prognosis in large, independent clinical cohorts; elucidating the causal mechanisms of copper-induced cell death and cellular senescence in immune metabolic imbalance and organ dysfunction using cell and animal models; and integrating these molecular markers into multi-omics clinical predictive models to improve early detection and precision intervention in sepsis.


## 5. Conclusion and future prospects
  This study pioneers the identification of systemic-level interconnected mechanisms of copper death, cellular senescence, and immune infiltration in sepsis while also developing an accurate diagnostic model utilizing core metabolic-immune genes. This work provides an explanation of the immune metabolic dysregulation in sepsis and suggests 1st hand new molecular bases for the accurate prediction and targeted intervention of sepsis. In addition, the study constructed the mRNA–miRNA and mRNA–TF interaction networks, illuminating some regulatory factors that could drive the pathogenesis of sepsis. Taking these findings into account, the next step will be to incorporate clinical mechanics that can help translate these concepts into the development of potentially transformative diagnostic and therapeutic tools for the sepsis clinic.
