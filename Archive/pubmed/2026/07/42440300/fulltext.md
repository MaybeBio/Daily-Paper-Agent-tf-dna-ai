# Gut‐Derived Sodium Butyrate Attenuates Liver Fibrosis by Inhibiting Aerobic Glycolysis via the HDAC3/c‐Myc Signaling Axis


## ABSTRACT
  Liver fibrosis remains a major global health challenge, driving interest in bioactive metabolites from natural sources as potential therapies. Sodium butyrate (NaB), a gut microbiota–derived short‐chain fatty acid with notable anti‐fibrotic activity, shows promise, although its mechanisms remain unclear. This study aims to explore the molecular mechanisms through which NaB attenuates liver fibrosis. This study integrates bioinformatics and network pharmacology to predict the molecular targets through which NaB exerts its anti‐fibrotic effects. Candidate targets were validated through molecular docking, histopathology, CETSA, and SPR. The inferred mechanisms were further validated in vitro (LX‐2 cells) and in vivo (CCl4‐induced mice) using Western blot, IF Co‐IP, RT‐qPCR, ELISA, and histopathological staining. HDAC3 was identified as a key driver of liver fibrosis. As a natural HDAC3 inhibitor, NaB markedly attenuated CCl4‐induced hepatic injury and fibrosis, accompanied by reduced expression of HDAC3, c‐Myc, glycolytic enzymes (HK2, PFKFB3, and PKM2), and fibrotic markers (α‐SMA and type I collagen) in fibrotic liver tissues and LX‐2 cells. Mechanistically, NaB exerts its anti‐fibrotic effects by inhibiting HDAC3 expression and deacetylase activity, promoting c‐Myc acetylation and downregulating its expression, thereby suppressing glycolytic metabolic reprogramming in hepatic stellate cells. These findings indicate that NaB attenuates liver fibrosis by inhibiting HDAC3/c‐Myc–mediated metabolic reprogramming, revealing a gut‐derived anti‐fibrotic mechanism and potential therapeutic targets.


## Introduction
  Liver fibrosis is a common pathological consequence of chronic liver injury caused by various etiologies, including viral hepatitis, excessive alcohol consumption, and metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease [1]. Characterized by excessive deposition of extracellular matrix (ECM), liver fibrosis progressively disrupts hepatic architecture and function, ultimately leading to cirrhosis, liver failure, and hepatocellular carcinoma [2]. Globally, liver diseases are responsible for approximately 2 million deaths annually, accounting for nearly 4% of all‐cause mortality, with most deaths resulting from complications of cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma [3]. Although advances in the understanding of fibrogenic mechanisms have facilitated the development of potential therapeutic approaches, no approved anti‐fibrotic therapy is currently available. Consequently, there is an urgent need to identify novel molecular targets and explore effective therapeutic strategies for the prevention and treatment of liver fibrosis.

  The gut–liver axis is a bidirectional communication network composed of the gut microbiota, intestinal barrier, and liver, and is increasingly recognized as a key regulator of liver health and disease. Under physiological conditions, the gut and liver communicate through the portal circulation, enterohepatic bile acid circulation, and immune signaling pathways, collectively maintaining metabolic and immune homeostasis [4, 5]. However, gut microbial dysbiosis and compromised intestinal barrier function can promote the translocation of harmful microbial components, such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS), to the liver, thereby triggering hepatic inflammation and fibrogenesis [6, 7]. Conversely, gut microbiota–derived metabolites have emerged as important mediators of gut–liver communication and can exert beneficial effects on liver function by modulating host metabolism, inflammatory responses, and epigenetic regulation [8, 9].

  Gut microbiota–derived metabolites, particularly short‐chain fatty acids (SCFAs), have emerged as important mediators of the gut–liver axis [10]. Among them, sodium butyrate (NaB), a major SCFA generated through microbial fermentation of dietary fiber, has been extensively studied for its anti‐inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti‐fibrotic activities [11, 12]. Increasing evidence indicates that NaB confers hepatoprotective effects by regulating immune responses, enhancing intestinal barrier function, and modulating metabolic communication between the gut and liver [12, 13, 14]. Notably, NaB is also recognized as an endogenous inhibitor of histone deacetylases (HDACs), suggesting that epigenetic regulation may contribute to its biological functions. However, the specific molecular targets and downstream signaling pathways through which NaB attenuates liver fibrosis remain poorly understood.

  HDACs are a family of enzymes that regulate chromatin structure and gene transcription and play critical roles in epigenetic regulation [15], metabolic control [16, 17], and disease progression [18]. Accumulating evidence indicates that NaB, a natural HDAC inhibitor, exerts beneficial effects in multiple liver diseases [19], including primary biliary cholangitis [20], portal hypertension [21], and biliary atresia–associated liver fibrosis [22], largely through suppression of HDAC activity, highlighting epigenetic regulation as a key mechanism underlying its hepatoprotective effects.

  Hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) are the principal fibrogenic cell population in the liver. In their quiescent state, HSCs store retinoids and contribute to the maintenance of hepatic sinusoidal homeostasis. Upon pathological stimulation, HSCs become activated and transdifferentiate into proliferative, contractile myofibroblast‐like cells, leading to ECM deposition [23, 24, 25]. Similar to the Warburg effect observed in cancer cells, HSC activation is accompanied by profound metabolic reprogramming to meet increased bioenergetic and biosynthetic demands, involving alterations in glycolysis, glutaminolysis, and lipid metabolism [26]. Activated HSCs upregulate glucose transporters such as GLUT1 and key rate‐limiting glycolytic enzymes, including HK2, PFKFB3, and PKM2, to enhance glycolytic flux, whereas inhibition of glycolysis effectively suppresses HSC activation [27, 28]. Therefore, targeting metabolic pathways in HSCs represents a promising therapeutic strategy for reversing liver fibrosis. c‐Myc, a member of the MYC proto‐oncogene family, has emerged as a central regulator of cellular metabolism, particularly glycolysis [29, 30]. Accordingly, the HDAC–c‐Myc signaling axis may serve as a critical molecular bridge linking epigenetic regulation to metabolic reprogramming in the anti‐fibrotic effects of NaB.

  This study aimed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which NaB attenuates liver fibrosis from an integrated perspective of the gut–liver axis and metabolic reprogramming, thereby providing novel theoretical insights and potential therapeutic strategies for the prevention and treatment of liver fibrosis.

### Human Liver Tissues
  All liver tissue samples were obtained from patients who underwent hepatic resection in the Department of Hepatobiliary Surgery at the Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou Medical University (Guizhou, China). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardians prior to inclusion in the study. The experimental protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Guizhou Medical University (Approval 2025, Ethics Review No. 349). Fibrotic liver tissues were collected from the peritumoral regions of patients with hepatocellular carcinoma or from liver transplant recipients with decompensated cirrhosis (n = 9), and normal liver tissues were collected from patients with intrahepatic bile duct stones or from areas distant from hepatic hemangiomas (n = 9).


### Bioinformatics and Network Pharmacology Analysis
  A transcriptomic dataset consisting of six normal liver tissues and 40 fibrotic samples was obtained from the GEO database (GSE25097). KEGG enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes was performed using R software (R4.4.1). The SMILES notation and molecular structure of NaB were obtained from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), and its potential molecular targets were predicted using the SwissTargetPrediction platform (https://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/). The predicted NaB targets were then intersected with liver fibrosis–related genes collected from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/) to identify overlapping targets. The three‐dimensional structure of HDAC3 was retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org), and the two‐dimensional structure of sodium butyrate (NaB) in SDF format was obtained from the PubChem database. The HDAC3 protein structure and NaB ligand files were subsequently uploaded to the CB‐Dock2 server for molecular docking analysis (http://clab.labshare.cn:10380/cb‐dock2/), and the binding energy (kcal/mol) was recorded. Binding energies ≤ −5.0 kcal/mol indicate some binding affinity, ≤ −7.0 kcal/mol indicate relatively stable binding, and ≤ −9.0 kcal/mol indicate strong binding. In addition, the STRING database (https://cn.string‐db.org/) was used to explore downstream proteins interacting with the candidate targets.


### Cell Culture and Treatment
  The human hepatic stellate cell line LX‐2, phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM), fetal bovine serum (FBS), and penicillin–streptomycin (P/S) were obtained from Pricella Biotechnology (Wuhan, China). Complete culture medium consisted of DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. LX‐2 cells were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. Following treatment with sodium butyrate (NaB) at indicated concentrations (0.5, 1, and 2 mM), cells were collected for downstream experiments.


### Transfection
  HDAC3 overexpression in LX‐2 cells was established using lentiviral vectors (GeneChem, Shanghai, China). Cells were seeded in T25 flasks at a density of 1 × 105 cells per flask and cultured to 20%–30% confluence prior to infection with LV‐HDAC3 or negative control (LV‐NC) at an MOI of 50 in the presence of 1× HiTransG A. After 16 h, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium, and cells were cultured for an additional 48 h. Stable cell lines were selected using puromycin (5 μg/mL) for 48 h. HDAC3 overexpression efficiency was verified by Western blotting.

  HDAC3 expression in LX‐2 cells was silenced using siRNA oligonucleotides synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China), with sequences listed in Table S1. Cells were seeded in T25 flasks at a density of 1 × 105 cells per flask and transfected at 40%–50% confluence using GP‐transfect‐Mate following the manufacturer's protocol. After 6 h, the transfection medium was replaced with complete DMEM. Cells were collected 72 h post‐transfection for further analyses. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed by Western blotting, and the most efficient siRNA sequence among four candidates was used in subsequent experiments.


### Animal Experiments
  All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH Publication No. 80‐23, revised 1978) and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Guizhou Medical University (Approval 2025‐5‐9, Ethics Review No. 2502902).

  Recent studies have reported recommended doses of sodium butyrate (NaB) for in vivo experiments [31, 32], and silymarin was used as a positive control to evaluate comparative therapeutic efficacy [33, 34]. After one week of acclimatization under specific pathogen‐free (SPF) conditions (temperature 22°C ± 2°C, humidity 55% ± 5%, 12‐h light/dark cycle) with free access to standard chow and water, 30 male C57BL/6J mice (8–12 weeks old, 20 ± 2 g; Vital River, Beijing, China) were randomly divided into five groups (n = 6 per group): Control, Model, NaB‐low (250 mg/kg), NaB‐high (500 mg/kg), and positive control (silymarin, 100 mg/kg).

  Liver fibrosis was induced by intraperitoneal injection of 10% CCl4 twice weekly for eight consecutive weeks, while control mice received an equal volume of corn oil. After 2 weeks of modeling, mice in the NaB‐low, NaB‐high, and silymarin groups were administered the corresponding treatments via oral gavage three times per week for 6 weeks, whereas the Control and Model groups received an equivalent volume of PBS. Twenty‐four hours after the final administration, mice were anesthetized, and serum and liver tissues were collected for subsequent analyses. Serum samples were used to measure liver function indicators (AST and ALT), pro‐inflammatory cytokines (TNF‐α and IL‐1β), and the anti‐inflammatory cytokine IL‐10 using ELISA kits. Liver tissues were harvested for histopathological staining and Western blot analysis.


### Western Blot (WB) Analysis
  Total proteins were extracted from cultured cells or liver tissues, using RIPA lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit, and equal amounts of protein were loaded for SDS–PAGE, with gel concentrations adjusted according to target protein molecular weights. Proteins were subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non‐fat milk, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C and then with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) system, and band intensities were analyzed using ImageJ software.


### Cellular Thermal Shift Assay (CETSA)
  LX‐2 cells treated with either 2 mM sodium butyrate (NaB) or phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) as a control. After 24 h of incubation, cells were harvested, resuspended, and equally divided into eight aliquots. Samples were heated at a series of predefined temperatures (37°C–65°C) for 3 min using a thermal cycler, followed by incubation at room temperature for 3 min. Cells were then subjected to three freeze–thaw cycles using liquid nitrogen and a 37°C water bath to ensure complete lysis. Lysates were centrifuged at 4°C for 15 min at high speed, and supernatants were collected for protein concentration determination. Equal amounts of total protein were subsequently used for Western blot analysis.


### Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR)
  The SPR was used to verify the binding affinity between NaB and HDAC3. HDAC3 protein was immobilized on a CM5 sensor chip activated with EDC/NHS, followed by ethanolamine blocking. Serial concentrations of NaB were injected over the chip surface, and binding responses were recorded in real time. The equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) was calculated using a steady‐state affinity model.


### Co‐Immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP)
  Cells were lysed in ice‐cold IP lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors for 2 h. The lysates were then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against HDAC3 or c‐Myc, followed by incubation with magnetic beads for 4 h. After incubation, the supernatant was removed, and the bead–antibody–antigen complexes were washed, eluted, and subsequently analyzed by Western blotting.


### Real‐Time Quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR)
  Total RNA was extracted from LX2 cells using a total RNA kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA concentration and purity were determined spectrophotometrically. cDNA was synthesized from total RNA using a cDNA synthesis kit. RT‐qPCR was performed using the SYBR Green Master kit on a real‐time PCR system. After amplification, relative mRNA expression levels were normalized to GAPDH and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with the control group serving as the calibrator.


### Cellular HDAC3 Enzyme Activity Assay
  LX‐2 cells were treated with 2 mM NaB or PBS for 24 h. Cells were then harvested, lysed, and adjusted to a protein concentration of 10 μg/μL. HDAC3 activity was measured using a colorimetric assay kit according to the manufacturer's protocol. Absorbance was recorded at 405 nm, and HDAC3‐specific activity was calculated by subtracting non‐specific activity from total activity.


### Cellular c‐Myc Acetylation Level Assay
  The acetylation level of c‐Myc was determined by immunoprecipitation combined with Western blotting. Briefly, cells were lysed in ice‐cold IP lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors for 2 h. The lysates were incubated overnight at 4°C with an anti–c‐Myc primary antibody, followed by incubation with magnetic beads for 4 h. The supernatant was removed, and the bead–antibody–antigen complexes were washed, eluted, and analyzed by Western blotting. Acetylated c‐Myc was detected using an acetylation monoclonal antibody, and total c‐Myc levels were determined using a c‐Myc polyclonal antibody.


### Immunofluorescence (IF) Staining
  Liver tissue sections or LX‐2 cell slides from different experimental groups were prepared for immunofluorescence staining. Samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X‐100 for 10 min. After blocking with 5% goat serum for 1 h, the samples were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. Subsequently, they were incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. The samples were then mounted with an anti‐fade mounting medium containing DAPI, and fluorescence images were captured using a Zeiss upright fluorescence microscope (Zeiss, Germany).


### Immunohistochemistry (IHC)
  Paraffin‐embedded liver tissue sections were subjected to standard deparaffinization and antigen retrieval procedures. The sections were then blocked with 5% goat serum for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, they were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. After color development using a DAB substrate, the sections were counterstained with 10% hematoxylin, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. Images were captured using a Zeiss light microscope (Zeiss, Germany), and quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software.


### Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E), Masson and Sirius Red Staining
  H&E Staining Kit (C0105M), Sirius Red Staining Kit (C0190M), and Masson's Trichrome Staining Kit (C0189S) were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Paraffin‐embedded liver tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series following standard protocols, with subsequent steps carried out according to the manufacturer's instructions. The sections were stained with H&E to visualize histopathological features, Masson trichrome to detect collagen fibers, and Sirius red to evaluate collagen deposition. Images were acquired using a Zeiss microscope for further analysis.


### Statistical Analysis
  All data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.1.2. Comparisons between two groups were made using Student's t‐test, and multiple group comparisons were analyzed by one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.


## Results

### Bioinformatics and Network Pharmacology Analysis of the Potential Mechanisms by Which NaB Alleviates Liver Fibrosis
  Analysis of a GEO dataset comprising six normal liver tissues and 40 fibrotic liver samples revealed a significant downregulation of the butanoate metabolism pathway in fibrotic tissues (Figure 1A), implicating a potential role for NaB in liver fibrosis. To explore the molecular mechanisms underlying the anti‐fibrotic effects of NaB, its SMILES notation and chemical structure were obtained from the PubChem database (Figure 1B). SwissTargetPrediction analysis identified 18 potential targets of NaB (Figure 1C). Cross‐referencing these targets with 10 782 liver fibrosis‐related genes from the GeneCards database yielded 9 overlapping targets (Figure 1D,E), including HDAC3, SLC6A1, EGLN1, PPARA, PPARD, FABP2, FABP3, FAPB4, and FABP5. The SwissTargetPrediction database was used to obtain prediction probability scores for nine intersecting targets. The results demonstrated that HDAC3 exhibited the highest predictive probability score, ranking first among all candidate targets (Table 1). In addition, HDAC3 belongs to the HDACs family, whereas NaB is a well‐characterized broad‐spectrum HDAC inhibitor, suggesting a strong concordance in their pharmacological mechanisms. Meanwhile, molecular docking analysis revealed a favorable binding affinity between NaB and HDAC3 (Figure 1F). Based on these results, we preliminarily identified HDAC3 as a promising candidate target of NaB and subsequently focused our investigation on liver fibrosis‐related phenotypes.


### HDAC3 Is Involved in the Progression of Liver Fibrosis
  A total of 18 human liver tissue samples were obtained from patients undergoing hepatic resection or liver transplantation at the Department of Hepatobiliary Surgery, Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou Medical University, including nine normal and nine fibrotic liver tissues. To investigate the clinical relevance of HDAC3 in liver fibrosis, IHC staining (Figure 2A) and WB analysis (Figure 2B) were performed to determine the expression levels of HDAC3 and α‐SMA. Compared with normal liver tissues, fibrotic liver tissues exhibited significantly higher expression levels of both HDAC3 and α‐SMA. These results indicate that HDAC3 is upregulated in human liver fibrosis and may contribute to fibrogenesis through its association with HSCs' activation.


### NaB Inhibits the Activation of LX‐2 Cells by Targeting HDAC3
  Previous studies have demonstrated that LX‐2 cells are intrinsically maintained in a partially activated state [35], and that exposure to 10% FBS further enhances their activation, making them a widely accepted model for studying HSC activation in vitro [36]. Therefore, LX‐2 cells cultured in medium supplemented with 10% FBS were used as the positive control in this study. Based on the CCK‐8 cytotoxicity assay results (Figure S1), NaB concentrations of 0.5, 1, and 2 mM were selected for subsequent experiments. As shown in Figure 3A,B, NaB treatment significantly downregulated the expression levels of the HSC activation markers α‐SMA and collagen I in a dose‐dependent manner compared with the control group. Given the predicted interaction between NaB and HDAC3, CETSA was employed to validate target engagement in cells. As shown in Figure 3C, NaB significantly enhanced the thermal stability of HDAC3 across a temperature range of 49°C–65°C, supporting the direct binding of NaB to HDAC3. Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) was employed to evaluate the direct interaction between NaB and HDAC3 by monitoring real‐time changes in the refractive index during ligand–analyte binding. As shown in Figure 3D, SPR analysis revealed a concentration‐dependent and reversible interaction between NaB and HDAC3. Steady‐state affinity fitting generated a KD value of 1.26 × 10−6 M, confirming the direct binding of NaB to HDAC3 with high affinity in vitro. Together with the molecular docking results, integrating network pharmacology predictions, molecular docking analysis, and experimental validation using CETSA and SPR, we identified HDAC3 as the key functional target mediating the anti‐fibrotic effects of NaB, and further mechanistic studies will focus on HDAC3 and its downstream molecular targets.


### NaB Inhibits LX2 Activation by Blocking Aerobic Glycolysis via the HDAC3/c‐Myc Axis
  To investigate the molecular mechanisms through which NaB regulates liver fibrosis, 25 HDAC3‐interacting proteins were identified using the STRING databases (Figure 4A). A network was subsequently constructed using Cytoscape 3.10.3 based on degree values (Figure 4B). The top five hub proteins were NCOR1, EP300, KDM1A, PPARG, and MYC (Table 2). Subsequently, Venn diagram analysis was conducted to identify the overlap between the top five targets ranked by degree value and liver fibrosis‐related targets obtained from the GeneCards database. The analysis identified EP300 and MYC as common targets (Figure 4C). Given that HSC activation is a critical driver of liver fibrogenesis [1] and that glycolytic reprogramming serves as an essential metabolic hallmark of activated HSCs [26]. MYC attracted particular attention due to its well‐established role in regulating glycolysis and cellular metabolism [29, 30]. Accordingly, we proposed that NaB ameliorates hepatic fibrosis by suppressing aerobic glycolysis via the HDAC3/c‐Myc signaling axis.

  To experimentally validate this hypothesis, WB analysis was first performed to determine the expression levels of HDAC3 and c‐Myc in LX‐2 cells treated with different concentrations of NaB (0.5, 1, and 2 mM). As shown in Figure 4D, NaB treatment dose‐dependently reduced the expression of both HDAC3 and c‐Myc compared with the control group. To further investigate whether NaB affects the interaction between HDAC3 and c‐Myc Co‐IP assays were performed in LX‐2 cells treated with or without 2 mM NaB. The results demonstrated that NaB treatment markedly weakened the interaction between HDAC3 and c‐Myc (Figure 4E,F). Given the critical role of c‐Myc in regulating glycolytic metabolism, the effects of NaB on aerobic glycolysis were subsequently evaluated by examining the expression of key glycolytic enzymes. WB analysis showed that NaB treatment dose‐dependently decreased the expression levels of HK2, PFKFB3, and PKM2 compared with the control group (Figure 4G). These findings suggest that NaB suppresses glycolytic reprogramming in LX‐2 cells, potentially through modulation of the HDAC3/c‐Myc signaling axis.


### NaB Inhibits LX‐2 Cell Activation by Suppressing HDAC3 Activity and Promoting c‐Myc Acetylation
  To further elucidate the mechanisms underlying the anti‐fibrotic effects of NaB, the regulatory relationship between HDAC3 and c‐Myc was investigated. RT–qPCR analysis showed that NaB treatment significantly reduced HDAC3 mRNA expression in LX‐2 cells, whereas no significant change was observed in c‐Myc mRNA levels (Figure 5A). In addition, HDAC3 activity assays showed that NaB significantly inhibited HDAC3 enzymatic activity (Figure 5B). Given that HDAC3 functions as a deacetylase, the acetylation status of c‐Myc was subsequently examined. As shown in Figure 5C, NaB treatment significantly increased the acetylation level of c‐Myc while concomitantly decreasing its protein expression. To further verify the regulatory role of HDAC3, lentiviral‐mediated overexpression was employed to increase HDAC3 expression in LX‐2 cells. Western blot analysis confirmed a significant elevation of HDAC3 protein levels following transduction (Figure 5D), which was accompanied by a marked increase in c‐Myc protein expression (Figure 5E,F). Importantly, HDAC3 overexpression decreased the acetylation level of c‐Myc and partially abrogated the NaB‐induced increase in c‐Myc acetylation. Moreover, HDAC3 overexpression attenuated the inhibitory effect of NaB on c‐Myc protein expression (Figure 5G). These findings suggest that NaB suppresses c‐Myc expression, at least in part, by inhibiting HDAC3 activity and promoting c‐Myc acetylation.


### NaB Suppresses Aerobic Glycolysis and the Activation of LX2 Cells via the HDAC3/c‐Myc Signaling Axis
  To determine whether the HDAC3/c‐Myc axis mediates the suppressive effects of NaB on HSC activation and glycolytic reprogramming, HDAC3 gain‐ and loss‐of‐function experiments were conducted in LX‐2 cells. Following successful HDAC3 overexpression or knockdown, the expression levels of c‐Myc, HSC activation markers (α‐SMA and collagen I), and key glycolytic enzymes (HK2, PFKFB3, and PKM2) were assessed by WB and ICC/IF in LX‐2 cells treated with or without 2 mM NaB. As shown in Figure 6A–C, HDAC3 overexpression markedly increased the expression of α‐SMA, collagen I, HK2, PFKFB3, and PKM2 compared with the control group. Furthermore, HDAC3 overexpression partially reversed the inhibitory effects of NaB on HSC activation and glycolytic enzyme expression.

  Conversely, siRNA‐mediated HDAC3 knockdown (Figure 7A,B) significantly suppressed the expression of c‐Myc (Figure 7C,D), HSC activation markers (Figure 7E,F), and glycolysis‐related enzymes (Figure 7G), irrespective of NaB treatment. Together with the HDAC3 overexpression results, these findings indicate that HDAC3 functions upstream of c‐Myc‐mediated glycolytic reprogramming and plays a critical role in the anti‐fibrotic effects of NaB.


### NaB Ameliorates Hepatic Fibrosis and Injury in CCl4
‐Treated Mice
  To investigate the anti‐fibrotic effects of NaB in vivo, a CCl4‐induced mouse model of liver fibrosis was established, and NaB was administered by oral gavage to evaluate its therapeutic effect. The experimental design is illustrated in Figure 8A. Histopathological changes and collagen deposition in liver tissues were assessed by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Sirius Red, and Masson's trichrome staining (Figure 8B–D).

  Compared with the control group, H&E staining revealed extensive hepatocellular necrosis surrounding the portal areas, accompanied by marked inflammatory cell infiltration and fibrotic changes in the livers of CCl4‐treated mice. Notably, NaB treatment dose‐dependently alleviated these pathological alterations. Consistently, Sirius Red and Masson's trichrome staining demonstrated substantial collagen accumulation in the periportal regions of fibrotic livers, whereas NaB administration markedly reduced collagen deposition in a dose‐dependent manner.

  To further evaluate liver injury and systemic inflammation, serum biochemical and cytokine analyses were performed. ELISA results showed that CCl4 treatment significantly increased the serum levels of ALT, AST, and the pro‐inflammatory cytokines TNF‐α and IL‐1β, while reducing the level of the anti‐inflammatory cytokine IL‐10 compared with the control group (Figure 8E–G). Importantly, these CCl4‐induced alterations were effectively reversed by NaB treatment in a dose‐dependent manner. Consistent with the in vitro findings, WB analysis demonstrated that the expression levels of HDAC3 and c‐Myc were significantly elevated in the liver tissues of fibrotic mice compared with those of the control group Figure 8H. Notably, NaB treatment markedly suppressed the expression of both HDAC3 and c‐Myc in a dose‐dependent manner. These results further support the involvement of the HDAC3/c‐Myc signaling axis in the anti‐fibrotic effects of NaB in vivo.


### NaB Ameliorates Hepatic Fibrosis by Inhibiting Aerobic Glycolysis Through HDAC3/c‐Myc Signaling Axis in CCl4
‐Treated Mice
  To further validate the involvement of the HDAC3/c‐Myc signaling axis in the anti‐fibrotic effects of NaB in vivo, IHC, IF, and WB analyses were performed to assess the expression of HDAC3, c‐Myc, fibrosis‐related markers (collagen I and α‐SMA), and glycolysis‐related enzymes (HK2, PFKFB3, and PKM2) in liver tissues from CCl4‐induced fibrotic mice. As shown in Figure 9A,B, HDAC3 expression level was significantly increased in the livers of fibrotic mice compared with those of the control group. Concurrently, the expression levels of collagen I, α‐SMA (Figure 9C–G), and the glycolytic enzymes HK2, PFKFB3, and PKM2 (Figure 9H) were markedly elevated. Importantly, NaB treatment dose‐dependently reduced the expression of all these proteins. Collectively, these results further support the notion that NaB attenuates liver fibrosis by suppressing HDAC3/c‐Myc‐mediated glycolytic reprogramming in vivo.


## Discussion
  Liver fibrosis is a pathological wound‐healing response to chronic liver injury, characterized primarily by the activation of HSCs [1]. Increasing evidence highlights the pivotal role of the gut–liver axis in the initiation and progression of liver fibrosis [37, 38]. Through the portal circulation, gut‐derived nutrients, microbial metabolites, and endotoxins can directly reach the liver and profoundly influence hepatic physiology and pathology [39]. Among these metabolites, NaB, a short‐chain fatty acid generated by commensal bacteria such as  Faecalibacterium prausnitzii  and Roseburia spp. through the fermentation of dietary fibers [40, 41], has attracted considerable attention because of its anti‐inflammatory, antifibrotic, and epigenetic regulatory properties [11, 12]. Previous studies have shown that depletion of butyrate‐producing bacteria and reduced intestinal butyrate levels are closely associated with the development of nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and liver fibrosis, whereas butyrate supplementation can effectively attenuate hepatic inflammation and fibrogenesis [42, 43, 44]. In line with these findings, our bioinformatics analysis demonstrated that butanoate metabolism was significantly suppressed in fibrotic liver tissues, further supporting the concept that butyrate acts as a crucial protective mediator within the gut–liver axis.

  In the present study, WB and IHC analyses demonstrated that HDAC3 expression was significantly upregulated in fibrotic human liver tissues compared with normal controls, suggesting a close association between HDAC3 and the progression of liver fibrosis. Activated HSCs are the primary source of ECM deposition, and enhanced glycolysis has been recognized as a critical metabolic adaptation that supports the energy demands and biosynthetic requirements of HSC activation [27]. As a master regulator of cellular metabolism, c‐Myc plays a central role in promoting aerobic glycolysis by transcriptionally regulating multiple glycolytic genes [29, 30]. Previous studies have shown that HDAC inhibitors suppress glycolysis and reduce ATP production in glioblastoma cells in a c‐Myc‐dependent manner [45]. Likewise, emerging evidence has demonstrated that NaB inhibits aerobic glycolysis through the c‐Myc/HK2 signaling axis in hepatocellular carcinoma cells, thereby inducing apoptosis [46]. Through an integrated approach combining network pharmacology, molecular docking, CETSA, and SPR analyses, our study further identified HDAC3 as a potential molecular target of NaB. Based on these findings, we propose that NaB attenuates HSC activation by targeting the HDAC3/c‐Myc signaling pathway. Specifically, NaB downregulates HDAC3 expression, thereby suppressing c‐Myc‐mediated aerobic glycolysis and reducing the expression of key glycolytic enzymes. This metabolic inhibition limits the activation of HSCs and consequently decreases the expression of profibrotic markers, ultimately alleviating liver fibrosis.

  The results of the present study demonstrated that NaB treatment significantly reduced HDAC3 expression in both fibrotic liver tissues and LX‐2 cells, indicating that the antifibrotic effects of NaB are closely associated with its targeted inhibition of HDAC3. Previous studies have shown that HDAC inhibitors exert anticancer effects by promoting c‐Myc acetylation, thereby reducing its protein stability and expression [47]. Similarly, in triple‐negative breast cancer, HDAC1‐mediated deacetylation of c‐Myc suppresses its ubiquitin‐dependent degradation, resulting in c‐Myc protein accumulation [48]. In this study, PPI network analysis, along with Co‐IP and WB assays, confirmed the interaction between HDAC3 and c‐Myc. Furthermore, NaB‐induced reduction of HDAC3 was accompanied by decreased expression levels of c‐Myc and key glycolytic enzymes. Significantly, RT‐qPCR analysis showed that NaB treatment reduced HDAC3 mRNA expression in LX‐2 cells, whereas c‐Myc mRNA levels remained unchanged. Consistently, HDAC3 activity assays demonstrated that NaB markedly inhibited HDAC3 enzymatic activity. The discrepancy between unchanged c‐Myc transcript levels and reduced protein expression suggested that c‐Myc is regulated by NaB at the post‐translational level rather than through transcriptional repression.

  To further investigate this possibility, we examined the acetylation level of c‐Myc and found that NaB treatment significantly increased c‐Myc acetylation while simultaneously reducing its protein abundance. These findings suggest that inhibition of HDAC3 by NaB promotes c‐Myc acetylation, thereby facilitating its degradation and limiting its regulation of glycolytic key enzymes.

  At the functional level, NaB treatment not only suppressed glycolytic activity in HSCs but also markedly reduced the expression of profibrotic markers, including α‐SMA and Collagen I, further highlighting the close link between glycolytic reprogramming and HSC activation. Notably, HDAC3 overexpression partially abrogated the inhibitory effects of NaB on glycolysis and fibrogenic responses, whereas HDAC3 knockdown attenuated HSC activation through the downregulation of c‐Myc expression. These findings provide strong evidence that HDAC3 serves as a critical mediator of the antifibrotic actions of NaB.

  Furthermore, as a gut microbiota–derived metabolite, NaB may exert its antifibrotic effects in part through regulation of the gut–liver axis. Dysbiosis and impaired intestinal barrier function can facilitate the translocation of harmful microbial metabolites, such as LPS, into the portal circulation, thereby activating hepatic stellate cells, Kupffer cells, and innate immune responses. This process promotes the excessive production of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, including TNF‐α, IL‐1β, and IL‐6, as well as profibrotic mediators such as α‐SMA and Collagen I, ultimately contributing to hepatic inflammation and fibrogenesis [5, 23, 49, 50]. Previous studies have demonstrated that NaB enhances intestinal barrier function and limits the translocation of LPS from the gut to the liver, thereby suppressing inflammatory signaling pathways and alleviating liver injury [11]. Consistent with previous reports and the anti‐fibrotic biological effects of silymarin [33, 34], NaB treatment significantly reduced serum levels of TNF‐α, IL‐1β, AST, and ALT, accompanied by marked improvements in hepatic injury and fibrosis in mice. These findings suggest that the protective effects of NaB may involve both direct regulation of HSC metabolic reprogramming and indirect modulation of the gut–liver axis.

  Collectively, our study identifies a previously unrecognized mechanism by which NaB attenuates liver fibrosis through the regulation of metabolic reprogramming. Specifically, NaB suppresses HDAC3 expression and activity, promotes c‐Myc acetylation‐dependent degradation, inhibits the regulatory effect of c‐Myc on glycolysis, and ultimately restrains HSC activation and fibrogenesis. These findings not only deepen our understanding of the molecular basis underlying the antifibrotic effects of NaB but also highlight the HDAC3/c‐Myc axis as a promising therapeutic target for liver fibrosis. Nevertheless, several questions remain unanswered. First, the sample size of the animal experiments was relatively small, which may limit the statistical power and generalizability of the results. Second, the specific c‐Myc acetylation sites regulated by HDAC3 have yet to be elucidated. Third, the efficacy of NaB should be validated in additional models of liver fibrosis, including BDL‐induced and MASLD‐associated fibrosis. Future studies with larger sample sizes, integrating transcriptomics, metabolomics, and single‐cell sequencing, will help elucidate the complex regulatory networks underlying NaB‐mediated anti‐fibrotic effects and provide a more robust theoretical basis for microbiota‐targeted therapeutic strategies in liver fibrosis.


## Conclusion
  In summary, these findings indicate that NaB attenuates liver fibrosis by inhibiting HDAC3/c‐Myc–mediated metabolic reprogramming, revealing a gut‐derived anti‐fibrotic mechanism and potential therapeutic targets.
