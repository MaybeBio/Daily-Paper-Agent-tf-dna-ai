# A spectroscopic and in silico study on ruthenium(ii) complexes bearing pyridine–carboxamide ligands interacting with DNA as potential therapeutics


## Abstract
  Two half-sandwich ruthenium(ii) complexes (C1 and C2) bearing N,N-chelating picolinamide ligands were synthesised and comprehensively characterised to elucidate the role of ligand electronics in governing stability and DNA interactions. The complexes were fully characterised by HR-ESI-MS, NMR spectroscopy, FT-IR spectroscopy, elemental analysis, and single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD), confirming a pseudo-octahedral piano-stool geometry with bidentate coordination through pyridyl and amide nitrogen donors. TD-DFT calculations reproduced the experimental UV-vis spectra and revealed dominant metal-to-ligand and mixed metal–metal–ligand charge-transfer transitions. Solution stability studies demonstrated marked substituent-dependent behaviour: the unsubstituted complex (C1) readily underwent chloride–water exchange to form aqua, C1-aqua, and hydroxo species under physiological conditions, whereas the aryl-fluorinated analogue exhibited enhanced resistance to ligand substitution across a wide pH range. ctDNA-binding investigations using ethidium bromide displacement assays revealed concentration-dependent fluorescence quenching for both complexes, with C1-aqua displaying higher quenching efficiency and apparent binding affinity, KSV 3.9 × 103 M−1 and 1.1 × 103 M−1, and log Ka of 4.17 and 2.38 for C1-aqua and C2, respectively. UV-CD, UV-LD, and thermal denaturation studies suggested that DNA binding involves a significant intercalative component, accompanied by helix stiffening and partial unwinding, although contributions from minor groove binding cannot be excluded based on the Hoechst-33258 displacement studies. Molecular docking and 100 ns molecular dynamics simulations further supported favourable interactions with AT-rich regions of DNA and indicated dynamically stable binding modes associated with local DNA distortion. Overall, the findings suggest that subtle ligand electronic modulation through aryl fluorination influences the stability, solution speciation, and DNA-binding behaviour of Ru(ii) half-sandwich complexes. These observations provide useful structure–activity insights for the continued development of ruthenium-based metallodrugs.


## Introduction
  The development of metal-based therapeutics has been a key area of research in medicinal chemistry, owing to the distinctive chemical and biological properties imparted by metal centres.1,2 Metal complexes exhibit unique biological properties due to their ability to interact with biomolecules such as DNA, and several proteins including enzymes through different modes of action, including ligand exchange mechanisms, redox activity and structural adaptability.3,4 In particular, their ability to bind DNA via intercalation, groove binding or electrostatic interactions underpin many of their therapeutic applications.5 Importantly, subtle modifications to ligand frameworks can significantly influence the electronic properties, steric environment, lipophilicity and binding affinities of metal complexes, thereby modulating their biological activity and selectivity.

  Several metal-based drugs have received regulatory approval and are currently used in clinical practice, highlighting the therapeutic relevance of metallodrugs. These include bismuth-containing compounds such as bibrocathol,6 gold-based drugs such as auranofin,7 palladium-based padeliporfin,8 and platinum-based agents (cisplatin, carboplatin and oxaliplatin).9 Despite their success, current therapies suffer from significant limitations, including the development of drug resistance, severe systemic toxicity and poor target selectivity.10 These challenges have driven sustained efforts to identify alternative metal-based systems with improved efficacy, bioavailability, stability and reduced adverse effects compared to conventional treatments.11–13

  Among the ligand classes explored for biomedical applications, picolinamide derivatives have attracted considerable attention due to their versatile coordination behaviour and tunable structural features.14 Picolinamide ligands are typically synthesized via amidation reactions involving picolinoyl chloride or picolinic acid and appropriate amines.15 Depending on their substitution patterns and coordination environment, these ligands can bind metal centres through neutral or monoanionic (N,N) or (N,O) donor sets. Metal complexes incorporating picolinamide ligands have demonstrated a broad range of biological activities including anti-inflammatory,16 antibacterial,17 antifungal,18 anticancer,19 and DNA-binding activities.20

  A wide variety of metal complexes bearing picolinamide-derived ligands have been reported, encompassing Rh(i/iii),21 Ir(iii),22 Ru(ii/iii),22,23 Os(ii),24 Au(iii),25 Pt(ii/iv),26,27 Pd(ii),28 and Co(ii)29 complexes. However, despite this structural diversity, comparatively few studies have focused on elucidating the molecular binding mechanisms of these complexes with biological targets, particularly in relation to their pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic behaviour. In this context, Ru(ii) complexes have emerged as promising metallodrug candidates, expanding their therapeutic potentials.

  Notably, Ru(ii) half-sandwich complexes bearing N-phenyl picolinamide have shown promising cytotoxicity towards colon, ovarian, and cisplatin-resistant ovarian human cancer cell lines, comparable to the clinical drugs carboplatin and cisplatin.30 Similarly, Ru(ii) half-sandwich complexes featuring N-phenyl picolinamide have been found to possess more cytotoxicity than cisplatin against HT-29 cells.31

  Building on these advances, the structural versatility of picolinamide ligands offers a valuable platform for the rational design of next-generation ruthenium-based therapeutics. In the present study, we report the synthesis and comprehensive characterization of two half-sandwich Ru(ii) complexes (C1 and C2) bearing picolinamide ligands. Furthermore, systematic investigations of DNA binding, protein interactions and solution stability were conducted to establish clear structure–activity relationships (SARs). These insights provide a deeper understanding of the factors governing biological interactions in Ru(ii)-picolinamide complexes and contribute to the informed design of metal-based therapeutics with enhanced activity and selectivity.


## Materials and methods

### Materials and chemicals
  Picolinic acid, aniline, 4-fluoroaniline, triphenyl phosphite, and ruthenium(iii) chloride hydrate were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used as received. The organic solvents such as ethanol (EtOH), methanol (MeOH), pyridine (C5H5N), triethylamine(Et3N), dimethylformamide (DMF), dichloromethane (CH2Cl2), acetone ((CH3)2CO), chloroform (CHCl3), toluene (C7H8), α-phellandrene (C10H16), diethyl ether ((CH3CH2)2O), acetonitrile (CH3CN) and thionyl chloride (SOCl2) were also purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and Promark Chemicals and were all used as received. The dichloro(p-cymene)ruthenium(ii) dimer and picolinoyl chloride were prepared by a modified procedure from the literature.34,35


### Complex characterization
  All reactions involving air and/or moisture-sensitive compounds were performed under purified dry nitrogen using normal Schlenk techniques. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were recorded on a Bruker Avance NMR spectrometer (1H NMR at 400 MHz, 13C{1H} NMR at 101 MHz) in DMSO-d6 solutions using TMS as an internal standard (δ = 0 ppm). All chemical shifts are reported in ppm using TMS as a reference. FT-IR spectra were recorded as solids on a Bruker Tensor 27 FT-IR spectrometer fitted with an Attenuated Total Reflectance (ATR) accessory. Melting points were determined using a Buchi melting point apparatus B-540. High-resolution electrospray ionisation mass spectrometry (HR-ESI-MS) was carried out on a Waters Synapt G2 electrospray ionisation mass spectrometer in negative- or positive-ion mode. Elemental analysis was performed using a Thermo Scientific Flash 2000 Series CHNS-O Analyser to determine the microanalytical data (C, H, and N) for the ligands and complexes.


### X-ray crystal structure determination
  Single-crystal X-ray diffraction data were collected on a Bruker APEX-II CCD diffractometer. The crystal was kept at 173.00 K during data collection. Using Olex2,32 the structure was solved with the SHELXT,33 structure solution program using intrinsic phasing and refined with the SHELXL, refinement package using least-squares minimization.34 All non-hydrogen atoms were refined anisotropically, while hydrogen atoms were placed in calculated positions using riding models. High-resolution molecular diagrams were generated with POV-Ray. The structures were deposited at the Cambridge Crystallographic Data Centre and allocated the number 2505681 for complex C2. The data were provided free of charge via the Access Structures service, a collaborative initiative between the Cambridge Crystallographic Data Centre and Fachinformationszentrum Karlsruhe.


### Cyclic voltammetry
  Cyclic voltammetry was performed at 298 K using a scan rate of 20 mV s−1. Solution concentrations of 0.5 mM in anhydrous acetonitrile were used, containing 0.1 M [tBuN][PF6] as the supporting electrolyte. Redox potentials were measured using a glassy carbon working electrode, a platinum wire counter-electrode, and an Ag/AgCl reference electrode.


### Stability assay
  To assess the stability of complexes C1 and C2 in an aqueous environment, both complexes were dissolved in KH2PO4 buffer (50 mM, pH 7.5) containing 5% (v/v) DMSO to a final concentration of 50 µM. The UV-vis spectrum was recorded over 300 min between 250 and 600 nm.


### Spectroscopic pKa determinations and solution species characterization
  pKa determination was performed by previously reported methods.35,36 In brief, a constant ionic strength AMT buffer stock solution containing 100 mM Na-acetate, 100 mM MES, and 200 mM TRIS was prepared, adjusted to pH 10 using KOH, and diluted to the required final volume. The initial titration solution consisted of 50 µM C1 and C2 in 5% (v/v) DMSO AMT buffer, made up to a total volume of 50.0 mL with ultrapure water. Titrations were performed at 37 °C in a thermostatted Metrohm titration vessel. The starting solution at pH 10 was fitted with a calibrated pH electrode maintained at 37 °C. During the experiment, the solution was continuously magnetically stirred and circulated through a 10 mm quartz UV-visible flow cuvette positioned in the spectrometer cell holder at 37 °C using a Watson–Marlow 120S peristaltic pump. An initial UV-visible spectrum was collected over the range 250–600 nm at pH 10. Small aliquots (<0.5 µL) of concentrated HCl (10.2 M) were then introduced gradually via a glass capillary to decrease the pH in discrete increments of approximately 0.2–0.5 units across the titration range. Following each addition, the solution was allowed to equilibrate until the pH stabilised, after which the corresponding UV-visible spectrum was recorded.


### DNA binding
  DNA binding was measured using extrinsic fluorescence with site specific probes as previously described.37 In brief a ctDNA stock was prepared by dissolving the sodium salt (Sigma-Aldrich) in KH2PO4 buffer (50 mM, pH 7.5). ctDNA concentration was determined spectrophotometrically (ε = 13 200 M−1 cm−1 at 260 nm). The two probes used were Hoechst-33258 (HS) and ethidium bromide (EtBr). ctDNA and each probe were used at (15 µM). The solution of HS was excited at 350 nm, and the spectral measurement was made between 360 and 650 nm, while EtBr was excited at 520 nm, and the spectral measurement was made between 540 and 750 nm. Thereafter, either C1 or C2 was titrated into the prepared solutions over the concentration range of 0–200 µM. Measurements were carried out in triplicate using 1.0 × 0.4 cm quartz cuvettes.


### Correction of fluorescence data
  Inner filter effect (IFE) correction was applied to all fluorescence data using eqn (1),381Fcorrected = Fobserved × 10Aex × dex/2−Aem × dem/2where Aex and Aem are the absorbance readings at the excitation and emission wavelengths, respectively, while d is the path length of the cuvette.


### DNA thermal denaturation studies
  Samples of native ctDNA, ctDNA·{EtBr}, ctDNA·{HS}, and ctDNA·{C1} were prepared in duplicate at 15 µM concentrations in 50 mM KH2PO4 buffer containing 5% (v/v) DMSO, pH 7.50. Measurements were carried out using quartz cuvettes with a 1.00 cm path length. Absorbance spectra at 260 nm were recorded on a PerkinElmer UV-vis Lambda 365 spectrometer equipped with a multicell unit and Peltier temperature controller. Spectra were collected at 10 °C intervals between 30 and 60 °C, 5 °C intervals between 60 and 70 °C, and 2 °C intervals between 70 and 90 °C. Data were corrected, normalized, and analysed with UV Express software (Version 4.1.2).39 The melting temperature (Tm), defined as the point at which 50% of the DNA was denatured, was determined from the resulting sigmoidal curves.


### In silico methods

#### Density functional theory simulations
  Time-dependent DFT (TD-DFT) calculations were performed at the CAM-B3LYP/DEF2-QZVP/GD3BJ level of theory to simulate the UV-vis electronic spectra of C1 and C2. A DMSO solvent continuum (Self-Consistent Reaction Field)40 was applied, and only 60 excited singlet states were computed to give good overlap with experimental spectra.


#### Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations
  All in silico DNA methods, i.e., molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations, were performed using previously reported methods.41,42 Full details of the methods are reported in the ESI.


## Results and discussion

### Synthesis and characterization of ligands and complexes
  The carboxamide ligands (L1 and L2) were synthesized following a modified reported procedure,43,44 by refluxing either picolinoyl chloride or picolinic acid with the corresponding amines (Scheme 1). Both desired complexes were isolated as brown solids with moderate yields between 65% and 76%, respectively. Ligand metalation was achieved by reacting the picolinamide ligands with sodium hydrogen carbonate, serving as a general base, in ethanol. Thereafter, RuCl2(p-cymene) was added and the reaction mixture heated under reflux for 24 hours (Scheme 1). During metalation, the amide NH groups are deprotonated, thereby forming the desired neutral complexes, in which the ligand is monoanionic.

  The Ru(ii) analogues have been fully characterized using various spectroscopic and analytical techniques, including high-resolution electrospray ionisation mass spectrometry (HR-ESI-MS), elemental analysis (CHNS), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR), and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy. The 1H NMR spectra for C1 and C2 are mainly presented and assigned to highlight the salient spectroscopic features of this class of compounds. Fig. S4 highlights the 1H NMR spectrum of C2, which reflects the coordination of the pseudo-octahedral Ru(ii) metal ion by the bidentate bis(picolinamide) chelate. The pyridyl aromatic proton signals resonate in the region between δH = 7.18 ppm and 8.74 ppm (DMSO-d6), downfield shifted from that of the Ru(ii) congener, which confirmed successful coordination of the ligand through the pyridyl nitrogen as well as the proximal amide nitrogen to Ru(ii) metal ion.

  In the 1H NMR spectra of C1 and C2, characteristic features highlighted the disappearance of the amide NH proton signals at δH = 10.63 and 10.75 ppm, respectively in DMSO-d6 from the ligands (L1 and L2) upon complexation (Fig. S1–S4). The aromatic protons signals were identified in the region between δH = 7.11 and 9.37 ppm for complexes C1 and C2 (Fig. S3 and S4). The 1H NMR spectra for this class of C1 and C2 exhibits another noteworthy feature: distinct splitting patterns as four separate doublets corresponding to the resonances of the aromatic protons of coordinated p-cymene in the expected region, δH = 4.88–5.72 ppm due to the loss of symmetry, upon complexation. This phenomenon has been reported for similar Ru(ii) p-cymene complexes.45 All other aliphatic proton peaks of the p-cymene moiety were accounted for in their expected regions.

  The 13C{1H} NMR spectra for C1 and C2 are presented and assigned in Fig. S7 and S8. Close inspection of the signals for amide carbonyl carbons reveals downfield shifts from the free ligands L1 and L2, δ = 162.92–162.97 ppm to the C1 and C2, δ = 166.76–166.91 ppm (Fig. S7 and S8). Both C1 and C2 showed the aliphatic carbons resonances between δ = 18.77 and 30.75 ppm, commensurate with the structures.

  The HR-ESI mass spectra of C1 and C2 recorded in the positive ion mode showed prominent isotope clusters corresponding to the [M + H]+ fragment ions at m/z = 469.0623 (C1) and m/z = 487.0529 (C2) (Fig. S11 and S12). Both C1 and C2 were further characterized by elemental analysis, which confirmed the complexes' bulk purity.

  The FT-IR spectra showed the disappearance of the amide NH stretch (3337.22–3341.34 cm−1) upon complexation with the Ru(ii) ion, consistent with metalation as depicted in Fig. S13 and S14.48 Additionally, the ν(C–N) bands for L1 and L2 observed in the region 1668.71–1674.78 cm−1, shifted towards lower wavenumbers (1615.11–1615.17 cm−1) upon complexation. The shift to lower frequencies was attributed to metal-to-ligand back-bonding interactions.


### Single-crystal X-ray crystallography
  Suitable orange single crystals of C2 were obtained by solvent evaporation from an acetonitrile/methanol mixture. Complex C2 crystallized in the monoclinic crystal system with the P21/C space group. Crystallographic data and structure refinement parameters for complex C2 are given in Tables 1 and S1. The complex exhibited a pseudo-octahedral molecular geometry. A summary of the bond angles and bond lengths for the complex C2 is summarized in Tables S2–S4.

  The Ru(ii) metal ion adopts the expected three-legged piano stool arrangement in which the Ru(ii) metal has a pseudo-octahedral molecular geometry within the bidentate structure (Fig. 1). In the asymmetric unit, Ru(ii) is coordinated to two nitrogen atoms from the ligand, the p-cymene ring at the tip of the stool and a chloride atom at the bottom of the stool. Further inspection of the crystal packing of C2 indicated that one asymmetric unit forms an inverted face-to-face stacked interaction with another asymmetric unit displaying interdigital channels, viewed along the a-axis (Fig. S18).


### Electronic spectroscopy
  The experimental UV-vis spectrum of C2 is presented in Fig. 2a and was assigned by analysis of the TD-DFT-calculated spectrum of the complex. The spectra for C1 are reported in the ESI (Fig. S15). The DFT-calculated electronic spectrum of C2, excluding vibronic transitions, showed a reasonably good correlation with the experimental spectrum after applying a 56 nm red-shift correction to the band energies and appropriate scaling of the intensities (ε-values). In the visible region for C2, the band at 402 nm (1st excited state) is assigned to the HOMO → LUMO transition. Analysis of the molecular orbitals (MOs) involved in the transition to the first excited state indicates it comprises 82% 1[Ru(4dxz),π → Ru(4dyz),π*] character. The unoccupied molecular orbital is thus a metal ligand wavefunction with predominantly MLCT character (Ru → L). Additional MLCT states occur at higher energies, corresponding to transitions in the 300–600 nm range.

  The visible region band at 325 nm is similar with 78% 1[Ru(4dxz),π → Ru(4dyz),π*] character. The far-UV experimental bands are most intense between 250 and 300 nm (ε > 1600 M−1 cm−1). The experimental peak at 284 nm aligns almost perfectly with the 286 nm band of the TD-DFT spectrum. This transition is predominantly metal-to-metal–ligand charge transfer (MMLCT) in character, i.e., 41% 1[Ru(4dxy) → Ru(4dyz),π*]. This transition is mainly composed of H-8 → LUMO and H-7 → LUMO. Significantly, both transitions are mixed 1MLCT/1LC (ligand-centred) transitions, which are in good agreement with the experimental UV-vis spectrum's charge-transfer character because they originate from an MO.


### Speciation of the Ru(ii) complexes
  Metal-based complexes are often susceptible to instability in solution, particularly under prolonged exposure to light and to aqueous or ionic environments. To assess the solution stability of C1 and C2, time-dependent UV-visible spectroscopy was performed. Spectral changes were monitored to distinguish between chemical transformation and environmental effects. The absorption maxima of C1 (Fig. 3a) exhibit negligible changes over time, and there are no isosbestic points in the measured spectrum. This indicates that C1 does not undergo any chemical reactions over the time interval investigated, with instrumental drift and slight solution evaporation accounting for the subtle absorbance changes.

  Alternatively, for the fluorine-substituted C2 (Fig. 3b), there are visible changes in the UV-vis region between 450 and550 nm. Specifically, the principal MLCT band at around 520 nm indicates a pronounced monotonic decrease in band intensity as a function of time and a pronounced hypochromic shift from approximately 520 nm to 500 nm, without accompanying isosbestic points or band broadening. This behaviour is consistent with changes in the local dielectric and ionic environment surrounding C2, which can stabilise the ruthenium-based dπ donor orbitals and thereby increase the HOMO–LUMO energy gap associated with the MLCT transition. Although the aryl-fluorinated substituent is not expected to engage in strong, direct coordination with phosphate (from the buffer), its high electronegativity and polarised C–F bond likely modulate the solvation shell through weak, non-covalent interactions and local dipolar effects. These subtle fluorine-mediated interactions may perturb the electronic distribution across the conjugated ligand framework, slightly reducing effective metal–ligand orbital overlap and contributing to the observed hypochromic effect. Collectively, the spectral changes are most consistent with outer-sphere solvation and ion-pairing effects rather than ligand substitution at the metal centre.

  Following satisfactory results indicating that both C1 and C2 were not prone to demetallation, we conducted a pH speciation study ranging from pH 2–10 (Fig. 3c and d). At first glance, both C1 and C2 appear to have two pKa values; however, this was not the case. 1H NMR spectra were acquired for both C1 and C2 in a d6-DMSO–D2O solvent mixture (Fig. S16 and S17). The 1H NMR spectrum of complex C1 after ligand exchange showed an upfield shift of about 0.6–0.7 ppm compared to the 1H NMR spectrum of complex C1 before ligand exchange, indicating exchange of Cl− with H2O (Fig. S16 and Scheme 2). This was accompanied by a shift of the distinct p-cymene signals from δ = 4.80–5.56 ppm to δ = 4.88–5.63 ppm. In contrast, the 1H NMR spectra of C2 before and after ligand exchange revealed no chemical shift changes, indicating no displacement (Fig. S17). The change in absorbance of C2 in Fig. 3d between pH 2 and 8 may be attributed to dilution of the complex as well as partial precipitation.

  The unsubstituted analogue C1 was more susceptible to ligand exchange as compared to the aryl-fluorinated ruthenium complex (C2). This behaviour can be attributed to the strong electron-withdrawing inductive effect of the fluorine substituent, which reduces the basicity of coordinated donor atoms and stabilises the metal–ligand framework against proton-induced perturbation. As a result, protonation and hydrolysis processes that are spectroscopically observable in the non-fluorinated complex are effectively suppressed in the fluorinated system. The enhanced stability of C2 is therefore consistent with fluorine-mediated electronic tuning of the ligand environment rather than fundamentally different coordination modes. However, this indicated that under the conditions tested, C1 will be a cationic species, while C2 remains as a neutral metal complex (Scheme 2).


### Cyclic voltammetry
  Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was used to evaluate the redox behaviour of C1 and C2 as depicted in Fig. 4. The electrochemical analysis revealed that both Ru(ii) half-sandwich complexes exhibited a quasi-reversible redox couple attributed to RuII/RuIII (Table S5). C2, with an electron-withdrawing F-substituent, exhibited a higher E1/2 value, +1.08 V, as compared to the unsubstituted C1, +0.89 V, which further indicated that C2 is more resistant to oxidation, which corroborates our observation that C2 exhibits greater resistance to ligand substitution.


### DNA binding studies
  A series of DNA-binding experiments (Fig. 5) using extrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy was employed using an ethidium bromide (EtBr) displacement assay. In this experiment, EtBr is the fluorophore. The ctDNA·{EtBr} adduct and ctDNA·{EtBr} adduct in the presence of C1-aqua and C2 were excited (λEx520nm), and fluorescence spectral acquisition emission spectra were recorded over the range of 540–750 nm (Fig. 5a and b). Progressive titration of both Ru(ii) complexes into the ctDNA·{EtBr} solution resulted in concentration-dependent quenching of the fluorescence emission intensity, with an emission maximum observed at ∼599 nm (Fig. 5a and b). The fluorescence intensity decreased monotonically as the concentrations of C1-aqua and C2 increased, indicating competitive displacement of EtBr from the DNA-binding sites.

  Unsurprisingly, C1-aqua was much better at displacing EtBr than C2, since the aqua species is cationic and has a higher affinity for the negatively charged backbone of ctDNA. These displacement data suggest that both Ru(ii) complexes bind in the same region as EtBr and displaces it from ctDNA.46

  Fluorescence quenching is normally described by the Stern–Volmer (SV) equation (eqn (2)),472I0/I = 1 + KSV[Q] = 1 + kqτ0[Q]where I0 is the fluorescence intensity of ctDNA·{EtBr} adduct, and I is the fluorescence intensity of ctDNA·{EtBr} adduct in the presence of C1-aqua and C2. KSV is the Stern–Volmer constant (M−1), [Q] is the molar concentration of the quencher, kq is the bimolecular quenching rate constant (M−1 s−1), and τ0 is the average lifetime of ctDNA·{EtBr} adduct fluorescence in the absence of any quencher (i.e., the Ru(ii) complexes in this case), and is 23 ns.48 The KSV values were delineated by using eqn (2) and was calculated from the slope of the line obtained by plotting I0/I values against [Ru(ii) complex] values (Fig. 5c). The calculated KSV values were extrapolated and reported in Table 2.

  Using the fluorescence lifetime τ0 of ctDNA·{EtBr}, the bimolecular quenching constant for C1-aqua and C2 were calculated to be 1.7 × 1011 M−1 s−1 and 0.48 × 1011 M−1 s−1, respectively. This Kq is around the threshold diffusion-limited rate (∼109–1010 M−1 s−1), indicating that collisional (dynamic) quenching is likely to be the primary pathway. IFE's were corrected as described in the methods and controls included the fluorescence emission of free EtBr and buffer resulted in negligible changes in fluorescence emission spectra.

  The measured KSV values for C1-aqua and C2 quenching of the ctDNA·{EtBr} adduct were 3.9 × 103 M−1 and 1.1 × 103 M−1, respectively. This is significantly lower than values reported in some recent studies with metal complexes. Al-Rashdi et al. (2023) found KSV values in the range of ∼104–105 M−1 when their PtII compounds displaced EtBr from DNA. This interaction was reported to be a static quenching effect, consistent with ground-state complex formation.49 Similarly, Mvelase et al. (2024) report KSV values of ∼105 M−1 for Pd based complexes, with associated KSV in the picosecond regime,50 and Valentová et al. (2024) showed dynamic quenching of ctDNA·{EtBr} by CuII ligands, with KSV values in the order of 103–104 M−1 and very high bimolecular rate constants (Kq ∼1011 M−1 s−1), indicating a strong and fast interaction.51 This study closely resembles our observation for our pair of Ru(ii) complexes. In summary, the low KSV and Kq values for the C1 and C2 systems strongly point to the displacement of EtBr from DNA; however, C2 was much less efficient at doing so.

  EtBr has an affinity constant of around 3.1 × 105 M,52 which is at least an order of magnitude greater than C1-aqua, and two orders greater than C2. These values are apparent values extrapolated from an extrinsic fluorescence quenching mechanism.

  The binding constant of complexes to DNA are usually calculated by the molecule's direct interaction with DNA. In this case we used extrinsic fluorescence, therefore, we can only report the Kapp binding. This was calculated by the quenching of ctDNA·{EtBr} adducts fluorescence intensity as a function of [Ru(ii) complexes] to calculate and report the binding equilibrium between the C1-aqua and C2 complex and ctDNA.

  The biophysical parameters that describe the ligand's affinity for the ctDNA (Ka) are obtained from a double log plot of the fluorescence intensity data at increasing complex concentrations (eqn (3)),533where the intercept and slope of the curve provide the binding constant and stoichiometry, respectively. Plots of the data for C1-aqua and C2 are shown in Fig. 5d and was extrapolated to be log Ka 4.17 ± 0.06 and 2.38 ± 0.03, respectively. A caveat of note is that the log Ka is considered to be the apparent log Ka. The exact binding affinity of C1-aqua to ctDNA could not be calculated using UV-vis spectroscopy because the complex undergoes ligand exchange (vide supra) and the electronic spectra were inconsistent.

  The binding constants of C1-aqua and C2 were compared with those of reported Ru(ii) half-sandwich complexes known to interact with DNA.54 It is important to note that the η6-p-cymene ligand itself is generally not responsible for DNA intercalation; instead, intercalative interactions typically arise from coordinated ligands possessing extended aromatic systems. The Kb value for C1-aqua was determined to be 1.50 × 104 M−1, whereas C2 exhibited a lower affinity (0.24 × 103 M−1). These results suggest that the interaction of C1-aqua and C2 with ctDNA may depend not only on the interaction of the pyridine–carboxamide ligand with the nucleobases but also on additional electrostatic interactions, particularly in the case of the aqua species. Comparable binding constants have been reported for [RuCl(η6-p-cymene)(diamine)]PF6 complexes following chloride/aqua ligand exchange, which displayed Kb values in the range of 4 × 103 − 1 × 104 M−1.55 In another study, a series of [(η6-arene)Ru(L)Cl] complexes were shown through viscosity experiments to interact with ctDNA via partial intercalation, exhibiting Kb values of approximately 5 × 104 M−1.56 Overall, the apparent binding constant of C2 is approximately at least an order of magnitude lower than those reported for several Ru(ii) p-cymene complexes. This reduced affinity may be attributed to weaker π–π interactions between the nucleobases and the carboxamide ligand, as well as the absence of additional electrostatic stabilisation that may be present in the aqua species. A caveat is that although Ru(ii) arene complexes have been extensively investigated as DNA-interacting agents, systematic studies examining the effect of fluorine substitution on ligand electronic properties and resulting biomolecular interactions remain relatively limited.

  A second site displacement assay was carried out with the ctDNA·{HS} adduct and the adduct in the presence of C1-aqua and C2. The adduct was excited (λEx350nm), and fluorescence spectral acquisition emission spectra were recorded over the range of 360–650 nm (Fig. S17). There was monotonic quenching of the ctDNA·{HS} adduct in the presence of both complexes. The fluorescence emissions of both EtBr and HS were quenched in the presence of the complexes, a result typically indicative of probe displacement. However, fluorescence quenching of these probes does not necessarily always indicate direct displacement, as metal complexes can reduce emission intensity through photoelectron transfer or DNA conformational changes. Consequently, both probes may exhibit decreased fluorescence even when the ligand predominantly binds through a single mode, such as intercalation.57 The site displacement experiments were not conclusive in predicting the precise ligand binding site of the Ru(ii) complexes; therefore, thermal denaturation and UV-LD experiments were conducted (Fig. 6 and 7).


### Thermal denaturation of ctDNA
  The ctDNA melting temperature (Tm) of 74.14 °C obtained from Fig. 6 agrees with values observed in literature.58 Typically, the binding of compounds to DNA results in an increase in the Tm. In particular, DNA intercalators stabilize the double helix by their π–π stacking interactions, which require more energy to break, thereby increasing the Tm.59,60 The binding of an intercalator may result in a small unwinding of the DNA helix to accommodate the compound, and this may result in DNA elongation, as observed in Fig. 7, indicated by the UV-LD spectra. Furthermore, Fig. 6 indicated that C1-aqua increased the Tm of ctDNA·{C1-aqua} adduct by 4.26 °C. This was consistent with the known intercalator, EtBr (Table 3). The known-groove binder, HS, showed minimal change in EtBr Tm (Table 3). Overall, this suggests that C1-aqua may be an intercalator of ctDNA.


### UV-CD and LD spectroscopy of ruthenium(ii) complexes binding to DNA
  The conformational changes of ctDNA in the presence of C1-aqua were analysed using UV-CD spectroscopy (Fig. 7a). B-form DNA in the absence of complex displays the typical four principal transition bands at 277, 244, 220, and 210 nm, respectively. The bands are due to ctDNA's double-helical B-conformation.61,62 The maxima transitions at 277 and 220 nm are associated with stacking of the nucleic acid base pairs, while, the minima transitions at 244 and 210 nm are correlated to the helicity of DNA.63,64 When C1-aqua was added to ctDNA, the UV-CD intensities of the 277 and 220 nm bands had minimal changes as a result of ligand binding, while the 244 nm minima band amplitude was reduced as a function of C1-aqua concentration increasing. Furthermore, the minimal peak perturbations suggested that ctDNA had not transitioned conformationally from B-form to A-form DNA. Furthermore, this was corroborated by the characteristic 244 nm (B-form) maxima diminishing in amplitude and not red-shifting to the characteristic 263 nm peak present in A-form DNA.65 There was no presence of an induced CD signal, and this may be attributed to a low molar absorptivity. The B-form DNA has been reported in our previous study.66

  UV-LD was used to delineate the binding mode of C1-aqua to ctDNA.67 UV-LD was conducted in KH2PO4 buffer (5% (v/v) DMSO, 50 mM, pH 7.5), with 100 µM ctDNA and 50 or 100 µM of C1-aqua. ctDNA's LD spectrum exhibited a strong negative minimum at 260 nm, which was attributed to the nucleotides (pyrimidine and purine base pairs)67 of ctDNA. Upon the addition of C1-aqua, there was a concentration-dependent increase in the 260 nm minima (Fig. 7b), which suggests enhanced DNA stiffening and indicates an elongation and/or increased rigidity of the double-helical DNA. UV-LD data indicates that ctDNA is intercalated by C1-aqua (Fig. 7b).68–70

  Following the site-displacement, thermal denaturation and UV-CD/LD experiments, it appears that C1-aqua primarily intercalates with ctDNA, but initial minor-groove binding cannot be ruled out. There have been reports of complexes that exhibit a dual-mode binding to DNA, initially through the minor groove and subsequently by intercalation.71 One such Ru(ii) example is Ru(ii) polypyridyl complexes that possess a flexible pyrene moiety known for its intercalative properties. Through MD simulations, it is shown that the Ru(ii) polypyridyl complexes initially bind to the major groove of DNA, which is an unstable confirmation (based on the RMSD). The Ru complexes then adopt a more stable intercalative conformation over the trajectory.72


### Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations
  Molecular docking studies were employed using Schrödinger's Glide XP to determine the binding mode of C1-aqua and C2 to an oligonucleotide of DNA. Two DNA X-ray structures were selected form the PDB and used: PDB: 4E1U (0.92 Å),73 and PDB: 425D (2.80 Å)74 for XP flexible ligand docking. A caveat about MD docking is that the major parameter obtained from ligand docking is the ΔGbind score, which typically does not correlate quantitatively with thermodynamic parameters (Ka or ΔG values).75,76 Therefore, only qualitative comparisons can be made from docking scores. In this study, the aim is to determine if XP docking scores corroborate our experimental findings.

  The key results are that both C1-aqua and C2 preferably bind to the PDB: 4E1U oligonucleotide, which is biased towards intercalating interactions, as it exhibits a more favourable ΔGbind score compared to PDB: 425D (Table 4). The lowest best docked ΔGbind score for PDB: 4E1U was in fact higher than the highest best docked ΔGbind score from PDB: 425D. The result corroborates the experimental data (vide supra), which indicates that both C1-aqua and C2 are intercalators of DNA. Furthermore, the Glide XP docking data suggest that the Ru(ii) complexes bind and intercalate at the central 5′-AT-3′ step of the oligonucleotide, though with moderate affinities ΔGbind = −6.15 kcal mol−1 and −5.90 kcal mol−1, for C1-aqua and C2, respectively. As expected, the AT cluster sites are the preferred binding locations because they have been shown to exhibit the lowest binding energy.77

  Following ligand docking of C1-aqua and C2 to the DNA oligonucleotide (PDB: 4E1U), a 100 ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulation was conducted to assess the stability of the best Glide XP docking pose (Fig. 8). These simulations can provide valuable insights into the interactions between the DNA strand and the Ru(ii) complexes over time, capturing the system's dynamic behaviour and offering a more accurate reflection of physiological conditions.

  Fig. 8 presents the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of ctDNA and ligand interactions (C1-aqua and C2) over a 100 ns trajectory. The RMSD for C1-aqua remains relatively stable throughout the simulation trajectory (deviating between 0.15 0.45 Å). There are minor deviations throughout the trajectory that are attributed to C1-aqua orientating into the most stable confirmation Fig. 8a.

  At the start of the 100 ns simulation, C1-aqua is bound to the oligonucleotide at residues DT(8), DA(9) and DC(10). This region is primarily an AT junction, which is lower in energy than GC pockets and is, therefore, often considered a more favourable binding site for intercalating molecules. This preference can be visualised using molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, as MD force fields allow for more dynamic interactions.

  Similarly, RMSD for C2 also remains relatively stable throughout the simulation trajectory, deviating slightly more (i.e., between 0.15 and 0.75 Å). This is a result of C2 orientating into its most stable confirmation (Fig. 8b). At the start of the 100 ns simulation, C2 is bound to the oligonucleotide at residues DT(8), DA(9) and DA(6). This is primarily an AT junction that is lower in energy than GC pockets, hence are often considered more favourable binding sites for intercalating molecules to bind to.

  This insertion of both C1-aqua and C2 into the residues of the palindromic DNA strands nucleic base pairs resulted in the displacement of the stacked nucleic base pairs and conformational changes such as DNA uncoiling.78 At the end of the 100 ns trajectory, the DNA oligonucleotide undergoes significant unwinding, disrupting the electrostatic interaction between C1-aqua and C2 and the respective nucleic acids (Fig. 7). Therefore, we observe that C1-aqua and C2 are only bound to DA(4). These MD simulation results are consistent with the experimental UV-LD data in Fig. 7b, where the increase in the 260 nm minimum amplitude indicates ctDNA unwinding as the Ru(ii) complex concentration increases. A point to note is that MD simulations provide insight into the stability of the ligand–DNA complexes; the analysis in the present study relied mainly on RMSD evaluation of the trajectory (Fig. 8). The docking and molecular dynamics results should be interpreted with certain limitations in mind. Docking scores provide a relative ranking of binding poses but do not correspond directly to binding free energies. In addition, the molecular dynamics simulations in this study were primarily evaluated using RMSD analysis to assess the stability of the DNA ligand complexes over the simulation time. More detailed analyses, such as residue-level RMSF calculations, hydrogen-bond persistence analysis, and rigorous binding free-energy estimations (e.g., MM-PBSA/MM-GBSA), could provide a more comprehensive description of the interaction. These analyses were beyond the scope of the present computational workflow using the Maestro Suite (2022-4). Therefore, the molecular dynamics simulations presented here should be regarded as providing qualitative support for the experimental observations rather than a full thermodynamic characterization of the binding process. A more extensive molecular dynamics investigation, such as that reported by Malagarriga et al.,72 would be required to obtain these additional parameters.


## Conclusion
  In this study, two half-sandwich Ru(ii) picolinamide complexes, C1 and C2, were successfully synthesized, fully characterized, and evaluated for their stability and DNA-binding properties using complementary spectroscopic and in silico approaches. NMR and SCXRD analyses confirmed the expected N,N-chelation mode and piano-stool geometry. At the same time, TD-DFT simulations closely reproduced the experimental UV-vis spectra, providing molecular-level insight into the MLCT and MMLCT transitions that dominate their electronic behaviour. Biophysical assays revealed substituent-dependent stability differences: the fluorinated C2 displayed enhanced resistance to ligand exchange and remained intact across a larger pH range investigated, whereas the unsubstituted C1 readily formed aqua, C1-aqua, and hydroxo species under physiological conditions. These findings align with the strong inductive effect of the para-fluoro substituent on the ligand backbone, which electronically stabilises the Ru–ligand scaffold. DNA-binding studies demonstrated that both C1-aqua and C2 displace intercalated EtBr and groove bound HS, although C1-aqua exhibits stronger quenching efficiency and higher apparent affinity for ctDNA. UV-CD, UV-LD, together with thermal denaturation studies, suggested that C1-aqua interacts with DNA through a binding mode containing a significant intercalative component accompanied by helix stiffening and partial unwinding, although contributions from groove binding cannot be excluded. Molecular docking and 100 ns MD simulations further supported favourable interactions with AT-rich DNA regions and indicated dynamically stable binding modes associated with localised DNA distortion. Overall, these findings highlight the role of ligand electronics in influencing the solution stability and DNA-binding behaviour of Ru(ii) half-sandwich complexes. The contrasting properties observed for C1-aqua and C2 provide useful preliminary structure–activity insights that may contribute to the future design and optimisation of ruthenium-based metallodrugs.
