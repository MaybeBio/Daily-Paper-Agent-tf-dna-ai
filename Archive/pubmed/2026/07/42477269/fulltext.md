# Betulinic Acid Promotes Motor Function Recovery After Spinal Cord Injury by Inhibiting Apoptosis Through Myc/NF-κB Signaling Pathway


## Abstract
  Spinal cord injury (SCI) imposes a remarkable burden on affected cases and their families, while current treatment options remain insufficient. This study aimed to identify and validate the molecular targets, signaling pathways, and mechanisms by which Betulinic acid (BA) exerts its therapeutic effects on SCI, providing new directions for clinical intervention. In vivo, Kunming mice underwent behavioral tests, Nissl staining, and Hematoxylin-eosin staining to evaluate motor function recovery and determine the expression levels of relevant biomarkers. Bioinformatics analyses were employed to investigate the genes involved in the SCI-BA interaction, elucidating key signaling pathways, followed by molecular docking of potential target genes. In vitro, lipopolysaccharide was used to induce a secondary SCI condition in cultured astrocytes, enabling the evaluation of BA’s effects on inflammatory and apoptotic responses. The results demonstrated that BA could significantly promote motor function recovery and reduce the expression levels of inflammation-related cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, in SCI mice. Through bioinformatics analysis, Myc was identified as a key target of BA in SCI, and NF-κB and apoptosis pathways could be implicated in its mechanism of action. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments confirmed that BA could target Myc to modulate the inflammatory response in astrocytes, reducing the activation of RELA and MAPK14. In conclusion, these findings indicate that BA attenuates neuroinflammation and apoptosis via Myc and the NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby promoting functional recovery following SCI.


## Introduction
  Spinal cord injury (SCI) is defined as structural damage and functional impairment of the spinal cord caused by traumatic forces, pathological conditions, or other etiological factors. This injury typically results in motor dysfunction, sensory deficits, and autonomic nervous system disorders in body regions below the level of injury [1]. Epidemiological studies indicated that SCI is a devastating condition characterized by high rates of disability and mortality, posing serious threats to human health and exerting a significant negative impact on social and economic development [2]. Globally, more than 27 million people are estimated to live with SCI-induced long-term disability [3]. In developing countries, the most common causes of SCI include falls from height, followed by traffic accidents and blunt trauma [4]. In recent years, drug therapy combined with cell-based approaches has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for SCI [5]. However, the complex pathophysiological mechanisms underlying SCI have limited the effectiveness of these drugs in promoting functional recovery. Elucidating the specific molecular pathways through which pharmacological agents act on the injured spinal cord microenvironment, both intracellular and extracellular, and identifying efficient, low-cost anti-inflammatory drugs are critical for advancing therapeutic strategies and improving clinical outcomes.

  Previous studies have demonstrated that Betulinic acid (BA) as one of the main chemical components of the characteristic Chinese medicinal herb star anise from Guangxi, possesses broad anti-inflammatory, antiviral, and antioxidant properties [6, 7]. It has been shown to exert preventive and protective effects against brain injury [8] and to attenuate peripheral sensory neuropathy associated with nerve damage [9]. Studies have reported that BA can protect healthy tissues by activating the Nrf2/SOD pathway to enhance antioxidant defense, inhibiting the pro-inflammatory TNF-α/NF-κB signaling pathway, and stabilizing mitochondrial integrity [10]. Furthermore, BA can induce apoptosis in human prostate cancer cells by stabilizing p53 and downregulating the NF-κB pathway [11]. However, its role in regulating apoptosis following SCI remains poorly understood, and the underlying mechanisms have yet to be elucidated.

  Astrocytes play a crucial role in the functional recovery of the central nervous system after injury [12]. Through their activation, they participate in nearly all stress responses and wound-healing processes, including inflammation, ischemia, infection, and glial scar formation [13]. Following acute SCI, progressive neuronal death occurs not only at the primary lesion site, but also in adjacent regions, leading to the disruption of multicellular interactions [14]. This secondary injury stage involves complex and interrelated molecular signaling pathways, characterized by astrocyte activation, upregulation of proinflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and activation of apoptosis-related signaling pathways that drive programmed cell death [15].

  In glial cells following SCI, apoptosis represents a regulated, energy-dependent form of programmed cell death [16]. This process occurs through two principal pathways: an exogenous pathway, initiated by TNF-mediated activation of death receptors, and an endogenous pathway, primarily by mitochondrial regulation [17]. Pharmacologically, caspases and TNF-α, which are central mediators of apoptosis, are closely linked to inflammatory signaling cascades, often interacting with glutamate, mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), and other cross-linking mediators [18]. Inflammatory mediators may trigger both cell death and apoptosis processes [19]. Moreover, the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathway, which has been consistently associated with apoptosis since its discovery [20], may play a critical role in modulating the correlation between inflammation and apoptotic cascade. The extent of post-injury scar formation in the spinal cord is regulated by NF-κB, which influences macrophage migration through modulation of macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) [21]. As a master transcription factor, Myc plays a central role in regulating genes involved in the cell cycle, apoptosis, and metabolism [22]. Furthermore, Myc expression can sensitize cells to a wide array of death-inducing signals [23].

  Given the abovementioned findings, we examined the changes in Myc expression level and apoptotic cascade factors in astrocyte post-SCI and hypothesized that the series of reactions induced by the NF-κB signaling pathway could influence astrocyte recruitment, inflammatory responses, and apoptosis-related phenotypes, thereby contributing to the improvement of neurological function following SCI.


## Results

### BA Reduced Inflammation and Improved Motor and Pathological Outcomes After SCI
  To evaluate the effects of BA on motor function after SCI, a behavioral assessment was performed on mice after modeling. Basso Mouse Scale (BMS) scores indicated that both the BA and SCI groups exhibited partial recovery beginning on day 3 after injury; however, recovery in the BA group was significantly greater. Notably, significant differences were identified between the BA and SCI groups on days 7 and 14 post-injury (Fig. 1A). Notably, while the BMS protocol specifies assessment on days 1, 3, 5, 7, 10, 14, 18, 21, 28, 35, and 42 post-injury, this study concentrated on early recovery by examining days 1, 3, 7, and 14. This choice is supported by literature defining day 14 as the threshold between the acute and sequelae phases [24].

  Hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining revealed that the area of inflammation was significantly reduced in the BA group (Fig. 1B-C, R^2 = 0.9312). Nissl staining also revealed significant neuronal loss in the anterior horn of the spinal cord in the SCI group, whereas BA treatment preserved neuronal integrity. Normal Nissl bodies are characterized by a well-defined nuclear membrane and clearly visible nucleoli (Fig. 1E–F, n = 3). In addition, analysis of inflammatory cytokines (IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α(F = 325.2, F = 2565, F = 3805)) in spinal cord tissue collected 7 days after injury confirmed that BA significantly attenuated the post-SCI inflammatory response (Fig. 1D, R^2 = 0.9503).

### BA Alleviated Apoptosis by Myc/NF-κB Signaling Pathway in Mice with SCI
  To further reveal the interaction between BA and SCI, the research team utilized the TCMSP database and network pharmacology approaches to identify the key molecular targets of BA. SCI-related targets were retrieved from GeneCards, the Therapeutic Target Database (TTD), OMIM, and DrugBank. A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using Cytoscape software. Analysis of network topology revealed that the betweenness centrality (BC), closeness centrality (CC), degree centrality (DC), and network centrality (NC) values of Myc and Caspase-3 exceeded the median thresholds. As these parameters are positively correlated with node importance, Myc and Caspase-3 were identified as potential core targets (Fig. 2A). The KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment in the NF-κB signaling pathway (Fig. 2B). Molecular docking was thereafter performed using AutoDock Vina, and visualization with PyMOL demonstrated that BA bound strongly to Myc and Caspase-3, with docking scores of − 13.62 and − 10.59, respectively, indicating extremely high binding affinities (Fig. 2D). Therefore, in human PBMCs, it was consistently found that the expression levels of Myc (P = 0.0023, t = 6.875) and Caspase-3 (P = 0.0007, t = 9.347) were significantly higher in patients with SCI compared with those in healthy controls (Fig. 2C).

  Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis and immunofluorescence (IF) experiment of spinal cord tissues further confirmed these findings. β-actin served as the housekeeping gene for normalization for RT-qPCR. Compared with the SCI group, BA-treated Kunming (KM) mice exhibited reduced expression levels of Myc (p < 0.001, F = 106.1) and Caspase-3 (p < 0.001, F = 86.16), as well as downregulation of RELA (p = 0.0002, F = 45.39) and MAPK14 (p = 0.002, F = 19.98), as two key molecules in the NF-κB signaling pathway (Fig. 2E). The results depicted in Fig. 2F demonstrate a significant increase in P65-positive cells in the gray matter of the spinal cord in the SCI group compared with the sham group. Conversely, in the SCI + BA group, there was a decrease in P65-positive cells.

### BA Inhibited LPS-Mediated Apoptosis and Inflammation of Astrocytes
  In vivo experiments demonstrated the protective effect of BA on mice with SCI. To further confirm its modulatory effect on SCI, in vitro assays were carried out in astrocytes with or without lipopolysaccharide (LPS) stimulation. Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay initially demonstrated that BA at concentrations of 0–20 µM did not significantly affect astrocyte viability after 48–96 h of stimulation (Fig. 3A). Consequently, concentrations of 5 and 10 µM were selected, and samples were collected after 48 h of stimulation for further analysis. To investigate the effects of BA on LPS-induced inflammatory responses in astrocytes, the mRNA expression levels of IL-1β (p < 0.001, F = 185.3) and TNF-α (p < 0.001, F = 189.7) were measured by RT-qPCR, and the expression of p65 protein was assessed by immunofluorescence. As shown in Fig. 3B, LPS stimulation markedly increased IL-1β and TNF-α expression levels, whereas at 10 µM, BA treatment significantly downregulated both cytokines (p < 0.05), and at 5 µM, BA did not show a significant reduction. At the protein level, BA reduced p65 expression in LPS-stimulated astrocytes (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, BA treatment inhibited apoptosis-related molecules (Myc (p < 0.001, F = 34.16) and Caspase-3 (p < 0.001, F = 35.26)), as well as downstream NF-κB pathway–related molecules (RELA (p < 0.001, F = 3804) and MAPK14 (p < 0.001, F = 1623)), as illustrated in Fig. 3C (p < 0.05). Consistent with these findings, apoptosis detection assays revealed that BA significantly reduced astrocyte apoptosis compared with the LPS group at both concentrations tested, BA exhibited a potent inhibitory effect on the protein expression of the inflammatory cytokine TNF-α (p < 0.001, F = 41.41) in astrocytes, as measured by cell-based ELISA (Fig. 4A-B).

## Discussion
  Spinal cord is an important pathway for sensory and motor conduction between the brain and the body [25]. SCI typically results in sensory and motor dysfunction below the level of injury [26]. Programmed death of astrocytes directly compromises central nervous system integrity and may lead to functional impairment [27]. Secondary injury processes, such as reduced local blood flow and oxidative stress, further aggravate damage [28]. Moreover, persistent inflammatory responses and the formation of fibrotic scars hinder tissue repair and neurological recovery [29]. Both glial cells and inflammatory cells play remarkable roles in damage progression, remarkably influencing overall neuronal function and clinical outcomes of SCI [30]. As apoptosis is closely regulated by inflammation, its inhibition is considered as a promising strategy to promote functional recovery post-SCI [31].

  The main objective of this study was to analyze the major chemical constituents of the Guangxi characteristic Chinese herbal medicine Illicium verum (star anise) and to investigate the biological mechanisms of its active compounds in SCI treatment. Using the TCMSP database, five bioactive components were identified, including BA, quercetin, (+)-catechin, kaempferol, and luteolin. These compounds were predicted to act synergistically on multiple target proteins, forming a multi-component, multi-target, and multi-pathway therapeutic mechanism. Among them, BA exhibited the highest drug-likeness and oral bioavailability. Additionally, molecular docking analysis revealed strong binding affinity between BA and SCI-related target genes. Based on these findings, BA was selected as the primary compound of interest. Previous in vitro studies demonstrated that BA could inhibit macrophage-mediated inflammatory responses induced by LPS stimulation by reducing nitric oxide production [32]. Further, animal experiments confirmed that BA could promote motor function recovery in SCI mice [33]. By locally delivering the sustained-release PBA-bFGF complex, motor function recovery was enhanced, while neuroinflammation and neuronal apoptosis were mitigated following SCI [34]. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that BA may play a remarkable role in the therapeutic effects of star anise for SCI, warranting further mechanistic exploration.

  The SCI-associated genes were identified from the GeneCards, Therapeutic Target Database (TTD), Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM), and DrugBank databases. Bioinformatic analysis was thereafter carried out to examine the overlap between these disease-related targets and the predicted targets of the active compounds. The PPI network analysis revealed that Myc, Caspase-3, AKT1, F2, PLG, CXCL8, RB1, and ESR1 (particularly Myc and Caspase-3) may serve as core therapeutic targets. Myc is widely recognized as a regulator of apoptosis in cancer [35–37]. Elevated Myc expression level in neurons has been shown to induce neuronal death and contribute to neurodegenerative phenotypes [38]. Caspase-3, a key executioner caspase, mediates programmed cell death via both the extrinsic (death receptor–mediated) and intrinsic (mitochondria-dependent) pathways [39, 40]. Its robust activation plays a notable role in determining cell fate in neurophysiology and disease, influencing processes such as synaptic plasticity, dendritic pruning, learning and memory, and apoptosis [41]. Despite this knowledge, the anti-inflammatory roles of these targets in astrocytes and their potential neuroprotective effects in SCI remain insufficiently explored.

  To further investigate the potential molecular mechanisms of BA in SCI treatment, molecular docking analysis was conducted between BA and Myc as well as Caspase-3, based on the results of compound screening and PPI target network construction. The results of molecular docking demonstrated a strong binding stability, with binding energies of − 13.62 kcal/mol for BA–Myc and − 10.59 kcal/mol for BA–Caspase-3.

  Consistent with the results of molecular docking and compound screening, molecular biology experiments were conducted to validate these predictions. Previous studies have demonstrated that BA exerts anti-inflammatory effects by regulating inflammatory mediators and alleviates neurological ischemic injury through modulation of oxidative stress [42, 43]. Furthermore, BA reduces neuronal apoptosis by restoring autophagic flux through activation of the AMPK-HDAC5-KLF2 axis, promoting the transition of microglia from the M1 to the M2 phenotype [44]. Accordingly, it was hypothesized that BA could promote motor function recovery after SCI by reducing apoptosis and suppressing inflammatory responses. In a mouse model of SCI, BA administration significantly improved motor function recovery, attenuated astrocyte-mediated neuroinflammation, and inhibited neuronal apoptosis, thereby facilitating axonal regeneration and functional restoration.

  To further investigate the role of BA in SCI, in vivo and in vitro analyses were performed. After an initial screening to determine optimal dosing through the CCK-8 assay, BA was applied to astrocyte models under various stimulation conditions. The results revealed that 10µL BA effectively reduced the expression levels of LPS-induced astrocytic inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β and TNF-α), as well as p65 protein level, thereby mitigating the inflammatory response associated with SCI. Notably, a 5µL dose of BA did not suppress astrocyte-derived inflammatory factors; instead, it was associated with an increase in their expression, potentially due to glial reactivity or an inappropriate dosing level. Moreover, under LPS stimulation, BA markedly downregulated the expression levels of Myc, RELA, MAPK14, and Caspase-3. These findings demonstrated that Myc and Caspase-3 could mediate inflammation and apoptosis through the NF-κB signaling pathway, and BA could attenuate these processes, thereby promoting axonal regeneration and repair following SCI. Specifically, BA modulates microglia/macrophage polarization by selectively inhibiting IκBα, consequently regulating the NF-κB signaling pathway [45]. Consequently, these results indicate that agents with both anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic properties, such as BA, hold promise as therapeutic strategies for SCI.

  Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. While multi-pathway and multi-target analyses based on database-derived information provide valuable insights, the key therapeutic mechanisms must still be validated experimentally. In addition, the use of LPS-stimulated astrocytes as a model for SCI has inherent limitations and may not fully recapitulate the complex pathological environment of SCI. Moreover, compounds or targets that have not yet been documented or validated in current databases might not be included in the analysis, which could restrict the overall comprehensiveness of the results. Furthermore, studies investigating the therapeutic effects of BA in SCI remain limited, highlighting the need for more systematic experimental and clinical investigations. Advancing research on traditional Chinese herbal medicines holds important theoretical and practical value for both the preservation and modernization of traditional Chinese medicine (TCM).

  In summary, this study integrated bioinformatics analysis, molecular docking, and experimental validation to preliminarily demonstrate that BA could promote neural repair and functional recovery following SCI by regulating the NF-κB/MYC/Caspase-3 signaling pathway, thereby suppressing astrocyte-mediated inflammation and neuronal apoptosis. These findings provide a scientific basis for the potential clinical application of Illicium verum (star anise) and present a practical framework for modern research into multi-component, multi-target therapeutic strategies derived from TCM.


## Materials and Methods

### Reagents
  Astrocytes were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). Moreover, BA (CAS No. 472-15-1; Lot No. MUST-23041510; purity ≥ 98%) was purchased from Chengdu Must Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, China). Tribromoethanol (CAS No. 75–80-9; Lot No. C15543378; purity ≥ 98%) was obtained from MACKLIN Co., Ltd. Lipopolysaccharide (CAS No. 93572-42-0) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). The CCK-8 (Lot No. C0037) and TUNEL assay kit (Lot No. C1098) were provided by Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). PCR primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The RNA extraction kit (AP-MN-MS-RNA-50G) was supplied by CORNING Life Sciences Technology Co., Ltd. Heat-inactivated premium fetal bovine serum (Lot No. 10099141) and SYBR Green Master Mix (Lot No. A25741) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Rat anti-NF-κB p65 antibody (No. 8242 S) and anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) antibody (DyLight 800 4X PEG Conjugate; No. 5151 S) were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA).


### Animals
  Female KM mice (18–23 g, 8 weeks) were procured from the Experimental Animal Center of Guangxi Medical University. Mice were randomly housed in standard cages under controlled environmental conditions, with the ambient temperature maintained at 21 ± 2 °C and a 12-h light/dark cycle (lights on from 07:00 to 19:00). Food and water were provided ad libitum. All animal experiments were reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Experimental Animal Center Affiliated to Guangxi Medical University (Approval No. 202206006). Every effort was made to minimize animal suffering and reduce the number of animals used in the experiments.


### SCI Model
  Following anesthesia induced by intraperitoneal injection of tribromoethanol (20 mg/kg), laminectomy was performed to expose the T10 spinous process in mice that served as the sham control group. For the SCI group, the spinal cord at this site was exposed and subjected to injury using an Allen’s impactor (2 g, 2.5 cm), as previously described [46]. Successful modeling was confirmed by the presence of bilateral hind limb convulsions accompanied by tail flicking, as well as immediate congestion and edema at the injury site, ultimately resulting in bilateral hind limb paralysis. Postoperatively, mice received intraperitoneal injections of penicillin (10,000 IU) to prevent infection, and bladder massage was performed twice daily at scheduled intervals to facilitate urination until bladder function was restored.


### Motor Function Analysis
  On postoperative days 1, 3, 7, and 14, locomotion function was assessed using the BMS scoring method [47]. Mice were allowed to freely explore an open field for 5 min, during which the movements of the hind paws and ankles were carefully observed. If no movement was detected below the ankle joint, the assessment was progressively extended to higher joints. Evaluations were performed independently by three trained experimenters under double-blind conditions.


### Histological Analysis
  Seven days after SCI, excised tissue samples were promptly fixed in formalin or paraformaldehyde. Following fixation, samples were dehydrated, embedded into paraffin, and deparaffinized through a xylene and ethanol gradient. Staining was performed using toluidine blue at 56 °C for 1 h or with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) according to standard protocols. After embedding into neutral resin, sections were examined under an optical microscope.

  The number of viable neurons in the spinal cord was determined 7 days after SCI using a Nissl’s staining kit. In brief, spinal cord tissue was collected 7 days after injury, paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and dewaxed by a graded dehydration series. Sections were then incubated in Nissl staining solution for 3–10 min at 37–50 °C to ensure uniform staining. Following washing, the sections were dehydrated in 95% ethanol, mounted with neutral resin, and examined under a Leica optical microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).


### Construction of a PPI Network for the BA-SCI Interaction
  The intersecting genes between the drug target genes and SCI-related target genes, as described in Sect. 1.3, were imported into the STRING database (https://www.string-db.org/) [48], with the species restricted to Homo sapiens. The minimum interaction score was set to high confidence (0.70). A PPI network was thereafter constructed to elucidate relevant biological processes, molecular interactions, and signaling pathways. Topological analysis of the network was performed using Cytoscape 3.7.2 software. Nodes with BC, CC, DC, and NC values exceeding the median were defined as core gene targets, in which a higher value indicated a greater node importance.


### Functional Enrichment Analysis of Intersection Genes
  Metascape database [49] was used to carry out gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses. The species was limited to Homo sapiens, and the results were sorted and visualized according to the p-value. GO enrichment analysis displayed the top 10 results for biological process, CC, and molecular function, while KEGG pathway enrichment analysis presented the top 20 results. The results were visualized using bar graphs and bubble charts, with a pathway map provided for the relevant pathways meeting the significance threshold of this study.


### Molecular Docking Verification
  Core components extracted from the TCM-component-disease target network and the core targets obtained from the PPI network were validated through molecular docking. The two-dimensional (2D) structure diagrams of small molecules corresponding to the core components were downloaded from the PubChem database [50]. These were thereafter converted into three-dimensional (3D) PDB files using Open Babel software [51]. The corresponding 3D structures of the receptor proteins for the core targets were retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB) (http://www.rcsb.org/) [52]. After pretreatment, including dehydration and hydrogenation using AutoDockTools, and identifying the active pocket of the receptor protein, molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina 1.1.2. The binding energy was calculated, and the docking model was visualized with PyMOL.


### Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cell (PBMCs) Sample Acquisition
  This study randomly enrolled three inpatients diagnosed with SCI from the Department of Rehabilitation Medicine at the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University between January and December 2024 based on accurate and complete clinical records. Additionally, three healthy volunteers were recruited during the same period, and PBMC samples were collected from all participants for analysis. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee (Approval No. 2024-S536-01).


### Cell Culture of Astrocytes
  DI TNC1 rat astrocytes were cultured in a Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, and 100 U/mL of penicillin and streptomycin. The cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Cell growth was monitored by assessing the confluence, with 80% confluence used as an indicator for processing. The growth of the cells was assessed in real-time under an inverted microscope.


### CCK-8 Assay
  Astrocytes were cultured in vitro and seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5000 cells per well. Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were treated with different concentrations of BA over a time gradient. Subsequently, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 3 h, and the absorbance at 450 nm (OD value) was measured to assess cell vitality.


### RT-qPCR
  According to the results of the CCK-8 assay, LPS-activated astrocytes were treated with two concentrations of BA in 6-well plates. The LPS concentration used in this study was determined with reference to the existing literature. While a concentration of 50 pg/mL has been reported to significantly enhance the production of TNF-α, IL-6, the TNF-α/IL-10 ratio, PGE2, and NO in RAW 264.7 cells, a concentration of 100 ng/ml is also commonly applied to induce inflammatory responses, such as in microglial cells [53]. Given this precedent, 100 ng/ml LPS was selected to establish the inflammatory model in astrocytes. The expression levels of relevant genes in astrocytes from different treatment groups were analyzed by RT-qPCR. Total RNA was extracted using the AxyPrep Total RNA Preparation kit following the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA synthesis was performed using the SweScript All-in-One RT SuperMix for RT-qPCR kit. RT-qPCR was conducted via the Applied Biosystems™ PowerUp™ SYBR™ Green Master Mix kit on an ABI StepOnePlus™ Rapid Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Gene expression was normalized to β-actin expression level, and relative expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. ### IF Staining
  Astrocytes from each group were cultured and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. After fixation, cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at room temperature for 5 min and then incubated with 3% bovine serum albumin for 30 min to block nonspecific binding. The samples were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody, followed by a 2-h incubation with the appropriate fluorescent secondary antibody at room temperature. After three washes with PBS, cell nuclei were stained with DAPI. The stained cells were observed using an automated cell imaging system (EVOS® FL Auto; Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA).

  Following dewaxing and rehydration, paraffin-embedded sections were subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval using an autoclave and antigen retrieval buffer. After retrieval, excess liquid was gently removed, and the tissue areas were delineated with an immunohistochemical pen. Bovine serum albumin (BSA) blocking solution was applied and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. Subsequently, a p65 primary antibody working solution, diluted at 1:500, was added, and the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C in the dark. The next day, the sections were incubated with the corresponding secondary antibody, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Finally, the sections were observed, and images were captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope.


### TUNEL Assay
  Treated astrocytes were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and then permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in 0.1% sodium citrate PBS buffer. The apoptosis detection was carried out using a commercial TUNEL assay kit, where cells were incubated with the dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) reaction mixture at 37 °C for 1 h. After the incubation, DAB staining was performed to visualize apoptotic cells. The assay was conducted in triplicate, and six non-overlapping regions in each 96-well plate were randomly selected for TUNEL-positive cell counting and statistical analysis.


### Statistical Analysis
  All data were statistically analyzed by GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) and compared between groups by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
