# Incorporating AI-optimized zinc finger proteins enhances the efficiencies and targeting ranges of miniature base editors


## Abstract
  The therapeutic application of base editors is limited by their large sizes, which are beyond the packaging capabilities of adeno-associated viral (AAV) vectors. Despite recent progress that has identified many compact CRISPR proteins, the resulting miniature base editors often exhibit reduced activities and limited targeting scope. Here, we introduce a zinc finger protein (ZFP)-enhanced miniature base editor (zmBE), which integrates programmable ZFPs to improve efficiencies and targeting scopes of miniature base editors, including those based on Un1Cas12f1 and OgeuIscB. Utilizing protein language models to optimize ZFPs designed by modular assembly further simplifies the development of zmBEs. Leveraging these methodologies, we engineer a zmBE that effectively induces the SMN2 exon 7 T:A(6) > C:G conversion, restores the exon 7 inclusion, and improves spinal muscular atrophy in a murine model after being delivered via a single AAV vector. Our study provides a versatile platform for developing miniature base editors for in vivo therapeutic applications.


## Introduction
  CRISPR-derived base editors are powerful tools for precise genome editing1–8, and have been widely utilized to treat genetic diseases by reversing pathogenic mutations9,10, disrupting gene expression11–16, or modulating RNA splicing17–20. Most widely used base editors consist of an spCas9 nickase protein and a deaminase (e.g., cytidine deaminase2–5 or adenine deaminases6) and/or a glycosylase21–24, enabling programmable nucleotide substitutions without eliciting double-stranded DNA breaks. However, these base editors are often too bulky to be packaged efficiently into a single adeno-associated virus (AAVs) particle with the sgRNA cassette, which has a size limitation of 4.7 kb packaging capacity25. Consequently, split-intein-based protein splicing strategies have been developed, which, however, require simultaneous delivery of two AAV vectors and thus limit their wide therapeutic applications in vivo.

  Recently, miniature CRISPR proteins, including Cas12f26–36, TnpB37–40, and IscB41–44 (Supplementary Data 1), have been identified with reduced sizes while maintaining high editing efficiencies. Although several miniature CRISPR-derived base editors have been reported (e.g., Cas12f-derived ABE&CBE45–48, IscB-derived ABE&CBE42–44,49–51), they still face several challenges before being widely adopted for therapeutic use. Compared to spCas9-derived base editors, miniature versions often have lower editing efficiencies. For instance, Un1Cas12f1-derived base editor dCasMINI (D143R/T147R/K330R/E528R)-ABE has an efficiency of less than 10% in most genomic sites examined45. Furthermore, more stringent protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) or target-adjacent motif (TAM) requirements also limit the targeting ranges of miniature base editors and potentially exclude certain genetic loci from editing. Lastly, off-target editing remains a concern for miniature base editors.

  Programmable DNA-binding domains (pDBDs), such as Cys2-His2 zinc finger proteins (ZFPs)52 or transcription activator-like effectors (TALEs)53 can be engineered to recognize nearly any sequence in the genome54. These proteins are modular such that their affinity and specificities can be rationally tuned by adjusting the number and composition of the incorporated modules. For instance, ZFPs offer precise DNA recognition, with each 28-residue ZFP repeat recognizing three nucleotides. This capability has facilitated the design of gene editing tools55, including base editors. Early zinc finger-based base editors (e.g., zinc finger fused with AID), however, induced low editing efficiencies56 mainly because zinc fingers lack helicase activities to actively unwind dsDNA to ssDNA substrates required by most cytidine deaminases57,58. A notable exception is DddA, which acts directly on dsDNA, and this property enabled the development of compact mitochondrial and nuclear base editors built on zinc finger-DddA fusions57–60.

  In this study, we demonstrate that the efficiency and targeting range of miniature base editors can be substantially enhanced by incorporating a programmable DNA-binding domain (e.g., ZFP), which is designated as ZFP-enhanced miniature base editor (zmBE). By optimizing orientation, distances, and composition of ZFPs, we show that zmBE enhances the efficiencies of Un1Cas12f1-derived ABEs by 1.3 to 128-fold, while simultaneously relaxing the PAM requirement from TTTR to NTTR and TTTN. This strategy also greatly improves efficiencies and expands targeting scopes of OgeuIscB-derived ABEs and CBEs. The use of protein language models (PLMs) further enables us to engineer highly efficient zmBEs without exhaustive experimental optimization. Building upon these findings, we engineer a zmBE that effectively induces T:A(6) to C:G conversion at exon 7 of the SMN2 gene, restores SMN2 exon 7 inclusion, and alleviates spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) in a murine model after being delivered via a single AAV vector. Our study thus establishes zmBE as an easy-to-use platform for engineering miniature base editors, paving the road for therapeutic base editing in vivo.


## Results

### Fusion of zinc finger proteins enhances dCas12f-ABE activities
  We reasoned that attenuated DNA-binding affinity of miniature CRISPR proteins may decrease their capacities to form a stable R-loop, leading to suboptimal activities when being converted into base editors. Previously, it has been demonstrated that the fusion of a programmable DNA-binding domain, such as TALE or ZFP, can stabilize R-loop formation and enhance the precision of an affinity-attenuated spCas9 nuclease61. We thus examined whether fusing a programmable DNA-binding domain to a miniature base editor (e.g., dCas12f-ABE45) could improve its editing efficiency, precision and expand its targeting range.

  We first fused either a ZFP59,61–65 or TALE66 to a previously reported Un1Cas12f1 variant, namely CasMINI (D143R/T147R/K330R/E528R)45 (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1a). Similar to previous reports45, the Cas12f-derived ABE showed relatively low editing efficiencies at endogenous genomic loci, with ~3% efficiencies observed at A3 of the PLXNB2-site 3 (Fig. 1b). Incorporating a ZFP that recognizes a 9-bp sequence downstream of the protospacer of PLXNB2-site 3 to C-terminus of dCas12f-ABE resulted in an over fifteen-fold increase in the editing efficiencies (A3>G, from 2.74% to 43.43%) (Fig. 1a, b). Likewise, at the TRAC-site 1, a ZFP that recognizes a 15-bp sequence upstream of the protospacer resulted in an over 98-fold increase in editing efficiencies after being fused with dCas12f-ABE (Fig. 1c). Similar to ZFP, fusing TALE to C-terminus of dCas12f-ABE also enhanced the editing efficiencies, although the magnitude was less than achieved using ZFPs (Supplementary Fig. 1a-c). Because the dCas12f-ABE-ZFP fusion proteins are more compact, we focused on employing ZFPs as the programmable DNA-binding domain in subsequent studies and designated it as ZFP-enhanced miniature base editor (zmBE). ### Optimization of designing properties of the zmBE framework
  We next optimized the designing strategies for zmBE. We found that TadA-TadA8e dimer and ZFP located at the N-terminus and C-terminus of the dCas12f protein respectively (Design 1) (Fig. 1a), had the best performance, which on average improved the editing efficiencies over 31 fold at eighteen endogenous loci in 293XT cells (Fig. 1b-d) and over 6 fold at fourteen endogenous loci in Neuro-2a (N2a) cells (Supplementary Fig. 1d, e). We also tested whether ZFP fusion could enhance editing efficiencies of dCas12f-ABE with TadA8e monomer. The results showed that incorporation of ZFP domains led to consistent increases in editing efficiencies for dCas12f-ABE containing TadA8e monomer; however, their efficiencies were still lower than those observed with the ZFP-fused dCas12f-ABE containing TadA-TadA8e dimer (Supplementary Fig. 1f, g). Consequently, the TadA-TadA8e dimer configuration was chosen for all subsequent experiments. In addition, incorporation of a ZFP didn’t increase indel rate nor affect the editing window of dCas12f-ABE (Supplementary Fig. 1h and Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). Despite the elevated activities, zmBEs had lower activities than their nCas9-based counterpart, likely because Cas12f cannot be easily engineered into a nickase45, precluding the nick-assisted strand bias that substantially enhances editing efficiency in nCas9-based base editors (Supplementary Fig. 3a-d).

  Next, we examined how the orientation of the ZFP-binding sequence relative to the protospacer affected editing efficiencies. We tested multiple configurations: ZFP-recognizing sequences at the 3’ end on the same strand as the protospacer (3’ & Watson), at the 5’ end on the same strand (5’ & Watson), at the 3’ end on the opposite strand (3’ & Crick), and at the 5’ end on the opposite strand (5’ & Crick) (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Using a plasmid reporter assay, we systematically altered the orientations between a fixed ZFP-recognizing sequence and the protospacer. All configurations improved editing efficiencies, though the 5’ & Crick configuration showed slightly lower efficiencies for some protospacers (Supplementary Fig. 4b).

  We also explored the impact of the distance between the protospacer and ZFP-binding sites on dCas12f-ABE-ZFP activities. Distances ranging from 1 bp to 25 bp similarly promoted efficiencies, while 0 bp and over 30 bp showed less magnitude of the enhancement (Supplementary Fig. 4c, d). Additionally, we demonstrated that a minimum of three zinc fingers, recognizing 9 bps, were required for optimal enhancement (Supplementary Fig. 4e, f). These findings indicate that ZFP fusion is flexible in terms of location and distance of ZFP-binding sequences from the protospacer, increasing the likelihood of achieving high-efficiency miniature base editors for any given locus.

  Additionally, the fusion of ZFP with dCas12f-ABE rendered endogenous loci containing non-cognate PAM accessible for editing. For instance, a ZFP fusion amplified the editing efficiencies of Pcsk9-site 15 loci containing an ATTA PAM by over 146-fold (from 0.12% to 18.12%) (Fig. 2d). Similar results were obtained for two other endogenous loci (Fig. 2e). ### Impact of ZFP fusion on off-target editing specificity
  Furthermore, the fusion of ZFP to dCas12f-ABE reduced its off-target editing on RNA. SpCas9-derived ABE is known to induce off-target editing on RNA69,70, which was also observed with dCas12f-ABE (Fig. 3a, b). In contrast, the fusion of a ZFP to dCas12f-ABE reduced the detectable A to I conversion on RNA by 66.2% (Fig. 3a, b). We also assessed the DNA off-target editing of the Cas12f ZFP fusion. In most tested loci, fusing ZFP to dCas12f-ABE greatly enhanced the on-target editing yet had minimal effects at predicted off-target sites (Fig. 3c–h). Even in a few sites where dCas12f-ABE ZFP fusion increased off-target editing (e.g. Off-target site 5 of the DMD-site 6 locus), the ratios of off-target to on-target editing were greatly reduced following the fusion of a ZFP (Fig. 3i). We also assessed off-target editing at off-target loci with non-cognate PAMs. For TRAC-site 2, zmBE induced low-level editing at two NTTR off-target sites (0.77% and 1.26%), comparable to dCas12f-ABE alone (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b), while no significant off-target editing was detected at NTTR or TTTN sites associated with PLXDC2-site 8 (Supplementary Fig. 6c, d).   Next, we assessed off-target editing near ZFP binding sites. While a few adenines exhibited statistically significant differences compared to mock controls, the absolute A > G conversion frequencies induced by zmBE remained below 0.17%. Because this level approaches the technical limits of HTS, it may not represent biologically meaningful ZFP-dependent off-target editing (Supplementary Fig. 7a). This suggests that ZFP binding alone is insufficient for zmBE to drive base editing without sgRNA-directed dCas12f recruitment, likely because TadA-derived base editors require ssDNA substrates7 that are only generated through R-loop formation by dCas12f and sgRNA. We then examined whether proximal ZFP binding could potentiate editing at sgRNA-dependent off-target sites. Most validated off-target loci lacked ZFP binding sites; however, a predicted71,72 ZFP binding site was identified near TRAC-site 2-OFF 7 (Supplementary Fig. 7b). The original ZFP (ZFP1, binding score: 4.25) only modestly increased off-target editing at this site (from 1.49% to 2.30%), whereas a second ZFP (ZFP2, binding score: 17.60) with higher predicted affinity substantially elevated off-target editing to 6.10%, albeit with reduced on-target efficiency (29.2% vs. 38.8%) (Supplementary Fig. 7b–e). These findings indicate that proximal ZFP binding can amplify sgRNA-dependent off-target editing in rare cases, particularly when ZFP binding affinity at the off-target site is high. In contrast, even moderate ZFP binding affinity can substantially enhance on-target editing efficiency, underscoring the critical importance of optimizing ZFP specificity in fusion designs.

  Previous reports have indicated that integrating a non-specific dsDNA binding domain, such as Sso7d, a 64-amino-acid protein from Sulfolobus solfataricus73, can enhance the activities of genome editors47,50,74. As reported47, fusing Sso7d or Sac7e to the C-terminus of dCas12f-ABE enhanced its activities at the TRAC-site 2 locus (A4>G from 5.58% to 20.95% and 18.64%, respectively) (Supplementary Fig. 8a, b). However, the enhancement was less significant than that achieved with ZFP at the same loci (Supplementary Fig. 8a, b). Conversely, fusing a non-specific ssDNA binding domain (e.g., RPA70-C)75 failed to improve the editing efficiencies of dCas12f-ABEs (Supplementary Fig. 8c–e).

  Notably, Sso7d or Sac7e also increased off-target activities when fused to dCas12f-ABE, resulting in similar off-target/on-target editing ratios. For example, fusing Sso7d increased on-target editing activities from 5.58% to 20.95% at the TRAC-site 2, but it also elevated off-target editing at one of the TRAC-site 2 off-target sites (off-target site #4, from 8.27% to 26.38%) (Supplementary Fig. 9a). In comparison, fusing ZFP slightly reduced off-target editing from 8.27% to 7.44%, while amplifying on-target editing by 5.3-fold (from 5.58% to 29.55%) (Supplementary Fig. 9a). Similar results were observed for another five endogenous loci (Supplementary Fig. 9b-f). Consequently, off-target/on-target editing ratios were greatly reduced for dCas12f-ABE after being fused with ZFP than being fused with Sso7d or Sac7e (Supplementary Fig. 9g). On the other hand, fusing ZFP had no drastic effect on the sgRNA-independent off-target editing in DNA within R-loops76–78 determined with orthogonal CRISPR assays (Supplementary Fig. 10).

  In contrast to miniature base editors, ZFP fusion had variable effects on the nCas9-derived base editor. Across five independent target sites tested with spCas9 nickase ABE, three showed no appreciable change upon ZFP fusion, one site exhibited a reduction in editing efficiency, and only one site (SMN2-site 12) showed a modest increase (Supplementary Fig. 13). These results suggest that ZFP fusion does not confer a consistent benefit to standard nCas9-based ABEs that already possess strong target DNA binding.

  Taken together, these results suggest that ZFP fusion could serve as a universal strategy for enhancing the efficiencies of miniature CRISPR-derived base editors. Unlike base editors, however, fusing a ZFP to miniature CRISPR nucleases such as Un1Cas12f1 (D143R/T147R/E151A, RRA)45, yielded inconsistent results. While ZFP fusion enhanced the nuclease activities of Cas12f at four of seven tested loci (TRAC-site 1, PLXNB2, DMD-site 4, and DMD-site 5), as determined by indels generated, it showed the same (TRAC-site 2 and CCR5-site 7 loci) or reduced (PLXDC2-site 8) activities at other loci (Supplementary Fig. 14). This suggests that the attenuated DNA binding affinity only partially contributes to the suboptimal cleavage activities of miniature CRISPR nucleases.


### Protein language model facilitates the design of ZFP and zmBE
  To obtain a high-affinity ZFP, the conventional approach typically involves the construction of many tandem arrays via modular assembly and subsequent large-scale in vitro validation of their DNA-binding affinities and specificities52,80. Such an approach, however, has a high failure rate and may yield less potent DNA-binding ZFPs81–83. The process of engineering zmBE may thus be time-consuming and costly by identifying high-performing zinc finger arrays.

  Protein language models (PLMs) are primarily categorized into masked language models and autoregressive language models84,85. Masked language models predict the masked amino acid in a protein sequence based on its surrounding residues84, while autoregressive models generate protein sequences by predicting the next amino acid using previously observed residues85. PLMs have recently been utilized to predict the direction of natural evolution86, facilitate the design of proteins87, improve affinities of human antibodies88, and develop base editors21. Thus, we examined whether PLMs could also be utilized to improve in vitro assembled ZFPs to be subsequently incorporated into a miniature base editor-ZFP fusion.

  We chose an in vitro assembled four-finger ZFP61,89 targeting DMD-site 5, which had a suboptimal activity after being fused with dCas12f-ABE (~6% A>G, Fig. 5a). We then chose two PLMs that, based on masked language models, independently identify function-enhancing variants of this ZFP, SaProt90 and ESM221,91–95. ### Engineering a zmBE for correcting SMN2 mutation and alleviating spinal muscular dystrophy in vivo
  The compact size, expanded targeting scope, and increased efficacy of zmBEs enable their delivery with a single AAV vector in vivo, along with an sgRNA to treat genetic diseases. This approach may provide therapeutic benefits while simultaneously reducing costs and minimizing potential immune responses against AAVs in therapeutic applications. Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) is a fatal neuromuscular disorder caused by mutations in the survival motor neuron 1 (SMN1) gene. A nearly identical gene, SMN2, cannot compensate for the loss of SMN1 due to a single nucleotide difference (T:A(6)>C:G) in exon 7 that leads to its skipping in most transcripts and thus, a truncated and less functional SMN protein. Although spCas9-derived ABEs have been employed to convert the SMN2 gene into SMN1 by inducing T:A(6)>C:G mutation, thus restoring the expression and functionality of the SMN protein, this approach required administration of two adeno-associated viruses (AAVs)96,97, and showed modest phenotypical improvement of the SMA mice96,97.

  We thus harnessed the above design principles to develop a zmABE that could be packaged into a single AAV particle with sgRNA. We designed an sgRNA targeting T:A(6), yet the sgRNA together with dCas12f-ABE only achieved ~4.5% A to G conversion at the targeted nucleotide. We then designed two ZFPs targeting a 9-bp sequence upstream of the protospacer via modular assembly. Fusing these ZFPs to dCas12f-ABE drastically enhanced the editing efficiencies from ~4.5% to ~40% (Fig. 6a, b). The only bystander editing was observed at A14 in the intronic region (~1.6% A>G) (Fig. 6c). We also evaluated the feasibility of using nIscB-ABE-ZFP; however, IscB targeting at this site resulted in substantial bystander editing within the narrow therapeutic window required for precise SMN2 correction (Supplementary Fig. 12b, Supplementary Fig. 15a, b). Considering the two dCas12f-based zmBEs showed similar activities (Fig. 6b), zmBE 1 was chosen for subsequent experiments. RT-PCR showed zmBE 1 induced 93.9% inclusion of SMN2 exon 7, while dCas12f-ABE showed a similar inclusion level compared to mock (Fig. 6d, e). We then assessed the DNA off-target editing of dCas12f-ABE and zmBE 1 at sites with cognate or non-cognate PAMs. The highest off-target editing induced by zmBE 1 was 5.39% (Supplementary Fig. 16a-c), yet located at a non-coding region (Supplementary Data 2). In addition, no significant indels induced by zmBE 1 were observed at the SMN2 locus (Supplementary Fig. 16d). To further improve the efficiency of the zmBE targeting SMN2, we first assessed if adding finger number to ZFP could further enhance T:A(6)>C:G efficiency. We thus designed six four-finger ZFPs targeting SMN2, and fused them to dCas12f-ABE. However, none of them showed editing efficiencies exceeding zmBE 1, indicating efficiencies of zmBE may not be enhanced by simply increasing finger number (Supplementary Fig. 17a, b). We next utilized the PLMs to optimize the ZFP protein of zmBE 1. Among 25 variants we tested, 12 improved the editing efficiencies, and the A48P variant showed a 1.08x increase in the T:A(6)>C:G conversions (Fig. 6f). We further optimized the sgRNA scaffold30,46, and found that the ge4.1 scaffold without an extra G appended at the 5′ of the sgRNA had increased efficiencies than the version with the extra G (39.41% vs 27.57%).   We then packaged the optimized zmABE together with the sgRNA into a single AAV vector98 using the neutron-tropic PHP.eB capsid99 (Fig. 6h), which was then used to treat the SMA mice (Smn−/−; SMN2 Tg; SMN2Δ7 Tg)100. The SMA mice are transgenic mice with the full-length human SMN2 gene and SMN2Δ7 cDNA, and they display SMA symptoms when the murine Smn gene is homozygously knockout100. Both female and male mice were included in this study. It has been reported that sex has no impact on SMA phenotype97.

  We then delivered the zmABE into the SMA mice through cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and intravenous (IV) injections to ensure SMN protein restoration in both the central and peripheral nervous systems96 (Fig. 6i). Righting reflex refers to spinal cord reflexes potentiated from the cerebral cortex that enable the body to regain an upright posture when displaced by tilting. Compared to the mock SMA mice, the righting reflex test indicated enhanced motor function in AAV-zmABE treated SMA mice on around 10 days post-treatment (Fig. 6j). Furthermore, on average, although the body weight of SMA mice began to decline around 12 days after birth, AAV-zmABE treated SMA mice were able to maintain their body weight for at least 7 additional days (Supplementary Fig. 18a, b). Consequently, the zmABE treatment enhanced the lifespan of SMA mice from a median of 21 days (maximum: 25 days) to a median of 28 days (maximum: 65 days) (Fig. 6k). To determine the in vivo base editing efficacies, brain and spinal cord of mice receiving AAV-zmABE (spiked with AAV-eGFP) treatment were collected, and GFP-positive nuclei were isolated. Through high-throughput sequencing analysis, we found T:A(6)>C:G conversions of the human SMN2 transgene in AAV-zmABE-treated SMA mice reached ~34% in the brain (median 12.45%) and ~43% in the spinal cord (median 14.64%) (Fig. 6l, Supplementary Fig. 18c).

  To provide an assessment of genome-wide off-target effects, we conducted whole-genome sequencing (WGS) on spinal cord GFP-positive nuclei isolated from AAV-zmABE-treated SMA mice across two independent experiments. This analysis identified a total of 78 SNVs consistently observed in treated samples compared to controls (Supplementary Fig. 19a, b). Notably, among these 78 SNVs, only one was located within an exonic region, and it was a synonymous variant with no predicted functional impact (Supplementary Fig. 19c, d). These results suggest minimal off-target DNA editing under therapeutic conditions, though we acknowledge that WGS may not capture all low-frequency events or indels. These data together demonstrate the feasibility of using zmBE for in vivo therapies of genetic diseases that are unamendable to current miniature base editors.


## Discussion
  Miniature base editors, derived from miniature CRISPR nucleases (e.g., Cas12f, TnpB, and IscB), are significantly smaller than their counterparts based on spCas9, thus facilitating efficient packaging and in vivo delivery via AAV vectors. However, their reduced size presents challenges such as decreased activities and limited targeting scopes. In this study, we have developed zmBE that incorporates programmable DNA-binding domains (e.g., ZFPs) to enhance the effectiveness and targeting scope of miniature base editors, including Cas12f-derived ABEs/CBEs and IscB-derived ABEs/CBEs. Utilizing a PLM to optimize ZFPs designed via a modular assembly strategy further simplified the engineering of efficient zmBEs. By combining these strategies, we designed a zmBE that efficiently corrected the SMN2 C:G(6)>T:A mutation, restored SMN2 exon 7 inclusion in transcripts, and alleviated phenotypes in a murine model of SMA following a single AAV-mediated delivery.

  zmBE has important advantages over other previously described systems that improve miniature base editors. Extensive protein engineering and substantial optimization are often necessary in enhancing and identifying miniature CRISPR enzymes37,42–45,48,49. Incorporation of non-sequence-specific DNA-binding domains increases the on-target editing at the cost of increasing off-target editing47,50,74. In contrast, zmBE, as an orthogonal strategy, was able to enhance the efficacy and broaden the application range of these nascent base editors. Moreover, zmBE should be compatible with current and future discovered miniature base editors. The combination of these strategies holds promise for advancing the development of AAV-delivered therapeutic base editing applications in a clinical setting.

  ZFPs have been used as part of an expanding toolbox available for genomic editing, including base editors58–60, recombinases101, and epigenetic editors102,103. Yet, designing ZFPs with high specificity and efficiency can be challenging for a given target. The modular assembly approach has a moderate success rate and often yields less potent ZFPs104. The further optimization of modular assembled ZFPs can be time-consuming and costly. Our results indicate that combination with a general PLM and experimental validation strategies will greatly facilitate designing high-affinity ZFPs. With the advance of other AI-based tools for ZFP design105, this process is predicted to be accelerated and more efficient. Interestingly, while our PLM predictions included mutations across both the variable recognition helix and constant backbone regions of the ZFPs, the experimentally validated variants conferring the greatest functional enhancements were predominantly localized to the recognition helix. This pattern underscores the importance of the helix in mediating sequence-specific interactions with DNA bases, where substitutions may more profoundly influence binding affinity and overall editing outcomes compared to backbone alterations. These findings suggest that future PLM-guided optimizations of ZFPs may benefit from prioritizing or weighting predictions in DNA-contacting domains, potentially streamlining variant selection and enhancing the efficiency of protein engineering pipelines for genome editing applications.

  The enhanced activity and broader targeting range of ZFP fusion with miniature base editors may reflect the bypass of a kinetic barrier to R-loop formation that follows PAM recognition106, which is required for the deamination reaction. pDBD fusion may increase the effective concentration of miniature CRISPR protein around the target site and thus stabilizing their interaction with PAM. Unlike spCas9/sgRNAs, which often have sufficient time to facilitate efficient progression to R-loop formation, miniature base editors may have decreased likelihood and require additional ZFP tethering to overcome this barrier, including non-cognate PAMs.

  Notably, the in vivo therapeutic benefit of zmBE for SMA was moderate. This may be partially due to the severity and early onset of neuronal damage in these mice, leading to lethality before the base editor and restored SMN protein could function effectively. Future studies could be warranted to optimize the treatment strategies, potentially in conjunction with other exon 7 splicing restoration strategies (e.g., antisense oligonucleotides)97. Compared to the two previously published studies employing intein-split dual-editor systems96,97, our miniature single-vector base editor offers several important advantages. First, the one-vector architecture eliminates the need for co-transduction of two separate AAVs, a process that is inherently stochastic and can substantially limit effective editing at the single-cell level25,107–109. This also removes the requirement to optimize the dosing ratio between two vectors. Second, the single-vector system reduces manufacturing complexity and cost, which is a critical consideration for large-scale production and clinical translation25. From a safety perspective, delivering a single AAV vector avoids potential issues related to uneven biodistribution and prolonged expression of split components that must reconstitute in vivo107,108.

### Base editing and indel analysis
  DNA of interest was amplified by site-specific primers containing common adaptor sequences (5’-ggagtgagtacggtgtgc-3’ and 5’-gagttggatgctggatgg-3’ added at the 5’ end) using 2×Hieff Canace® Plus PCR Master Mix (YEASEN, China). ### Plasmid reporter assay

### Lentiviral production and transduction
  1 × 106 per well 293XT cells were seeded in 6-well plates one day before transfection using DMEM without P/S. 2 μg plasmid containing the desired sequence was transfected into each well of 293XT cells with 1.5 μg pSPAX2 and 0.5 μg VSV-G using 10 μL Polyethylenimine Linear (YEASEN, China). Genomic DNA was extracted, and editing efficiencies were analyzed as described.


### RNA off-target analysis using RNA-seq
  293XT cells were transfected with dCasMINI-ABE and zmBE, respectively, together with corresponding sgRNA as described before. Cells were harvested five days after transfection. RNA was extracted with the HP Total RNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, USA) and analyzed with NGS (Novogene, China). Briefly, poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads were used to purify mRNA from total RNA. Fragmented mRNA was obtained by divalent cations. After cDNA synthesis, 3’ ends of DNA fragments were adenylated and ligated to an adaptor with a hairpin loop structure. Fragments were purified by AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, USA) and amplified by PCR. Then the library was purified by AMPure XP beads again, and the quality was evaluated by Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Index-coded samples were clustered on a cBot Cluster Generation System with TruSeq PE Cluster Kit v3-cBot-HS (Illumina, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Finally, the prepared library was sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq platform, and 150 bp paired-end reads were obtained.

The sites with coverage higher than 25, a mutation count of at least 6, and mutation ratios over 5%119 were collected as filtered sites.

### PLM-guided design of ZFPs for zmBE
  Briefly, two modular assembly methods could be used to obtain initial ZFPs for further optimization. One was using online tools, including ZFP-Finder and Zinc Finger Tools61,89. Selected zinc finger modules were then assembled into the backbone provided by Zinc Finger Tools.

  To optimize assembled ZFPs using protein language models, the amino acid sequences of original ZFPs were input into SaProt or ESM2 for predicting fitness-enhancing variants. The top 20 variants predicted by ESM2 and the top 10 variants predicted by SaProt for each original ZFP were selected for downstream analysis and experimental validation with three exceptions. These ZFP variants were classified based on their mutations’ location within the ZFP, including the N-terminus, C-terminus, and each finger module of the input ZFP. Then the top validated variants at different parts of the ZFP were chosen for combination to further improve editing efficiencies. Meanwhile, different ZFP scaffolds58 could also be tested to achieve further improvement. Detailed information on top variants predicted by ESM2 and SaProt, as well as experimental outcomes, was listed in Supplementary Data 11.


### Splicing quantification
  293XT cells were transfected with dCas12f-ABE or zmBE 1 and an sgRNA targeting SMN2. Five days after the transfection, total RNA was extracted from cell pellets by HP Total RNA Kit. Reverse transcription was performed by PrimeScript™ FAST RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa, Japan). Then cDNA was amplified by Gloria Nova HS 2X Master Mix (ABclonal, China) with a pair of primers targeting Exon 6 and Exon 8 of SMN1/2, respectively. Amplicons were purified by AFTSpin Multifunction DNA Purification Kit (ABclonal, China) and were digested by DdeI (NEB, USA) for 1 h at 37 °C to distinguish SMN2 from SMN1124. The products were then analyzed by ImageJ after agarose gel electrophoresis.


### Animals
  Animal experiments were performed following the approved institutional ethical guidelines. SMNΔ7 mice (FVB.Cg-Grm7Tg(SMN2)89Ahmb Smn1tm1Msd Tg(SMN2*delta7)4299Ahmb/J) were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (#005025)100 and were housed in specific pathogen-free conditions with a 12 h/12 h light/dark cycle, 20–26 °C ambient temperature, and 40–70% humidity. Ample access to food and water was provided. Mouse were weighed daily or every other day and were monitored daily for morbidity and mortality.


### AAV package and injections
  ZmABE and SMN2 sgRNA contained PHP.eB AAV particles (1 × 1013 vg/mL; sequence was listed in Supplementary Data 12) were produced by PackGene Biotech (China). PHP.eB-eGFP-NLS and PHP.eB-eGFP-H2B were purchased from PackGene Biotech (China) and Brain Case (China), respectively. For cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) injections, for each dose, 5 µL PHP.eB-zmABE was mixed 10:1 (v:v) with PHP.eB-eGFP-NLS or PHP.eB-eGFP-H2B together with a small amount of Fast Green (Yeasen, China). Before injections, pups were put on ice bags for 2–3 min for anaesthetization. CSF injections were performed as previously described125. Briefly, the syringe needle was bent at an angle of 90–110°, and the bent needle tip was ~2–3 mm. Then the bent needle was connected to one end of a PTFE capillary tube, and the other end of the capillary tube was connected to a 10 µL pipette tip and a pipette. After aspirating the desired volume of AAV into the capillary tube by pipette, the bent needle was vertically inserted into the skin at the back of the foramen magnum of the pup. When the CSF flowed into the capillary tube, AAV was injected slowly into the CSF circulation by pipette. Each SMA pup received two doses of CSF injection when ≤ P3. For each intravenous (IV) injection, 25 µL PHP.eB-zmABE was mixed 10:1 (v:v) with PHP.eB-eGFP-NLS or PHP.eB-eGFP-H2B. After ice anaesthetization, pups received an IV injection as previously described126. Briefly, pups were positioned on a surgical board, and the temporal vein was visually identified; then the viral solution was administered by a 31 g needle inserted into the vein. IV injections were given once per pup on P1 or P2. In this experiment, 15 AAV-zmABE-treated mice and 15 mock controls were included, both female and male. Previous studies had reported that sex has no impact on SMA phenotype97.


### Nuclei isolation and sorting
  Mice subject in nuclei isolation and sorting were treated with PHP.eB-zmABE together with PHP.eB-eGFP-H2B as described above. In order to measure in vivo editing efficiencies accurately, these mice were also injected with Risdiplam daily through intraperitoneal injection, and were sacrificed four weeks after the AAV treatment. Nuclei isolation from the brain and the spinal cord was performed as previously described127,128. Briefly, brain and spinal cord were separated from mice and were chopped into small pieces with sterile scissors and tweezers in nuclei isolation buffer (250 mM sucrose, 25 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 10 mM Tris-HCl, 100 mM DTT, 0.1% Triton X-100, protease inhibitor, RNase inhibitor, and Hoechest)128. The mixture was then transferred into a glass Dounce homogenizer, and the tissues were dissociated by around 15 strokes. The contents were transferred into a 1.5 mL tube, followed by centrifuging at 100 × g for 7 min at 4 °C. Pellets were then washed, and were resuspended in nuclei storage buffer (167 mM sucrose, 5 mM MgCl2, 10 mM Tris-HCl, 100 mM DTT, protease inhibitor, and RNase inhibitor)128 followed by filtering through 40 µm cell strainer. GFP-positive nuclei were sorted by the MA900 Multi-Application Cell Sorter. Genomic DNA was extracted from sorted nuclei, and mutations were determined by HTS as indicated before.


### Righting reflex test
  Righting reflex test was performed on P12 mice as previously described97. Briefly, the mice were placed on their backs, and the time taken for mice to right themselves was recorded by a stopwatch. The procedure ended either when the animals right themselves, or when the latency period reaches 30 s. Each data point for an individual mouse represents the mean of four trials. 15 AAV-zmABE-treated mice and 15 mock controls were included in this experiment, both female and male.


### WGS off-target analysis
  WGS was performed on GFP-positive nuclei of the spinal cord of AAV-zmABE-treated SMA mice, and on spinal cord tissue of untreated SMA mice as controls. For the WGS analysis, first, adapter sequences were removed from reads in the high-throughput sequencing data using fastp (v.0.23.2)129 (https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp). Then, paired-end sequencing reads were aligned to the mouse genome build 38 (GRCm38/mm10) by BWA-MEM2 (v.2.2) (https://github.com/bwa-mem2/bwa-mem2)130. Next, bam files were sorted, optical duplicates were marked, and base-quality recalibration was performed with GATK (v.4.2.4)131 tools of MarkDuplicates, BaseRecalibrator, and ApplyBQSR. Known variants in the GATK resource bundle were used during base quality recalibration. SNVs of AAV-zmABE-treated SMA mice from whole-genome data were detected by Mutect2132 using the Tumor/Normal variant calling strategy with built-in post-calling filters. Briefly, two mapped BAM files from the untreated SMA mice were considered as “normal tissue”; two BAM files from the AAV-injected mice were considered as “tumor tissue” and were individually compared to the normal tissue using Mutect2, and SNVs in repeat regions annotated by RepeatMasker were excluded132,133. The obtained SNVs were then filtered by these settings: FILTER = “PASS” or “Clustered_events” or “Clustered_events & Haplotype” (because base editors may induce bystander editing, variants labeled as these were kept), DP > 15, and TLOD > 4. Overlapping variants between two independent experiments were extracted. SNVs were annotated via ANNOVAR (v.2019oct24)134.


### Statistics and reproducibility
  Data are shown as mean±s.d. (or medians if noted) from at least three independent experiments. Statistical tests were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.1.2, including unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test, Welch’s t-test, Mann-Whitney test, paired Student’s t test, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, Tukey tests after ordinary one-way ANOVA, Dunnett T3 tests after Welch and Brown-Forsythe ANOVA, Dunn tests after Kruskal-Wallis test, and Mantel-Cox test. P  <  0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No data were excluded from the analyses. The experiments were not randomized. The Investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment.
