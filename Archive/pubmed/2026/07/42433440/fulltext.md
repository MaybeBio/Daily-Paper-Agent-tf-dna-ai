# Triphenylphosphonium–triazole hybrids as mitochondria-targeted anticancer agents: design, DNA binding, cytotoxicity study, fluorescent apoptosis imaging and molecular docking


## Abstract
  In this study, three novel triphenylphosphonium-containing 1,2,3-triazole derivatives (7–9) were synthesized and structurally characterized by FT-IR, 1H NMR, and elemental analysis. DNA binding properties were investigated by UV-Vis titration and fluorescence competitive displacement experiments. Spectral changes, including hypochromic and hyperchromic effects, showed a strong affinity towards FSds–DNA, with binding constants on the order of 105 M−1. Fluorescence quenching studies using ethidium bromide and Hoechst 33258 revealed effective probe displacement with higher Stern–Volmer constants for the Hoechst system, suggesting a preferential minor groove binding mode. Lipophilicity assessment (Log P = 1.60–2.05) showed that para-substitution significantly altered hydrophobicity while preserving drug-like properties. Biological evaluation demonstrated potent antiproliferative activity against lung (A549, Calu-1, H1650) and bone (Saos-2) cancer cell lines and exhibited lower GI50 values (∼1 µg mL−1) compared to 5-fluorouracil (5FU). Compounds containing electron-attracting substituents (7 and 9) exhibited enhanced cytotoxicity, moderate TGI and LC50 values, and improved tumor selectivity indices (TSI = 1.97 and 2.78, respectively) while maintaining low toxicity against normal cells. LDH assays confirmed limited membrane damage (<20%) at TGI concentrations, and fluorescence microscopy (DAPI, Rhodamine-123, Hoechst/PI) demonstrated mitochondrial membrane depolarization and apoptosis induction, particularly for compounds 7 and 9. Overall, the results highlight that modulation of para-substituents critically influences DNA interaction, lipophilicity, mitochondrial targeting, and anticancer activity. These triphenylphosphonium–triazole derivatives represent promising mitochondria-targeted anticancer candidates for further optimization. The binding affinity and interaction modes of compound 9, which showed the highest inhibitory activity in both DNA-binding and anticancer assays, were elucidated via molecular docking studies.


## Introduction
  Cancer is characterised by the uncontrolled division and progression of sporadic cells throughout the body.1 These atypical cells have the capacity to infiltrate adjacent tissues and disseminate to disparate anatomical regions through either the lymphatic or circulatory systems. Tumours are masses that develop due to abnormal cell division. A plethora of factors have the potential to induce carcinogenesis, including congenital abnormalities, ubiquitous components, lifestyle choices, and specific diseases.2 Lung cancer was the biggest cause of cancer death, with 1.8 million people dying from it. It was followed by colorectal and liver cancers.3 The most frequently diagnosed cancers were lung, breast (female) and colorectal cancers. By 2050, the number of new cancer cases is expected to reach 35 million a year, emphasising the necessity for effective prevention and control strategies.4 Furthermore, bone cancer (BC) is relatively uncommon. These complex malignancies usually affect the long bones and pelvis. BC is also categorised as osteosarcoma (OS), chondrosarcoma (CHS) or Ewing sarcoma (EWS). There are many different types of BC.4,5 Some originate in the bone itself and are called primary tumours, while others spread to the bone from elsewhere in the body and are called secondary or metastatic tumours. Patients with metastatic osteosarcoma also tend to respond poorly to the chemotherapeutic treatments currently available.6,7 It is vital to find new ways to treat cancer that have fewer side effects and can stop the disease spreading. This will help people to live longer.8,9 Anticancer agents that are DNA-targeted are among the most effective types of drugs used in clinical practice today. The use of these drugs, either on their own or in combination with other treatments, has led to a significant improvement in patient survival rates.10–12 It is known that small molecules bind to double-stranded DNA via three primary interaction modes intercalation, groove binding, or electrostatic interactions. The minor groove of B-form double-stranded DNA, in particular, is attracting attention as an important target for new drug design because it allows numerous small molecules to interact non-covalently and with high sequence specificity.13,14

  In recent years, scientists have been working hard to develop new medicines that can fight cancer in different ways. These medicines can target more than one part of a cancer cell, which makes them very effective. When it comes to anticancer drug discovery, DNA is still one of the most important targets inside cells. This is because interactions with DNA can disrupt processes like replication and transcription, which can in turn slow down the growth of tumour cells. Independently, epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) has been comprehensively validated as a therapeutic target due to its pivotal role in tumour growth, survival, angiogenesis and metastasis. This means that compounds that have both DNA-interacting properties and the ability to inhibit EGFR are particularly interesting because these activities may contribute to anticancer efficacy through different and complementary mechanisms.15,16 The biological evaluation of the synthesised triphenylphosphonium–triazole hybrids showed that they can interact with DNA and also inhibit EGFR. This suggests that the triphenylphosphonium–triazole framework is a good starting point for the development of anticancer agents that have many different functions.

  Compounds containing triphenylphosphonium (TPP) are hydrophobic cationic molecules.17 They can pass through cellular membranes without requiring ionophores or carrier proteins. This is due to their hydrophobic nature molecules. In contrast to hydrophilic compounds, the activation energy needed for TPP to traverse the hydrophobic core of lipid membranes is significantly low. The activation energy is made up of two main components: the first is a repulsive electrostatic energy component (Born energy), and the second is attractive hydrophobic force.18 TPP has a large ionic radius and a localized positive charge, which means the enthalpy input for it is low. This reduced dissolution leads to a weaker electric field, which can polarise water molecules on the TPP surface.19 When lipophilic TPP cations are involved, this force makes it appealing for a large hydrophobic surface area molecule to be carried into a lipid due to water loss. The greater the hydrophobic surface area, the greater the effect. The positive charge of TPP cations facilitates TPP movement across biological membranes due to plasma and mitochondrial membrane potentials.20,21 The positive charge of lipophilic cations enables them to enter mitochondria.

  The simple “click chemistry” approach enables the rapid and efficient assembly of large libraries of triazole-containing compounds, establishing the triazole moiety as a valuable scaffold or pharmacophore in medicinal chemistry.22 The triazole ring exhibits remarkable stability under physiological conditions and is resilient to a wide range of chemical environments. It demonstrates resistance to hydrolysis, oxidation, and reduction, which ensures its metabolic stability in vivo and allows it to withstand rigorous synthetic manipulations. Furthermore, 1,2,3-triazoles are recognized as bioisosteres of amide bonds and other functional groups, allowing them to mimic hydrogen-bonding patterns and spatial arrangements when interacting with biological targets, while offering enhanced metabolic stability.23 The triazole ring is inherently polar and capable of engaging in diverse noncovalent interactions. Its nitrogen atoms can function as hydrogen bond acceptors, and under appropriate protonation or substitution, the ring can also act as a weak hydrogen bond donor. These characteristics enable 1,2,3-triazoles to establish critical interactions within enzyme binding sites, contributing to high-affinity binding. Compounds containing triazole motifs exhibit a broad spectrum of bioactivities, including antimicrobial, antiviral, antiparasitic, anticonvulsant, and anticancer effects.24 Several triazole-based drugs have been successfully developed for clinical use, such as the antibacterial agent tazobactam.

  In this study, triphenylphosphine–triazole hybrid compounds containing a triphenylphosphonium group were synthesized, and their biological properties were investigated in detail. The interactions of the obtained hybrids with DNA were evaluated through competitive ethidium bromide and Hoechst displacement assays. Furthermore, their cytotoxic activities were investigated on A549, Calu-1, H1650, and Saos-2 cancer cell lines. These studies aim to reveal the potential roles of triphenylphosphine–triazole hybrid structures as potential anticancer agents in drug development processes.

### Biological activity studies

#### SAR study
  Triphenylphosphonium (TPP)-based conjugates have attracted significant attention. They are used as mitochondria-targeted anticancer agents. Strobykina et al. (2021)29 synthesized (TPP) conjugates of 1,2,3-triazolyl nucleoside analogues and conducted studies on their antimicrobial activity (Fig. 1). However, studies focusing on TPP–1,2,3-triazole hybrid systems are still very limited in the literature. The study was done because of the good results seen with these rare chemicals.

  The aim was to design different TPP-containing 1,2,3-triazole derivatives through a smart structure–activity relationship (SAR)-oriented approach. Within the devised molecular structure, the delocalized lipophilic cationic properties of the TPP fragment were integrated to enable targeted mitochondrial amassing propelled by the heightened mitochondrial membrane potential of malignant cells. So, the 1,2,3-triazole ring was used not only as a chemically stable linker but also as a versatile pharmacophoric unit, right? And this unit can contribute to hydrogen bonding, dipole interactions, and biomolecular recognition processes, especially in DNA-associated interactions. What's more, special groups of atoms were added to see what effect they have on how electrons are shared, how well they stick to things, how much they can get into DNA, and how strong they are at stopping cancer. The electronic environment around the aromatic region may be significantly affected by modulation, according to the proposed SAR perspective. This could result in alterations to the overall biological activity profile, as well as to membrane permeability and mitochondrial targeting efficiency. Furthermore, the equilibrium between hydrophilic and hydrophobic structural characteristics in the designed compounds was deemed paramount for optimising cellular uptake and selective toxicity towards cancer cells. Unlike the limited TPP–triazole systems previously reported, the synthesised derivatives possess a differentiated structural architecture aimed at enhancing mitochondrial dysfunction, inducing apoptosis, and exhibiting tumour-selective antiproliferative activity simultaneously. Therefore, the present molecular design provides a novel, structure–activity relationship (SAR)-guided platform for developing mitochondria-targeted anticancer agents with improved biological performance.


#### DNA binding studies

##### UV-vis absorption measurements
  To obtain UV-vis absorption spectra of dsDNA in the wavelength range of 200–600 nm at room temperature, a Tris–HCl buffer solution (pH 7.4) was used. In the absorbance measurement experiment, the ligand concentration was kept constant at 150 µM, while the dsDNA concentration was varied between 50 and 200 µM. To create various ligand-dsDNA combinations, different concentrations of dsDNA were added to the compound solution, and the absorption spectra of the resulting combinations were measured. The DNA binding constant of the drug, Kb, was then calculated using the titration data. The Benesi–Hildebrand equation was then used to calculate the binding constant.30[DNA]/(εa − εf) = [DNA]/(εb − εf) + 1/Kb(εb − εf)where εa is the calculated absorption coefficient, Aobsd/[compound], εf is the compound's absorption coefficient when it is free, and εb is the compound's absorption coefficient while bound to DNA.


##### Fluorescence measurements
  The concentrations of the 5 µM ethidium bromide (EtBr, an intercalating substance) and 50 µM dsDNA solutions were maintained throughout all fluorescence studies. Different combinations were prepared by combining various quantities of compounds preparation with the dsDNA solution at room temperature: [compound]/[dsDNA] = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 8. Finally, the volume of the resulting mixtures was adjusted to 5 mL using a Tris–HCl buffer solution.

  The DNA minor-groove binder Hoechst 33258 (concentrations of the 5 µM), which emits vivid blue fluorescence, was also employed in these investigations. A study was conducted on Hoechst 33258, a chemical that emits light at 475 nm and is stimulated at 343 nm. The emission wavelengths ranged from 375 to 650 nm. Higher fluorescence levels are seen when dye EtBr is attached to DNA. The emission spectra of EtBr spanned from 550 to 730 nm when stimulated at 530 nm, with emission being detected around 605 nm.31 The diminishing emission intensity of the dye-DNA solution when the drug was added in increasing amounts was used to assess certain properties. ##### Viscosity assay
  Viscosity experiments were performed at a controlled temperature of 25.00 ± 0.50 °C. These experiments investigated the viscosity of dsDNA solutions. They were performed in the presence of compounds. The aim was to understand the binding mechanism. The flow times of three replicates were recorded using a digital timer and the average flow time was calculated and recorded. The plot of viscosity values for free dsDNA and the compound-dsDNA complex was made using the formula (η/η0)1/3, where r is the ratio of compound concentration to dsDNA concentration. In this equation, “η” represents the specific viscosity of dsDNA in the presence of compounds, and “η0” represents the specific viscosity of dsDNA alone. In this experiment, the concentration of compounds ranged from 10 to 200 µM. This was in a 100 µM dsDNA solution. The viscosities (η and η0) were determined using the following equations.η0 = (t − tb)/tbIn this particular instance, the symbol ‘t’ is utilised to denote the duration of the flow of dsDNA when subjected to varying concentrations of compounds, whilst the symbol ‘tb’ is employed to denote the duration of the flow of the buffer solution.


### Lipophilicity assay
  To predict their potential to cross the blood–brain barrier, their lipophilicity will be experimentally determined using the shake-flax method described in the literature.32 The compounds will be added to a separating funnel containing phase-separated 1-octanol and water, the funnel will be shaken thoroughly, and then allowed to stand to allow phase separation to occur. The distribution of the compound in the 1-octanol and water phases will then be determined by UV-Vis absorption spectroscopy, and the obtained concentrations will be substituted into the following equation to calculate the Log P (partition coefficient) value.Log P = Log[concentration of the compound in the octanol phase/concentration of the compound in the water phase]


### Cancer cell lines and cell culture
  Work A549 (ATCC, CCL-185), Calu1 (ATCC, HTB-54), and H1650 (ATCC, CRL-5883) lung cancer cell lines, Saos2 (ATCC, HTB-85) bone cancer cell line, Beas2B (RRID, CVCL-0168) normal lung cell line, and HC (Sigma-Aldrich, 402-05A) chondrocyte normal cell line were used. All cell preparation procedures were performed in a sterile environment in a laminar cabinet. Cell lines were cultured in DMEM and RPMI-1640 media containing 10% FBS and 2% Pen Strep solution at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Measurement plates were seeded at 10.000 cells per well. After approximately 16 hours of pre-incubation, test agents were added, and measurements were performed after 24 hours of incubation.


### Cell proliferation measurement and determination of NCI-60 survival parameters
  The MTT test was used to measure the effects of test agents on cell proliferation and NCI-60 survival parameter values.33 This test protocol was applied after 24 hours of incubation of the test agents with the cancer cell lines. Results were reported as % cell inhibition, and it was assumed that the optical density of cells treated with solvent (DMSO) was 100%. Accordingly, the following formulas were used to measure NCI-60 survival parameters (GI50, TGI, and LC50), and the logarithmic function was analyzed on the logarithmic curve prepared from the absorbance values obtained; cell proliferation: [(Ti − Tz)/(C − Tz)] × 100 if Ti ≥ Tz (cytocytic effect) or [(Ti − Tz)/Tz] × 100 if Ti < Tz (cytocidal or cytotoxic effect) (Tz; zero point, C; control growth, Ti; inhibition by test agent). GI50: concentration value that reduces growth by 50% ([(Ti − Tz)/(C − Tz)] × 100 = 50), TGI: concentration value that reduces growth by 100% (Ti= Tz), LC50: concentration value that kills 50% of the cells in the environment ([(Ti − Tz)/Tz] × 100 = −50). Tumor selectivity index (TSI) was determined according to the following formula: TGI concentration of normal cells/average TGI concentration of cancer cells.


### Cytotoxicity test
  The cytotoxicity or cytostaticity of test substances to cells was determined by the LDH method.34,35 Depending on the substances tested, an increase in dead cells during the incubation period leads to increased LDH in the culture supernatant. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) is a cytoplasmic enzyme present in most cells and is stable. An LDH cell cytotoxicity kit was used according to the manufacturer's procedure. Briefly, the change in the amount of formazan produced as a result of LDH enzyme activity was measured and evaluated using the following formula:% Cytotoxicity = [(substance absorbance − low control/high control − low control) × 100].


### DAPI–Rhodamine123 and Hoechst 33258 propidium iodide double staining test
  For DAPI–Rhodamin123, cells were washed with DPBS and then incubated with Rhodamin123 stain at 37 °C for 15 min. At the end of this period, the cells were washed with DPBS and fixed in cold methanol for 10 min. After this period, the cells were washed with DPBS and incubated with DAPI stain at 37 °C for 15 min. Rhodamin123 and DAPI stains were visualized at 520 nm and 490 nm, respectively, under fluorescence microscopy. For Hoechst 33258–propidium iodide36 cells were washed with DPBS and incubated with propidium iodide stain at 37 °C for 15 min. At the end of this period, the cells were washed with DPBS and fixed in cold methanol for 10 min. After this period, the cells, which had been washed with DPBS, were incubated with Hoechst 33258 stain at 37 °C for 15 min. Hoechst 33258 stain was visualized at 460 nm, and propidium iodide stain at 575 nm under fluorescence microscopy.


### In silico protocol
  Molecular docking studies were then performed using AutoDock Tools in order to elucidate the possible interaction profiles, binding conformations, and binding energies of compound. To evaluate the validity and reliability of the docking protocol, a widely used re-docking approach, Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor (EGFR) tyrosine kinase (PDB (Protein Data Bank) ID: 1M17) consistent with our previous studies and the literature, was employed.37–41 For this purpose, obtained from the Protein Data Bank database, were used as receptors. The docking protocol was validated by the re-docking method, and the optimal binding positions of the selected compounds were determined using the Lamarckian Genetic Algorithm.42 The obtained binding conformations and protein-ligand interactions were visualized and analyzed using Discovery Studio43 and PyMOL44 programs.


## Results and discussion

### Chemistry and characterization
  In this article, triazole–triphenylphosphine derivatives have been designed and synthesis (Scheme 1). Three new 1,2,3-triazole ligands containing triphenylphosphonium groups were prepared, and their structures were characterized using spectroscopy and analytical methods. The structures of the ligands were investigated by FTIR (Fourier Transform Infrared), 1H NMR (Proton Nuclear Magnetic Resonance Spectroscopy), and elemental analysis. The elemental analysis data were found to be consistent with theoretical values. The FT-IR spectra of all synthesized compounds were examined in the 4000–450 cm−1 range, and the spectral data are provided in the experimental section. The characteristic shifts in the spectra of all compounds were consistent with the proposed structures. All relevant spectra are presented in the SI (Fig. S1–3). When the FTIR spectra of compounds 1–3 are examined, the disappearance of the characteristic peaks belonging to the azide groups (–N3) confirms the presence of the 1,2,3-triazole ring the click reaction. The disappearance of the hydroxyl peaks presents at 3200 cm−1 as a result of the chlorination process indicates that the chlorination reaction has occurred (Fig. S4–6). Examination of the FTIR spectra of compounds 7–9, obtained by attaching the triphenylphosphine compound to the structure of the parent compound (see Fig. S7–S9), revealed a new P–C bond vibration band at around 830 cm−1. The 1H/13C NMR spectrum of triazole–Cl compounds is given in Fig. S13–18. 1H NMR spectra of the ligands were taken in CDCl3 (Fig. S21). When examining the 1H NMR spectrum of compound 8, the signal observed around δ 3.85 ppm is attributed to the protons of the –OCH3 group. The doublet signal observed around 5.57–5.74 ppm originates from the –CH2 protons bonded to phosphorus. The aromatic group protons were observed as doublet signals at δ 6.95 and δ 7.93 ppm. The signals at δ 8.96 ppm were identified as belonging to the 1,2,3-triazole group protons. Upon examining the 13C NMR spectrum, the –C signal belonging to the phosphorus group was observed as two peaks at 22.34 and 21.82 ppm. The signals at 159–114 ppm were attributed to aromatic carbons (Fig. S22).


### DNA binding studies

#### UV-vis absorption titration studies
  One of the oldest and most common ways to learn about how drug candidate molecules interact with and bind to DNA is UV-Vis spectroscopy. Prior to experimental studies, the long-term stability of the compounds was assessed by monitoring their UV-Vis spectra at three different time points (Fig. S25 and 26). No significant changes in absorption band positions or intensities were observed during this period. The absence of spectral variation indicates that the compounds remain structurally stable in solution under the applied experimental conditions and exhibit good short-term stability. Although compounds 7–9 exhibited significant DNA-binding affinity, the present experiments were not designed to evaluate DNA strand cleavage or DNA damage responses. Therefore, no conclusions can be drawn regarding the induction of single- or double-strand DNA breaks. In this context, we recorded the UV-Vis spectra of an FSds–DNA solution (0.75–1.2 × 10−4 M) in the presence of compound 7–9 at different concentrations that corresponded to different [compound]/[DNA] mixing ratios (r). We took these measurements to see if the triphenylphosphine–triazole compounds and FSds–DNA were changing the spectra in any way. These kinds of interactions can change the transition bands of the compounds while they are being titrated. Fig. 2 shows the UV-Vis spectra of FSds–DNA with compound 9 at different r values. This is just one example; the spectral behavior of other compounds was very similar. The small drop in absorption intensity at λmax = 312 nm (hypochromic effect) shows that FSds–DNA and the compound are interacting, which could mean that a new DNA-complex adduct is forming.

  Compound 9 (1 × 105 M) shows increased amounts of FSds–DNA when we examine its UV spectrum (r = [DNA]/[compound]). The intraligand band at 316 nm (band I) exhibits hypochromism up to 29% (Fig. 3). It also shows hyperchromism in the other two bands (at 265 nm and 410 nm) (r = 0–0.8). The response of compounds 7–8 to the addition of FSds–DNA is quite similar. Hyperchromism ranges from 40–21% (Table 1). The hypochromism observed in compound 9 can be explained by the interaction between the complex's aromatic chromophore (from the N-donating ligands) and the DNA base pairs. This interaction is consistent with an intercalative binding mode. However, the hyperchromism and redshift seen in the other bands point to a minor groove binding mode. The current results indicate that the complexes can bind to FSds–DNA. This binding may occur via intercalation or small groove. However, the precise pattern of DNA binding cannot be determined solely by UV spectroscopic titration studies.

#### Competitive binding studies
  Determining the method by which compounds bind to dsDNA is possible through the use of a technique called fluorescence. Information about the molecular binding between triphenylphosphine–triazole compounds and dsDNA was provided by fluorescence studies that used the fluorescent dyes EtBr and Hoechst 33258. The intensity of the faint fluorescent emissions in EtBr has been shown to increase significantly when bound to dsDNA and decrease when combined with an intercalating agent. Like EtBr, Hoechst 33258's low fluorescent emission increases significantly when it interacts with dsDNA and is observed when it is bound to the groove.

  For the fluorescence studies, the following measurements were performed: solutions containing 50 µM of double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) and 5 µM of ethidium bromide (EtBr) were combined with solutions of triphenylphosphine–triazole compounds (7–9) at various concentrations. As illustrated in Fig. 4 and 5, fluorescence spectra were collected from the solutions between 550 and 750 nm, using an excitation wavelength of 526 nm. The intensity of the EtBr–dsDNA emission decreased as the concentration of compounds 7–9 increased. The quenching ratios of the compounds were found to be 41%, 38% and 55% respectively (Fig. 4 and 5). A slight blue shift was observed when the compounds were added to the EB–DNA complex.

  The same protocol was applied in Hoechst 33258 substitution studies, which were conducted to investigate the effects of substitutions on the structure and properties of the compound. The fluorescence spectra of the solutions were recorded between 375 and 650 nm (see Fig. 6 and 7), with excitation at 343 nm. The fluorescence studies revealed that an increase in the concentration of triphenylphosphine–triazole compounds resulted in a decrease in the fluorescence intensity of the dsDNA and Hoechst 33258 mixture. At the same time, a slight red shift in wavelength was observed with the addition of DNA. The quenching percentages of the compounds were calculated to be 51%, 38% and 65% respectively, as presented in Table 1.

  Information about the fluorescence quenching abilities of compounds is provided by the Stern–Volmer equation. Stern–Volmer quenching constants are calculated using this equation. So, for both EtBr–dsDNA and Hoechst–dsDNA, we created a graph showing the emission level (I0/I) versus the number of triphenylphosphine–triazole compounds. We did this using fluorescence titration measurements. KSV values were calculated using the graph slopes (Fig. 6 and 7). Stern–Volmer graphs were used to measure the fluorescence quenching of EtBr and Hoechst 33258 bound dsDNA mixtures by the compounds. The KSV values calculated for EtBr and Hoechst 33258 are given in Table 1. Comparing the KSV values of the compounds revealed that Hoechst 33258 has a larger KSV value than ethidium bromide. This suggests that compounds 7–9 can bind to DNA via minor groove binding rather than intercalation.


#### Viscosity assay
  The interaction of small molecules with DNA can be evaluated in a highly sensitive and effective manner using the technique of viscosity. As viscosity is remarkably sensitive to alterations in DNA length, it has been established as a vital and accurate approach for the analysis of the binding of external ligands to DNA. The unravelling of the structure is caused by intercalator ligands, which disrupt the hydrogen bonds that stabilise the DNA double helix. The process of unwinding not only lengthens the DNA but also increases its relative viscosity. In contrast, ligands that bind within the grooves of DNA have a negligible effect on the viscosity of double-stranded DNA. In consideration of these observations, a viscosity graph showing the ratio of the viscosity of the compound to the viscosity of the double-stranded DNA solution (dsDNA) was constructed and is presented in Fig. 8. The addition of compounds at varying concentrations to the double-stranded DNA solution resulted in negligible changes in relative viscosity. The reason for this is that compounds tend to interact with dsDNA via a groove-binding mechanism. When the slope ratios in the graph are examined, this phenomenon is confirmed by the fact that the slope of EB-DNA is higher and the slope of compound-DNA is lower. Similarly, studies using Hoechst DNA stain show that the slope is similar to that of the compounds, confirming groove binding with DNA.


### Lipophilicity assay
  Lipophilicity of the synthesized compounds was evaluated by calculating Log P(O/W) values to predict membrane permeability and potential biological activity. Lipophilicity is a critical parameter affecting drug absorption, distribution, and interaction with biological membranes. The calculated Log P(O/W) values revealed that compound 7, which carries a keto group in the para position of the phenyl ring, has a Log P(O/W) value of 1.60. Compound 8, containing a methoxy substituent in the same position, showed a slightly higher Log(O/W) value of 1.65. In contrast, compound 9, containing an ethyl ester group, showed a significantly higher Log P(O/W) value of 2.05. The increase in Log P(O/W) values from 7 to 9 can be attributed to the increasing lipophilic character of the substituents. The keto group in compound 7 is a polar functional group capable of hydrogen bonding, which reduces lipophilicity. In compound 8, the methoxy group, despite containing an oxygen atom, also contributes to the hydrophobic character due to the methyl group, resulting in a slight increase in lipophilicity compared to compound 7. The highest Log P(O/W) value was observed in compound 9, which contains an ethyl ester group that increases the hydrophobic surface area and improves the transition to the organic phase. These results demonstrate that the substitution in the para position of the phenyl ring significantly affects the lipophilicity of the molecules.

  The Log(O/W) values of all compounds fall within the range generally considered suitable for drug-like molecules (Log P ≈ 1–3), suggesting that these compounds may possess suitable membrane permeability and bioavailability. In particular, the higher lipophilicity of compound 9 may increase cell membrane permeability and potentially enhance biological activity. However, excessively high lipophilicity may lead to poor water solubility. Therefore, the observed Log(O/W) value for compound 9 appears to be within the optimal range. Despite the permanent charge on the phosphonium centre, the extensive hydrophobic surface provided by the triphenylphosphonium moiety and the aromatic triazole-phenyl scaffold compensates for the ionic character, resulting in moderate lipophilicity values that are compatible with membrane permeation and mitochondrial accumulation. Overall, the results demonstrate that the nature of the substituent in the para position plays a key role in determining the lipophilicity of the compounds and thus their potential pharmacokinetic behavior. In general, these results show that while triphenylphosphine and triazole groups provide a stable pharmacophoric framework, substituents in the phenyl ring play a crucial role in regulating lipophilicity. This tunable lipophilicity may be important for optimizing membrane permeability, targeting to mitochondria, and ultimately biological activity.


### Measurement of cell proliferation of test substances
  To evaluate how the newly synthesized complexes affect the proliferation of lung and bone cancer cells, our research team conducted an MTT (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) assay.35 The GI50 value indicates the concentration required to inhibit cell growth by 50% and serves as a sensitive marker for early cytostatic effects. All tested compounds (7, 8, 9) share a common molecular backbone with a triphenylphosphonium group attached to a triazole ring, but differ in the para-substituent in the phenyl ring. Therefore, the observed differences in biological activity can primarily be attributed to the electronic and steric properties of these substituents. Compounds (7, 8, 9, and 5FU) exhibited low GI50 values in the µg mL−1 range in A549, Calu-1, H1650, and Saos2 cell lines. This increased potency is due to the presence of the lipophilic cationic TPP+ group, which supports the selective accumulation of the compounds in cancer cell mitochondria due to the elevation of mitochondrial membrane potentials. In particular, the keto and para-acetyl-substituted derivatives showed strong antiproliferative activity with GI50 values between 1 and 1.2 µg mL−1 in all cell lines (Tables 2 and 3). The acetyl group, acting as a moderately electron-withdrawing substituent and hydrogen bond acceptor, can enhance cytotoxic activity by facilitating stronger interactions with mitochondrial or intracellular targets. In contrast, the para-methoxy substituted derivative compound 8 showed weaker proliferation inhibition, particularly in Calu-1 cells, and had a higher GI50 value of 5.72 µg mL−1 (Tables 2 and 3). The electron-donating property of the methoxy group and its contribution to increased lipophilicity may support growth inhibition while being insufficient to trigger apoptotic or necrotic pathways. As expected, 5FU was effective at the GI50 level in all cell lines, but required higher concentrations compared to other novel compounds. These results demonstrate that the ability of the novel compounds to suppress proliferation is similar to or even exceeds that of 5FU in certain cell types. The TGI, or concentration at which cell growth completely stops, signals the transition from cytostatic to cytotoxic effects.

  Both 7 and 9 showed TGI values ranging from 20 to 40 µg mL−1 in all cancer cell lines tested (see Table 2). In contrast, compound 8 and 5FU exhibited much higher TGI values, particularly in lung cancer cells. These findings suggest that TPP–triazole derivative compounds are more effective at arresting the cell cycle, while compound 8 and 5FU primarily reduce cell proliferation. LC50 indicates the concentration required to kill 50% of the cells and reflects true cytotoxicity. Compound 7 and compound 9 exhibited LC50 values between 100-200 µg mL−1 in cancer cells. In contrast, compound 8 and 5FU did not reach LC50 levels (>500 µg mL−1) in the tested range and primarily caused cytostatic effects. In normal cell lines (Beas-2B and HC), all compounds. They showed LC50 values exceeding 500 µg mL−1 (see Tables 2 and 3). These findings demonstrate that TPP–triazole derivative compounds cause controlled cytotoxicity in cancer cells while largely protecting normal cells. TSI is an important parameter indicating the selectivity of the compound against cancer cells and is generally measured by the division of normal cell TGI to cancer cell TGI. Compound 7 (TSI = 1.97) and compound 9 (TSI = 2.78) showed high tumor selectivity (see Table 3). Compound 8 (TSI = 1.59) showed moderate selectivity, while 5FU (TSI = 0.82) showed no preference against normal cells (see Table 3). These findings indicate that the p-(ethoxycarbonyl) substituted derivative exhibited a balanced activity profile with low GI50 values and moderate TGI and LC50 values in all cell lines. In particular, this compound was effective in H1650 cells. By demonstrating enhanced activity in the cell line, it suggests a cell line-specific degree of sensitivity. The moderately electron-withdrawing and conformationally flexible ester function can optimize membrane permeability and intracellular interactions, leading to combined antiproliferative and cytotoxic effects. Taken together, these findings highlight that electron-withdrawing substituents in the phenyl ring tend to enhance cytotoxic activity, while electron-donating groups support cytostatic effects. The results clearly demonstrate that para-substituent modulation plays a critical role in tuning the biological profile of TPP+–triazole derivatives. In conclusion, these compounds represent promising anticancer agents with mitochondria-associated mechanisms of action, and further structural optimization focusing on electron-withdrawing and hydrogen bond-accepting substituents may lead to better therapeutic candidates. TSI is a crucial parameter indicating the compound's selectivity against cancer cells, usually measured as normal cell TGI divided by cancer cell TGI. Compound 7 (TSI = 1.97) and compound 9 (TSI = 2.78) demonstrated high tumor selectivity (see Table 3). Compound 8 (TSI = 1.59) exhibited moderate selectivity, while 5FU (TSI = 0.82) showed no preference for normal cells (see Table 3). These findings suggest that new compounds could offer a wider therapeutic window compared to traditional chemotherapeutic agents.


### Evaluation of cytotoxic activity of test molecules
  Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release serves as a reliable marker for necrotic and late-stage cytotoxicity, indicating cell membrane damage. In this study, the cytotoxicity of triphenylphosphonium-bound 1,2,3-triazole derivatives was evaluated at TGI concentrations in lung cancer cell lines (A549, Calu-1, H1650), bone cancer (Saos-2), and normal cells (Beas-2B, HC). Compound 7 caused 14–19% LDH release in all cancer types and showed similar effects in normal lung cells (Beas-2B: 19.3%) (Table 4). Compound 9 increased LDH levels by 12.5–18.0% in cancer cells but was less cytotoxic to HC chondrocytes (7.2%). Compound 8 caused significantly lower LDH release, particularly in Calu-1 (3.7%) and Saos2 (6.1%) cells, and showed minimal toxicity (3.5–3.9%) in normal cells (Table 4). 5FU showed consistent cytotoxicity across cell types, ranging from 9.7% to 11.8%. Overall, LDH-related cytotoxicity remained below 20% at TGI concentrations for all compounds tested. This indicates that although the acetyl group enhances cytotoxic activity, cell selectivity is only minimally maintained. The organic compound containing a methoxy group showed the weakest effect, with low cytotoxicity values in both cancer and normal cell lines; consequently, it suggests that the electron-donating methoxy group suppresses biological activity. The derivative containing an ethoxycarbonyl group (compound 9) showed a significant cytotoxic effect in cancer, while exhibiting lower cytotoxicity, particularly in chondrocyte lesions, revealing selective cell selectivity.


### Evaluation of fluorescent staining results of test substances
  Fluorescent staining is a critical tool for evaluating the cellular effects of newly synthesized complexes, particularly in terms of mitochondrial function and cell death.35 Using fluorescence microscopy, lung and bone cancer cells treated with the synthesized triphenylphosphonium-linked 1,2,3-triazole derivatives compound 7–9 were analyzed after DAPI and Rhodamine-123 staining. These compounds differ structurally in terms of their para-substituents on the phenyl ring: compound 7 contains an electron-withdrawing acetyl group, while compound 9 contains a lipophilic ethoxycarbonyl ester group, and compound 8 carries an electron-donating methoxy group. DAPI staining showed a slight increase in cell death in the treated cells, as indicated by the more intensely blue stained nuclei compared to controls (Fig. 9 and 10). This effect was more pronounced in cells treated with compound 7 and compound 9, associated with their electron-withdrawing and lipophilic substituents known to enhance mitochondrial targeting and apoptotic induction. In contrast, cells treated with compound 8 exhibited less intense nuclear staining, consistent with the electron-donating methoxy group, which appears to reduce cytotoxic potential. Additionally, Rhodamine-123 fluorescence intensity decreased after treatment, reflecting a partial loss of mitochondrial membrane potential. While control cells showed strong green fluorescence (Fig. 11), treated cells, particularly with compound 7 and 9, showed significantly reduced staining intensity (Fig. 9 and 10), supporting their role in inducing mitochondrial dysfunction. The lower Rhodamine-123 signal in these groups is consistent with the increased degradation of mitochondrial membrane potential due to its constitutive properties, whereas compound 8 showed a relatively lesser decrease. To characterize apoptotic events in more detail, Hoechst 33258/PI double staining was performed. In control samples, nuclei were homogeneously stained blue, and no PI-positive cells were detected (Fig. 11). In contrast, treatment with the synthesized complexes resulted in decreased cell density and the appearance of numerous PI-positive apoptotic residues (Fig. 9 and 10). Again, compounds 7 and 9 caused more pronounced PI staining, indicating advanced apoptosis and membrane damage, while compound 8 showed relatively milder effects.

  Evaluating the GI50, TGI, LC50, and TSI parameters together reveals distinct differences in how the tested compounds act, which can be correlated to their structural features. Compounds 7 and 9, both containing electron-withdrawing acetyl and lipophilic ethoxycarbonyl para-substituents on the phenyl ring, respectively, exhibited low GI50 and TGI values along with medium LC50, indicating a strong cytostatic effect followed by controlled cytotoxicity. These substituents likely enhance mitochondrial targeting and apoptotic induction, explaining their effective suppression of cancer cell proliferation and subsequent cell death. In contrast, compound 8, with its electron-donating methoxy group, and the classical chemotherapeutic 5FU primarily exhibited proliferation-inhibiting effects without causing significant cell death even at higher doses, reflecting a more cytostatic and less cytotoxic profile. Elevated TSI (Tissue Selectivity Index) values observed for the compounds 7 and 9 derived compounds suggest enhanced cancer cell-specific targeting or exploitation of metabolic vulnerabilities, possibly due to their triphenylphosphonium moiety facilitating mitochondrial accumulation. This multi-parameter analysis offers deeper insights than evaluations based solely on IC50 or GI50, providing a more nuanced understanding of the compounds' therapeutic potential and safety profiles. LDH release assays complement these findings by confirming the predominantly cytostatic nature of the compounds. Low LDH release (<20%) indicates minimal membrane damage or necrosis at concentrations sufficient to inhibit growth (TGI). Compound 7 and compound 9 showed somewhat elevated LDH release in cancer cells, consistent with controlled induction of apoptosis rather than necrosis, aligned with their structural propensity to disrupt mitochondrial function. Conversely, compound 8 demonstrated high cellular tolerability with minimal LDH leakage in both cancer and normal cells, supporting its antiproliferative effect via cell cycle arrest or early apoptosis rather than overt cytotoxicity. The LDH profile of 5FU, showing uniform release independent of cell type, reflects the broad, non-specific cellular stress typical of traditional chemotherapeutics. The absence of significant LC50 values from LDH data, alongside TSI results, indicates necrotic cell death plays a minor role under these experimental conditions. Instead, the antiproliferative effects likely involve both direct interactions such as DNA/BSA adduct formation and activation of apoptosis primarily through mitochondrial dysfunction and inhibition of protein synthesis. Supporting this mechanism, fluorescent staining analyses with DAPI/Rhodamine123 and Hoechst33258/PI revealed intense luminescence in apoptotic nuclei, particularly in compound 7 and 9 treated cells, consistent with mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis. Due to their low toxicity, potent anticancer activity, and cancer-selective behaviour linked to their para-substituent chemistry, these complexes are promising candidates for further preclinical and clinical phase investigations.


### In silico study
  In vitro, the most active compound 9 was inserted into the DNA duplex of the DNA d(CGCGAATTCGCG)2 dodecamer sequence. Docking into the minor groove of compound 9 was observed. The binding affinity of the most stable conformation of compound 9 during docking was −9.45 kcal mol−1, and the binding positions are given in Fig. 12. Compound 9 formed a hydrogen bond with the DA6 (1.7 Å) nucleotide. Additionally, the phenyl ring DC21 (5.38 Å) and the triphenylphosphonium ring DG22 (5.84 Å) of compound 9 underwent a π–π shaped interaction. Moreover, π–sigma interactions were observed between the ethyl terminal of the ester group of compounds 9 and the nucleotides DA6 (4.77 Å), DT7 (5.50 Å), and DT20 (5.45 Å). In addition, π–alkyl interactions were detected between DA5 (3.92 Å) and the phenyl ring of the triphenylphosphonium moiety. Compound 9 formed several hydrophobic interactions within the DNA-binding site, thereby enhancing stability of the complex.

  The antiproliferative potentials of the compounds were evaluated in vitro on A547 and Saos2 cancer cell lines. Cell viability was assessed using standard colorimetric methods, and based on the obtained data, a molecular docking study was performed to investigate the interactions of the most potent compound 9, with the active site of the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR). The fact that EGFR acquires constitutive activity in many malignant tumors as a result of overexpression, gene amplification, or activating mutations makes this receptor an important molecular target for targeted cancer therapies. Inhibition of the EGFR signaling pathway suppresses downstream signaling cascades associated with cell proliferation, survival mechanisms, and resistance to apoptosis. The binding behavior and inhibitory potential of the compounds against EGFR were further evaluated through molecular docking studies, enabling an energy-based assessment of their interactions with the receptor's active site. The high degree of overlap between the predicted binding conformation and the co-crystal structure supports the accuracy of the applied method (Fig. S19).46 The most stable conformation in the erlotinib region exhibited an RMSD value of 1.82 Å. This low RMSD value indicates that the predicted conformation closely resembles the experimental structure, strongly validating the reliability of the docking protocol.

  Molecular docking studies were performed for compound 9, which exhibited the highest activity among the compounds evaluated by the in vitro assays. The docking results revealed that the most stable binding conformation of compound 9 possessed a binding affinity of −9.31 kcal mol−1. In addition, molecular docking analyses were also carried out for both the co-crystallized ligand, erlotinib, and the reference compound, gefitinib. The calculated binding energies for erlotinib and gefitinib were −7.70 kcal mol−1 and −9.06 kcal mol−1, respectively. Compared with both the co-crystallized ligand erlotinib and the reference inhibitor gefitinib, Compound 9 demonstrated a lower binding energy, indicating a stronger binding affinity, which is consistent with its superior biological activity. In the EGFR active binding site, compound 9 exhibits a π–anion interaction with the Asp831 (3.19 Å) residue of the triazole ring and a π–cation interaction with Lys721 (3.20 Å) of the phenyl ring, as well as numerous hydrophobic interactions, significantly contributing to the formation of a stable structure. These results strongly support the evaluation of the compound as a suitable anticancer candidate molecule for rational drug design in terms of its structure–activity relationship.


## Conclusion
  This study examined the synthesis, characterization, DNA binding, and anticancer properties of TPP–triazole hybrid compounds. FTIR and NMR spectroscopy were used to figure out the structures of the compounds that were made. We used UV-Vis and fluorescence spectroscopy to look at how the TPP–triazole hybrids interact with double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) in a way that is similar to how they would in the body. The findings demonstrated that hybrid structures comprising a TPP core and a triazole ring exhibit substantial interactions with dsDNA. The UV-Vis spectra demonstrated a significant hypochromic effect for compound 9, whereas other compounds displayed hyperchromic effects, indicating distinct DNA binding modes. The found that compound 9's binding constant (Kb) was 1.15 × 105 M−1. Displacement studies using Hoechst 33258 and ethidium bromide (EB) demonstrated increased quenching in the Hoechst system, suggesting minor groove binding. The lipophilic ethoxycarbonyl ester group in compound 9 makes it bind very well to DNA. This is because it increases van der Waals, hydrophobic, hydrogen bonding, and dipole–dipole interactions with the DNA minor groove. These interactions are what make the binding affinity so strong in experiments. In the second part of the study, compounds 7 and 9 exhibited a superior pharmacological profile relative to standard chemotherapeutic agents, displaying significant antiproliferative activity at low doses, total growth inhibition at moderate doses, and selective cytotoxicity at elevated doses. The integrated assessment of GI50, TGI, LC50, and TSI data suggests that these compounds are promising preclinical candidates. LDH cytotoxicity assays demonstrated negligible membrane damage, even at TGI concentrations, indicating that the antiproliferative effects are primarily linked to cytostatic effects and early apoptosis rather than necrosis. It is important to note that compound 8 had a high safety profile because it released very little LDH. The LDH results back up the MTT results, which suggest that these compounds might be better for combination therapies or long-term cytostatic treatment plans than for very aggressive cytotoxic treatments. Fluorescence staining studies revealed that these compounds can activate mitochondrial apoptotic pathways and inhibit protein synthesis, thereby augmenting antiproliferative activity without significant cytotoxicity. In general, TPP–triazole hybrid compounds look like they could be good candidates for cancer treatment because they have a good safety–efficacy profile. More preclinical research is needed. Molecular docking studies have revealed that compound 9 exhibits a strong and stable binding tendency to both DNA and the active site of EGFR. This interaction is further stabilized by multiple hydrophobic contacts, contributing to the formation of energetically favorable and structurally stable complexes. This demonstrates its significant potential as a bifunctional inhibitor capable of effectively interacting with both DNA and EGFR. Accordingly, compound 9 may serve as a promising lead scaffold for further anticancer drug development efforts.
