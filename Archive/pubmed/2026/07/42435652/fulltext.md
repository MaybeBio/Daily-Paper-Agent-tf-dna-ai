# Melatonin reprograms antioxidant defenses to suppress ferroptosis via Homer1a/mGluR1-Nrf2/xCT signaling after retinal ischemia-reperfusion


## Abstract
  Melatonin (Mel) exerts antioxidant and anti-ferroptosis effects not only via the canonical receptors Mt1/Mt2 but also via metabotropic glutamate receptor 1 (mGluR1)/glutamate-mediated signaling. Nevertheless, the regulatory mechanism by which Mel modulates mGluR1/glutamate signaling in retinal ischemia-reperfusion (I/R) injury and its downstream effects on ferroptosis and oxidative stress remain poorly understood. Here, bulk RNA sequencing identified that the Mel receptors Mt1/Mt2 and ferroptosis/iron-redox regulators (Gpx4, Fth1, and xCT) were significantly downregulated after retinal I/R. Mel improved retinal structural and visual function, visual-guidance behavior, and electrophysiological responses following I/R while concurrently reducing ferroptotic and inflammatory injury markers. These benefits were largely abolished by Mel membrane receptor antagonists. Mechanistically, Mel upregulated Homer1a in vivo and in vitro in a receptor-dependent manner. Conditional deletion of Homer1a in transgenic mice, as well as Homer1a knockdown in retinal ganglion cells (RGCs), abolished the protective effects of Mel on visual function, along with its anti-ferroptotic and anti-inflammatory activities. Molecular docking and immunoprecipitation assays demonstrated that Mel restored the interaction between Homer1a and mGluR1 and activated the xCT/GSH/Gpx4 antioxidant axis. Pharmacologic inhibition of xCT with the buthionine sulfoximine (BSO) simulation pr genetic interference with xCT expression counteracted the protective effects of Mel. Mel also enhanced Nrf2-mediated transcriptional expression of cCT in a Homer1a/mGluR1-dependent manner. Collectively, our results define a Mel-Homer1a/mGluR1-Nrf2/xCT signaling cascade that promotes RGCs survival after I/R by suppressing ferroptosis/iron-redox reactions, thereby providing a potential translatable strategy for receptor-targeted intervention in I/R-related retinal damage.


## Introduction
  Retinal ischemia-reperfusion (I/R) injury induces oxidative stress, which damages or kills key functional retinal cells, including retinal ganglion cells (RGCs), and initiates a self-propagating lipid peroxidation cascade via continuous oxidation of membrane lipids, further aggravating cellular injury and death. Importantly, this process is coupled with the core mechanism of ferroptosis [1]. Therefore, following I/R, the fate of cells, such as RGCs, is determined not only by the level of reactive oxygen species (ROS) produced during oxidative stress, but also by whether these cells can effectively inhibit lipid peroxidation via endogenous antioxidant defense systems within the reperfusion window, thereby avoiding the breakdown of homeostasis and irreversible ferroptosis.

  Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2)/xCT (also known as solute carrier family 7 member 11, SLC7A11) is a key component of the antioxidant defense system, with a strong capacity to promote cellular adaptation to oxidative stress by upregulating various genes involved in antioxidant and redox homeostasis. Its protective effects are closely linked to its dynamic activation [2,3]. If a sufficient or sustained Nrf2-driven response cannot be established following retinal I/R injury, lipid peroxidation chain reactions are more likely to amplify over time, thereby increasing the susceptibility to ferroptosis [4]. Therefore, elucidating how upstream oxidative stress signals modulate Nrf2/xCT-dependent antioxidant reprogramming following retinal I/R injury is essential for mitigating oxidative stress, suppressing ferroptosis, and maintaining RGCs survival.

  Studies have shown that Nrf2 is not independently activated because its upstream regulation often relies on receptor-signal complexes that shape the kinetics of cellular responses [5]. In the nervous system, metabolic dysregulation induced by injury stimuli, particularly glutamate imbalance mediated by the metabotropic glutamate receptor 1 (mGluR1), may be one of the primary factors that induce Nrf2 alterations, triggering oxidative stress and ferroptosis in cells [6]. This is because prolonged, recurrent glutamate abnormalities can lead to the exhaustion of Nrf2 signaling, thereby impairing the ability of xCT to counteract oxidative stress and ferroptosis. Furthermore, abnormally elevated levels of excitatory amino acids can cause excessive activation of mGluR1, which triggers the upstream amplification of ROS and oxidative stress signaling [7]. In addition, literature reports that mGluR1 downstream kinases such as protein kinase C (PKC) phosphorylate Nrf2, promoting its translocation from the cytoplasm to the nucleus and binding to the antioxidant response element, thereby initiating the transcription of antioxidant genes [8].

  Melatonin (Mel), a naturally occurring sleep-regulating antioxidant hormone secreted by the pineal gland, improves sleep quality by activating the Mt1 receptor, thereby inhibiting cortical excitability and reducing the release of excitatory neurotransmitters such as glutamate [9]. Mel can also modulate mGluR activity and glutamate signaling, thereby improving memory and synaptic plasticity in Alzheimer's disease (AD)-like mouse models by counteracting oxidative stress [10]. This suggests that Mel influences antioxidant defense via mGluR/glutamate signaling. However, to date, no studies have elucidated whether Mel exerts antioxidant and neuroprotective effects against retinal I/R injury by modulating mGluR1-mediated glutamate imbalance.

  In this study, we used transgenic mice (TG), combined with bulk RNA sequencing, visual electrophysiology, and molecular biology techniques to investigate mGluR1-mediated changes in glutamate levels and their effects on the regulation of oxidative stress and ferroptosis. We particularly focused on the functions and regulatory mechanisms underlying Mel-mediated antioxidant and neuroprotective effects through this signaling pathway. These findings may provide a theoretical basis and potential intervention strategies aimed at strengthening antioxidant defense within the reperfusion window after I/R.


## Materials and methods

### Animals
  Eight-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were obtained from the Shanghai Model Organisms Center® were used. Each mouse weighed approximately 20 ± 2 g. All animal experiments were designed according to the 3R principles. The mice were housed under a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle. The experimental procedures adhered to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, and the study was approved by the Animal Experiment Ethics Committee of the Air Force Military Medical University (approval number: IACUC-20220630).


### Construction strategy for RGCs-specific conditional Homer1a knockout mice (TG mice)
  The Homer1 gene is transcribed and translated into several proteins, including Homer1a (amino acids 1-186) and Homer1b/c (amino acids 1-366), with the first 186 amino acids of Homer1a and Homer1b/c being identical [11,12]. This shared genetic sequence within the Homer1 gene means that disrupting any exon common to both Homer1a and Homer1b/c would affect both protein isoforms, posing challenges to conventional gene knockout strategies that aim to selectively target Homer1a without altering the functions of Homer1b/c. Based on methods outlined in previous studies [13] and with support from the Shanghai Model Organisms Center®, we implemented a targeted gene-editing strategy. We chose exon 5 and intron 5 of the Homer1-203 transcript as the target regions for editing. Using homologous recombination and embryonic stem cell targeting, we inserted the cDNA sequence of exons 5 to 9 from the rat Homer1-204 transcript into the 3′ end of exon 5. This targeting approach was specifically designed to knock out Homer1a while preserving the function of Homer1b/c. A detailed genotyping protocol for Homer1flox/-/Homer1a+/− mice has been described in our previous study on the role of Homer1a in neurological disorders [14].

  By combining this gene-editing strategy with cell type-specific Cre recombinase, we can, in principle, selectively delete Homer1a in any cell type without perturbing Homer1b/c function. Published studies have shown that the γ-synuclein (mSncg) promoter drives RGCs-specific expression in mice and is likewise effective in human RGCs [15]. Accordingly, in this study, Homer1flox/-/Homer1a+/− mice were infected with AAV/DJ-mSncg-Cre to achieve stable ablation of Homer1a protein expression in RGCs via intravitreal injection.


### Mouse model of I/R
  The I/R model was established as previously described [16]. Briefly, the mice were anesthetized with isoflurane. Anesthesia was induced with 3.5% isoflurane in oxygen (0.5 L/min) for 5 min and then maintained with 1.5% isoflurane at 1 L/min. After anesthesia was confirmed, the mice were placed on the experimental platform. The cornea was topically anesthetized with 0.5% tetracaine hydrochloride, and the pupils were dilated with 0.5% tropicamide-phenylephrine ophthalmic solution. The anterior chamber of the right eye was cannulated through the nasal limbus using a 32-gauge sterile needle connected to a reservoir containing sterile normal saline. Intraocular pressure (IOP) was elevated to 110 mmHg by raising the reservoir to 150 cm above the eye and was maintained for 1 h. The needle was withdrawn to allow reperfusion. The left eye, which was cannulated without elevation of the reservoir, served as the control (sham) eye. Immediately after I/R induction, 1 μL of 30 μM ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) was administered to the operated eye by intravitreal injection. Throughout the procedure, chloramphenicol ophthalmic drops (Bausch & Lomb®) were intermittently applied to keep the cornea moist and reduce the risk of infection, and body temperature was maintained at 37.5°C using a digitally controlled heating pad. In addition, 2 μL of Ras-selective lethal 3 (RSL3, 30.5 mM) was injected into the vitreous cavity immediately prior to the introduction of retinal I/R.


### Primary RGCs culture
  Primary RGCs were isolated from the retinas of postnatal day 2-4 C57BL/6J mouse pups using a two-step immunopanning procedure as previously described [17]. Briefly, the retinas were dissected and dissociated into single-cell suspensions. Cell suspensions were incubated on plates coated with Bandeiraea simplicifolia lection (Vector Laboratories, Burlington, ON, Canada) to deplete non-neuronal cells. The non-adherent cells were subsequently transferred to flasks coated with anti-Thy1.2 monoclonal antibody (Bio-Rad, Oxford, UK) to selectively capture RGCs.

  Purified RGCs were incubated in Neurobasal medium (50 mL) supplemented with 1× B-27, 1× penicillin-streptomycin, and 2 mM l-glutamine. Cells were plated onto culture dishes pre-coated with poly-d-lysine and laminin (127-2.5, Sigma-Aldrich®). Primary RGCs were identified by immunostaining for Brn3a and βIII-tubulin.


### Primary RGCs oxygen-glucose deprivation/reperfusion (OGD/R) model
  To mimic retinal I/R injury in vitro, an OGD/R model was established using primary RGCs [18]. Briefly, for OGD, RGCs were transferred to glucose-free DMEM (11966025, ThermoFisher Scientific®) and incubated in a hypoxic chamber at 37°C under 95% N2 and 5% CO2 for 4 h. The cells were then transferred to high-glucose DMEM and returned to normoxic conditions for 12 h of reoxygenation. According to the study design protocol, drug treatment was administered after OGD/R induction (Mel: 50 μM, Luzindole/Luz: 10 μM, 4-P-PDOT/4P: 0.1 μM, mGluR1 activator/Act.: 1 μM for 24 h. Buthionine sulfoximine [BSO]: 500 μM, Fer-1: 2.5 μM, RSL3: 0.25 μM for 6 h). Cells were harvested 24 h after treatment for subsequent analyses. Control RGCs were cultured under normoxic conditions (37°C and 5% CO2).


### Design and construction of lentivirus and adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors
  AAV/DJ-mSncg-Cre vectors were designed and constructed by Beijing Kezhongzhi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Briefly, the mSncg promoter was inserted between the NdeI and EcoRI sites of the AAV-CAG-WPRE plasmid to replace the original CAG promoter while partially retaining the CMV enhancer.

  AAV/DJ-mSncg‐xCT‐shRNA, an AAV/DJ viral vector carrying the mSncg promoter and xCT-targeting shRNA, was constructed by Beijing Kezhongzhi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. AAV/DJ containing the mSncg promoter without shRNA (AAV/DJ-mSncg) was used as a control viral vector. Four weeks before I/R induction, 1 μL of AAV (1012 vg/mL) was administered by intravitreal injection.

  Stable knockdown of Homer1a, xCT, and Nrf2 and overexpression of xCT in RGCs using lentivirus. For the Homer1a-knockdown lentivirus, the siRNA target sequence 5′-CAGCACTCGAGCTCATGTCTT-3'. The oligonucleotides for knocking down xCT were 5′-CATTAGCAGTCCCGATCTTTG-3'.


### Visual electrophysiological assessment
  Visual electrophysiological recordings were performed in accordance with the latest standards of the International Society for Clinical Electrophysiology of Vision (ISCEV) using a Mabwell system (Mabwell, France) [19,20]. All examinations were conducted in a dark room under dim-red illumination after 12 h of dark adaptation. Mice were anesthetized with a mixture of 1% sodium pentobarbital (10 g/0.3 mL) and 50 μL of 10% chloral hydrate, and mydriasis was induced with 0.5% tropicamide-phenylephrine ophthalmic solution. The animals were then positioned on an electrophysiology platform, and 1-2 drops of 0.5% tetracaine hydrochloride were applied to each eye for corneal anesthesia. For electroretinography (ERG), an active Ag–Cl ring electrode was placed on the cornea, a reference stainless-steel needle electrode was inserted subcutaneously beneath the facial skin, and a ground stainless-steel needle electrode was inserted subcutaneously at the tail base. For flash visual evoked potentials (F-VEPs), stainless-steel needle electrodes were used for all positions: the active electrode was placed subcutaneously at the midpoint between the ears, and the reference and ground electrodes were positioned as for the ERG. ERG analysis primarily included the amplitudes of the dark-adapted 3.0 response a-wave, b-wave, and oscillatory potentials (Ops) O2 wave, whereas the P2-wave amplitude was used to evaluate the F-VEP outcomes.

  Measurement of malondialdehyde (MDA), 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), glutathione (GSH), superoxide dismutase (SOD), reactive oxygen species (ROS), glutamate, and Fe2+ levels.

  Levels of MDA, 4-HNE, GSH, SOD, ROS, glutamate, and ferrous iron were measured in each group using a malondialdehyde (MDA) lipid peroxidation assay kit (S0131 M, Beyotime®), 4-HNE assay kit (ab238538, Abcam), GSSG/GSH quantification kit II (G263,Dojindo®), SOD assay kit (S311, Dojindo®), ROS assay kit-highly sensitive DCFH-DA (R252, Dojindo®), Glutamate Assay Kit (G269,Dojindo®), and FerroOrange fluorescent probe (F374, Dojindo®), respectively. Standards and/or samples were added according to the loading scheme and volumes specified in the kit manual. All procedures were performed in strict accordance with the manufacturer's instructions.


### Bulk RNA sequencing
  Mice in each group were euthanized after completion of the designated treatments according to the experimental design, and retinal tissues were collected for bulk RNA sequencing (n = 3). Bulk RNA sequencing and data analysis were performed by GeneDenovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China).

  Cellular and retinal-section immunofluorescence, MTT assay, TUNEL (TdT-mediated dUTP nick-end labeling) assay, Western blotting (WB), reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR), hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, co-immunoprecipitation, light and dark box test, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay.

  The experimental procedures are described in detail in our previously published studies [[21], [22], [23]]. Detailed information on the primer sequences used for RT-PCR is provided in Supplementary Table 1, and detailed information on the reagents and antibodies used is provided in Supplementary Table 2.


### Mitochondrial membrane potential detection
  Mitochondrial membrane potential was measured using the JC-1 Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay Kit (C2006, Beyotime®). Adherent cells cultured in six-well plates were washed once with PBS and incubated with 1 mL culture medium and 1 mL of JC-1 staining working solution at 37°C for 20 min. During this period, 1× JC-1 staining buffer was prepared by diluting 5× stock solution 1:4 with distilled water and maintained on ice. After incubation, the cells were washed twice with 1× JC-1 staining buffer, replenished with 2 mL of culture medium, and imaged using a confocal microscope. JC-1 monomers were detected at excitation/emission wavelengths of 490/530 nm, whereas JC-1 aggregates were detected at 525/590 nm.


### Lipid peroxidation assay
  Lipid peroxidation was measured using the lipid-soluble, ratiometric fluorescent probe C11 BODIPY 581/591 (RM02821, ABclonal®). A 10 mM stock solution was prepared by dissolving 1 mg of C11-BODIPY 581/591 in 198.2 μL DMSO. Cells were seeded at an appropriate density and treated as indicated to establish the experimental model. C11-BODIPY 581/591 was added to a final concentration of 5 μM, and cells were incubated for 1 h. Cells were subsequently washed twice with PBS to remove excess dye and analyzed by flow cytometry. Fluorescence was detected in single-channel mode using 488 nm excitation and a 505-550 nm emission window.


### Propidium iodide (PI) staining
  Propidium iodide (PI, S7109, Sigma-Aldrich®) staining was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions. Retinal sections were incubated with PI at a final concentration of 30 μM for 30 min, followed by counterstaining with DAPI for 10 min. Fluorescence signals were examined using a fluorescence microscope.


### Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay
  ChIP was performed using a ChIP assay kit (P2078, Beyotime®) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, RGCs were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde to fix protein-DNA interactions, followed by cell lysis. Cell lysates were sonicated to shear the chromatin into fragments of 200-1000 bp. The chromatin samples were then incubated overnight at 4°C with a specific primary antibody or control IgG under rotation. ChIP-grade Protein G magnetic beads were added, and incubation continued for 2 h. The beads were washed sequentially with low-salt and high-salt wash buffers, and chromatin was eluted by incubating with 65°C ChIP elution buffer for 30 min (with gentle mixing). After immunoprecipitation, the crosslinks were reversed, and DNA was purified from the immunoprecipitated chromatin. Quantitative PCR was performed using a Bio-Rad T100 Thermal Cycler, and the data were analyzed using StepOne Plus software.


### Dual-luciferase reporter assay
  Modified cell lines were co-transfected with firefly luciferase reporter vectors containing the xCT promoters, together with a Renilla reporter plasmid and an Nrf2 overexpression plasmid. The empty pcDNA3.1 vector served as the control vector. A reporter construct lacking the corresponding promoter was used as the negative control (NC). Cells were collected 24 h after transfection, and luciferase activity was determined using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (E1910, Promega®). The luciferase reporter plasmid (RV-xCT), empty pcDNA3.1, Nrf2 overexpression plasmid, and NC were designed and constructed by Hanbio Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Plasmid transfection was performed using jetPRIME DNA Transfection Reagent (PolyPlus®) according to the manufacturer's instructions.


### Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)
  Primary RGCs from each treatment group were collected and fixed overnight at 4°C in 2.5% glutaraldehyde prepared in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). The samples were then post-fixed in 2% osmium tetroxide, dehydrated, and embedded in Epon 812. Ultrathin sections (60 nm thick) were prepared and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. The images were acquired using a transmission electron microscope.


### Molecular docking
  The receptor protein was mGluR1 (AF-P97772-F1-model_v6.pdb), and the ligand were Mel (PubChem CID:896) and Homer1a, with the following amino acid sequence: MGEQPIFSTRAHVFQIDPNTKKNWVPTSKHAVTVSYFYDSTRNVYRIISLDGSKAIINSTITPNMTFTKTSQKFGQWADSRANTVYGLGFSSEHHLSKFAEKFQEFKEAARLAKEKSQEKMELTSTPSQESAGGDLQSPLTPESINGTDDERTPDVTQNSEPRAEPTQNALPFPHRYTFNSAIMIK. A three-dimensional structure was obtained by homology modeling using SWISS-MODEL for subsequent docking. Molecular docking was performed using HADDOCK, and the results were analyzed and visualized using PyMOL (Version 3.1.6.1). Docking results were subsequently evaluated using the PDBePISA scoring system. In addition, 100 ns molecular dynamics simulations were using GROMACS (v2025.2) with the AMBER99SB-ILDN force field, the TIP3P water model, and Na+ or Cl− ions to maintain system charge neutrality. Molecular docking prediction and molecular dynamics analysis were carried out with assistance from Shanghai Mutiin Scientific Service Co. Ltd.


### Statistical analysis
  Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software) for macOS. An a priori power analysis was conducted to determine the appropriate sample size. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Parametric tests were performed assuming homogeneity of variance. Depending on the comparison, one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Sidak's or Tukey's multiple comparison tests, as appropriate. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

### Mel treatment alleviated retinal structural abnormalities and dysfunction following retinal I/R injury
  To define a safe dose of Mel and characterize its receptor dynamics, we first examined the impact of different Mel doses on normal retinal architecture. H&E staining and morphometry results showed that chronic administration of 100-300 μg/kg Mel did not alter laminar organization or total retinal thickness (Supplementary Fig. 1A–B), indicating that 300 μg/kg is the highest dose without overt structural toxicity. We then assessed the Mel receptor expression after I/R. WB and RT-PCR demonstrated a time-dependent decline in Mt1 and Mt2 levels after I/R, with no significant differences among the 3-, 7- and 14-day time points (Supplementary Fig. 1C–E), suggesting that expression of endogenous Mt1/Mt2 becomes stable 3 days after retinal I/R.

  Functionally, Mel markedly improved performance in the light-dark box (Fig. 1D) and increased the P2 amplitude of F-VEP (Fig. 1E) and the O2, a-, and b-wave amplitudes of ERG (Fig. 1F), indicating that Mel produces more effective visual transmission and visually guided behavior. Co-administration of Luz or 4P largely abolished these effects (Fig. 1D–F), demonstrating that Mel-mediated functional protection is strongly receptor-dependent. At the molecular level, I/R reduced Mt1/Mt2, downregulated Gpx4, Fth1, FPN1, and xCT, and upregulated ACSL4, PTGS2, Tfr1, and DMT1 (Fig. 1G) three days after retinal I/R. Mel reversed these changes by upregulating Mt1/Mt2, Gpx4, Fth1, FPN1, and xCT and downregulating ACSL4, PTGS2, Tfr1, and DMT1, whereas Luz or 4P partially or completely negated these effects (Fig. 1G), indicating that Mel limited ferroptosis through its membrane receptors.

  Consistent with these molecular changes, retinal I/R injury induced a ferroptotic and inflammatory phenotype characterized by increased MDA (Fig. 1H) and 4-HNE (Fig. 1I) levels, depletion of GSH and SOD (Supplementary Fig. 1F and G), accumulation of labile Fe2+ (Fig. 1J, Supplementary Fig. 1H), and reduced Gpx4 immunoreactivity (Supplementary Fig. 1I). Mel markedly reversed these alterations, whereas the blockade of its membrane receptors attenuated this protection (Fig. 1H–J and Supplementary Fig. 1F–I). Mel also shifted the retinal cytokine microenvironment toward an anti-inflammatory state, increasing IL-10 while suppressing TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-18, and IL-6. These effects were lost following receptor antagonism (Supplementary Fig. 1J–N). Thus, Mel inhibited I/R-induced ferroptosis and inflammation by preserving iron redox homeostasis through receptor-dependent signaling.


### Mel attenuated OGD/R-induced ferroptosis and mitochondrial dysfunction in RGCs through Mel membrane receptor-dependent mechanism
  To validate the cytoprotective effects of Mel in an in vitro OGD/R model and to determine receptor dependence, we first confirmed the phenotype of primary RGCs, which were positive for Brn-3a and βIII-tubulin by immunofluorescence (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Dose/time-response analyses confirmed that 50 μM Mel for 24 h is suitable for subsequent OGD/R experiments (Supplementary Fig. 2B–D). TEM showed mitochondrial shrinkage, cristae loss, and increased membrane density after OGD/R, which are canonical ultrastructural hallmarks of ferroptosis [24]. Mel largely preserved mitochondrial architecture, whereas Luz or 4P blunted this effect (Fig. 2A). Consistently, OGD/R reduced Gpx4, Fth1, FPN1, and xCT expression (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Fig. 2E), increased ACSL4, PTGS2, Tfr1 and DMT1 expression (Supplementary Fig. 2E), ROS (Fig. 2C) and labile Fe2+ accumulation (Fig. 2D), and collapsed mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. 2E). Each alteration was significantly reversed by Mel and partially restored by receptor blockade (Fig. 2B–E). OGD/R also enhanced lipid peroxidation, as indicated by increased oxidized C11-BODIPY fluorescence (Fig. 2F), elevated MDA and 4-HNE levels (Fig. 2G and H), and depletion of GSH and SOD activities (Fig. 2I and J). Mel suppressed these ferroptotic indices, whereas Luz and 4P attenuated its protective effects (Fig. 2F–J). OGD/R further triggered inflammatory activation, increasing TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-18, and IL-6 while reducing IL-10 (Supplementary Fig. 2F–J), consistent with the contribution of inflammatory imbalance to ischemia-associated neuronal ferroptosis. Mel normalized this cytokine profile, an effect that was weakened by Luz and 4P (Supplementary Fig. 2F–J). ### Mel upregulated Homer1a expression both in vivo and in vitro in a Mel membrane receptor-dependent manner
  To identify key regulators associated with Mel, we performed transcriptomic differential expression analysis between Mel-treated and solvent-treated groups after injury induction. Unsupervised clustering of differentially expressed genes revealed a clear separation between the two expression profiles (Fig. 3A). Gene ontology enrichment analysis indicated that the differentially expressed genes were predominantly enriched in synapse-related structures and functions, including the synaptic membrane, postsynaptic density, and neuron-to-neuron synapses (Fig. 3B). ## Discussion
  This study found that the Mel receptor, Mt1/Mt2, and key ferroptosis/iron redox regulators (Gpx4, Fth1, and xCT) were markedly downregulated after retinal I/R. Functionally, Mel significantly preserved the retinal structure and visual structure function, enhanced visually guided behavior, and restored retinal electrophysiological responses. Mechanistically, Mel increased Homer1a expression, restored Homer1a/mGluR1 coupling, and activated the xCT/GSH/Gpx4 antioxidant axis. Consistently, the pharmacological inhibition of xCT abolished the beneficial effects of Mel. Moreover, Mel promotes xCT transcription in a Homer1a/mGluR1/Nrf2-dependent manner. Together, these findings identify a Mel-Homer1a/mGluR1-Nrf2/xCT signaling cascade that safeguards RGCs survival by suppressing ferroptosis and iron-associated redox imbalance, providing a theoretical basis and potential intervention strategy to strengthen antioxidant defense within the reperfusion window after retinal I/R.

  One key finding of this study was that Mel signaling is integrated with Homer1a/mGluR1-dependent glutamatergic control, regulating downstream levels of oxidative stress. The Homer family comprises postsynaptic scaffold proteins that regulate receptor function and downstream transduction [12,30]. Previous studies have reported that phosphorylation of the Homer1/mGluR1 complex promotes endoplasmic reticulum stress after brain injury and enhances mGluR1-mediated excitotoxicity. Homer1a, a 186-amino-acid truncated isoform of Homer1, has been reported to exert effects that oppose those of Homer1. In this study, we found that Homer1a mediates Mel-dependent regulation of mGluR1, linking membrane receptor signaling to the regulation of ferroptosis. Mel enhanced the coupling between Homer1a/mGluR1, whereas I/R injury weakened this interaction. This finding is consistent with previous reports demonstrating that Homer1a and Homer1 bidirectionally regulate mGluR1 function during glutamatergic signaling. More importantly, our findings provide a mechanistic explanation for how Mel coordinates multiple pathological processes, including mitochondrial dysfunction, ferro-oxidative imbalance, lipid peroxidation, and dysregulated inflammation, by restricting pathological glutamatergic signaling upstream. Although glutamate excitotoxicity is often discussed as a contributing factor in neuronal death pathways, our findings suggest that glutamate is an upstream amplifier of ferroptotic stress in RGCs. These observations provide a new framework for targeting oxidative stress and ferroptosis after retinal I/R injury.

  Our results indicate that the xCT/GSH/Gpx4 axis constitutes the core mechanism underlying Mel-mediated resistance to ferroptosis. xCT-dependent cysteine import sustains glutathione biosynthesis, thereby promoting Gpx4-dependent detoxification of lipid peroxides [31]. Consistent with this mechanism, disrupting xCT or depleting GSH using genetic or pharmacological approaches significantly attenuated the protective effect of Mel, whereas the upregulation of xCT partially restored these protective effects. These findings indicated that the protective effects of Mel depend on its ability to sustain thiol-dependent antioxidant metabolism. Therefore, therapeutic strategies targeting both the xCT/GSH/Gpx4 axis and its upstream receptor signaling may exert synergistic effects.

  We further confirmed that Mel regulates Nrf2-mediated antioxidant programs through the Homer1a/mGluR1 pathway and selectively enhanced xCT promoter activity at the transcriptional level. Although Nrf2 serves as a master regulator of antioxidant defense, its role in receptor- and synaptic scaffold-dependent regulation of ferroptosis remains poorly understood. Our results indicate that the effective activation of Nrf2 and the establishment of antioxidant transcription are not only determined by Mel but also depend on the integrity of Homer1a and appropriate regulation of mGluR1 signaling. Therefore, Homer1a/mGluR1 constitutes a key upstream module in Nrf2-driven antioxidant transcription. This hierarchical signaling structure integrates excitatory signaling with ferroptosis-related biochemical pathways, thereby coordinating antioxidant transcription through a unified mechanistic cascade that provides a more comprehensive framework for understanding ferroptosis regulation.

  This study not only delineated the molecular mechanism of Mel against ferroptosis but also revealed that its therapeutic effect may be constrained at three levels: receptor availability, synaptic function, and transcriptional execution. First, I/R downregulates the expression of the membrane receptors Mt1/Mt2. The beneficial effects of Mel were markedly dependent on Mt1/Mt2 and were inhibited by Luz and 4P. Notably, although Luz and 4P are commonly used pharmacological tools, Luz preferentially targets Mt2, whereas 4P is 300-fold more selective for Mt2 than Mt1. This suggests that, in our study, Mel's benefit was more likely to rely on Mt2 rather than Mt1. Therefore, in the early phase of reperfusion, even when exogenous Mel is administered, cells may be unable to complete the required signaling cascade because of insufficient receptor subtype availability and the presence of off-target effects, thereby limiting the initiation intensity and duration of antioxidant and anti-ferroptosis responses. Accordingly, the therapeutic efficacy of Mel depends not only ligand availability but also on preservation of functional receptor signaling. These results have potential translational significance and indicate that future clinical or pharmacological optimization should focus not only on Mel dosage but also on receptor availability and temporal matching of downstream signaling. Second, the involvement of mGluR1 provides a broader mechanistic perspective. Ferroptosis is not driven solely by redox dysregulation but can also be dynamically regulated through synaptic and excitatory signaling pathways. After retinal I/R, glutamatergic signaling becomes dysregulated and may alter key determinants of ferroptosis susceptibility through receptor-mediated signaling, such as the amplification of lipid peroxidation and the supply of substrates for antioxidant metabolism. Under these circumstances, the association between altered synaptic function and lipid peroxidative damage extends beyond simple correlations and establishes a clear mechanistic causal relationship. These findings broaden the current understanding of ferroptosis initiation and offer a plausible explanation for differences in cellular susceptibility despite comparable oxidative stress. Mechanistically, altered receptor coupling appears to modulate the threshold for lipid peroxidation and substrate accumulation through changes in excitatory signaling. Third, we observed that regulation of ferroptosis does not rely solely on suppression of ROS production. Instead, it depends on the functional xCT/GSH/Gpx4 axis, whose initiation and maintenance are coupled with Nrf2-mediated transcriptional activation. These findings suggest that ferroptosis regulation operates through two complementary kinetic dimensions. At the metabolic level, xCT provides cystine uptake and supports GSH synthesis, thereby determining whether the antioxidant buffering pool is sufficient. At the transcriptional dimension, Nrf2 drives the expression of xCT and its downstream antioxidant genes, determining whether the antioxidant machinery can be stably maintained. When metabolic substrates are deficient, the antioxidant system fails to form an effective dynamic buffer even with the transient activation of transcriptional signals. Conversely, insufficient transcriptional execution undermines the sustainability of early metabolic compensation. Overall, Mel unified the two pathways via the receptor/synaptic scaffold/Nrf2 axis and sustained a more stable antiferroptotic phenotype. This synergistic mechanism highlights the importance of sustained and potent antioxidant reprogramming during the reperfusion window.

  Although this study identified the ferroptosis phenotype in a relatively comprehensively manner using approaches such as C11-BODIPY, Fe2+, detection of ferroptosis-related proteins (Gpx4/xCT/Fth1/FPN1/ACSL4/PTGS2/Tfr1/DMT1), RSL3/Fer-1 intervention, and TEM-based morphological assessment, the current evidence is insufficient to directly demonstrate that other modes of cell death are absent or negligible in the complex I/R microenvironment. In contrast, apoptosis is characterized by caspase-related signal activation and morphological changes [32]. Necroptosis relies more on the RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL axis and shows features such as membrane rupture and amplified inflammation [33,34]. Inflammation-associated cell death (e.g., death driven by inflammatory amplification) is mainly evidenced by the upregulation of inflammatory factors/pathways and phenotypes [35]. Therefore, this study supports a clear association between Homer1a/mGluR1-related signaling and the ferroptosis phenotype, with functional relevance. However, we posit that ferroptosis is a key validated mechanism in I/R injury, rather than an exclusive explanation for all modes of cell death.

  In designing the therapeutic regimen for this study, we carefully considered three critical parameters (dosage, administration route, and timing) to maximize efficacy, safety, and clinical translational feasibility. Regarding dose selection, the Mel dose used in this study (300 μg/kg, i.p.) was determined based on the maximum safe dose reported in the literature [36] and validated by our pilot experiments. This dose has been shown in previous studies to exert robust neuroprotective effects without inducing obvious toxicity or behavioral abnormalities, thereby achieving an optimal balance between therapeutic benefit and safety [36]. With respect to the administration route, intraperitoneal injection offers significant advantages over intravitreal injection, including ease of operation, excellent repeatability, and low risk of infection. Moreover, Mel absorbed intraperitoneally can effectively cross the blood-retinal barrier to reach therapeutic concentrations in target tissues, making it well-suited for long-term repeated administration and thus more practically valuable in preclinical translational research. More importantly, this study is the first to employ a post-reperfusion regimen in the retinal I/R model, with administration initiated immediately after reperfusion and continued daily for 14 consecutive days. This extended treatment schedule not only fully covers the acute injury phase, the core window of oxidative burst, inflammatory cascade amplification, and early apoptosis, but also extends into the subsequent chronic repair and remodeling stages, including neuronal synaptic regeneration, glial scar formation, and long-term adaptive changes in the endogenous antioxidant system, thereby enabling a more comprehensive assessment of Mel's cumulative protective effects and long-term safety [37,38]. From a clinical translational perspective, the vast majority of patients with retinal ischemic diseases (such as central retinal artery occlusion, non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy, and acute glaucomatous attacks) present for medical attention within hours of symptom onset, which provides a realistic and feasible time window for initiating Mel intervention immediately after reperfusion, fully consistent with the “time is retina” principle in emergency management. Nevertheless, we acknowledge the inherent gaps between animal models and human diseases, including differences in disease etiology complexity, individual heterogeneity, concomitant medications, and underlying comorbidities. Additionally, the immediate intervention strategy employed in this study may face the challenge of patient presentation delay in clinical settings. Furthermore, the potential long-term effects of chronic Mel use on circadian rhythm, endocrine systems, and drug-metabolizing enzymes warrant systematic evaluation in future large-animal studies and clinical trials. Collectively, the integrated dose-route-timing regimen established in this study provides a robust pharmacological foundation and feasible intervention framework for the clinical translation of Mel therapy in ischemic retinal diseases.

  Despite a series of functional assays (including receptor blockade, Homer1a silencing, mGluR1 activation, and regulation of the xCT/GSH/Gpx4 axis) establishing the indispensability of this pathway, several key questions remain to be addressed. First, Mel regulates circadian rhythms, anxiety-like behaviors, and overall voluntary locomotor activity. These factors may confound vision-related behavioral readouts such as the light-dark box test. Although we performed simultaneous visual electrophysiological measurements, future studies should incorporate additional vision-related behavioral experiments. Second, in the clinical context of retinal ischemia (e.g., retinal artery occlusion), patients typically reach the outpatient clinic several hours after the ischemia occurs. Therefore, future studies should examine the therapeutic time window to objectively assess its translational value. Finally, Homer1a is defined as a critical hub activated by Mt1/Mt2 in this study. However, the detailed regulatory mechanisms between Mel and Homer1a remain unclear. In subsequent work, we will further validate Homer1a's transcriptional and translational mechanisms along the candidate pathways downstream of Mt1/Mt2. Nonetheless, the integrated framework proposed herein provides a foundation for clinical translation. Targeting Mel receptor signaling to restore Homer1a/mGluR1 coupling and enhance the Nrf2/xCT antioxidant transcriptional program may offer a promising strategy for shifting irreversible ferroptosis during retinal I/R injury toward an interventionally tractable anti-ferroptotic steady state.
