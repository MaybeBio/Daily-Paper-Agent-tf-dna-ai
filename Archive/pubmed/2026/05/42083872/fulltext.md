# xBind: an integrated webserver for large language model-enabled cross-molecular protein binding site prediction


## Abstract
  xBind is an interactive, freely accessible, and fully configurable webserver for large language model (LLM)-enabled cross-molecular protein binding-site prediction. xBind leverages LLM embeddings from the ESM-2 model together with sequence- and structure-derived features to predict protein–protein, protein–DNA, and protein–RNA binding sites using symmetry-aware deep graph neural networks. The input to xBind is either a single-chain protein sequence in FASTA format or a monomer protein structure in PDB or mmCIF format and it outputs predicted residue-level binding sites of the input protein with its pre-selected interaction partner. The customizable xBind web interface provides: (i) choice of interaction partners including protein–protein, protein–DNA, and protein–RNA; (ii) on-the-fly AlphaFold-based protein structure prediction for sequence-only inputs; (iii) on-demand selection of the likelihood threshold for calibrating structure-aware binding site annotations; (iv) interactive and interpretable web-based results, including sequence and structural visualizations and plots of residue-level binding likelihoods with user-adjustable threshold calibration; and (v) extensive help information for usage and results interpretation through a web-based tutorial and guide. xBind is freely available at https://fusion.cs.vt.edu/xBind.


## Introduction
  Protein–protein and protein–nucleic acid interactions underpin virtually all cellular processes, including signal transduction, gene regulation, replication, and post-transcriptional control [1–6]. A key step in characterizing these interactions is identifying the residues on a protein surface that bind molecular partners such as proteins and nucleic acids [7–9], commonly referred to as protein binding sites. Although experimental methods can identify protein binding sites, they are often time-consuming, costly, and limited in coverage, leaving most proteins without residue-level binding annotations [10–13]. As a result, computational prediction of protein–protein and protein–nucleic acid binding sites remains essential for bridging this gap [14]. However, unified protein binding-site prediction is still challenging because many existing methods are designed for only one interaction type and rely heavily on high-quality experimental structures [15, 16], without fully leveraging the rapidly growing availability of proteome-scale predicted structures generated by modern deep learning methods such as AlphaFold and RoseTTAFold [17–20].

  To improve binding-site prediction, we recently developed methods for protein–protein [7] and protein–nucleic acid [8] binding-site prediction by combining protein large language model (LLM) embeddings with symmetry-aware deep graph learning. These studies showed that embeddings from pretrained protein LLMs can reduce dependence on computationally expensive evolutionary profiles while maintaining strong predictive accuracy [8]. They also demonstrated that the E(3)-equivariant graph neural architectures used in these methods provide strong robustness to input structure quality, enabling comparable performance on AlphaFold-predicted and experimentally determined structures [7, 8]. Despite these advances, public tools that seamlessly support both protein–protein and protein–nucleic acid binding-site prediction using either experimental or predicted structures are still limited. Therefore, a unified, publicly accessible webserver for accurate and scalable cross-molecular protein binding-site prediction would have broad utility for the community.

  Here, we present xBind, an interactive and fully configurable webserver for cross-molecular protein binding-site prediction. xBind predicts residue-level binding probabilities for protein–protein, protein–DNA, and protein–RNA interactions by integrating ESM-2 embeddings [17] with sequence- and structure-derived features in symmetry-aware E(3)-equivariant graph neural networks [21–27]. The server accepts either a single-chain protein sequence or a monomeric structure as input and can optionally perform on-the-fly structure prediction for sequence-only submissions, enabling the use of predicted structural information when experimental structures are unavailable. To support diverse use cases, xBind offers customizable probability thresholding and interactive outputs, including sequence and 3D structure visualizations, per-residue likelihood plots, and downloadable result files. Additional details of the network architecture and training procedures are provided in Text S2 of the Supplementary Information. The xBind webserver is freely available at https://fusion.cs.vt.edu/xBind.


## Materials and methods

### Overview of the xbind pipeline
  Figure 1 shows the xBind webserver workflow, which consists of front-end and back-end modules. The front-end module provides a web-based interface for job submission, allowing users to submit binding-site prediction jobs, monitor job status and progress, and retrieve results after successful completion. The back-end module executes the cross-molecular protein binding-site prediction jobs and can optionally notify users by email when a job is completed, if an email address is provided. Users can also protect the privacy and anonymity of their submitted jobs by selecting appropriate job privacy settings.

  Front-end module. The front-end module of xBind provides a web-based interface for job submission. Users can submit either a monomeric protein structure, if available, or a protein sequence, from which a monomeric protein structure will be predicted on the fly using AlphaFold2, together with a job name as required input. The interface also allows users to customize protein binding-site prediction jobs by choosing the interaction type, including protein–protein, protein–DNA, or protein–RNA interactions, and by setting job privacy to protect the anonymity of submitted jobs. Two independently trained LLM-enabled E(3)-equivariant deep graph neural network frameworks are available, one focusing on protein–protein interactions [7] and the other on protein–nucleic acid interactions [8], both using LLM embeddings from the ESM-2 model [17] for on-demand selection of protein binding-site prediction tasks. The front-end module performs dynamic validation of user inputs, enables job submission, passes the submitted job to the back-end module for execution, displays job status and progress during execution, and presents completed results to the user through quantitative and visual analytics.

  Back-end module. The xBind back-end module implements a sequential pipeline for job management, queuing, execution, and reporting. After receiving a validated job submission, the webserver layer registers job metadata, stages inputs, and initializes status tracking. Jobs are placed in a centralized queue and scheduled on available compute resources, with status updates reflected on the Job Queue page, such as queued, running, finished, or failed. During job execution, xBind performs preprocessing, optionally carries out on-the-fly structure prediction for sequence-only submissions, generates LLM embeddings from the ESM-2 model [17], performs featurization, and integrates sequence- and structure-derived features together with LLM embeddings using E(3)-equivariant deep graph learning architectures for residue-level cross-molecular protein binding-site prediction according to the selected interaction type, producing residue-level probability scores. Upon successful completion, xBind renders an interactive results page and can optionally send an email notification to the user if an email address is provided. Detailed descriptions of feature generation, graph representation, and input feature construction are provided in Text S1 of the Supplementary Information.


## Results

### Performance assessment
  The cross-molecular protein binding-site prediction pipeline employed in xBind has been rigorously benchmarked on multiple datasets across a diverse set of predictive modeling scenarios, ranging from experimental structures to AlphaFold-predicted structures. An interacting residue is defined as a residue on the surface that lost absolute solvent accessibility of <1.0 Å2 on complex formation [28, 29]. Detailed statistics of all benchmarking datasets are provided in Supplementary Table S1 and Text S3 of the Supplementary Information. As shown in Table 1, the binding-site prediction methods underlying the xBind webserver, namely EquiPPIS for protein–protein interactions [7] and EquiPNAS for protein–DNA and protein–RNA interactions [8], demonstrate state-of-the-art performance and statistically outperform competing methods at the 95% confidence level (P <.05), both in terms of the area under the curve (AUC) for the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC) and area under the precision–recall curve (PR-AUC) across all test sets. In particular, EquiPPIS significantly outperforms the closest competing method, GraphPPIS [30], on protein–protein interaction prediction using AlphaFold2-predicted structural models as input. Meanwhile, EquiPNAS achieves superior performance for both protein–DNA and protein–RNA binding-site prediction compared with the closest competing method, GraphBind [31], demonstrating robust identification of functional residues across nucleic-acid-binding contexts. On the additional protein–DNA benchmark Test_129, EquiPNAS consistently exhibits state-of-the-art results, achieving statistically significantly better performance compared to GraphBind and GraphSite, in terms of both ROC-AUC and PR-AUC metrics. These results were validated through 10-fold resampling and significance testing, including Anderson–Darling and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, demonstrating accurate, robust, and generalizable predictions across different input modalities and interaction types.


### Case study
  In Fig. 2, we present cross-molecular protein binding-site prediction results for six representative targets from our benchmark test datasets, spanning different interaction types, including two targets each for protein–protein, protein–DNA, and protein–RNA interactions. The first two examples represent protein–protein interactions: 1T6B_Y, the B. anthracis Protective Antigen complexed with the human Anthrax toxin receptor; and 4M70_H, the Potato Rx-CC domain in complex with the RanGAP2-WPP domain, as shown in Fig. 2A. xBind achieves a Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) of 0.6840 and an F1-score of 0.7143 on target 1T6B_Y, while successfully capturing key interface residues on target 4M70_H with an F1-score of 0.7660. For protein–DNA interactions shown in Fig. 2B, the two targets are 6C31_A, the TetR family transcriptional regulator Rv0078 in complex with DNA, and 6EE8_M, the M. tuberculosis RNA polymerase promoter unwinding intermediate complex. xBind attains an MCC of 0.7977 and a PR-AUC of 0.9438 for 6C31_A, and a ROC-AUC of 0.9946 for 6EE8_M. The final two examples shown in Fig. 2C represent protein–RNA interactions: 6EXN_N, the post-catalytic P complex spliceosome with the 3′ splice site docked, and 4Z7L_A, the endoribonuclease Cas6b. In both cases, xBind exhibits accurate binding-site predictions, achieving a ROC-AUC of 0.9467 on 6EXN_N and an MCC of 0.4373 with a PR-AUC of 0.6960 on 4Z7L_A.


## Web server

### Hardware and software
  xBind is hosted on a Linux cluster with 2.50 GHz Intel Xeon Silver 4215 32-core processors, 192 GB RAM, and 144 TB of storage. The web application is implemented primarily in PHP with client-side JavaScript, and job metadata and status are maintained in a MySQL/MariaDB database through mysqli-based modules. Model execution is orchestrated by the PHP layer, which invokes the prediction pipeline through exec calls into dedicated Python environments for EquiPNAS and EquiPPIS. xBind also communicates with a local back-end service exposed through an HTTP API at 127.0.0.1:8000 for task dispatch and result assembly. Interactive molecular visualization on the results page is powered by the WebGL-based 3Dmol.js viewer. The webserver is compatible with modern browsers including Mozilla Firefox, Google Chrome, Safari, and Microsoft Edge.


### Input and output
  xBind accepts either a monomeric protein structure or a protein amino acid sequence as input. Structural jobs may be submitted using pasted coordinates or uploaded .pdb, .ent, .cif, or .mmcif files, whereas sequence-only jobs require a plain amino acid sequence and trigger on-the-fly structure prediction powered by AlphaFold2 [18]. For reliable execution and computational efficiency, proteins of ∼10–500 residues are recommended. Each job also includes a user-defined job name, while email notification and public/private visibility remain optional. For each completed job, xBind returns residue-level binding probabilities for the selected interaction type. The results page presents these predictions through sequence-based score visualization together with an interactive three-dimensional structural view, allowing spatial inspection of predicted interface residues. User-adjustable probability thresholds are provided to facilitate interpretation of higher-confidence predictions. All results can be downloaded, including the structure used for prediction, a residue-level probability file, and a compressed archive containing the complete outputs and associated run metadata.


## Conclusion
  xBind presents a publicly available webserver for accurate and unified cross-molecular protein binding-site prediction. xBind integrates LLM embeddings from the ESM-2 model with sequence- and structure-derived features in symmetry-aware E(3)-equivariant graph neural networks to predict residue-level binding sites for protein–protein, protein–DNA, and protein–RNA interactions. The web-based interface of xBind supports both structure-based and sequence-only submissions, enabling seamless use of predicted structural models from AlphaFold in the absence of experimental structures, with the option of on-the-fly AlphaFold-based structure prediction for sequence-only inputs. xBind webserver provides an interactive and user-friendly interface for job submission, monitoring, retrieval, and analysis of prediction results, with extensive help information on usage and results interpretation. Future work may further improve the versatility of the xBind webserver by accounting for the modeling uncertainties for predicted structural models for cross-molecular binding-site prediction. In this regard, residue-wise experimental uncertainties for experimental inputs (e.g. B-factors for crystal structures) or per-residue confidence estimates for predicted inputs (e.g. pLDDT scores for AlphaFold structural models) can be used to calibrate the predicted likelihood values of residue-level binding sites. Another promising direction for future work is to consider the conformational states and dynamics of the interacting partners by capturing multistate conformational dynamics during training and inference, leading to improved cross-molecular protein binding-site prediction.
