# Exploring the prognostic role of senescence-related genes in gastric cancer through multi-omics integration and machine learning


## Abstract
  Cellular senescence plays a context-dependent role in gastric cancer (GC), functioning both through tumor-suppressive arrest and the tumor-promoting senescence-associated secretory phenotype. However, its systematic integration into prognostic models remains limited. Here, we develop a novel interpretable framework to identify and validate a robust senescence-related gene signature for GC prognosis. We first introduce a dual-model interpretable feature selection strategy that integrates a biologically informed Kolmogorov-Arnold Network with a tabular foundation model to identify cancer-associated senescence genes. From the initial candidates, an ensemble of ten machine learning algorithms distills a core 4-gene signature to construct a Senescence Risk Score (SRS). The SRS proves to be a powerful and independent prognostic indicator, effectively stratifies patients into high- and low-risk groups with distinct overall survival across multiple cohorts. High-risk patients exhibit an "immune-hot" but potentially dysfunctional tumor microenvironment, characterized by enriched immune cell infiltration, elevated checkpoint expression, and distinct metabolic reprogramming favoring pathways such as angiogenesis and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). Furthermore, the SRS correlates with differential somatic mutation profiles and suggests potential sensitivity to specific chemotherapeutic agents. In vitro functional assays confirmed the oncogenic role of SERPINE1, a top-ranked core gene, in promoting GC cell proliferation. Regulatory network analysis revealed potential upstream transcription factors and miRNAs governing the signature. Collectively, we present a validated senescence-related prognostic signature that enables effective risk stratification of patients with gastric cancer.


## Introduction
  Gastric cancer, predominantly presenting as stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD), remains a significant global health burden, characterized by high incidence and mortality rates [1, 2]. The inherent molecular heterogeneity of GC and the frequent diagnosis at advanced stages contribute to a persistently poor prognosis, underscoring the critical need for robust biomarkers and precise prognostic models to guide personalized therapeutic strategies.

  Cellular senescence, a state of stable cell cycle arrest, plays a context-dependent and complex role in cancer biology[3, 4]. It functions as a potent tumor-suppressive mechanism by halting the proliferation of damaged or pre-malignant cells [5]. However, persistent senescent cells within the tumor microenvironment can paradoxically promote tumor progression through the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), releasing a plethora of pro-inflammatory cytokines, growth factors, and extracellular matrix remodeling enzymes [6]. This duality makes senescence-related genes both pivotal and challenging to interpret as biomarkers, as their expression patterns may reflect either protective responses or pathogenic processes driving malignancy, immune evasion, and therapy resistance[7].

  Recent advances in high-throughput sequencing and multi-omics data integration have provided unprecedented opportunities to dissect this complexity. By combining transcriptomic, genomic, epigenomic, and clinical data, a more holistic understanding of the molecular landscape of GC can be achieved[8]. However, effectively mining these multidimensional datasets to identify robust and interpretable prognostic signatures requires sophisticated computational approaches. Traditional machine learning algorithms have been widely applied, but there is a growing need for methods that can incorporate prior biological knowledge to enhance interpretability and biological plausibility, while also leveraging the power of large-scale, data-driven foundation models to capture broader correlations [9–11].

  In this study, we aim to systematically identify and validate a prognostic signature based on senescence-related genes in GC by integrating multi-omics data with innovative computational frameworks. We developed a biologically informed Kolmogorov-Arnold Network (BioKAN) that explicitly embeds structured pathway knowledge, and concurrently employed a tabular foundation model (TabPFN), to ensure interpretable, data-driven feature selection. By integrating the complementary insights of these distinct computational paradigms with differentially expressed genes, we identified a robust candidate pool of senescence genes dysregulated in GC. Subsequently, we applied an ensemble of ten machine learning algorithms to refine a prognostic model, leading to the construction of a senescence risk score. We comprehensively evaluated the prognostic value, clinical relevance, and biological implications of the SRS, including its association with the immune microenvironment, metabolic pathways, mutational landscape, and drug sensitivity. Furthermore, the functional role of a core gene within the signature was experimentally validated in vitro. Our work establishes a novel, interpretable, and multi-faceted prognostic framework that links cellular senescence to GC progression, offering potential biomarkers for risk stratification and personalized treatment planning.


## Materials and methods

### Data source
  RNA-seq datasets and corresponding clinical information for TCGA-STAD, GSE84437, and GSE84433 were obtained from the TCGA and GEO databases, respectively. Copy number variation (CNV) data and methylation data for TCGA-STAD patients were obtained from the UCSC Xena database [12]. Data related to intestinal flora of TCGA-STAD patients were obtained from The Cancer Microbiome Atlas (TCMA) database. 502 genes associated with aging were obtained from the Aging Atlas database (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/aging/index) [13].

BioKAN is a trainable architecture specifically designed to embed structured biological priors, whereas TabPFN leverages frozen parameters from large-scale pre-training and is directly applied for prediction. #### TabPFN: foundation model for zero-shot tabular inference
  Complementing our domain-driven approach, we employ TabPFN, a Transformer-based foundation model specifically optimized for small-to-medium-sized tabular datasets[18]. By utilizing frozen parameters pre-trained on diverse synthetic datasets generated via structural causal models, TabPFN offers exceptional robustness against feature heterogeneity. This inference provides a strictly data-driven perspective, capturing global correlations through its bidirectional attention mechanism that might be overlooked by constrained biological models.


#### Identification of cancer-associated senescence genes
  By harnessing the synergistic strengths of both models, we integrated the interpretability outputs from both models. For TabPFN, we utilized SHAP [19] to quantify feature contributions, while for BioKAN, we employed the node attribution metric to rank gene importance. We retained the top 80% of informative genes identified by either TabPFN-SHAP or BioKAN.

### Combination of ten machine learning
  We integrate up to ten machine learning algorithms, including Random Survival Forest (RSF), Elasticity Network (Enet), Lasso, Ridge, Stepwise Cox, CoxBoost, Cox Partial Least Squares Regression (plsRcox), Supervised Principal Components (SuperPC), Generalized Boosted Regression Modeling (GBM), and Survival-Support Vector Machines (SVM). The TCGA-STAD dataset was used as the training dataset, and GSE84437 and GSE84433 were used as the validation datasets. KM curves were performed using the R package "survminer" to show the difference in the overall survival (OS) between the high and low-risk groups. Time ROC curves were plotted using the "timeROC" package.


### Immune micro environment analysis
  We used the "immunedeconv" package (which contains the MCPcounter, XCELL, and EPIC immune deconvolution algorithms) and the GSVA package (which provides the ssGSEA algorithm) to calculate the immune cell infiltration for each patient[20]. The "estimate" package was also used to calculate three immune-related scores for the patients.


### Gene enrichment analysis
  The R package "limma" was used to identify differentially expressed genes between the high and low-SRS risk groups. The Hallmark gene set and the metabolism-related gene set were downloaded from the Molecular Signature Database (MSigDB). ### Statistical analysis
  Continuous variables were compared between two groups using Student’s t test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as appropriate. Associations between categorical variables were assessed using the chi-square test. Correlations between continuous variables were evaluated using Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation analysis, depending on data characteristics. For analyses involving multiple comparisons, including gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) and correlation analyses in GSVA-based functional association analyses, P values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Unless otherwise specified, a two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant; for multiple-testing settings, adjusted P < 0.05 was considered significant.

## Results

### Identification of senescence-associated genes in GC
  In this study, we developed a biological pathway–informed Kolmogorov–Arnold Network and concurrently integrated the tabular foundation model, leveraging senescence-related genes from the Aging Atlas database and transcriptomic data from TCGA-STAD to discriminate between tumor and adjacent normal tissues and to identify cancer-associated senescence genes (Fig. 1A). Model performance evaluated by five-fold cross-validation across multiple metrics is shown for BioKAN in Fig. 1B and for TabPFN in Supplementary Figure 1A. Supplementary Figure 1B-D further illustrates the hyperparameter optimization for the BioKAN model, utilizing the mean F1-score as the selection criterion. Differential expression analysis of data from TCGA-STAD identified approximately 12,000 genes (Fig. 1C). A total of 112 overlapping genes were identified between the differentially expressed genes and the interpretable cancer-associated senescence genes(Fig. 1D).


### Identification and functional correlations of prognostic senescence-related genes
  To identify genes with prognostic predictive value from the overlapping gene set, we performed univariate Cox analysis (Fig. 2A). This analysis revealed sixteen genes significantly associated with the prognosis of gastric cancer patients. Among these, thirteen genes (CTF1, EGF, FOS, GHR, SERPINE1, SNCG, SOCS2, PRKAA2, IGFBP1, IL6, ADCY5, APOC3, and AR) were correlated with poor prognosis. Friends analysis indicated that AR exhibited the greatest functional similarity to other genes within this set (Fig. 2B). Functional enrichment analysis demonstrated that these sixteen genes are involved in key aging-related pathways and functions, including aging itself, growth factor activity, damaged DNA binding, and regulation of the apoptotic signaling pathway (Fig. 2C). To further elucidate the interrelationships among these genes, co-expression and correlation heatmaps were generated (Fig. 2D, E). A particularly strong correlation was observed among AR, ADCY5, and GHR (Fig. 2F). Given the critical role of tumor immune infiltration in patient prognosis, we also investigated the correlation between these sixteen genes and immune checkpoint pathway genes (Fig. 2G). The results showed that SERPINE1, AR, and IL6 had significant positive correlations with most immune checkpoints, suggesting their potential involvement in immune function modulation.


### Construction and validation of a senescence-related prognostic model
  To refine the prognostic gene signature, we employed a comprehensive ensemble of ten machine learning algorithms to analyze the sixteen candidate genes. The predictive performance of each algorithm was evaluated using the concordance index (C-index) across both training and validation datasets. The combination of the stepwise Cox (forward) and Random Survival Forest (RSF) algorithms yielded the optimal model (Fig. 3A). This process identified four core senescence-related genes: SNCG, EGF, IGFBP1, and SERPINE1. Using these genes, we constructed a prognostic model and derived a SRS. Patients were stratified into high- or low-risk groups based on the median SRS. Survival analysis demonstrated that patients in the high-risk group had significantly shorter overall survival (OS) compared to those in the low-risk group, a finding consistent across the training set (TCGA-STAD) and two independent validation cohorts (GSE84437 and GSE84433) (Fig. 3B-D). The model’s discriminatory power was further validated by time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. In the TCGA cohort, the area under the curve (AUC) for predicting OS was 0.622, 0.687, and 0.769 at 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively (Fig. 3E-G). ### Clinical correlation and independent prognostic value of the SRS
  To evaluate the clinical relevance of the SRS, we investigated its association with standard clinicopathological characteristics. The distribution of the SRS significantly differed between patient subgroups stratified by age, gender, pathological stage, and TNM stage (Fig. 4A). Importantly, the SRS retained its robust prognostic predictive ability across various clinically defined subgroups, including those based on age, gender, tumor stage, and histological grade (Fig. 4B-K). To determine whether the SRS is an independent prognostic factor, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed. The results confirmed that the SRS serves as a significant independent predictor of overall survival in gastric cancer patients (Fig. 4L). To enhance its clinical utility, we constructed a nomogram that integrates the SRS with key clinical parameters for personalized survival probability estimation at 1, 3, and 5 years (Fig. 4M). The calibration curves demonstrated excellent agreement between the nomogram-predicted probabilities and the actual observed outcomes, confirming the model’s accuracy (Fig. 4N). These findings collectively indicate that the SRS provides a precise and practical tool for individualized prognosis assessment in gastric cancer.

  To characterize the immune microenvironment associated with the SRS, we applied four distinct algorithms (ssGSEA, MCPcounter, XCELL, and EPIC) to evaluate immune cell infiltration. The analysis revealed that patients in the high-SRS group exhibited significantly higher levels of immune cell infiltration compared to the low-SRS group (Fig. 5A), and a strong positive correlation was observed among most of these infiltrating immune cells (Fig. 5B). Notably, two of the core signature genes, SNCG and SERPINE1, showed significant positive correlations with multiple immune cell types.   To elucidate the differences in oncogenic and metabolic characteristics between the high- and low-SRS groups, we performed Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA). The results revealed distinct metabolic profiles, with significant differences highlighted in the heatmap (Fig. 6A). Specifically, the high-SRS group showed enrichment in metabolic pathways such as drug metabolism via cytochrome P450, linoleic acid metabolism, lipid metabolism in senescent cells, and alanine and aspartate metabolism. In contrast, the low-SRS group was more associated with galactose metabolism and inositol phosphate metabolism. Further analysis using the Hallmark gene sets demonstrated that the high-SRS group exhibited heightened activity across a broad spectrum of cancer-related pathways, particularly angiogenesis, apoptosis, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (Fig. 6B). ### SRS stratification reveals distinct mutation profiles and in silico prediction of potential drug sensitivity
  To further characterize the molecular differences between the high- and low-SRS groups, we analyzed their somatic mutation landscapes and in silico drug sensitivity. Mutation analysis revealed that missense mutations were the most common variant type in both groups (Supplementary Figure 2A). Genes such as TP53, MUC16, and LRP1B exhibited high mutation frequencies across the cohort. We next investigated the potential of SRS to predict therapeutic response by analyzing drug sensitivity data from the GDSC database. Significant differences in predicted sensitivity to specific agents were observed between the risk groups. Ihe silico prediction suggested that patients in the low-SRS group showed higher predicted sensitivity to dasatinib, canertinib, methotrexate, and doxorubicin (Supplementary Figure 2B-E). Conversely, the high-SRS group was predicted to be more sensitive to gemcitabine, tanespimycin, daporinad, and neratinib (Supplementary Figure 2F-I). These findings may suggest that the SRS could serve as a valuable biomarker to guide the selection of therapeutic strategies for gastric cancer patients.


### Assessment of gut microbiota composition across SRS groups
  Given the established links between aging, cancer development, and alterations in the gut microbiota, we investigated whether the SRS was associated with specific changes in microbial community composition9. Utilizing publicly available data from The Cancer Microbiome Atlas (TCMA), we compared the gut flora profiles between the high- and low-SRS groups. Our analysis revealed no statistically significant difference in the overall distribution of gut microbiota between the two risk groups (Supplementary Figure 3). This suggests that, within our cohort, the prognostic stratification defined by the senescence-related gene signature is not strongly correlated with major compositional shifts in the gut microbiome.

## Discussion
  Cellular senescence plays a complex and context-dependent role in cancer, functioning as both a tumor-suppressive mechanism and a facilitator of tumor progression through the SASP[23, 24]. In this study, we systematically identified senescence-associated genes with prognostic significance in GC. By integrating transcriptomic data with machine learning, we developed and validated a robust four-gene SRS comprising SNCG, EGF, IGFBP1, and SERPINE1. This signature not only serves as an independent prognostic indicator but also delineates distinct biological, immunological, and therapeutic features between high- and low-risk GC patients.

  To systematically uncover senescence-associated genes dysregulated in GC, we developed a biological pathway-informed Kolmogorov–Arnold Network and integrated it with a tabular foundation model. BioKAN explicitly embeds structured biological priors through a trainable architecture, while TabPFN exploits extensive pre-trained knowledge to capture complex, data-driven correlations. By combining the trainable and biologically grounded and the pre-trained and prediction-oriented methods, we aimed to harness both mechanistic insight and robust statistical association. To leverage the complementary insights of both models, we integrated interpretability outputs from both models, including SHAP analysis for TabPFN and node attribution metrics for BioKAN. By integrating the top-ranked features identified by the models with differentially expressed genes from TCGA-STAD, we preliminarily identified a candidate senescence-associated gene set in GC. From this pool, univariate Cox analysis distilled sixteen genes with prognostic value. Notably, a strong functional correlation network centered on AR, ADCY5, and GHR emerged, hinting at a coordinated biological module influencing GC outcome. The significant positive correlation between several prognostic genes (e.g., SERPINE1, IL6) and immune checkpoint expression provided an early indication of an interplay between senescence and immune modulation in the GC microenvironment[25].

  The application of a multi-algorithm machine learning framework refined the prognostic signature to four core genes. The constituent genes have individually been implicated in cancer biology: SERPINE1 is a key regulator of fibrinolysis and cell migration[26]; EGF drives epithelial proliferation and survival[27]; IGFBP1 modulates IGF signaling, a pathway critical in growth and metabolism[28]; and SNCG is involved in cytoskeletal dynamics and metastasis[29]. Their combined power in predicting patient survival across independent cohorts suggests that they capture a synergistic aspect of senescence-driven tumor progression. The subsequent construction of a nomogram integrating the SRS with clinical variables offers a practical tool for individualized risk assessment.

  Stratification based on the SRS revealed profound differences in the tumor immune landscape. High-risk patients exhibited significantly enriched immune infiltration, including activated dendritic cells, CD8+ T cells, and NK cells, along with elevated expression of immune checkpoints like PD-L1 and CTLA-4. This "immune-hot" phenotype in the high-SRS group paradoxically correlates with worse prognosis, suggesting the presence of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment that may render these infiltrating cells dysfunctional or exhausted[30]. This aligns with the known capacity of SASP factors to recruit immune cells while simultaneously activating immunosuppressive pathways[31]. Moreover, this phenomenon aligns with findings in other malignancies where specific molecular subtypes, despite exhibiting high immune enrichment, are linked to immunosuppressive profiles and poor prognosis due to the presence of regulatory T cells or dysfunctional immune states[32, 33]. The elevated expression of immune checkpoints in the high-SRS group suggests that these patients are prime candidates for immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy. However, the concurrent presence of an immunosuppressive TME indicates that monotherapy may be insufficient. Our SRS could serve as a predictive biomarker for patient stratification: high-SRS patients might benefit more from combination therapies (e.g., anti-PD-L1 combined with SASP-modulating agents), while low-SRS patients, characterized by a "cold" immune environment, might require strategies to "prime" the TME before initiating ICB.

  Beyond immunology, the SRS delineated distinct metabolic and oncogenic pathway activities. High-risk tumors were enriched for pathways related to cytochrome P450 drug metabolism, lipid metabolism in senescence, angiogenesis, and EMT. These findings are consistent with the metabolic reprogramming and increased invasive potential often associated with senescent cells and aggressive cancers[34, 35]. Furthermore, the differential predicted sensitivity to chemotherapeutic agents (e.g., low-SRS sensitivity to dasatinib, high-SRS sensitivity to gemcitabine) highlights the potential utility of the SRS in guiding therapeutic strategy selection. These in silico findings regarding differential drug sensitivity between SRS groups should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, providing a prioritized list of candidate agents for future experimental and clinical validation in gastric cancer.

  Our functional validation focused on SERPINE1, the gene demonstrating the strongest and most stable prognostic performance and correlation with invasive phenotypes. In vitro knockdown of SERPINE1 significantly impaired GC cell proliferation and colony formation, confirming its functional role in supporting tumor growth. This experimental evidence, coupled with bioinformatic analyses linking it to EMT and inflammatory responses, positions SERPINE1 as a central effector molecule within the senescence-associated prognostic network.

  Finally, the construction of regulatory networks predicted upstream transcription factors and miRNAs potentially governing the expression of the core genes. This analysis provides a rationale for future mechanistic studies aimed at understanding the regulatory hierarchies that drive this deleterious senescence-associated program in GC.

  Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the analysis is primarily based on retrospective public datasets, and prospective clinical cohorts are needed to validate the prognostic utility of the SRS. Furthermore, while in vitro experiments support a functional role for SERPINE1, the precise biological roles of SNCG, EGF, and IGFBP1 within the context of GC senescence require further experimental dissection.

  From a translational perspective, the SRS framework offers a multi-tier pathway toward clinical implementation. First, the nomogram integrating the SRS with clinicopathological parameters provides a robust decision-support tool for personalized risk stratification and post-operative surveillance. Second, the distinct immunometabolic landscapes associated with the SRS support its use as a predictive biomarker; high-SRS patients with an “immune-hot but suppressed” phenotype may derive greater benefit from ICB combination regimens. Third, the functional validation of SERPINE1 as a central effector suggests that components of the SRS represent actionable therapeutic vulnerabilities beyond their prognostic value. Finally, the deployment of the BioKAN architecture ensures biological interpretability, facilitating integration into AI-driven diagnostic workflows. Collectively, these findings underscore the potential of the SRS to transition from a computational model to a clinically validated assay, warranting prospective evaluation.

  In conclusion, this study establishes a novel senescence-related gene signature that effectively stratifies GC patients into distinct prognostic subgroups with unique immune, metabolic, and mutational profiles. The SRS provides a framework for understanding the multifaceted impact of senescence on GC progression and offers a promising biomarker for prognosis prediction and personalized therapeutic planning.
