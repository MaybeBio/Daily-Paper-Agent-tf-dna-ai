## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text (Abstract, Introduction, Materials and methods, Results, Discussion, Supplementary figure/table legends referenced but not provided)
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific soundness, technical rigor, and support of claims based solely on the provided manuscript text. No external data, figures, or supplementary materials were available for evaluation.
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors propose that hypoxia drives OSCC progression through a FOSL2-mediated transcriptional activation of DYNC1H1, a gene regulated by a super-enhancer. They support this with bioinformatic analyses (bulk and single-cell RNA-seq, ChIP-seq) and in vitro functional assays (proliferation, invasion, glucose uptake, lactate production) following knockdown and overexpression.
- **Visible evidence base** Text descriptions of results, including references to Figures 1-6 and Supplementary Figures S1-S3 and Tables SI-SII. No actual data, images, or statistical outputs are visible.
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** All figures, supplementary figures, tables, and raw data. No cell line authentication or mycoplasma testing information. No details on antibody validation for ChIP-qPCR. No information on the number of biological replicates for each specific assay beyond a general statement. No in vivo data.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** The manuscript presents a potentially interesting axis (FOSL2/DYNC1H1) in hypoxia-driven OSCC. The bioinformatic pipeline is logical, and the combination of transcriptomic, epigenetic, and functional data is commendable. However, the current evidence base is insufficient to fully support the central claims. The most significant weaknesses are the lack of mechanistic depth (e.g., no direct evidence that FOSL2 binding to the SE is required for DYNC1H1 activation), the reliance on a single ChIP-seq dataset for SE identification, and the absence of in vivo validation. The manuscript is technically sound in its design but falls short of the high bar required for a top-tier journal due to these gaps.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in the fields of oral cancer biology, hypoxia signaling, transcriptional regulation, and super-enhancer biology. The identification of a specific SE-regulated gene and its transcription factor in a clinically relevant context (hypoxia in OSCC) would be of interest to those seeking new therapeutic targets or biomarkers.
- **Major Strengths** The study integrates multiple public datasets (TCGA, GEO) with experimental validation, which is a robust approach. The focus on super-enhancers in the context of hypoxia is timely and relevant. The functional assays are appropriate for the claims made. The identification of a specific transcription factor (FOSL2) and its target gene (DYNC1H1) provides a clear and testable model.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **R1-M1** (Severity: Major, Blocking: Yes, Axis: Evidence, Claim pointer: "DYNC1H1 is identified as a hypoxia-responsive SE-regulated gene", Evidence pointer: Results section, Figure 4A-B, "location not provided"): The identification of DYNC1H1 as an SE-regulated gene is based solely on H3K27ac ChIP-seq data from two cell lines (HSC3 and SAS) from a single dataset (GSE205455). The manuscript does not provide evidence that this SE is functional, e.g., via CRISPR deletion or dCas9-KRAB repression. Furthermore, the claim that it is "hypoxia-responsive" is based on increased H3K27ac enrichment under hypoxia, but the functional consequence of this increased enrichment is not directly tested. The claim that DYNC1H1 is an SE-regulated gene is therefore not fully established.
  - **R1-M2** (Severity: Major, Blocking: Yes, Axis: Evidence, Claim pointer: "FOSL2 activated DYNC1H1 transcription by binding to enhancer regions", Evidence pointer: Results section, Figure 5E, "location not provided"): While ChIP-qPCR shows increased FOSL2 binding to the TE and SE regions under hypoxia, this is correlative. The manuscript lacks a direct test of whether FOSL2 binding is necessary for DYNC1H1 activation. For example, mutating the predicted FOSL2 binding sites in a reporter assay, or performing a FOSL2 loss-of-function experiment followed by RNA-seq to assess DYNC1H1 expression, would strengthen this claim. The current data do not exclude the possibility that FOSL2 binding is a consequence, rather than a cause, of the open chromatin state.
  - **R1-M3** (Severity: Major, Blocking: Yes, Axis: Evidence, Claim pointer: "The FOSL2/DYNC1H1 axis drives the hypoxia-induced malignant progression of OSCC", Evidence pointer: Results section, Figure 6, "location not provided"): The functional rescue experiments are the core of this claim. However, the manuscript only shows that DYNC1H1 OE partially rescues the effects of FOSL2 KD. This is a good start, but it does not prove that the axis is the *driver*. The rescue is incomplete (e.g., 60-77% for proliferation and lactate), suggesting other FOSL2 targets are involved. A more definitive experiment would be to show that a mutant DYNC1H1 that cannot be activated by FOSL2 fails to rescue, or to use a more specific inhibitor of DYNC1H1 function. The claim of a "pivotal regulatory hub" is overstated based on the presented data.
  - **R1-M4** (Severity: Major, Blocking: Yes, Axis: Reproducibility, Claim pointer: "DYNC1H1 knockdown significantly abrogated hypoxia-induced proliferation, invasion, glucose uptake and lactate production", Evidence pointer: Results section, Figure 4I-L, "location not provided"): The manuscript states that data are from at least three biologically independent experiments, but the specific number of replicates for each assay is not provided. More importantly, the statistical analysis is described as using Student's t-test or ANOVA, but the exact statistical test used for each comparison, the p-values, and the effect sizes are not reported in the text. This makes it impossible to assess the robustness of the findings. The authors must provide a detailed figure legend with all statistical details.
- **Minor Comments**
  - **R1-m1** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Clarity, Affected element: Introduction, Evidence pointer: "location not provided"): The introduction is well-written but could be more concise. The rationale for focusing on super-enhancers specifically in the context of hypoxia could be strengthened by citing more specific examples of SEs responding to environmental cues.
  - **R1-m2** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Reproducibility, Affected element: Materials and Methods, Evidence pointer: "location not provided"): The Materials and Methods section for ChIP-qPCR is missing critical details, such as the specific antibody used for FOSL2 (catalog number, lot number), the number of cells per immunoprecipitation, and the method for data normalization (e.g., percent input). This information is essential for reproducibility.
  - **R1-m3** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Clarity, Affected element: Results, Evidence pointer: "location not provided"): The text describing the machine learning analysis (Figure 3C-F) is dense. A brief explanation of what the "StepCox(backward) + Enet (α=0.8)" model represents in biological terms would be helpful for the non-specialist reader.
  - **R1-m4** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Evidence, Affected element: Discussion, Evidence pointer: "location not provided"): The discussion speculates on the mechanism of DYNC1H1 action (e.g., "directional delivery of cargoes") without any supporting data. While this is acceptable for a discussion, it should be clearly framed as speculation, not as a conclusion of the study.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** The lack of functional validation of the SE (e.g., CRISPR deletion) and the lack of a direct test of FOSL2's necessity (e.g., reporter assays with mutated binding sites) are the primary technical failings. The incomplete reporting of statistical details and the absence of in vivo data are also significant.
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** 
  - **Originality**: Moderate. The specific FOSL2/DYNC1H1 axis in OSCC hypoxia is novel, but the general concept of hypoxia-induced transcription factors activating SE-regulated genes is not new.
  - **Scientific importance**: Moderate. The findings could have implications for OSCC treatment, but the lack of in vivo validation and mechanistic depth limits the immediate impact.
  - **Interdisciplinary readership**: Low. The manuscript is primarily of interest to cancer biologists and transcriptional regulation specialists. The clinical relevance is mentioned but not deeply explored.
  - **Technical soundness**: The bioinformatic and in vitro approaches are generally sound, but the missing functional validation of the SE and the incomplete statistical reporting are significant weaknesses.
  - **Readability for nonspecialists**: The abstract and introduction are accessible, but the results section is dense and assumes a high level of familiarity with bioinformatics and epigenetics.
- **Recommendation posture** Currently not established from the provided evidence. The manuscript has a solid foundation, but the central claims regarding the SE regulation of DYNC1H1 and the FOSL2/DYNC1H1 axis as a driver are not fully supported. The authors need to provide functional validation of the SE, a more direct test of FOSL2's role, and complete statistical reporting. If these concerns are addressed, the manuscript could be considered for publication in a specialized journal.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that DYNC1H1 is a "super-enhancer-regulated gene" is unsupported without functional validation of the SE.
- The claim that FOSL2 "drives" DYNC1H1 transcription is not fully supported; the data show correlation, not causation.
- The claim that the FOSL2/DYNC1H1 axis is a "pivotal regulatory hub" is an overstatement based on the incomplete rescue data.
- The clinical relevance and therapeutic potential of targeting this axis are speculative and not supported by any in vivo or patient-derived data.
- The prognostic model's clinical utility is claimed but not validated in an independent cohort or in a prospective setting.
