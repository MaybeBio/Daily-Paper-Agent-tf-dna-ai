# GATA Family Transcription Factor OsGATA1 Regulates Grain Size and Brassinosteroid Biosynthesis in Rice


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12284-026-00917-5.


## Background
  Grain size is an important determinant of rice grain yield and grain appearance quality. In recent years, many genes that control rice grain size have been cloned and characterized, such as GS3 (Fan et al. 2006), GW2 (Song et al. 2007), GS5 (Li et al. 2011), GS9 (Zhao et al. 2018), and COR1 (Xu et al. 2025). These genes are involved in the ubiquitin‒proteasome pathway, G-protein signaling, mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling, phytohormone signaling, or transcriptional regulation (Li et al. 2018; Ren et al. 2023). However, the molecular mechanisms governing rice grain size are still not fully understood.

  GATA transcription factors recognize the WGATAR DNA motif (in which W indicates A or T, and R indicates A or G) through a type IV zinc finger domain and are widely distributed among eukaryotes. Human GATAs are essential for various developmental processes, such as hematopoietic differentiation and cardiac development (Grunert et al. 2024; Liao and Bresnick 2024). In plants, GATAs play critical roles in diverse biological processes, such as photomorphogenesis, chlorophyll biosynthesis, phytohormone response, and the development of leaves, flowers, and roots (Schwechheimer et al. 2022).

  The rice genome harbors 28 GATA genes (Reyes et al. 2004; Gupta et al. 2017), several of which have been functionally characterized. NL1/SNFL1/OsGATA15 regulates organogenesis in rice, and its malfunction results in small panicles, overgrown bracts, and abnormal upper internodes (Wang et al. 2009; He et al. 2018). The expression of OsCGA1/OsGATA11 is modulated by cytokinin, gibberellin, light, and nitrogen (Hudson et al. 2013). Transgenic modification of OsCGA1 expression influences chloroplast development and plant architecture. Overexpression of OsGATA12 increases leaf chlorophyll content and reduces the numbers of leaves and tillers (Lu et al. 2017). OsGATA7 modulates plant architecture and grain size by participating in brassinosteroid (BR) signaling (Zhang et al. 2018). OsGATA6 negatively regulates heading date and grain size but positively affects grain number per panicle (Zhang et al. 2022). OsGATA16 plays an important role in regulating chlorophyll biosynthesis and chloroplast development (Lim et al. 2024). In addition, OsGATA8 has been identified as a key regulator of nitrogen uptake and productive tiller formation, and OsGATA8-H is a critical haplotype for improving the nitrogen use efficiency of rice cultivars (Wu et al. 2024). Collectively, these studies highlight the crucial roles of rice GATA genes in controlling important agronomic traits. However, the biological functions of other rice GATA genes remain elusive.

  BRs are a class of steroidal phytohormones that play essential roles in plant growth and development. In rice, BRs reportedly modulate various agronomic traits, such as plant height, leaf angle, tiller number, grain size, and senescence (Yang et al. 2024). Although BR biosynthetic pathways are well understood in Arabidopsis thaliana (Zebosi et al. 2024), knowledge of these pathways in cereal crops, including rice, remains limited. Several rice genes involved in BR biosynthesis, including D2, D11, OsCPD1, OsCPD2, BRD2, OsDWARF, and OsDWARF4, have been identified through mutation analysis (Zebosi et al. 2024). Most of these genes encode cytochrome P450s. SLG encodes a putative BAHD acyltransferase and participates in BR homeostasis by regulating endogenous BR levels in rice (Feng et al. 2016). Enhanced expression of SLG increases BR levels but does not affect BR signaling. FBX206 is a subunit of the SCF E3 ligase complex and regulates BR biosynthesis (Sun et al. 2024). Suppression of FBX206 via RNA interference increased the levels of 6-deoxocastasterone (6-deoxoCS) and typhasterol (TY), two precursors of BRs, whereas overexpression of FBX206 led to the opposite results. Furthermore, the suppression of FBX206 expression resulted in the accumulation of 28-homobrassinolide (28-homoBL), one of the active BRs. Moreover, FBX206 also participates in BR signaling by modulating the stability of OsOFP8 and OsOFP19, two regulators of BR signaling (Sun et al. 2024).

  In the present study, we identify the GATA family transcription factor OsGATA1 as a negative regulator of grain size and weight. OsGATA1 acts as a transcriptional activator and physically interacts with GS9, an important regulator of grain size. Genetic analyses revealed that OsGATA1 and GS9 act in a common genetic pathway to control grain size. We also show that OsGATA1 plays a role in regulating BR biosynthesis in rice.


## Results

### Mutants of OsGATA1 Present Large Grains
  To identify regulators of rice grain size, we screened our clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)/CRISPR-associated protein 9 (Cas9) gene-edited mutant library and identified a mutant line (No. CR20193) whose grains were significantly larger than those of the wild type (WT) Zhonghua 11. Sanger sequencing revealed that this mutant harbored a 1 bp deletion at the gene-editing target site (Target 1) of the GATA family gene OsGATA1 (LOC_Os05g44400), and it was named osgata1-1 (Fig. 1A; Fig. S1). The grain length and grain thickness of the osgata1-1 mutant were similar to those of the WT (Fig. 1B, C; Fig. S2). However, the grain width and 1000-grain weight of the mutant were 5.8% and 5.4% greater than those of the WT, respectively (Fig. 1D-F). To validate the function of OsGATA1 in controlling grain size, we designed a novel target site for this gene (Target 2; Fig. 1A) and generated gene-edited mutants. We selected a homozygous mutant that also harbored a 1 bp deletion at the target site for phenotypic analysis and named it osgata1-2. Compared with those of the WT, the grain width and 1000-grain weight of osgata1-2 were increased by 5.3% and 4.8%, respectively, whereas the grain length and grain thickness were not significantly altered (Fig. 1B-F). In addition, the tiller number per plant and the grain number per panicle of the osgata1-1 and osgata1-2 mutants were similar to those of the WT, whereas the plant height was reduced (Fig. S3). We next performed reverse-transcription quantitative PCR (RT‒qPCR) analyses on several genes involved in the regulation of grain width, including GW2 (Song et al. 2007), GS5 (Li et al. 2011), WG7 (Huang et al. 2020), OsSPL18 (Yuan et al. 2019), and OsbZIP47 (Hao et al. 2021), and found that only the expression of OsbZIP47 was significantly altered (i.e., downregulated) in osgata1 mutant plants (Fig. S4).

### OsGATA1 Affects Cell Division in Spikelet Hulls
  To elucidate the cellular mechanisms underlying grain size changes in osgata1 mutants, we performed cross-sectional analyses of spikelet hulls. Spikelet hulls from WT and mutant plants just before heading were sampled, and the middle region was imaged using paraffin sections. The outer parenchymal cell layers of osgata1-1 and osgata1-2 spikelets were longer than that of the WT and harbored more cells (Fig. 2A-C). However, the outer parenchymal cell sizes of osgata1-1 and osgata1-2 were similar to that of the WT (Fig. 2D). These findings suggest that OsGATA1 influences mainly cell division in spikelet hulls. RT‒qPCR analyses revealed that the expression levels of several genes related to the cell cycle, including CAK1A, CDKA1, CYCT1, and H1, were increased in young panicles of osgata1 mutant plants, confirming that OsGATA1 is involved in the regulation of cell proliferation (Fig. 2E).

### Characterization of OsGATA1
  The expression profile of OsGATA1 in different tissues was first explored via RT‒qPCR. The results revealed that OsGATA1 was expressed in all tissues examined, with the highest expression level observed in roots at the heading stage and the lowest level detected in young leaves (Fig. 3A). Next, we generated transgenic plants expressing the β-glucuronidase (GUS) reporter gene under the control of the OsGATA1 promoter. GUS activity was detected in roots, stems, leaves, and panicles, and it increased with panicle development (Fig. 3B-H).

  To determine the subcellular localization of OsGATA1, a p35S::OsGATA1-GFP recombinant vector was constructed and introduced into rice protoplasts. A nuclear localization signal (NLS) peptide fused with mKate was used as a nuclear localization marker. GFP fluorescence was strong in the nucleus and weak in the cytoplasm (Fig. 3I; Fig. S5). These findings suggested that OsGATA1 is localized to both the nucleus and the cytoplasm. To test whether OsGATA1 has transactivation activity, we carried out a yeast two-hybrid assay. Compared with yeast cells harboring the pGBKT7 and pGADT7 empty vectors, those containing BD-OsGATA1 and pGADT7 grew well on SD-Leu-Trp-Ade-His selective medium, indicating that OsGATA1 is a transcriptional activator (Fig. 3J). To validate the transactivation activity of OsGATA1, a dual-luciferase reporter (dual-LUC) assay was performed. The relative LUC activity of rice protoplasts transfected with the GAL4BD-OsGATA1 effector construct was significantly higher than that of protoplasts transfected with the GAL4BD empty vector, demonstrating that OsGATA1 functions as a transcriptional activator (Fig. 3K, L).

### Overexpression of OsGATA1 Reduces Grain Size
  To further investigate the biological function of OsGATA1, we generated proUbi::OsGATA1 transgenic lines. Two independent lines (OX1 and OX2) exhibiting significantly elevated OsGATA1 expression levels were selected for further analysis (Fig. 4A). Compared with the WT, grain width in the overexpression lines was reduced by 8.9–9.8% (Fig. 4B, C). Grain thickness was also slightly decreased (Fig. 4D), whereas grain length was not significantly altered (Fig. 4E). Consequently, grain weight in the overexpression lines decreased by 11.4–12.5% (Fig. 4F). In addition, the number of grains per panicle in the overexpression lines was comparable to that of the WT, whereas the number of tillers per plant increased and plant height decreased (Fig. S3). These findings suggest that OsGATA1 negatively regulates grain size and weight.

### OsGATA1 Physically Interacts with GS9
  To elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which OsGATA1 regulates grain size, we screened its interacting proteins using an artificial intelligence–based approach. We first used the ESMfold model (Lin et al. 2023) to generate structural PDB files of OsGATA1 and other rice genomic proteins. Subsequently, we calculated the docking energy scores between OsGATA1 and each genomic protein with the HDOCK server (Version 1.1; Yan et al. 2020). Among the top 100 proteins with the lowest docking energy scores, most were annotated as putative proteins lacking functional characterization (Table S1). Notably, GS9, a protein known to be associated with grain size regulation, was identified among these candidates. GS9 encodes a transcriptional activator without a known functional domain and has been reported to regulate rice grain size by modulating cell division (Zhao et al. 2018).

  To validate the interaction between OsGATA1 and GS9, a bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay was performed. The results showed that OsGATA1 interacts with GS9 in leaf cells of Nicotiana benthamiana (Fig. 5A; Fig. S6). We also carried out a glutathione-S-transferase (GST) pull-down assay to validate the OsGATA1‒GS9 interaction. GS9 was specifically pulled down by OsGATA1, confirming the interaction between the two proteins (Fig. 5B). Moreover, a dual-LUC assay revealed that the relative LUC activity in protoplasts coexpressing GAL4BD-OsGATA1 and GS9 was markedly higher than that in protoplasts expressing GAL4BD-OsGATA1 alone (Fig. 3L). Given that GS9 also functions as a transcriptional activator (Zhao et al. 2018), these findings provide strong evidence that OsGATA1 physically associates with GS9.

### OsGATA1 and GS9 Act in a Common Genetic Pathway
  To elucidate the genetic relationship between OsGATA1 and GS9, we used CRISPR/Cas9 technology to knock out GS9 in the Zhonghua 11 and osgata1-1 mutant backgrounds (Fig. S7). Phenotypic analysis revealed that the gs9 single mutant presented slender grains (Fig. 6), as previously reported (Zhao et al. 2018). Notably, the grain length and grain width of the osgata1gs9 double mutant were comparable to those of the gs9 single mutant (Fig. 6), suggesting that OsGATA1 and GS9 function in a common genetic pathway to regulate grain size, with OsGATA1 acting upstream of GS9.

### Haplotype Analysis of OsGATA1
  To investigate natural variations in the OsGATA1 gene, we conducted haplotype analyses using data from the 3 K Rice Genomes Project (Wang et al. 2018). Using the genomic sequence of the japonica rice cultivar Nipponbare as the reference, we identified 11 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and one insertion/deletion (InDel) in the promoter region of OsGATA1, along with one SNP in the coding region. These genetic variations classified the rice accessions into seven haplotypes (Fig. 7A, B). Among them, Hap1, Hap2, and Hap3 were the most prevalent, accounting for 47.5%, 22.4%, and 16.0% of all accessions, respectively. Notably, Hap1 was predominantly distributed in the indica subpopulation, whereas Hap2 and Hap3 were mainly present in the japonica subpopulation (Fig. 7C). Statistical analysis revealed that accessions carrying Hap2 exhibited significantly wider grains than those belonging to Hap1 or Hap3 (Fig. 7D). In addition, the 1000-grain weight of accessions carrying Hap2 or Hap3 was significantly greater than that of accessions carrying Hap1 (Fig. 7E). Interestingly, grains from accessions carrying Hap2 were slightly shorter than those from accessions carrying Hap1 or Hap3 (Fig. 7F).

### OsGATA1 Affects BR Biosynthesis in Rice
  Previous studies have suggested that several rice GATA factors are involved in BR–related growth regulation (Zhang et al. 2018, 2022). To test whether OsGATA1 plays a role in the BR pathway, we first examined the effect of exogenous BR treatment on OsGATA1 expression in seedlings of the WT rice variety Zhonghua 11. However, the transcript level of OsGATA1 was not significantly altered following treatment with brassinolide (BL), the most active BR (Fig. 8A). Subsequently, lamina joint bending and coleoptile elongation assays were performed on osgata1 mutant plants. Both WT and osgata1 mutant plants displayed dose-dependent lamina joint inclination and coleoptile elongation under BL treatment (Fig. 8B, C). However, the sensitivity to BL was comparable between osgata1 mutant plants and the WT (Fig. 8B, C). Consistent with these observations, the expression levels of several genes involved in BR signaling, including OsBRI1, OsBZR1, and OsBU1, were not significantly altered in osgata1 mutant plants (Fig. 8D).

  In contrast, gene expression analysis revealed that several genes involved in BR biosynthesis, including D2, OsDWARF, OsDWARF4, and OsCPD1, were markedly upregulated in osgata1 mutant plants (Fig. 8D), implying that OsGATA1 may influence BR biosynthesis. To test this hypothesis, we conducted chemical analyses of osgata1 mutant and WT seedlings. 6-deoxoCS and TY are two precursors of BRs (Bajguz et al. 2020). The levels of both compounds were significantly higher in osgata1 mutant plants than in the WT (Fig. 8E). BL, castasterone (CS), and 28-homoBL are the three most important BRs due to their biological activity (Bajguz and Tretyn 2003; Zhang et al. 2019). 28-homoBL was undetectable in either genotype, whereas the levels of CS and BL were significantly higher in osgata1 mutant plants than in the WT (Fig. 8F). Collectively, these findings suggest that OsGATA1 influences BR biosynthesis in rice.

  To determine whether GS9 is also involved in BR homeostasis, we analyzed the expression of BR biosynthetic genes in seedlings of the gs9 mutant. Compared with WT plants, the transcript levels of D2, OsDWARF, OsDWARF4, and OsCPD1 were significantly increased in the gs9 mutant (Fig. S8). Chemical analyses revealed that 28-homoBL was undetectable, whereas the levels of 6-deoxoCS, TY, CS, and BL were significantly higher in the gs9 mutant than in the WT (Fig. 8E, F). Moreover, similar patterns of BR accumulation were observed in the osgata1gs9 double mutant (Fig. 8E, F). Collectively, these findings indicate that OsGATA1 and GS9 cooperatively regulate BR biosynthesis in rice.


## Discussion
  Grain size is a critical agronomic trait that affects crop yield. Therefore, regulation of grain size is a key strategy for improving grain production. In this study, we showed that disruption of OsGATA1 led to large and heavy grains, whereas overexpression of OsGATA1 caused small and light grains. These findings suggest that OsGATA1 negatively regulates grain size and weight.

  Rice grain growth is constrained by the spikelet hull, which determines the final grain size (Ren et al. 2023). The size of the spikelet hull is regulated by both cell proliferation and cell expansion (Li et al. 2018). Our histological analyses revealed that OsGATA1 primarily affects cell proliferation in the spikelet hull (Fig. 2). Similarly, OsGATA6 and OsGATA7 have been reported to regulate grain size by modulating cell proliferation (Zhang et al. 2018, 2022). Notably, all three genes belong to subfamily I of the rice GATA family (Reyes et al. 2004), suggesting that subfamily I GATA factors may play conserved roles in the regulation of cell division and grain size. Transactivation analysis in yeast cells revealed that OsGATA1 functions as a transcriptional activator (Fig. 3J), which was further confirmed by a dual-LUC assay (Fig. 3L). Similarly, OsGATA6 activates the expression of OsMFT1 by binding to its promoter (Zhang et al. 2022). In contrast, two subfamily II rice GATAs, OsGATA8 and OsGATA12, have been reported to function as transcriptional repressors (Lu et al. 2017; Wu et al. 2024).

  GS9 is a transcriptional activator that controls grain size (Zhao et al. 2018). OsOFP8 and OsOFP14, two OVATE family proteins, associate with GS9 and repress its activity. In this study, we showed that OsGATA1 is a novel protein that interacts with GS9 (Fig. 5). Genetic interaction analysis suggested that OsGATA1 and GS9 function in a common pathway to control grain size, with OsGATA1 acting upstream of GS9 (Fig. 6). Interestingly, OsGATA1 and GS9 play opposite roles in controlling grain width (Fig. 6). These results indicate that OsGATA1 and GS9 act antagonistically to orchestrate grain growth and development. Notably, OsOFP8, another interacting protein of GS9, also plays an antagonistic role relative to GS9 in grain size control (Zhao et al. 2018). In addition, subcellular localization analysis revealed that OsGATA1 is localized to both the nucleus and the cytoplasm (Fig. 3I), whereas GS9 is exclusively distributed in the nucleus (Zhao et al. 2018). These findings indicate that OsGATA1 may interact with additional partners to perform distinct functions. Furthermore, our gene expression analyses revealed that the transcript level of OsbZIP47, a negative regulator of grain width and weight, was significantly reduced in osgata1 mutant plants (Fig. S4), suggesting that OsGATA1 may influence the expression of genes associated with grain size.

  Natural variation analysis identified seven haplotypes of OsGATA1, with Hap1, Hap2, and Hap 3 being the most prevalent (Fig. 7A, B). Interestingly, most indica rice accessions belong to Hap1, whereas most japonica rice accessions belong to Hap2 and Hap3 (Fig. 7C). These findings indicate clear genetic divergence of OsGATA1 between indica and japonica subpopulations. Phenotypic data analysis revealed significant differences in grain width among the three major haplotypes (Fig. 7D), confirming that OsGATA1 is involved in the determination of grain width. In addition, the grain weight of accessions carrying Hap2 or Hap3 was significantly greater than that of accessions carrying Hap1 (Fig. 7E), indicating that Hap2 and Hap3 may be superior haplotypes for grain weight improvement.

  BRs constitute the sixth class of essential plant hormones and play pivotal roles in regulating important agronomic traits, including grain size, in rice. The primary BR signaling pathway in rice has been well characterized and involves the OsBRI1–OsSERK1 receptor complex and several families of proteins, including BSK kinases, PPKL phosphatases, GSK3/SHAGGY-like kinases, and BZR-family transcription factors (Yang et al. 2024). Nevertheless, the mechanisms underlying BR homeostasis in rice remain elusive. RAVL1, a B3-domain transcription factor, regulates BR homeostasis by activating the expression of BR biosynthetic genes and OsBRI1 (Je et al. 2010). SLG positively modulates endogenous BR levels and is a novel regulator of BR homeostasis in rice (Feng et al. 2016). In the present study, the response of osgata1 mutant plants to exogenous BL was similar to that of the WT, and the expression of BR signaling genes was not altered in osgata1 (Fig. 8B-D). These findings suggest that OsGATA1 is not involved in BR signaling. However, several BR biosynthetic genes were significantly upregulated in osgata1 mutant plants (Fig. 8D). Accordingly, the endogenous levels of 6-deoxoCS, TY, CS, and BL were increased in osgata1 mutant plants compared with the WT (Fig. 8E, F). These findings suggest that OsGATA1 negatively regulates BR biosynthesis. Given that OsGATA1 acts as a transcriptional activator, it is unlikely that OsGATA1 directly regulates the expression of BR biosynthetic genes. Instead, it may affect BR homeostasis in an indirect manner, which requires further investigation. Furthermore, similar to RAVL1 and SLG (Je et al. 2010; Feng et al. 2016), OsGATA1 itself does not respond to exogenous BL treatment (Fig. 8A). Notably, GS9 also influences BR biosynthesis (Fig. S8; Fig. 8E, F), indicating that the OsGATA1‒GS9 module plays an important role in modulating BR homeostasis in rice.

  In this study, mutation of OsGATA1 significantly increased grain width and weight (Fig. 1). In contrast, the tiller number per plant and the grain number per panicle in osgata1 mutants were not significantly affected, whereas plant height was slightly reduced (Fig. S3). These results indicate that OsGATA1 is a promising target for rice molecular breeding to improve grain weight. For instance, breeding lines with increased grain weight could be efficiently developed through targeted mutagenesis of OsGATA1 using CRISPR/Cas9 technology. Moreover, the haplotypes Hap2 and Hap3 (Fig. 7) may be utilized in marker-assisted selection to enhance grain weight in rice breeding programs.


## Conclusions
  The results of our study suggest that the GATA family transcription factor OsGATA1 negatively regulates rice grain size and weight by affecting cell proliferation in the spikelet hull. OsGATA1 interacts with GS9, a transcriptional activator that regulates grain size. Moreover, OsGATA1 and GS9 function in a common genetic pathway to modulate grain size. In addition, OsGATA1 plays a role in regulating BR biosynthesis in rice. Our results shed light on the function of rice GATA genes and provide a gene resource for improving grain weight in rice.


## Materials and Methods

### Plant Materials and Growth Conditions
  The osgata1 and gs9 single mutants, osgata1gs9 double mutants, overexpression lines, and WT Zhonghua 11 (Oryza sativa ssp. japonica) were cultivated in experimental fields in Jiangsu Province during the summer or in Hainan Province during the winter. The rice seedlings were also grown in Yoshida nutrient solution in a growth chamber at 30/25 ℃ (day/night) with a relative humidity of 70% and a photoperiod of 14/10 h (light/dark). Transgenic-positive rice plants were selected on 1/2 Murashige and Skoog medium supplemented with 50 mg/L hygromycin.


### Vector Construction and Plant Transformation
  The target sites for CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing were selected using CRISPR-P 2.0 (Liu et al. 2017). The CRISPR/Cas9 plasmids were constructed as previously described (Gao et al. 2020). The coding sequence of OsGATA1 was amplified from the root cDNA of Zhonghua 11 and cloned and inserted into the pEGOEPubi-H vector to generate the OsGATA1-overexpressing construct. For promoter analysis of OsGATA1, a 2.5 kb-fragment upstream of the translation start site was amplified from the leaf DNA of Zhonghua11 and inserted into the pBWA(V)HG vector. Rice transformation was performed using an Agrobacterium-mediated transformation method (Nishimura et al. 2006). The primers used are listed in Table S2.


### Subcellular Localization and GUS Activity Assays
  The coding sequence of OsGATA1 was cloned and inserted into the pAN580 vector to generate the p35S::OsGATA1-GFP plasmid. A NLS peptide fused with mKate was used as a nuclear localization marker. The plasmids were subsequently transformed into rice protoplasts as previously described (Gao et al. 2021). The primers used are listed in Table S2.

  A GUS activity assay was performed using a GUS reporter gene staining kit (Sigma‒Aldrich, St. Louis, USA). Briefly, plant tissues were submerged in staining solution and degassed for 30 min in a vacuum desiccator. The samples were then incubated at 37 °C overnight. Chlorophyll was removed by destaining the samples with ethanol.


### RNA Extraction and RT‒qPCR
  Total RNA was isolated using an RNAsimple Total RNA Isolation Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China). Reverse-transcription and RT‒qPCR were conducted as previously described (Gao et al. 2021). Melting curve analysis of the amplicons was performed to confirm the PCR specificity. The rice Ubiquitin gene (LOC_Os03g13170) was used as an internal reference. The primers used are listed in Table S2.


### Screening of OsGATA1-Interacting Proteins Using Artificial Intelligence
  The ESMfold model was used to generate the structural PDB files of each protein with default parameters (Lin et al. 2023). Afterward, the docking energy score between OsGATA1 and each genomic protein was calculated using the HDOCK server version 1.1 (Yan et al. 2020). A more negative docking score indicates a more likely interaction.


### Yeast Two-Hybrid, BiFC, Dual-LUC, and GST Pull-Down Assays
  Transactivation analysis in yeast cells was performed as previously described (Gao et al. 2025). For the BiFC assay, the coding sequences of OsGATA1 and GS9 were cloned and inserted into the pGreenII 62SK-VN and pGreenII 62SK-VC vectors, respectively. The constructs were subsequently cotransformed into Nicotiana benthamiana leaves via Agrobacterium tumefaciens infiltration. Fluorescence was monitored using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon C2-ER, Tokyo, Japan). For the dual-LUC assay, the coding sequence of OsGATA1 was cloned and inserted into the pGreenII 62SK-GAL4BD vector to generate the effector construct. The pGreenII 0800-LUC-GAL4TATA vector, which contains the LUC gene preceded by five copies of GAL4-binding sites and the REN gene driven by the CaMV 35 S promoter, was used as the reporter. The effector and reporter constructs were cotransfected into rice protoplasts. In addition, the effector and reporter constructs were cotransformed with the pGreenII 62SK-GS9 vector. The relative LUC activities were measured using a Dual Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China).

  For the GST pull-down assay, the GST-GS9 and GST bait proteins were incubated with glutathione agarose beads (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 2 h at the room temperature. The prey protein MBP-OsGATA1 was subsequently added, and the mixture was incubated at 4 °C overnight. The beads were then washed thoroughly and boiled in SDS Sample Buffer (1×). Protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE and detected by immunoblotting with anti-GST (Yeasen, Shanghai, China) and anti-MBP (Proteintech, Wuhan, China) antibodies. The primers used are listed in Table S2.


### BR Sensitivity Assays
  BR sensitivity assays were performed as previously described with minor modifications (Gao et al. 2025). For the lamina joint bending assay, the leaf segments of 5-d-old seedlings were excised and incubated in various concentrations of BL (Sigma‒Aldrich) in the dark for 48 h at 30 °C. For the coleoptile elongation assay, plants were grown on 1/2 MS agar medium supplemented with designated concentrations of BL in the dark for 7 d at 30 °C. ImageJ software was used to measure the lamina joint angle and coleoptile length.


### Measurement of Endogenous BRs
  Rice seedlings were used for the quantification of endogenous BRs. The samples were ground into powder with liquid nitrogen. Approximately two grams of sample was dissolved in 95% methanol (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), and the solution was stored at 4 ℃ for extraction. [2H3]-Epibrassinolide (ZZstandard, Shanghai, China) was added to the sample as an internal standard for quantification. After centrifugation, the precipitates were extracted again with methanol, and the supernatants were pooled. The solution was then passed through an activated MAX extraction column (500 mg, 6 ml, Anpel, Shanghai, China) and collected for drying under a nitrogen stream. The collection was redissolved in methanol and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane prior to analysis. BR concentrations were measured using an ultraperformance liquid chromatography in combination with triple quadrupole mass spectrometry (UPLC‒MS/MS) system (UPLC, Agilent 1290, Agilent, USA; MS, Qtrap 6500, AB Sciex, USA).


### Agronomic Trait, Cellular, and Haplotype Analyses
  The plant height, tiller number per plant, and grain number per panicle were measured at the maturity stage. Fully filled grains were used to determine the grain length, grain width, grain thickness, and 1000-grain weight. For histological analysis, spikelet hulls just before heading were harvested and fixed in FAA (50% ethanol, 5% glacial acetic acid, and 5% formaldehyde) overnight. The samples were then dehydrated in a gradient series of ethanol and embedded in paraplast (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). Slices (10 μm in thickness) were cut, stained with safranin O‒fast green, and observed with an optical microscope (Nikon Eclipse E100). The cell size was measured using ImageJ software. Haplotype analysis was performed as previously described (Liu et al. 2025). The PopART 1.7 software was used to construct the haplotype network.


### Statistical Analysis
  All the data are presented as the means and standard deviations of the replicates. One-way ANOVA or Student’s t test was used to determine the statistical significance of differences in means. The charts were generated using SigmaPlot 10.0 software.
