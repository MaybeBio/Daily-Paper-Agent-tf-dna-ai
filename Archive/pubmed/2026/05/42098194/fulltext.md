# Piceatannol ameliorates intestinal barrier dysfunction in urosepsis by modulating the Nrf2/ROS/NF-κB pathway


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47996-4.


## Introduction
  Urosepsis refers to sepsis originating from the urinary tract or male genital tract, and it accounts for 9–31% of all sepsis cases1. As a life-threatening condition, urosepsis is associated with a high mortality rate2, and obstructive uropathy is a major etiology of this disease. Sepsis, which is a systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) induced by infection, is pathologically linked to intestinal barrier dysfunction3,4. The intestinal epithelial barrier, which serves as the body’s first line of defense against pathogens, is primarily maintained by tight junctions (TJs)5,6. In sepsis, intestinal ischemia-reperfusion injury, excessive inflammatory mediators, and gut dysbiosis synergistically promote epithelial apoptosis, TJs disruption, and mucus layer degradation. These alterations facilitate bacterial translocation and endotoxin leakage into circulation, exacerbating systemic inflammation and immune dysregulation7, thereby perpetuating a vicious cycle of gut-derived sepsis. Notably, intestinal barrier dysfunction not only predisposes to sepsis but also drives multi-organ failure6,8, with mechanisms involving Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)/nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) and other pathways9,10. Elucidating the sepsis-intestinal barrier interaction network may inform novel therapies targeting microbiota modulation and barrier preservation.

  Studies in both human and animal models have demonstrated that LPS-induced inflammatory responses and oxidative stress play a critical role in the pathogenesis of sepsis-associated intestinal barrier injury11,12. Ureteral obstruction leads to urinary stasis, promoting bacterial proliferation in the urinary tract and subsequent urinary tract infection. LPS, a key pathogenic component of Escherichia coli and other Gram-negative bacteria cell walls, is released into the urine upon bacterial death or lysis. LPS may then enter systemic circulation through compromised urothelial integrity or via lymphatic/blood reflux resulting from elevated renal pelvic pressure13,14. LPS binds to TLR4 on immune cells, activating NF-κB signaling and triggering massive release of proinflammatory cytokines, thereby driving a potent inflammatory cascade15. Concurrently, LPS-induced inflammation is typically accompanied by oxidative stress, mediating the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS)16,17. The combined effects of LPS-induced oxidative stress and inflammatory responses ultimately lead to intestinal barrier dysfunction18. Therefore, therapeutic strategies targeting both intestinal oxidative stress and inflammatory responses may alleviate sepsis-induced intestinal barrier injury19.

  Piceatannol (PIC), a naturally occurring polyphenolic compound found in various fruits and vegetables, exhibits multiple biological activities, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer properties20. The nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) is a key molecular target through which PIC mediates its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects21,22. PIC promotes Nrf2 protein translation, enabling its dissociation from Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1) and subsequent nuclear translocation. In the nucleus, Nrf2 binds to antioxidant response elements (AREs), activating downstream antioxidant genes such as heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1)23. The degradation of heme is catalyzed by HO-1, generating bioactive products with potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties that enhance ROS scavenging24. Nrf2 activation also upregulates the synthesis of antioxidant enzymes, such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione peroxidase (GPx), which directly neutralize free radicals (a subset of ROS)25. Moreover, PIC suppresses IκB kinase (IKK) activity, thereby preventing inhibitor of κB (IκB) degradation and the subsequent nuclear translocation of p65. This ultimately reduces the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and inflammatory mediators26. Nrf2 activation not only enhances cellular antioxidant capacity but also suppresses NF-κB-mediated inflammation, establishing a dual mechanism with both antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects27. By activating Nrf2 to enhance antioxidant defenses and inhibiting pro-inflammatory pathways like NF-κB, PIC exerts synergistic antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects21,22, a mechanism shared by other plant-derived flavonoids in LPS-induced intestinal injury28. For instance, 5,4’-dihydroxy-6,8-dimethoxy-7-O-rhamnosylflavone, a flavonoid isolated from Indigofera aspalathoides Vahl, has been shown to alleviate LPS-induced intestinal damage by suppressing TLR4/NF-κB signaling and enhancing antioxidant capacity. Xie et al.29 reported that PIC exerts a protective effect against sepsis-induced myocardial dysfunction by directly inhibiting JAK2. Similarly, Kiliç demonstrated that PIC protects against liver injury in septic mice by suppressing oxidative stress and promoting hepatocyte regeneration30. As a stilbenoid, PIC mitigates intestinal inflammation, preserves intestinal function, and maintains homeostasis31. These multi-target actions highlight its therapeutic potential for sepsis-induced intestinal barrier dysfunction.

  Current clinical management of urosepsis relies heavily on early source control, such as percutaneous nephrostomy or ureteral stenting within 6 h, and this approach significantly reduces 28-day mortality32. Antibiotic therapy is key, and newer agents like cefiderocol have been approved for complicated urinary tract infections caused by multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacteria pathogens33,34. Cefiderocol uses a Trojan horse mechanism that grants stability against β-lactamases35. However, these interventions do not address the downstream pathophysiology of intestinal barrier breakdown, oxidative stress and uncontrolled inflammation, which drive organ failure34. Procalcitonin (PCT) aids diagnosis but not treatment36. Thus, there is an unmet need for agents targeting intestinal barrier and immune dysregulation, which highlights the necessity of screening novel compounds like PIC.

  This study aims to investigate the protective effects of PIC on intestinal barrier dysfunction induced by urosepsis and its underlying mechanisms. Using an LPS-induced cellular model and a murine model of urosepsis, we aim to examine whether PIC mitigates intestinal barrier injury in urosepsis by regulating Nrf2 to inhibit oxidative stress and attenuate inflammation.


## Results

### Screening the common target genes of PIC and Urosepsis
  Figure 1A presented the chemical formula of PIC, and its three-dimensional (3D) structure was illustrated in Fig. 1B. To evaluate the affinity between PIC and the Nrf2 protein, we performed molecular docking analysis using Autodock Vina v.1.2.2. The binding poses and interactions were analyzed, revealing a binding free energy of -6.7 kcal/mol (Fig. 1C). The results demonstrated that PIC binds to the Nrf2 protein through hydrogen bonds and strong electrostatic interactions, while simultaneously occupying its hydrophobic pockets. To elucidate the biological functions of PIC target genes, Gene Ontology(GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses were conducted (Fig. 1D). Similarly, GO and KEGG analyses of urosepsis-related genes were performed (Fig. 1E). A Venn diagram was used to identify overlapping target genes between PIC and urosepsis (Fig. 1F), which may serve as potential therapeutic targets. Collectively, these integrated analyses provide insights into the molecular mechanisms of PIC and its potential role in urosepsis.

### PIC alleviates LPS-induced intestinal barrier injury in NCM460 cells
  We established an LPS-induced NCM460 cell injury model. As shown in Fig. 2A, LPS treatment reduced the viability of NCM460 cells in a dose- and time-dependent manner. Based on these preliminary results, we selected an LPS concentration of 5 µg/mL for all subsequent experiments. To evaluate the protective effect of PIC against infectious intestinal injury, NCM460 cells were pretreated with different concentrations of PIC for 1 h prior to LPS exposure. Cell viability increased with rising PIC concentrations. PIC at 40 µM significantly improved the survival rate of LPS-induced NCM460 cells, but no further enhancement was observed at concentrations above 80 µM (Fig. 2B). To determine whether PIC could preserve intestinal epithelial barrier integrity, we selected PIC concentrations of 20 µM and 40 µM for further experiments. WB analysis was performed to assess the protein expression of TJ components, including ZO-1 and occludin. In the LPS group, the protein levels of ZO-1 and occludin were lower than those in the control group. Compared with the LPS group, the PIC-treated groups exhibited upregulated expression of ZO-1 and occludin, with these effects being dose-dependent (Fig. 2C). The figure shows a cropped Western blot, the original, uncropped blot is shown in Supplementary Material Fig. 2C. Notably, the protein levels of ZO-1 and occludin in the 40 µM PIC group were significantly higher than those in the 20 µM PIC group (P < 0.05), indicating a significant dose-dependent enhancement of the barrier-protective effect of PIC. Thus, we confirmed that PIC maintains intestinal barrier integrity by modulating TJ components. Additionally, we conducted FITC-dextran permeability assays to further validate intestinal epithelial barrier permeability. Consistent with the WB results, LPS significantly increased FITC-dextran permeability compared with the control group. In contrast, PIC treatment exerted a protective effect on the intestinal barrier, counteracting the LPS-induced increase in monolayer NCM460 cell permeability (Fig. 2D). These findings demonstrate that PIC protects intestinal epithelial TJs from LPS-induced disruption.

### PIC attenuates LPS-induced oxidative stress and inflammatory responses in NCM460 cells
  To elucidate the mechanism by which PIC modulates Nrf2 and its downstream protein HO-1, we performed WB analysis. The results demonstrated that LPS treatment significantly suppressed the expression of Nrf2 and HO-1, whereas PIC administration restored their expression in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3A). The figure shows a cropped Western blot, the original, uncropped blot is shown in Supplementary Material Fig. 3A. The upregulation of Nrf2 and HO-1 in the 40 µM PIC group was significantly more pronounced than that in the 20 µM PIC group (P < 0.01). To validate the direct transcriptional activation of Nrf2 by piceatannol (PIC), this study employed immunofluorescence for Nrf2 nuclear translocation and ChIP-qPCR targeting ARE-binding sites. Immunofluorescence revealed that LPS stimulation markedly suppressed Nrf2 nuclear accumulation, whereas PIC treatment dose-dependently enhanced Nrf2 nuclear translocation and fluorescence intensity(Fig. 3B). ChIP-qPCR confirmed that PIC significantly increased Nrf2 binding to ARE promoter regions in a dose-dependent manner, with maximal effect at 40 µM (P < 0.01). These findings demonstrate that PIC directly activates Nrf2 transcriptional activity by promoting its nuclear translocation and enhancing its binding affinity to AREs, providing functional evidence for its core mechanistic role (Fig. 3C). To further investigate the anti-inflammatory effects of PIC, we assessed the protein levels of NF-κB pathway components in LPS-stimulated NCM460 cells. Notably, LPS markedly upregulated the expression of TLR4 and p-P65 compared to the control group, while PIC treatment reversed these changes dose-dependently (Fig. 3D). The figure shows a cropped Western blot, the original, uncropped blot is shown in Supplementary Material Fig. 3D. Meanwhile, 40 µM PIC exerted a significantly stronger inhibitory effect on TLR4 and p-P65 upregulation than 20 µM PIC (P < 0.05). These findings suggest that PIC inhibits LPS-induced downregulation of Nrf2/HO-1 and upregulation of TLR4/p-P65. To validate the impact of PIC on oxidative stress, we employed DHE staining. As expected, LPS treatment significantly increased DHE fluorescence intensity (indicating ROS accumulation), whereas PIC attenuated this effect dose-dependently (Fig. 3E). Subsequent colorimetric assays revealed that LPS reduced SOD and L-Glutathione reduced (GSH) levels while elevating Malondialdehyde (MDA) levels compared to the control. In contrast, PIC dose-dependently restored SOD、GSH activities and suppressed MDA production (Fig. 3F). Consistently, 40 µM PIC significantly restored SOD and GSH activities and inhibited MDA production to a greater extent than 20 µM PIC (P < 0.05).

### PIC mitigates intestinal barrier damage by activating Nrf2 to enhance antioxidant capacity and suppress inflammatory responses
  Since Nrf2 is a key transcription factor regulating cellular antioxidant defense and inflammatory responses, to verify whether PIC exerts its effects through Nrf2 modulation in oxidative stress and inflammation, we pretreated NCM460 cells with the Nrf2 inhibitor ML385 (1 µM) for 1 h, followed by PIC (40 µM) treatment for 1 h, and finally exposed the cells to LPS (5 µg/ml) for 24 h. WB analysis revealed that LPS treatment significantly reduced Nrf2 and HO-1 expression but increased p-P65 levels versus the control group. In contrast, PIC treatment reversed these effects by upregulating Nrf2/HO-1 and downregulating p-P65. However, ML385 abolished the protective effects of PIC, as evidenced by decreased Nrf2/HO-1 and increased p-P65 in the ML385 group versus PIC group (Fig. 4A). The figure shows a cropped Western blot, the original, uncropped blot is shown in Supplementary Material Fig. 4A. Next, intracellular ROS levels were measured using DHE staining. LPS treatment significantly increased ROS production compared to the control group, whereas PIC treatment markedly suppressed this LPS-induced ROS elevation. However, the inhibitory effect of PIC was significantly attenuated by ML385, as evidenced by the substantially higher ROS levels in the ML385 group compared to the PIC group (Fig. 4B). Finally, to determine whether PIC-mediated protection of intestinal epithelial barrier function depends on Nrf2 activation, we examined the expression of ZO-1 and occludin by WB. LPS treatment significantly reduced ZO-1 and occludin expression compared to the control group. PIC treatment effectively attenuated the LPS-induced disruption of intestinal epithelial TJs, as evidenced by the upregulation of ZO-1 and occludin expression. However, this protective effect was abolished by ML385, with the ML385 group showing decreased ZO-1 and occludin expression compared to the PIC group (Fig. 4C). The figure shows a cropped Western blot, the original, uncropped blot is shown in Supplementary Material Fig. 4C.

### PIC protects against intestinal barrier injury in mice with urosepsis by activating Nrf2
  An LPS-induced uroseptic mouse model was established to investigate the protective effect of PIC on intestinal injury. To further confirm that PIC protects the intestinal barrier in uroseptic mice by activating Nrf2, we examined morphological changes and barrier integrity in intestinal tissues using HE staining. The results showed severe intestinal barrier damage in uroseptic mice, including mucosal congestion, epithelial disruption, necrosis, and increased inflammatory cell infiltration. PIC administration mitigated LPS-induced colonic epithelial damage and restored structural integrity. However, ML385 abolished the protective effect of PIC against LPS-induced epithelial injury (Fig. 5A). Additionally, we assessed intestinal permeability by orally administering FITC-dextran, a high-molecular-weight glucose polymer that is not digested or absorbed in healthy mice. The results demonstrated that LPS treatment significantly increased intestinal permeability, as indicated by elevated serum FITC-dextran levels in the LPS group compared with the sham group. In contrast, PIC treatment markedly reduced serum FITC-dextran levels. Notably, ML385 administration reversed the protective effect of PIC, resulting in significantly higher serum FITC-dextran levels than those in the PIC group (Fig. 5B). Finally, we evaluated the expression of TJ proteins ZO-1 and occludin in intestinal epithelial tissues using immunofluorescence. The results revealed that LPS induction severely impaired the expression of ZO-1 and occludin in the mouse colonic epithelium. Treatment with PIC protected TJ complexes from LPS-induced damage, significantly upregulating the expression of ZO-1 and occludin compared to the LPS group. However, ML385 abolished the protective effect of PIC on TJ complexes, resulting in decreased expression of ZO-1 and occludin compared to the PIC group (Fig. 5C). In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that PIC maintains intestinal barrier integrity and protects against LPS-induced intestinal injury in uroseptic mice by activating Nrf2.

### PIC mitigates intestinal oxidative stress and inflammation in uroseptic mice via Nrf2 activation
  To further validate that PIC modulates Nrf2 to influence oxidative stress and inflammation in the intestinal epithelium of mice with urosepsis, we first performed WB to assess the expression of Nrf2, HO-1, TLR4, and p-p65. The results demonstrated that LPS significantly downregulated the expression of Nrf2 and HO-1 in the intestinal epithelium but upregulated TLR4 and p-P65. PIC treatment effectively reversed the LPS-induced suppression of antioxidant capacity and activation of inflammatory pathways. Specifically, WB analysis revealed that, compared to the LPS group, the PIC group showed significantly higher expression of Nrf2 and HO-1 but lower expression of TLR4 and p-P65. However, the therapeutic effect of PIC was partially blocked by ML385, as evidenced by a significant reduction in Nrf2 and HO-1 expression and elevated TLR4 and p-P65 levels in the ML385 group compared to the PIC group (Fig. 6A). The figure shows a cropped Western blot, the original, uncropped blot is shown in Supplementary Material Fig. 6A. Subsequently, ROS in the intestinal epithelium was detected using DCFA-DA staining. Compared to the sham group, LPS treatment markedly increased DCFH-DA fluorescence intensity, whereas PIC administration significantly attenuated this effect. The therapeutic effect of PIC was notably diminished by ML385, as demonstrated by a pronounced resurgence in DCFH-DA levels (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, colorimetric assays were employed to measure SOD, MDA, and GSH levels. The LPS group exhibited lower SOD and GSH levels but higher MDA levels than the sham group. PIC treatment significantly restored SOD and GSH levels while reducing MDA levels compared to the LPS group. However, ML385 reversed the protective effects of PIC, resulting in a decrease in SOD and GSH levels and an increase in MDA levels relative to the PIC group (Fig. 6C). Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was performed to quantify the levels of tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and interleukin-1β (IL-1β) in mouse intestinal tissues. LPS significantly elevated the concentrations of these pro-inflammatory cytokines compared to the sham group, whereas PIC treatment substantially mitigated this effect. The anti-inflammatory effect of PIC was counteracted by ML385, as evidenced by significantly higher TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β levels in the ML385 group than in the PIC group (Fig. 6D). In summary, our findings confirm that PIC alleviates oxidative stress and inflammatory responses in the intestinal epithelium of uroseptic mice by activating Nrf2. To determine whether the local intestinal protective effects of piceatannol against urosepsis can be translated into systemic disease alleviation and improved clinical outcomes, we first assessed two core biomarkers of renal function, serum creatinine (Scr) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN), in the kidney, which is the primary target organ of urosepsis (Fig. 6E). Piceatannol treatment significantly mitigated histopathological damage in renal tissues and decreased the serum levels of Scr and BUN, and these nephroprotective effects were partially reversed by the Nrf2 inhibitor ML385, which is highly consistent with the pathophysiological characteristics of urosepsis. We further quantified the serum levels of TNF-α, IL-6 and interleukin-1β (IL-1β) using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) to evaluate the systemic anti-inflammatory efficacy of piceatannol (Fig. 6F). LPS challenge for urosepsis induction resulted in a marked increase in the serum levels of all three pro-inflammatory cytokines compared with the sham group, which was significantly suppressed by piceatannol administration. This inhibitory effect was partially abrogated by ML385, verifying that the systemic anti-inflammatory activity of piceatannol is primarily dependent on Nrf2 activation. Finally, we performed survival analysis within 72 h after model establishment and recorded the survival outcomes of mice in each group (Fig. 6G). The results showed that the survival rate of the LPS model group decreased rapidly, while piceatannol treatment significantly improved the 72 h survival rate of uroseptic mice (P < 0.01). Notably, co-treatment with ML385 reversed this survival benefit, confirming that piceatannol improves the survival prognosis of uroseptic mice through activating the Nrf2 signaling pathway.

## Discussion
  Urosepsis has an insidious onset but progresses rapidly, with bacteria and their toxins entering the bloodstream within a short period, thereby triggering SIRS37. This process activates immune cells, leading to excessive release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which induce vascular endothelial injury, microcirculatory dysfunction, and tissue hypoxia. Additionally, these inflammatory mediators disrupt intestinal epithelial TJs, increasing intestinal permeability7. The resulting breach of intestinal barrier integrity facilitates microbial translocation, further amplifying systemic inflammation and potentially progressing to multi-organ failure and fatal outcomes3,4,7. In recent years, intestinal barrier injury in sepsis has emerged as a critical trigger of systemic inflammatory responses, a major driver of disease progression, and a key determinant of sepsis-related mortality2. Therefore, preserving intestinal barrier integrity and function represents a crucial therapeutic target. However, managing urosepsis remains challenging due to limited treatment options, often leading to delayed diagnosis, septic shock, and multi-organ failure. Given these limitations, discovering drugs that effectively mitigate urosepsis-induced intestinal barrier damage holds significant clinical promise.

  In this context, piceatannol PIC, a naturally occurring hydroxylated analog of resveratrol, presents a compelling candidate for intervention. Pharmacokinetically, PIC exhibits low oral bioavailability due to rapid phase II metabolism, which is primarily glucuronidation and sulfation, mediated by UDP-glucuronosyltransferases (UGTs), notably UGT1A6, UGT1A7, UGT1A8, and UGT1A938. This extensive first-pass metabolism limits its systemic exposure, suggesting that co-administration with UGT inhibitors or formulation via nanocarriers may be necessary to enhance therapeutic efficacy39. Pharmacodynamically, PIC demonstrates potent multi-target activity, including suppression of NF-κB-driven inflammation, activation of Nrf2-mediated antioxidant responses, inhibition of lipolysis, induction of autophagy via Beclin-1, and modulation of mitochondrial function40–42. These properties position PIC as a pleiotropic modulator capable of simultaneously addressing oxidative stress, inflammation, and cellular homeostasis, which are the key pathological axes in sepsis-induced intestinal barrier dysfunction.

  Persistent LPS exposure causes chronic NF-κB activation, a key mechanism implicated in sepsis, autoimmune disorders, and cancer. Urosepsis is frequently caused by urinary tract infections associated with LPS exposure43. Previous studies have demonstrated that LPS induces intestinal barrier injury in sepsis animal models11,12. To investigate the role of LPS-induced intestinal barrier damage in urosepsis, we established an in vitro model using LPS-treated NCM460 cells. Additionally, we developed an in vivo mouse model of urosepsis by injecting LPS into the renal pelvis, thereby simulating intestinal barrier injury at the animal level.

  The TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway is critically involved in mediating inflammatory processes44. LPS activates the NF-κB signaling pathway through TLR4, leading to p65 phosphorylation. Phosphorylated p65 (p-p65) translocates into the nucleus, where it initiates the transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6), thereby modulating the inflammatory response15. Moreover, LPS impairs the antioxidant defense system, leading to the accumulation of ROS and inducing oxidative stress. The exacerbation of oxidative stress, in turn, further upregulates the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Excessive pro-inflammatory cytokines disrupt intestinal epithelial TJs, thereby compromising barrier integrity and increasing intestinal permeability7,18. Downregulation of TLR4/NF-κB signaling attenuates inflammatory processes and upregulates the expression of intestinal TJs. This helps maintain intestinal barrier integrity and mitigate sepsis45. Our results showed that LPS significantly upregulated TLR4 and p-p65 expression in both NCM460 cells and mouse intestinal tissues, along with markedly elevated levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6. Meanwhile, LPS downregulated SOD and GSH levels while promoting the accumulation of ROS and MDA. Concurrently, LPS downregulated the expression of ZO-1 and occludin, ultimately increasing intestinal permeability in NCM460 monolayer cells and murine intestinal barrier. These findings align with previous studies.

  Stilbene compounds are a group of phenolic substances widely found in plants such as grapes, passion fruit, and peanuts. They possess various biological activities, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and cancer-preventive effects. Among them, PIC is considered to exhibit relatively prominent bioactivity20,46. Resveratrol exerts a protective effect against LPS-induced intestinal barrier injury in sepsis via the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway47. As a metabolite of resveratrol, PIC demonstrates superior antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties compared to resveratrol itself. Venn diagrams predicted an association between PIC and urosepsis. Recent research has reported that PIC confers protection against sepsis-induced myocardial dysfunction and hepatic injury29,30. We evaluated the therapeutic effects of PIC on intestinal barrier injury induced by urosepsis using both cellular and animal models. Meng et al. reported that stilbene compounds protect intestinal epithelial barrier function through multiple mechanisms, including enhancing intestinal epithelial immune responses, regulating metabolic pathways, maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis, and modulating gut microbiota composition31. In our experiments, LPS significantly disrupted intestinal TJs and increased barrier permeability. In contrast, PIC treatment alleviated LPS-induced epithelial damage and reduced permeability, ultimately protecting the intestinal barrier. Furthermore, in a murine model of urosepsis, PIC administration significantly ameliorated intestinal mucosal congestion and edema, restored the integrity of the intestinal mucosal barrier, and repaired disrupted intestinal TJs. Consequently, intestinal permeability was substantially reduced. These findings suggest that PIC exerts therapeutic effects against urosepsis, likely mediated by its ability to enhance intestinal epithelial barrier function.

  Regarding human applicability, current evidence on PIC’s safety and efficacy remains largely preclinical. Clinical trials involving standardized Cyperus rotundus extract that contains PIC alongside scirpusins have demonstrated favorable safety profiles in obese populations, with no reported adverse events over 90-day interventions and improvements in anthropometric and lipid parameters48,49. Allergenic potential appears low, as intravenous piceatannol-3′-O-β-D-glucopyranoside did not elicit histamine release or allergic reactions in murine or RBL-2H3 cell models50,51. However, PIC’s UGT-inhibitory activity raises concerns regarding potential food-drug interactions. Co-administration with drugs primarily metabolized by UGT1A6 to UGT1A9, including imipramine, propofol and mycophenolic acid, may elevate plasma concentrations of these agents and increase toxicity risk38. Furthermore, PIC’s interaction with Ginkgo Amillaria oral solution through UGT1A9 inhibition has been shown to significantly increase PIC’s Area Under the Curve (AUC) and half-life in vivo, suggesting clinically relevant pharmacokinetic modulation when combined with herbal medicines51. While PIC shows no overt organ toxicity in animal models, comprehensive human safety data including dose-limiting toxicities, long-term tolerability, and reproductive safety are lacking. Thus, cautious translation to clinical sepsis management is warranted pending further Phase I/II trials.

  Molecular docking predicted that PIC likely exerts its effects via the Nrf2 pathway. As the core regulator of antioxidant responses, Nrf2 is tightly regulated at transcriptional, translational, and post-translational levels52. Previous studies have shown that PIC induces HO-1 expression in human endothelial cells and mammary epithelial cells by activating Nrf253,54, and suppresses NF-κB pathway activation in mouse osteoarthritis through the Nrf2/HO-1 axis55. Specifically, PIC upregulates Nrf2 and HO-1 expression while promoting Nrf2 nuclear translocation, thereby enhancing the transcription of antioxidant enzymes and mitigating oxidative stress23. Under physiological conditions, Nrf2 is stabilized by binding to Keap1. Upon activation, it dissociates from Keap1, translocates into the nucleus, and binds to AREs to initiate the transcription of HO-1 and other antioxidant enzymes23. Functioning as a central regulatory hub, Nrf2 coordinately modulates HO-1 and classical antioxidant enzymes. HO-1 bolsters cellular antioxidant defenses through its metabolic products, whereas canonical antioxidant enzymes directly scavenge ROS23–25. The Nrf2/HO-1 pathway, an upstream regulator of NF-κB, has emerged as a promising therapeutic target for inflammatory diseases51. Mechanistically, Nrf2/HO-1 signaling inhibits NF-κB-mediated inflammation by blocking p65 nuclear translocation26,27.

  This crosstalk between Nrf2 and NF-κB is particularly relevant in the context of intestinal barrier injury during sepsis. Prior studies in septic models confirm that activation of the Nrf2/ROS/NF-κB axis is pivotal. Oxidative stress through ROS accumulation triggers NF-κB nuclear translocation, amplifying pro-inflammatory cytokine release and TJ degradation, while Nrf2 activation counteracts this by quenching ROS and suppressing NF-κB nuclear import56–60. For instance, Radix Sanguisorbae protected septic rats by activating Hypoxia-Inducible Factor-1α (HIF-1α)/HO-1 to reduce ferroptosis and barrier leakage61. Similarly, Schisanhenol rescued intestinal epithelial cells from Mycophenolic Acid-induced damage via Nrf2/HO-1 upregulation60. PIC mirrors this mechanism. It disrupts Keap1-Nrf2 binding, stabilizes Nrf2, enhances its nuclear translocation, and subsequently induces HO-1 and endogenous antioxidants SOD and GSH, thereby dampening ROS levels and interrupting the pathological cascade in which ROS accumulation drives NF-κB activation, promotes proinflammatory cytokine release, and ultimately leads to intestinal barrier disruption59. Importantly, ML385-mediated Nrf2 inhibition reversed PIC’s protective effects in both cellular and murine models, confirming mechanistic dependency on this pathway. Thus, PIC’s efficacy in urosepsis-associated intestinal injury is not merely antioxidative or anti-inflammatory in isolation, but stems from its ability to recalibrate the Nrf2/ROS/NF-κB signaling equilibrium, which is a master regulatory node previously validated in multiple sepsis-related organ injuries41,59,62. Consistent with these findings, our study demonstrates that PIC significantly attenuates LPS-induced ROS accumulation and MDA upregulation, while downregulating TLR4 expression, suppressing NF-κB activation (p-p65), and reducing proinflammatory cytokine levels in NCM460 cells and mouse intestinal tissues. Concurrently, PIC upregulates the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway, increases SOD activity, and elevates GSH levels. These data suggest that PIC protects against LPS-induced intestinal barrier dysfunction primarily by activating the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway, thereby coordinately suppressing oxidative stress and inflammatory responses.

  To validate the pivotal role of Nrf2 in PIC’s therapeutic effects against urosepsis-associated intestinal injury, we employed the Nrf2 inhibitor ML385. In vitro experiments confirmed that PIC alleviated oxidative stress and inflammation through Nrf2 activation. However, ML385-induced Nrf2 inhibition reversed the protective effects of PIC against LPS-induced oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and epithelial barrier disruption in NCM460 cells. Further substantiating Nrf2’s central role, a murine urosepsis model demonstrated that PIC administration significantly ameliorated LPS-induced intestinal structural damage, restored barrier function, and attenuated oxidative stress and inflammation in intestinal tissues. Notably, pharmacological inhibition of Nrf2 by ML385 partially abolished the beneficial effects of PIC on intestinal barrier injury, oxidative stress, and inflammation in uroseptic mice.

  This study systematically elucidates the molecular mechanism by which PIC alleviates urinary sepsis associated intestinal barrier injury through the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway. The conclusions of this work are subject to certain limitations. All experimental data were derived exclusively from LPS induced cellular and murine models. The capacity of PIC to activate the Nrf2 pathway and its intestinal protective effects have not yet been validated in actual clinical sepsis patients. Existing clinical studies indicate that Cyperus rotundus extracts containing PIC demonstrate favorable safety profiles in obese populations. Serum levels of NRF2 and HO-1 have been confirmed to possess predictive value for renal protection in sepsis patients63. However, the pharmacodynamic characteristics of PIC in human sepsis, the optimal therapeutic window, and its dose response relationship remain undefined. The pathophysiology of sepsis involves multi organ crosstalk and regulation across multiple signaling axes, including the α7 Nicotinic Acetylcholine Receptor (α7nAChR) mediated cholinergic anti inflammatory pathway and the HIF 1α regulated ferroptosis pathway18. This study focused solely on the Nrf2 pathway. Whether PIC interacts synergistically or antagonistically with other pathways requires further investigation. Although ML385 mediated inhibition experiments confirmed Nrf2 as the central node for PIC’s pharmacological action, this inhibitor may still influence other Keap1 independent pathways. Future studies should employ Nrf2 gene knockout animal models to exclude potential off target effects. This study elucidates the molecular mechanism by which piceatannol alleviates urosepsis-associated intestinal barrier injury via activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway. However, it does not directly investigate interactions between piceatannol and the gut microbiome, a critical mediator of polyphenol bioactivity in vivo. Published evidence indicates that structurally related stilbenoid polyphenols can reshape dysbiotic microbiota during sepsis, enriching beneficial genera such as Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, and Akkermansia muciniphila, thereby enhancing short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production. SCFAs synergistically activate the Nrf2 antioxidant axis while suppressing NF-κB-driven inflammation, aligning with and potentiating the core mechanism identified here. Moreover, gut microbial metabolism may enhance piceatannol’s oral bioavailability and systemic anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects.

  In murine models, the effective dose of piceatannol cannot be directly translated into a feasible dietary intake for humans. Current research prioritizes three strategies to enhance its clinical translatability: formulation optimization, structural derivatization, and combination therapies, aiming to resolve the core conflict between low bioavailability and high-dose requirements. Targeted delivery systems have demonstrated enhanced therapeutic efficacy with reduced dosing; for instance, a colon-targeted system in rat colitis models improved anti-inflammatory outcomes while circumventing hepatic first-pass metabolism64. Soni et al. encapsulated piceatannol in cubic liquid crystalline nanoparticles, enhancing stability and demonstrating superior antioxidant and neuroprotective effects in PC-12 neuronal cells65. Chemical or biological modifications also improve stability and bioactivity: microbial-derived methyl glucoside derivatives exhibit stronger GPR119 agonism and insulinotropic activity66, while trimethylated and prenylated analogs show increased metabolic stability and potency67. Synergistic combinations with everolimus, polymyxin B, or cisplatin enable efficacy at lower doses in gastric cancer, bacterial infection, and breast cancer models, respectively42,68. Although high-dose requirements in animals limit direct use as a dietary supplement, multidimensional approaches are expanding its potential as a clinical candidate. Future priorities include human pharmacokinetic validation, long-term safety profiling, and precise indication matching to facilitate clinical translation.

  Based on the current research foundation and existing limitations, subsequent investigations may advance along multiple dimensions. Prospective Phase I/II clinical trials should be conducted to evaluate the safety, pharmacokinetic profile, and impact of PIC on intestinal permeability, inflammatory biomarkers, and Nrf2 pathway activity in sepsis patients. These trials must pay particular attention to the risk of drug interactions between PIC and commonly used UGT metabolized agents such as propofol and mycophenolic acid. At the mechanistic level, further exploration should assess whether PIC enhances intestinal barrier function by modulating gut microbiota host immune interactions, mitochondrial autophagy, or endoplasmic reticulum stress. Similar regulatory mechanisms have been partially validated in studies of analogous natural compounds including paeoniflorin69, benzoic acid microcapsules70, and resveratrol71. In terms of pharmaceutical development, targeted delivery systems such as nanocarriers or enteric coatings may be engineered to enhance the local bioavailability of PIC in the intestinal tract while minimizing systemic exposure and potential drug interactions. Although ML385-mediated inhibition confirms Nrf2 as a central node in piceatannol’s pharmacological action, potential off-target effects on Keap1-independent pathways cannot be excluded; future studies should employ Nrf2-knockout models for validation. Additionally, direct modulation of the gut microbiome by piceatannol in uroseptic mice remains unverified due to the absence of 16 S rRNA sequencing or fecal microbiota transplantation experiments. Subsequent research will utilize germ-free models and multi-omics integration to systematically delineate piceatannol’s regulatory effects on microbial ecology and metabolite profiles.

  In summary, PIC as an Nrf2 pathway activator demonstrates clear therapeutic potential for protecting the intestinal barrier in sepsis. Its clinical translation must bridge the translational gap from rodent models to human patients. The current mechanistic evidence provides theoretical support for establishing precision intervention strategies.

### Rationale for Dose Selection
  The PIC dose used in this study was selected based on prior in vitro and in vivo research data, as well as the physiological relevance of the compound in humans. PIC is a polyphenolic compound naturally found in various fruits and vegetables, possessing antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities. We referenced multiple studies evaluating the effects of different PIC doses in cellular and animal models. Chang et al.72 demonstrated that 40 µM PIC significantly attenuated benzoapyrene-induced DNA damage in human colonic epithelial cells. Thus, we chose PIC concentrations of 20 µM and 40 µM for in vitro experiments. Xie et al.44 reported that 20 mg/kg PIC significantly protected mice against sepsis-induced myocardial dysfunction. Additionally, Hu et al.46 showed that 20 mg/kg PIC markedly alleviated intestinal injury in uremic mice. Therefore, we selected a PIC dose of 20 mg/kg for in vivo experiments. The physiologically relevant dose of PIC in humans can be estimated from food intake. Per prior research, daily dietary PIC intake in humans typically ranges from 10 to 50 mg20. To ensure physiological relevance of our dose selection, we converted the 20 mg/kg PIC dose to a human equivalent dose (HED). Dose conversion from animal models to HED commonly uses the following framework:

  

  For mice, the body surface area factor is approximately 0.007 m²/kg, while for humans, it is approximately 0.025 m²/kg. Thus, a mouse dose of 20 mg/kg converts to a HED of approximately 71.43 mg/day. Per food databases, approximately 150 g of grapes contain roughly 1–2 mg of PIC. Thus, to achieve a HED of 71.43 mg/day, a person would need to consume roughly 35–70 cups of grapes daily. Clearly, this is not realistic. Therefore, our dose selection was based not only on physiological relevance but also considered practical applicability.


### Cell culture conditions and treatments
  The NCM460 cell line was obtained from Guangzhou Ren Tai Biotechnology Corporation (Guangzhou, China). The cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Chang et al. reported thar PIC showed no significant cytotoxicity toward NCM460 cells72. For experiments, NCM460 cells were treated with LPS at varying doses for 6, 12, or 24 h. For pretreatment, cells were incubated with different concentrations of PIC for 1 h prior to stimulation with LPS (5 µg/mL). To investigate whether the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects of PIC were mediated through Nrf2 activation, cells were pretreated with the Nrf2 inhibitor ML385 (1 µM) for 1 h before PIC administration.


### Cell viability assay
  The CCK8 assay (DOJINDO, Guangzhou, China) was used to evaluate cell viability. NCM460 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10³ cells per well and incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, the test compounds were added. At designated time points, 10 µL of CCK8 solution was added to each well. The cell suspension was gently agitated to ensure homogeneity and further incubated for 4 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was then measured using a microplate reader (ThermoFisher, USA).


### Western blot (WB) analysis
  Proteins from NCM460 cells and murine intestinal tissues were homogenized in chilled lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. After protein extraction, concentrations were quantified using a BCA assay kit (Macklin, Shanghai, China). A total of 30 µg of total protein from each sample was loaded separated by SDS-PAGE electrophoresis at 120 V for 40 min. Subsequently, the proteins were transferred onto a PVDF membrane via electroblotting at 200 mA for 90 min. The membrane was blocked with skim milk for 2 h, followed by incubation with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer at 4 °C overnight. After washing, the membrane was incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody prepared in blocking buffer. Target proteins were finally visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate kit (Macklin, Shanghai, China), and the gray value of protein bands was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, USA) as previously reported80,81.


### FITC-dextran permeability assays
  In vitro intestinal barrier permeability was assessed as previously described75. NCM460 cells were seeded in 12-mm Transwell® inserts (0.4-µm porous polycarbonate membrane, 12-well plate) at 2 × 10⁵ cells/mL in the apical compartment, while the basolateral chamber contained fresh medium supplemented with 1% nonessential amino acids. After 10 days of incubation, a confluent monolayer was formed. Test compounds were then added to the apical chamber. To evaluate intestinal barrier integrity, FITC-dextran (MW 4000) was applied to the apical side for 2 h, and fluorescence intensity was measured at 485/528 nm.

  For in vivo assessment of intestinal permeability, the assay was performed according to a previously validated protocol76. For in vivo assessment, mice received FITC-dextran via intragastric gavage. Blood samples were collected 4 h post-administration, and serum was isolated. Aliquots (200 µL) of serum were transferred to a black 96-well plate, and fluorescence was quantified at 488/525 nm using a microplate reader.


### ROS probe detection
  Intracellular ROS production was detected using DHE staining as previously described77. NCM460 cells were plated in 6-well plates and allowed to adhere for 24 h. After drug treatment, the cells were incubated with 10 µM DHE staining solution (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 30 min, followed by two washes with PBS.

  Intracellular ROS production was assessed using a fluorescence imaging system (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). For intestinal tissue analysis, freshly isolated samples were fixed, embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) medium, and rapidly frozen. Section (10 μm thick) were prepared and stained with 10 µM 2’,7’-Dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) solution according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After incubation at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min, ROS levels were measured by fluorescence microscopy, with fluorescence intensity quantified by ImageJ software78,79.


### Measurements of SOD, MDA and GSH levels
  We measured the levels of superoxide dismutase (SOD), malondialdehyde (MDA), and GSH in NCM460 cells and murine intestinal tissues using commercially available assay kits (Solarbio, Beijing, China), strictly following the manufacturer’s instructions and previously validated protocols80–82.


### Blinding implementation
  All experiments in this study were performed in a single-blinded manner to eliminate subjective bias. The grouping and administration of experimental animals were completed by researchers who did not participate in the subsequent data collection and result analysis. Two independent researchers who were blinded to the grouping information completed the following assessments: histopathological evaluation of intestinal sections, quantitative analysis of WB band gray values, immunofluorescence fluorescence intensity measurement, biochemical index detection (SOD, MDA, GSH), and ELISA cytokine quantification. The final results were averaged from the two independent assessments, and inconsistent results were re-evaluated by a third senior researcher blinded to the grouping.


### Animal experiment design
  To establish a urosepsis mouse model, the following procedures are performed: After anesthesia with pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg, intraperitoneal), mice are placed in a right lateral position on a thermostatic surgical platform. A 1 cm incision is made parallel to the left subcostal margin along the paravertebral region to expose the left kidney. The surrounding adipose tissue is dissected, and the renal pelvis and ureter are carefully isolated. The upper ureter is clamped with a microvascular clip. LPS (10 mg/kg) is then injected into the renal pelvis using a 33-gauge, 25 µL microsyringe. After 10 min, the clip is removed, and the incision is sutured.

  The Ethics Committee for Animal Welfare at China Medical University evaluated and approved the procedures used in this study (Approval No. CMUXN2023102). Male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of China Medical University (Shenyang, China). The mice were housed under controlled conditions at 25 ± 1 °C, with 50% humidity and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. After a 7-day acclimatization period, they were randomly divided into four experimental groups (n = 6 per group): (1) Sham group: Mice underwent surgery without LPS injection. (2) LPS group: Mice underwent surgery and received a LPS (10 mg/kg) injection into the left renal pelvis. (3) LPS + PIC group: LPS-induced model mice received daily intraperitoneal injections of PIC (20 mg/kg), with the first dose administered 1 h before LPS exposure. (4) LPS + PIC+ML385 group: Mice received ML385 (20 mg/kg, intraperitoneal) 1 h before PIC treatment.

  During the experimental period, all mice underwent standardized, phase-based behavioral and health status monitoring. In the acute phase (0–24 h post-modeling), observations were conducted every 2 h. From 24 h post-modeling until study termination, assessments occurred every 12 h. Parameters recorded included body weight fluctuation, mental status, spontaneous locomotor activity, food and water intake, stool consistency, and sepsis-associated clinical signs such as piloerection, shivering, hunched posture, dyspnea, and surgical incision healing. All observational procedures were performed by two researchers blinded to group allocation using a single-blind protocol. Mice meeting predefined humane endpoint criteria were immediately euthanized in accordance with ethical guidelines.

  Humane endpoints were pre-defined in strict accordance with the regulations of the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of China Medical University. The pre-defined humane endpoints include the following manifestations: (1) persistent coma or unresponsiveness to external stimuli; (2) inability to eat or drink for more than 12 h; (3) severe dyspnea or cyanosis; (4) body temperature drop of more than 4 °C below the normal range; (5) severe abdominal distension with no defecation for more than 24 h; (6) self-mutilation or severe motor impairment. Mice that met any of the above criteria were immediately euthanized by pentobarbital sodium overdose (150 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection) to avoid unnecessary pain and distress. During the entire experiment, no mice reached the pre-defined humane endpoints before the preset experimental endpoint, and all animals were euthanized humanely at the end of the experimental observation period in accordance with the approved protocol.

  Pre-experiments showed that PIC at 20 mg/kg exerted a significant protective effect against urosepsis-induced intestinal injury in mice, and no significant additional benefit was observed at a higher dose of 40 mg/kg. Thus, 20 mg/kg was selected as the optimal dose for in vivo experiments to ensure the stability of the model and avoid potential non-specific effects of high-dose drugs. Drug doses were determined based on literature references35,36 and experimental optimization.


### HE staining
  To assess the morphological composition and structural integrity of the intestinal mucosal barrier, HE staining was performed as previously described83. Upon euthanasia after experiment termination, distal colon segments were excised, gently flushed with pre-cooled PBS to remove intestinal contents, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. The fixed specimens were then paraffin-embedded and sliced into 3 serial 4 μm-thick sections per sample for HE staining using conventional protocols. For each section, 5 non-overlapping visual fields were randomly selected under a 20× bright-field microscope for histological evaluation and image acquisition. Histological damage was scored by two blinded independent pathologists based on the degree of epithelial damage, inflammatory cell infiltration, and mucosal structural destruction.


### Immunofluorescence staining
  Immunofluorescence staining was performed according to a previously validated protocol. Mouse distal colon tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, dehydrated in 30% sucrose solution for 48 h at 4 °C, and embedded in OCT compound. Serial 10-µm-thick frozen sections were prepared, with 3 sections obtained per intestinal sample. After washing with PBS, the sections were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 15 min and blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. The sections were then incubated with primary antibodies against ZO-1 and occludin at 4 °C overnight. After PBS washing, the sections were incubated with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min and slides were mounted with ProLong Gold antifade mountant. For each section, 5 non-overlapping visual fields were randomly selected under a 20× laser scanning confocal microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) for image acquisition, and fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software by two blinded independent researchers.


### ELISA
  Mouse intestinal tissues were weighed and homogenized in pre-cooled PBS, then centrifuged at 4 °C for 15 min to collect the supernatant. Protein concentrations were determined by BCA assay, and samples were diluted to a uniform concentration. TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β levels were quantified using commercial ELISA kits (Solarbio, Beijing, China). Cytokine concentrations (pg/mg of protein) were calculated from the standard curve.


### Molecular docking
  To analyze the binding affinities and interaction modes between the drug candidate and its targets, molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina 1.2.2, a computational protein–ligand docking software. The molecular structure of PIC was retrieved from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), while the 3D coordinates of Nrf2 were obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB; http://www.rcsb.org/). For docking analysis, all protein and ligand files were converted into the PDBQT format after removing water molecules and adding polar hydrogen atoms. A grid box with dimensions of 30Å×30Å×30Å was centered to encompass the binding domain of each protein, allowing for unrestricted ligand movement. The grid point spacing was set to 0.05 nm. The docking simulations were conducted using AutoDock Vina 1.2.2 (http://autodock.scripps.edu/).


### Gene ontology and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis
  To explore the biological functions of the identified targets, we conducted GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were analyzed using the DAVID database, with the whole human genome as the background. For GO analysis, terms were classified into biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF). Statistically significant terms (p-value < 0.05, FDR < 0.1) were selected, and the top 10 enriched terms in each category were further analyzed. Similarly, KEGG pathways with p-value < 0.05 and FDR < 0.1 were considered significantly enriched.


### Venn diagrams
  Potential therapeutic targets were identified by analyzing overlaps between compound-predicted targets and disease-related targets (sourced from GeneCards, DrugBank, OMIM, and DisGeNET) using an online Venn diagram tool.
