# Direct amidinium–phosphate interactions guide AT-selective DNA binding by a potent antileishmanial drug


## Abstract
  Neglected tropical diseases caused by kinetoplastid parasites remain a major global health challenge, with current chemotherapies offering limited efficacy and safety. JNII40 is a promising bis(pyridine-2-carboxamidine) ligand with potent antiparasitic activity and selectivity in vivo against Leishmania parasites. The compound specifically targets AT-rich regions of DNA, predominant in the mitochondrion of these parasites. We used NMR spectroscopy to determine the high-resolution structure of a DNA duplex containing four consecutive AT repeats in complex with JNII40. The structure reveals that JNII40 binds deeply into the DNA minor groove through a network of direct and water-mediated hydrogen bonds, including direct interactions between amidine protons and DNA phosphate oxygens. These contacts, supported by extensive intermolecular NOEs, explain the high affinity and sequence selectivity toward AT-rich regions observed by calorimetry and NMR. Restrained molecular dynamics simulations highlight the critical role of two ordered water molecules that bridge the ligand and DNA, preserving the geometry of the complex. Ligand insertion induces a noticeable narrowing of the minor groove. Altogether, these findings provide a structural framework for the rational drug design of selective DNA-targeting molecules against kinetoplastid parasites such as Leishmania.

  


## Introduction
  Kinetoplastid parasites such as Trypanosoma brucei, Trypanosoma cruzi, and Leishmania account for some of the most devastating neglected tropical diseases (NTDs) causing tremendous suffering and disability to millions of people worldwide1. Most treatments for these NTDs (i.e., human African trypanosomiasis, Chagas disease, and leishmaniasis, respectively) involve long and high dosage (mostly parenteral) regimen associated with serious side effects2–4. Additionally, most of these treatments lack the potency to completely eliminate parasites. Therefore, there is an urgent need for safe, effective, and short-course treatments for these diseases.

  A promising therapeutic strategy against these parasites lies in targeting the kinetoplast, a distinctive organelle that contains mitochondrial DNA (kDNA) and is composed of catenated maxicircles and minicircles that are exceptionally AT-rich5,6. This difference from the host genome offers an opportunity for the design of minor-groove binders (MGBs) that are selective for these parasites7–11. We have shown earlier that the bis(2-aminoimidazoline) compound FR60 (Fig. 1a)12, an AT-specific DNA MGB that acts directly on the kinetoplast of T. brucei by altering the structure and replication of kDNA13, was 100% curative in a mouse model of first stage African trypanosomiasis by oral dosage14. However, FR60 was less efficient against the intracellular parasites T. cruzi and Leishmania, despite kDNA being a common target of these parasites. Structure activity relationship (SAR) studies allowed the discovery of the bis(pyridine-2-carboxamidine) analogue JNII40 (Fig. 1a), a potent submicromolar inhibitor of T. brucei, T. cruzi and Leishmania growth10,15 that binds to the minor groove of DNA and shows selectivity for AT-rich sequences. In vivo pharmacokinetic16,17 and efficacy studies (unpublished) showed that JNII40 is a promising antileishmanial compound.Fig. 1Structures of FR60 and JNII40 and synthetic route to JNII40.a Chemical structures of the minor groove binders FR60 and JNII40. b Multigram (5 g) scale synthesis of JNII40.

  In this study, we present high-resolution NMR data for the complex formed by JNII40 and a DNA duplex containing four consecutive AT repeats [(AT)4]. We quantified binding affinity and determined the structure of the complex via bidimensional NMR. High-resolution structure shows key DNA-ligand contacts, including direct amidine–phosphate interactions, elucidating the structural basis for its affinity and specificity toward the minor groove of AT-rich DNA duplexes. In addition, restrained molecular dynamics (rMD) simulations unveil the critical role of water-mediated interactions in stabilizing the complex. Finally, we report an optimized synthetic procedure that enables the high-yielding preparation of JNII40 on a multigram scale, which will be useful while the compound advances through further preclinical studies.


## Results and discussion

### Synthesis of JNII40
  Compound JNII40 was synthesized via the intermediate S-(2-naphthylmethyl)-2-pyridylthioimidate hydrobromide18 (1) as shown in Fig. 1b. Importantly, an improved 88% yield of JNII40 was achieved when the formation of the bis(pyridin-2-carboxamidine) derivative was performed in DMF instead of EtOH/CH3CN mixtures (23%) as have been previously used for pyridin-2-carboxamidine synthesis19–21. This protocol allowed to prepare JNII40 on a multigram scale with 44% overall yield without the need of chromatography in any of the purification steps (see Methods section).


### Thermodynamics of JNII40–DNA binding

### NMR analysis of JNII40–DNA interaction
  The binding of JNII40 to the (AT)4 oligonucleotide was monitored by focusing on the interchangeable region of the 1D NMR spectra, as shown in Fig. 2. The spectrum of free DNA displays exchangeable signals between 12 and 14 ppm corresponding to the eight thymine and two guanine imino protons engaged in canonical A:T and G:C base pairs stabilizing the (AT)4 duplex. Owing to the duplex symmetry, these appear as five distinct signals, each integrating two protons (Fig. 2). Upon titration with JNII40, significant chemical shift changes are observed in the imino signals of T3, T5, and T7, with a smaller but noticeable shift in T9. In contrast, the imino proton signal of G10 remains mostly unaffected. By comparison, titration of JNII40 with the duplex (CG)4 does not produce any significant change in the imino region of the NMR spectra (Supplementary Fig 2). However, two well defined signals near 9 ppm that increase in intensity proportionally to ligand concentration are observed in the 1D spectra of both titrated (AT)4 and (CG)4. These signals are attributed to the JNII40 aromatic protons adjacent to the nitrogen atoms of the pyridine rings. In the free ligand and in the (CG)4 complex, these resonances are separated by only a small chemical-shift difference (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig 2), likely reflecting subtle electronic effects within the conjugated JNII40 scaffold. In contrast, upon binding to (AT)4, this separation becomes markedly larger (Fig. 2), consistent with the two pyridine rings experiencing distinct local chemical environments in the complex. Additionally, around 9.5 ppm, broad signals corresponding to interchangeable protons of JNII40 are only observed in the spectra of titrated (AT)4 (Fig. 2). ### Structure and binding mode of the (AT)4–JNII40 complex
  Based on the experimental constraints obtained from the NMR spectra (Supplementary Table 5), we determined the structure of the (AT)4:JNII40 complex using restrained molecular dynamics (rMD). Two representative views of the final structure (PDB ID: 9SQC) are shown in Figs. 6a, b. The resulting ensemble (Fig. 6c) displays low overall energy values, with no average distance violation exceeding 0.29 Å (Supplementary Table 5). All residues are well defined, with global RMSD values of approximately 0.6 Å (Supplementary Table 5).Fig. 6Structure and binding mode of the (AT)4–JNII40 complex.a Representative solution structure of the (AT)4:JNII40 complex (PDB 9SQC) showing the DNA surface and ligand bound to the minor groove (sticks). Hydrogen-bond network stabilizing DNA–ligand interactions around the Py-N (e) and Py-O (f) pyridine rings.

  The structure of the (AT)4:JNII40 complex reveals the ligand bound within the DNA minor groove, spanning approximately four residues from each strand: A4–A8 on strand A and T5–T9 on strand B (Fig. 6a, b). This binding site agrees with the pronounced chemical-shift changes observed for the minor-groove protons (H1′ and H4′) of residues T3–T9 (Supplementary Fig 3 and 6), whereas C1, A2, and G10 remain unaffected. Consistently, analysis of the groove width shows a marked narrowing upon ligand binding (Supplementary Fig 7), further supporting JNII40 insertion into the minor groove. This induced-fit adjustment, facilitated by the intrinsic DNA flexibility, stabilizes the complex through optimized van der Waals and hydrogen-bonding interactions, in line with previous studies on minor-groove binders23–25.

  The central amide proton of the ligand is positioned at hydrogen-bonding distance from the O2 of T7 in chain B (Fig. 6d), resembling the interaction between the amide proton of FR60 with the O2 of T4 observed in the crystal structure of the complex with the d(AAATTT)2 duplex13. The observation of this proton signal at 9.51 ppm in the 1D spectra (Fig. 2), together with several NOEs with both (AT)4 and JNII40 protons (Fig. 4), indicates its participation in a hydrogen bond that slows solvent exchange and renders it visible in NMR spectra. The two aryl rings flanking the amide group adopt an almost coplanar arrangement (Fig. 6d), consistent with the intense cross-peaks observed between the ligand amide proton and neighboring aryl protons H8 and H11 (Fig. 4).

  The terminal pyridine rings also fit within the minor groove, although they behave differently: Py-N displays greater conformational flexibility, adopting alternative orientations across the groove, while Py-O assumes a more defined position (Fig. 6c). The pyridine rings do not form hydrogen-bonding interactions with nearby DNA atoms. Similarly, in the crystal structure of a related arylimidamide (DB1880) with terminal pyridinyl substituents, these rings do not contact DNA directly, although one interacts with a water molecule hydrogen-bonded to a phosphate group26. In contrast, the terminal imidazoline rings of FR60 engage in hydrogen bonds with base heteroatoms, interfacial water molecules and, in some cases, phosphate groups through the crystal lattice13. However, it should be noted that the DNA sequence in both DB1880 and FR60 complexes are different from the alternating AT sequence used for JNII40, which can influence minor groove geometry and ligand accommodation27,28.

  The rMD simulations reveal two water molecules stably located at the DNA–ligand interface, mediating interactions between the amidine NH groups and DNA bases. Each water oxygen accepts hydrogen bonds from ligand NH groups, while its hydrogens interact with DNA heteroatoms. The water molecule near Py-N occupies two main positions: one bridging consecutive residues of strand B (N3 of A8 and O4′ of T9) (Fig. 6e), and another connecting both strands (N3 of A4 in strand A and N3 of A8 in strand B) (Supplementary Fig 5a). The water molecule close to Py-O samples have three alternative orientations that mediate ligand–DNA contacts: (i) O4′ of A8 and O2 of T7 (Fig. 6f), (ii) O2 of T7 and N3 of A6 (Supplementary Fig 5b), and (iii) O2 and O4′ of T7 (Supplementary Fig 5c), all from strand B. Monitoring the distances between these water oxygens and the interacting atoms throughout the rMD trajectories shows that the Py-N–associated water alternates between its two main sites, whereas the Py-O–associated one remains more confined, only sporadically visiting alternative positions (Supplementary Fig 8). Such ordered interfacial waters act as linkers within the hydrogen-bond network, critically contributing to complex stabilization and specificity of minor-groove recognition, a role that has also been reported in other minor-groove ligand complexes13,29–34.

  A distinctive feature of the binding mode is the orientation of the NH2⁺ protons from the amidine groups which are arranged nearly perpendicular to the pyridine ring plane. This geometry favors hydrogen-bond formation with backbone phosphate oxygens facing the minor groove. Specifically, one of the NH2⁺ protons adjacent to Py-N forms hydrogen bonds with the phosphate oxygen of A6 in chain A (Fig. 6e) and alternatively with that of T9 in chain B, oscillating between both positions during the dynamics (Fig. 6c). In the case of the NH2⁺ group adjacent to Py-O, hydrogen bonding is observed either with the phosphate of T7 in strand B (Fig. 6f) or with that of A8 in strand A (Fig. 6c). These direct interactions between the cationic amidine groups and the anionic phosphate oxygens, which were predicted in previous docking studies with the d(TTAA)2 sequence35, introduce an additional electrostatic stabilization component that complements the network of conventional hydrogen bonds and van der Waals contacts within the groove. Interestingly, in most known minor groove binders, the amidine groups form hydrogen bonds with base or sugar atoms located at the groove floor36–38. Hydrogen bonds between minor groove ligands and DNA phosphate oxygens have been reported only rarely and, when present, are most often mediated by ordered water molecules31–33,39. Very few examples of non–water-mediated ligand–phosphate contacts have been described, either as direct interactions40 or through crystal lattice contacts between neighboring DNA molecules13. However, no prior structure has revealed such a direct interaction involving amidine groups, which are among the most common cationic functionalities in classic minor-groove binders41,42. The direct amidine–phosphate contacts observed here therefore represent a unique binding feature that expands the known interaction repertoire of minor-groove ligands. In this context, amidine groups of JNII40 may play a dual role, contributing both to electrostatic stabilization of the DNA backbone and to the specific positioning of the ligand within the groove.

  We have shown earlier that JNII40 can inhibit the interaction between AT-DNA and the AT-hook 1 peptide (KRGRGRPRK) from ‘high mobility group’ A (HMGA) proteins35. In the crystal structure of AT-hook 1 bound to d(CGTTAATTAACG)2, the guanidine group of Arg5 interacts with the phosphate backbone of T7, stabilizing the interaction. Hence, the binding mode observed in the present study is consistent with the great capacity of JNII40 to compete with AT-hook 1 for binding to DNA35. This is interesting from a biological mechanistic point of view because AT-hook proteins such as LamAT-Y are believed to play an essential role in the biology of Leishmania parasites, contributing to key cellular processes such as gene expression, DNA replication, and DNA repair43.


### Sequence selectivity and binding to extended AT-rich DNA
  The structural information presented here provides a basis for the AT specificity of JNII40. Direct hydrogen bonds, such as the interaction between the ligand amide proton and the O atom of T7, as well as water-mediated contacts involving thymine oxygen atoms, explain favoured binding to thymine-containing sequences. Moreover, the orientation of the adenine H2 proton toward the minor groove places it in close proximity to the ligand, suggesting that the presence of the guanine exocyclic amino group at the equivalent position would sterically hinder ligand accommodation within the groove. In addition, AT-rich duplexes display a characteristically narrower (and often deeper) minor groove compared to GC-rich DNA44,45, which has been described to be associated with ordered hydration, and may better accommodate interactions of the ligand with DNA backbone atoms.

  Based on the binding mode observed for (AT)4 and the defined length of the binding site, one could anticipate that longer AT tracts might accommodate more than one ligand molecule. To explore this possibility, we extended the number of AT repeats to ten and investigated ligand binding. ITC experiments with the corresponding (AT)10 sequence did not provide interpretable binding isotherms (Supplementary Fig 1). Native PAGE analysis showed that this sequence forms at least two species in equilibrium (Supplementary Fig 9), most likely hairpin and duplex, which explains the complex titration profiles observed. We therefore investigated this system by SPR using hairpin constructs, which can be immobilized through the 5′-biotin tag.

  SPR sensograms and the corresponding binding plots were first obtained with the h(AT)4 hairpin, which was used as a reference to facilitate comparison with the longer hairpin (Supplementary Fig 10). In this case, two apparent dissociation constants are observed for both FR60 and JNII40 (Supplementary Fig 10). The higher-affinity interaction is of the same order as that obtained for the corresponding duplex sequences (Table 1), whereas the weaker interaction (Kd = 33.7 and 36.2 μM for FR60 and JNII40, respectively) is likely associated with nonspecific ligand binding in the loop region of the hairpin46. In contrast, measurements performed with h(CG)4 hairpin show no detectable binding to JNII40 (Supplementary Fig 11), further confirming the AT selectivity of the ligand.

  For the longer hairpin h(AT)10, two dissociation constants are also observed for both ligands (Supplementary Fig 12). However, in this case the second Kd value (6.7 and 13 μM for FR60 and JNII40, respectively) reflects a substantially stronger interaction than the weaker binding event observed for h(AT)4. Therefore, we postulate that for h(AT)10 both ligands bind within the stem of the hairpin, although the second binding event occurs with lower affinity, possibly due to structural changes in the minor groove induced by binding of the first ligand.

  These observations are consistent with the possibility that multiple JNII40 molecules could bind to extended AT-rich tracts present in mitochondrial DNA, a relevant target for minor-groove ligands in parasites such as Leishmania.

  In conclusion, the high-resolution structure of the (AT)4:JNII40 complex reveals a distinct binding mode for arylimidamide ligands, involving both direct amidine–phosphate and water-mediated interactions that shape the minor-groove environment. This distinctive interaction mode provides key insights for developing selective DNA-targeting agents with improved stability and therapeutic promise against kinetoplastid parasites.


## Methods

### Materials for ligand synthesis
  All reagents were purchased from commercial sources and used without further purification unless otherwise stated. Thin-layer chromatography was carried out using pre-coated aluminum sheets with silica gel 60 F254 (Merck).


### Methods for ligand characterization
  One-dimensional NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker DPX-400 Advance spectrometer, operating at 400.13 MHz and 600.1 MHz for 1H NMR and 100.6 MHz and 150.9 MHz for 13C NMR. Shifts are referenced to the internal solvent signals (DMSO-d6: 1H NMR δ 2.50, 13C NMR δ 39.52. NMR data was processed using MestReNova 14.2.1software. Mass spectra were recorded in negative and positive mode using electrospray ionization technique (ESI+ or ESI-) using three different cone voltages. Apparatus: HPLC–MS WATERS integrated with a separation module 2695, detector PDA (photodiode array) 2996 and Micromass ZQ spectrometer. Analytical liquid chromatography (HPLC) was performed in reverse phase with SunFire C18-3.5 μm column, 100 Å porous size, (2.1 mm × 50 mm). Elemental analysis was carried out at the Microanalysis Laboratory, “Manuel Lora Tamayo” Organic Chemistry Centre – CSIC. High resolution mass spectra were recorded at Servicio Interdepartamental de Investigación (SIDI) at the Universidad Autónoma de Madrid.


### Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC)
  ITC experiments were carried out on a PEAQ-ITC microcalorimeter (Malvern). DNA oligonucleotides (AT)4 (5′-CATATATATG-3′) and (AT)10 (5′-CATATATATATATATATATATG-3′) were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT). Both oligonucleotides and compounds JNII40 and FR60 were dissolved in MES buffer (10 mM 2-(N-morpholino)ethanesulfonic acid, 1 mM EDTA, 100 mM NaCl, pH 6.25). Oligonucleotide concentrations were determined in triplicate using a JASCO UV–Vis spectrophotometer at 90 °C to ensure complete strand separation prior to applying the extinction coefficient for single-stranded DNA. In each titration, 2 μL injections of 0.9 mM JNII40 were delivered into a 200 μL cell containing 30 μM of either (AT)4 or (AT)10. Experiments were performed at 25 °C, with injections spaced at 300 s intervals and a stirring speed of 750 rpm. Control titrations, consisting of ligand injections into buffer, were performed to obtain baseline heats of dilution and mixing, which were subtracted from the experimental data. Data were analyzed with the PEAQ-ITC analysis software. Each titration was repeated with at least two independent sample preparations.


### Native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE)
  Native polyacrylamide gel (20%) was prepared in 50 mM MES pH 6.25 buffer. DNA samples were dissolved in 10 mM MES pH 6.25, 100 mM NaCl buffer. Electrophoresis was carried out using 50 mM MES pH 6.25 as running buffer. Gel was run at 120 V for 4 h in a cold chamber maintained at 4 °C.


### Surface plasmon resonance (SPR-biosensor)
  SPR binding and affinity experiments were performed at 25 °C with a Biacore X-100 apparatus (Biacore GE). Running buffer containing MES (10 mM 2-(N-morpholino)ethanesulfonic acid), 1 mM EDTA, 100 mM NaCl, 0.005% surfactant P20, pH 6.25, was used for the experiments with FR60 and JNII40 compounds. The 5’-biotin labelled DNA hairpins were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT) with reverse-phase HPLC purification. The DNA hairpin sequences used were: 5’-biotin-CATATATATGCCCCATATATATG-3’ [h(AT)4], 5′-biotin-CATATATATATATATATATATGCCCCATATATATATATATATATATG-3 [h(AT)10], and 5’-biotin-TGCGCGCGCATTTTGCGCGCGCA-3’ [h(GC)4]. The oligonucleotides were dissolved in the same buffer but without surfactant at an initial concentration of 100 μM. The DNA hairpins were immobilized on a streptavidin-derivatised gold chip (SA chip from Biacore) by injection of a 100 nM DNA solution with a flow rate of 1 µL/min until approximately 400 RU were reached. Flow cell 1 was used as reference while flow cell 2 was immobilized with the DNA oligonucleotide in different chips. Direct binding of compounds JNII40 and FR60 was measured by injection of increasing concentrations (0.01–30 µM) over the immobilized DNA surfaces at a flow rate of 50 µL/min for a period of 80 s followed by a dissociation period of 250 s. Regeneration of the surface was made with NaCl 200 mM/NaOH 10 mM using a flow rate of 10 µL/min during 30 s. The RU values in the steady-state region of the sensorgrams at each concentration were averaged over a 10 s time zone and converted to r (moles of compound bound per mole of DNA hairpin). The binding affinity was determined by fitting the results to a two-sites binding model giving Kd1 and Kd2.


### Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)
  Oligonucleotides (AT)4 (5′-CATATATATG-3′) and (CG)4 (5′-ACGCGCGCGT-3′) were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT). For DNA–ligand experiments, JNII40 was dissolved in deuterated d6-DMSO, except for the acquisition of the 1D 1H NMR spectrum of the free ligand in buffer, which was recorded in D2O using 10 mM sodium phosphate at pH 6.5 and 100 mM NaCl. Samples for NMR analysis were prepared in 200 μL of either D2O or a 9:1 H2O/D2O mixture, using a buffer composed of 10 mM sodium phosphate at pH 6.5 supplemented with 100 mM NaCl. Final oligonucleotide concentrations, determined by UV–Vis spectrophotometry, were approximately 0.4 mM in D2O and 0.3 mM in 9:1 H₂O/D₂O sample. Spectra were acquired on a Bruker Avance instrument operating at 600 MHz and equipped with cryogenic probes. Data were processed using Bruker TopSpin software. TOCSY spectra were collected employing the standard MLEV-17 spin-lock sequence with an 80 ms mixing time. NOESY spectra were acquired with mixing times ranging from 75 to 250 ms. In both 1D and 2D experiments (NOESY, TOCSY and DQF-COSY), water suppression was achieved by incorporating an excitation sculpting scheme47 into the pulse sequence prior to acquisition. Two-dimensional experiments were performed at 5 °C. Peak assignment and semiquantitative NOE analysis were carried using POKY software48.


### Experimental NMR constraints
  Distance restraints were derived from the relative intensities of cross-peaks in the NOESY spectra. NOE intensities were categorized as very strong, strong, medium or weak, corresponding to upper distance limits of 3, 3.5, 4.0, and 5 Å, respectively. In total, 16 unambiguous DNA–JNII40 restraints were included, based only on non-overlapping NOEs. In addition, a distance restraint of 2.0 Å was imposed between the O2 atom of thymine 7 in strand B and the amide proton of JNII40. This restraint is justified by the observation that this proton (9.5 ppm) forms a hydrogen bond, which slows its exchange with H₂O and makes it detectable in the NMR spectra, where it gives NOEs both with neighboring protons of the ligand and with several DNA protons. In addition to these NMR-derived restraints, target values for hydrogen-bond distances and angles within base pairs were taken from related X-ray crystallographic structures49. Force constants were set to 29 kcal·mol⁻¹·Å⁻² for base-pair hydrogen bonds and 20 kcal·mol⁻¹·Å⁻² for experimental distance restraints. Angular restraints for dihedral angles were obtained from qualitative analysis of intra-residual H1′–H2′ and H1′–H2′′ cross-peaks in DQF-COSY spectra. Loose restraints were applied to ν0, ν1, and ν2 torsion angles to restrict the deoxyribose conformation of C1, A2, T3, A4 and T9 in both strands to the south domain, equivalent to pseudorotation phase angles between 144° and 180°.


### Structural calculation
  Refined structures were generated using the SANDER module of the AMBER molecular dynamics package50. As a starting point, we built a structure by manually placing JNII40 close to the minor groove of (AT)4. This initial complex underwent standard, unrestrained energy minimization, thermalization and stabilization stages. This process on its own drew the ligand inside the DNA minor groove, and its position within the groove was optimized by implementing the Experimental NMR Constraints described above. The molecules were placed in a truncated octahedral box of water molecules modeled using TIP3P model and with a minimum distance of 8 Å between solute and box edges. The system was neutralized by the addition of sixteen sodium ions. The BSC1 force field51 was employed for DNA. Partial atomic charges of JNII40 were calculated with ANTECHAMBER using the AM1-BCC model52 and considering a net charge of +2 for JNII40, consistent with protonation of both amidine groups at pH 6.5. Input geometries for ANTECHAMBER were obtained from Gaussian53, where JNII40 structure was optimized at the 6–31 + G(d) level of theory. Additional force field parameters needed were obtained from GAFF54.

  The refinement protocol used for AMBER refinement has been described in detail elsewhere55. In this case, it consisted of simulated annealing in explicit solvent, followed by restrained molecular dynamics (rMD) trajectories of 5 ns, applying distance and angular constraints derived from NMR data and evaluating long-range electrostatic interactions using the Particle Mesh Ewald method56. A total of 5000 structures were produced along the rMD trajectories. The trajectories were divided into consecutive 0.5 ns intervals, and from each interval the last 10 structures (corresponding to the final 10 ps of dynamics) were selected. These structures were averaged and subsequently energy-minimized, yielding one representative conformation per interval. Representative initial, intermediate and final frames from the molecular dynamics trajectory are provided as Supplementary Data File 1. The resulting ten representative conformations were then further refined by removing planarity constraints while maintaining the same set of NMR-derived restraints, providing the final structural ensemble used for analysis. The structures retain the two water molecules directly participating in ligand binding and omit all other solvent molecules.

  Analysis of the representative structures and molecular dynamics trajectories was carried out with CPPTRAJ57, VMD58, PyMOL59 and X3DNA60,61.
