# Constitutive activation of a hybrid two-component regulator reveals cross-regulation of polysaccharide utilization genes in Bacteroides


## Abstract
  Human gut microbes, such as Bacteroides, rely on specialized gene clusters known as polysaccharide utilization loci (PULs) to metabolize diverse dietary and host-derived glycans. A major class of transcription regulators of these PULs is the hybrid two-component system (HTCS) containing a histidine sensor kinase and a response regulator within a single transmembrane polypeptide chain. Characterizing HTCS-mediated PUL regulation is often challenging, because the specific glycan signals required to activate most HTCSs remain unknown. Here, we characterized structural details of a highly conserved HTCS activation mechanism and developed a universal activation strategy by mutating the interdomain latch motif that inhibits the DNA-binding activities. Using the response regulator portion of BT4124 from Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron as a model system, crystallographic analyses reveal a “closed” inactive conformation anchored by a hydrogen-bond network formed by the conserved latch residues between the receiver and DNA-binding domains. Molecular dynamic simulation with the deep-learning BioEmu shows that the “AD” mutation of the latch residues destabilizes the inhibitory interface, shifting the conformation equilibrium predominantly to an active, “open” conformation. This constitutively active variant, BT4124RAD, allows us to map specific DNA-binding sites within the potential regulated promoters in vitro and characterize transcription regulation in cells. Induced expression of BT4124RAD not only down-regulates local homogalacturonan (HG) utilization genes, but also cross-represses multiple PULs associated with other homogalacturonan-related pectic glycans. These findings highlight a complex cross-regulatory network governing pectin degradation and establish the targeted latch mutation as a potential broadly applicable tool for deciphering the regulatory networks of HTCSs in Bacteroides.


## Results

### Conserved latch residues at the interdomain interface
  An inhibitory interaction between the REC and DBD domains of the unphosphorylated RR portion of BT4124 (BT4124R) has been suggested based on previous studies on phosphorylation kinetics and AlphaFold predictions (20). To characterize the details of the interdomain interaction, the crystal structure was determined for unphosphorylated BT4124R (Table S1). The overall structure of BT4124R follows the signature β/α fold of the REC domain and the helix-turn-helix fold of the AraC family of DBDs, except for the poorly ordered α4 region (Fig. 1B). Each asymmetric unit contains two BT4124R molecules forming a domain-swapped dimer with the DBD domain of one molecule interacting with the REC domain of the other. A subunit of the dimer containing the REC of one molecule and the DBD of the other presumably represents an unswapped monomer. The two monomers within the asymmetric unit are almost identical with a RMSD of 0.69 Å for Cα atoms (Fig. S1A).

  The active site residues surrounding the phosphorylation site D1240 show a typical geometry as observed in inactive RECs (19), such as PhoB (23) from Escherichia coli (Fig. S1B). Two residues involved in Y-T coupling, an allosteric mechanism for phosphorylation-induced conformational changes (19, 24), also have similar positioning to that of inactive PhoB, rather than its active counterpart (25), with the conserved S1270 at the β4-α4 loop pointing away from the phosphorylation site and the Y1289 at β5 not buried inside (Fig. S1, B and C). These structural features are consistent with an inactive conformation of the REC domain in the absence of phosphorylation.

  The relative orientation of the REC and DBD domains is similar to the AlphaFold prediction with an RMSD of 1.35 Å for Cα atoms between the un-swapped structure and the AlphaFold predicted model (Fig. 1C). The structure is a “closed” conformation with one of the two DNA recognition helices, α8, held at the interface. The interdomain interface involves the α5 helix, α2-β3, and α3-β4 loops from the REC domain, as well as α6 and α8 from the DBD domain, burying a total area of 933 Å2 (Figs. 1D and S1C). At the center of the interface are the residues within the conserved C/SHIP-D.K motif, including C1262 and H1263 in the α3-β4 loop, D1344 in α6, and K1383 in α8. K1383 is within hydrogen bond distance of H1263 (3.1 and 3.2 Å for both protomers), D1344 (2.9 and 3.3 Å), C1262 (3.3 and 3.8 Å), and C1262 is also close to D1344 (3.7 and 3.8 Å). The crystal structure confirms the crucial role of these highly conserved residues in interdomain interaction. They anchor a hydrogen bond network that latches the two domains together, preventing the access of the DBD to DNA.


### BT4124R exists as a monomer in vitro independent of activation
  Domain-swapped dimers have been observed for several RRs (26, 27, 28), but the physiological relevance is not clear. Formation of these dimers has often been attributed to high protein concentrations under crystallization conditions that allow partial unfolding for domain-swapping (26, 29). To investigate the relevance of the domain-swapped dimer observed in the crystal structure of BT4124R, we examined the oligomeric states of BT4124R proteins in solution by analytical gel filtration (Fig. 2A). The unphosphorylated protein exhibits a single elution peak with an apparent molecular weight (MW) of 37 kDa, which is only slightly above the theoretical monomer MW of 32 kDa. Therefore, dimerization is unlikely under experimental conditions. Phosphorylation with a small molecule phospho-donor, phosphoramidate, or mutation of the conserved latch residues can activate the protein (20). Unlike many RRs that dimerize upon activation (19, 30), phosphorylated protein BT4124R-p and the active variant BT4124RAD show elution peaks almost identical to the unphosphorylated protein, suggesting that BT4124R activation does not promote protein dimerization.Figure 2Activation of BT4124R does not promote dimerization. Mean values of elution volumes from at least two experiments are shown in (A) for molecular weight (MW) standards (diamonds) or indicated proteins (circles).   Oligomeric states of these proteins were further investigated by analytical ultracentrifugation (AUC), which allows for the continuous presence of phosphoramidate to keep the protein phosphorylated. The predominant sedimentation species for all three samples have similar sedimentation coefficients, corresponding to a MW of 32.3, 34.7, and 33.0 kDa for unphosphorylated BT4124R, BT4124R-p, and BT4124RAD (Fig. 2B). These values are consistent with a monomer MW of 32 kDa. The monomeric states appear identical at different protein concentrations of 30 μM and 10 μM (Fig. S2). A very small population of species with higher sedimentation coefficients was only observed at 30 μM for the active proteins, BT4124R-p (76.7 kDa, 3.3%), and BT4124RAD (69.6 kDa, 6.7%). Nonetheless, the predominant species appears to be monomers for both inactive and active proteins. The domain-swapped dimer observed in the structure appears to be an artifact of crystallization conditions.

  Fitting the AUC profile also yields the parameter value of the frictional ratio, which indicates the shape and hydration of the sedimentation species. A large frictional ratio often indicates a nonspherical or elongated shape of protein molecules. The active proteins, BT4124R-p and BT4124RAD, both have the frictional ratio values around ∼1.45, higher than that of BT4124R (Figs. 2B and S2), suggesting a more extended conformation for the active protein than the unphosphorylated inactive protein.


### Impacts of the conserved latch residues on conformation dynamics
  Activation of BT4124R for DNA binding requires breaking the hydrogen bond network among the conserved latch residues, relieving the inhibitory REC–DBD interaction and opening the “closed” conformation to a more extended form. Mutation of the latch residues would potentially alter the conformational equilibrium between the “closed” and the active forms. Conformation dynamics of BT4124R and BT4124RAD were simulated with BioEmu, a deep-learning protein dynamics emulator (31). Besides the termini and the loop connecting the REC and DBD domains, α4 has significant conformational flexibility (Fig. 3A), consistent with previous discoveries of the α4 helix as one of the most altered regions for propagation of phosphorylation-induced changes (19, 32, 33). Other regions with large conformational changes include α5, α3-β4, and α2-β3 loops, all involved in the interdomain interaction. On the other hand, the DBD is most likely moving as a rigid body with little local conformational motion.Figure 3The conserved latch residues impact the conformation equilibrium emulated by BioEmu.A, local conformational mobility. Root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), calculated at each residue for individual domains from 4000 emulated conformations, is mapped onto the predicted structure. Colors from blue to red correspond to RMSF values from low to high. B, schematic illustration and representative examples of the “closed” and different “open” conformations. C, emulated conformational landscapes. Conformation distributions are shown as either 1D probability density (top) based on RMSD to the crystal structure or 2D landscape (bottom) based on the structural similarity as well as domain orientation indicated by the dihedral angle of selected atoms (see Experimental procedures for details).

  Comparison of emulated conformations with the crystal structure reveals two apparent states, one similar to the “closed” structure, and the other “open” state with high RMSD values to the inactive BT4124R (Fig. 3, B and C). In the “open” states, the entire DBD moves away from the latch residues at α3-β4, exposing both DNA recognition helices, α8 and α11, for DNA access. With little restriction from the REC domain, the DBD in the “open” states can adopt diverse domain orientations as indicated by the widely distributed dihedral angles from defined residues from the two domains (Fig. 3C). A substantial population of the “closed” state (∼50%) has been predicted for WT BT4124R containing the conserved latch residues. Hydrogen-bond-disrupting mutations of the latch residues cause the "closed" state to nearly vanish in BT4124RAD. As a result, BT4124RAD predominantly stays in the active “open” state, which explains the constitutive DNA-binding activity of the AD variant.


### Identification of the DNA-binding site with active BT4124RAD
  DNA-binding activities were evaluated using EMSAs with DNA fragments corresponding to the promoter region of bt4114. Besides the large fragment from −170 to 30 bp of the start codon (Full), we created two additional DNA fragments, the upper region (Up) containing the tandem repeat of half-sites predicted by previous computational genomic analyses (18, 34), and the lower half (Dn) with no predicted binding sites, serving as a control (Fig. 4A). Unphosphorylated BT4124R did not bind to DNA while the active variant BT4124RAD showed DNA-binding activity with an apparent KD of 1.6 μM toward the Full fragment. Surprisingly, even though no binding site is predicted to reside in the Dn fragment, BT4124RAD binds to both Up and Dn fragments with comparable affinity, but weaker than to the Full fragment. A similar binding pattern was also observed for the phosphorylated protein, BT4124R-p (Fig. S3). This raises questions about the accuracy or completeness of the computational predictions.   The lability of high-energy acyl phosphate makes RR phosphorylation often short-lived, and mapping the DNA-binding site can be challenging due to the need to phosphorylate and maintain the phosphorylation status of RRs. Keeping an RR phosphorylated usually requires the continuous presence of the highly reactive phosphoryl donors that may interfere with various reactions necessary for DNA-mapping, such as DNase I footprinting. The AD variant enables activation of BT4124R without the need for phosphorylation.

  DNase I footprint assays were used to identify binding sites with BT4124RAD. Fluorescent DNA fragments were treated with DNase I, and the digestion pattern was measured by fragment analysis using a DNA sequencer (35, 36). Peak intensities reflect DNase I activities (Fig. 4B), and the ratios of peak intensities in the absence or presence of BT4124RAD were calculated (Fig. 4C) to identify the protected DNA regions (see Experimental procedures for details). Multiple regions in the bt4114 promoter are protected by BT4124RAD, including one at the −126 to −88 bp region that contains two hypersensitive loci spaced ∼21 bp apart. This suggests two half-sites (Ia and Ib), agreeing well with the predicted tandem half-sites (underlined). Another protected region is at −75 to −34 bp of the start codon. Two half-sites (IIa and IIb) are apparent with a central gap in the protected region and two hypersensitive loci spaced ∼22 bp apart. This binding site is within the range of the Dn fragment, consistent with the EMSA results.

  Footprint assays with the same protected regions were reproducible in replicate experiments using DNA fragments with fluorescent labels at different ends (Figs. 4D and S4). Two sites, one matching the prediction and one previously not discovered, have been identified, with each containing two half-sites. A similar pattern of DNA-binding with two hypersensitive loci and two half-sites was also observed for the promoter of a different gene, bt4108 (Fig. S4, C and D). Even though the RR portion of BT4124 does not dimerize, binding to a tandem repeat of DNA half-sites requires two DBDs, which may reflect the dimeric nature of HTCSs due to the linked HK dimer. The active AD variant facilitates identification of the DNA-binding sites in corresponding promoters.


### AlphaFold prediction of the protein-DNA recognition complex
  Identification of the DNA-binding sequence allows us to predict the structure of the protein-DNA complex with AlphaFold (37). To mirror the binding of active BT4124 to the tandem half-sites, the complex structure was modeled with two copies of the entire cytoplasmic portion of BT4124 containing the dimeric HK domains that could potentially facilitate binding of the two tethered RRs to the tandem sites. A great diversity of structural models were predicted by AlphaFold, ranging from one containing the intact inactive REC–DBD interaction with little DNA contact to another with the unrestricted DBD, making extensive contacts with DNA with indistinguishable prediction scores (Fig. S5A). Because current AlphaFold has limitations in predicting the effects of mutations and conformation dynamics (38), we focused on predictions with two copies of only the DBD to ensure no interference from other domains. Structural prediction with the extended “long” DNA from −187 to −88 bp did not yield a complex with a correct positioning of protein to the DNA site (Fig. 5A). Shortening the DNA to an approximate size of the identified site improves the prediction, but still results in great diversity in the arrangement of two DBDs (Figs. 5A and S5). While the two DBDs have diverse head-to-tail and head-to-head configurations, eight of the top 10 ranked predictions show a converged binding mode for one DBD with one of the half-sites (Figs. 5B and S5).Figure 5AlphaFold-predicted structures of the BT4124 DBD-DNA complex.A, overall structures of the DNA-binding complex predicted with DNA of different lengths. The DNA-binding site mapped by DNase footprint is highlighted in orange. B, comparison of the converged structure prediction with the DNA-bound structure of MarA (PDB id: 1BL0), another member of the AraC family. C, DNA minor groove widths of the structures. Solid lines represent the mean, and the red shaded area indicates the width range of the 8 converged structural predictions. DNase hypersensitive sites are colored teal in (B and C). (D) Sequence logo of the half-site. DBD, DNA-binding domain; PDB, Protein Data Bank.

  The converged part of structural prediction exhibits great similarity to the DNA complex structures of other proteins from the AraC family of DBDs (39, 40) and explains the hypersensitivity pattern of the experimental DNase footprinting. Similar to arrangements in a crystal structure of E. coli MarA (39), the two DNA recognition helices α8 and α11 in BT4124 bind to adjacent major grooves on the same face of DNA (Fig. 5B). DNA is bent toward the major groove to accommodate the two helices due to a shorter spatial separation of α8 and α11 than the typical distance between adjacent DNA major grooves. This leads to an increase in the minor groove width at bases opposite to the recognition helices and a decrease in the minor groove width at bases between the two helices when compared to the DNA-only predictions (Fig. 5C). High efficiency of DNase I digestion has been associated with a wider minor groove at the cleavage base as well as a narrower minor groove width 2 to 3 bp upstream of the cleavage base (41). Both cleavage-enhancing conditions are satisfied at bases opposite to the recognition helix, where the hypersensitivity was observed for MarA (42) and BT4124RAD (Fig. 5, B and C).

  The top-ranked DBD complex structure was provided to DeepPBS, a geometric deep-learning model to predict DNA-binding specificity (43). The resulting predicted DNA-binding logo displays considerable similarity to the half-site logos derived from footprint assays or computational predictions from RegPrecise (34), a curated database of bacterial regulation (Fig. 5D). Overall, despite substantial variability in predicting domain arrangements within the protein-DNA complex, the consistent portion of the DBD and half-site structure offers important insights into the DNA recognition details of BT4124. Such conserved predictions rely on the identification of the binding sites with the active HTCS variant to correctly restrict the positioning of DBDs for AlphaFold prediction.


### BT4124RAD suppresses gene expression across different PULs

Inducible expression of BT4124RAD protein was confirmed using immunoblotting and no significant protein is detectable in the absence of aTc (Fig. 6A). BT4124 belongs to the PUL75 that targets HG.   In addition to the experimentally identified DNA sites before bt4108 and bt4114, two more binding sites within PUL75 are documented on RegPrecise: one at the intergenic region between bt4116 and bt4117, and another before the operon bt4119-bt4122 (Fig. 6B). MCAST from the MEME suite (44, 45) was used to search for additional clusters of half-sites across the genome. Binding site clusters are predicted before the operons bt4136-bt4132 from PUL76, bt4187-bt4185, etc (Table S2). Five genes from PUL75 and PUL76 were selected to measure how their mRNA levels change with aTc-induced expression of BT4124RAD (Fig. 6B). Because aTc can potentially impact gene expression levels, WT B. theta was used as a control. Fold changes of mRNA levels in response to aTc were compared between WT and the protein-expressing strain, Δbt4124+BT4124RAD. Upon aTc induction, the mRNA levels of BT4108, BT4114, and BT4135 all displayed moderate decreases that are significantly different from WT, suggesting that BT4124RAD suppresses expression of these PUL genes. In contrast, expression of BT4118 and BT4121 increased similarly by more than 30-fold in both the AD variant strain and WT. This increase observed in both strains may stem from a universal response to aTc. It is not clear whether BT4124RAD has no regulatory effect on the two genes or if the regulation is masked by a large global aTc response.

  It has been shown that structurally related glycans can coregulate expression of multiple PULs (16, 17) although the regulatory mechanism is not known. HG, the glycan potentially associated with BT4124 in PUL75, is one of the backbone components of pectin, a common dietary fiber found in plant cell walls of fruits and vegetables (Fig. 6C). Other pectic glycans include arabinan, galactan, rhamnogalacturonan I (RGI), and rhamnogalacturonan II (RGII) (17, 46). The TonB-dependent glycan transporter susC genes are usually good indicators of expression of their cognate PULs. Thus, expression of susC genes from PULs for these pectic glycans was measured to examine the potential regulatory activity of BT4124RAD (Fig. 6D). The susC/susD genes from RGI-PUL, BT4168 and BT4169, showed significant down-regulation of mRNA levels in the BT4124RAD-expressing strain while the corresponding mRNA levels did not alter much in WT. Expression of the other three susC genes from PULs for arabinan, RGII, or galactan decreased in the BT4124RAD-expressing strain and increased in WT. For all five genes, fold changes in the AD variant strain are significantly lower than in WT, suggesting a repressing role for BT4124. Taken together, the active variant BT4124RAD appears capable of regulating gene expression in B. theta cells and BT4124 cross-regulates expression of a wide variety of PULs.


## Discussion
  Understanding the regulation of glycan utilization pathways is important for advancing our comprehension of microbe–microbe and host–microbe interactions in the gut microbiome. Characterization of PUL regulation in Bacteroides is often limited by the inability to selectively activate specific regulators. Here, we demonstrate that the constitutive activation of HTCS regulators via the AD mutation of the C/SHIP-D.K motif provides an effective strategy to overcome this limitation. Structural insights from BT4124 highlight the central role of the conserved motif in the inhibitory interdomain interaction and conformation equilibrium. By disrupting the inhibitory interaction, we successfully characterized the DNA-binding properties and potential regulated PUL genes of an HTCS regulator without needing its native, unknown glycan signal. The activation strategy developed based on BT4124 may be broadly applicable because of the high conservation of this motif in Bacteroides HTCSs.


### Structural insights into the inhibitory "latch"
  Although previous AlphaFold predictions successfully captured the overall domain orientation of BT4124R (20), AlphaFold often produces less accurate predictions on sidechain packing, with the error amounting to one-third of all hydrogen bonds and van der Waals contacts (47). Our crystal structure of the unphosphorylated BT4124R provides a detailed view of the interaction surface between the REC and DBD domains. A complex hydrogen bond network formed by the conserved motif residues functions as a molecular latch to anchor the REC–DBD interaction. BioEmu simulation of conformation states suggests that BT4124R exists in both the “closed” and the active “open” states. Presence of the active conformation without activation is not uncommon for RRs (48, 49). The REC domain has been shown to exist in equilibrium between inactive and active conformations, with phosphorylation shifting the equilibrium to the active states (19, 48). While the exact numbers of predicted conformation populations remain to be validated, BioEmu appears to be capable of predicting the effect of the mutations on conformation ensembles (31). AD mutation of the latch residues shifts the active “open” state population from ∼50% in BT4124R to over 95% in the AD variant, highlighting the importance of these latch residues and providing a mechanistic basis for the HTCS activation strategy with latch mutations.

  The activation mechanism in WT proteins is expected to occur via phosphorylation-induced intramolecular rearrangements to relieve the latch inhibition, rather than promoting intermolecular interaction for dimerization. Even though our crystal structure showed a domain-swapped dimer, subsequent gel filtration and AUC analyses confirmed that BT4124R exists predominantly as a monomer in solution, regardless of its activation state. Two residues corresponding to the Y-T coupling allosteric mechanism (19, 24), S1270 and Y1289, are also highly conserved in HTCSs (Fig. S1D). HTCSs could share a similar conformation propagation mechanism with phosphorylation-induced rearrangement of the T/S correlated with reorientation of a Y residue in the β5 strand. Such reorientation is often accompanied by the remodeling of the α4-β5-α5 surface that is involved in various protein interactions (19, 25, 32, 48). Consistently, BioEmu simulations identify the α4, α5, α3-β4, and α4-β5 loops as the most dynamic regions, likely reflecting the concerted conformational changes. Two additional highly conserved residues within these regions, K1256 at the end of α3 and D1287 at the α4-β5 loop, contribute to the positioning of the α3-β4 loop and α5 helix (Fig. S1C). Conformational changes induced by phosphorylation may propagate to the latch residues within the α3-β4 loop through these residues. In contrast, the AD mutation provides a shortcut to activation by directly disrupting the REC–DBD interaction.


### Cross-regulation of multiple PULs by BT4124RAD
  The AD variant has been successfully used to identify the DNA-binding site and discover the regulated genes of BT4124. BT4124 is assigned to the HG-utilizing PUL75 (BT4108-BT4124) (16, 17), with binding sites predicted before multiple genes (18, 34). Our data validated the presence of the binding sites before bt4108 and bt4114, and showed suppression of both genes by the active AD variant. Exact levels of repression activities may differ between BT4124RAD and the active full-length BT4124, because the detached RR portion of BT4124 cannot dimerize as the full-length HTCS protein does for recognition of the tandem DNA half-sites. Nevertheless, BT4124 appears to function as a repressor that fine-tunes the PUL regulation, while BT4111, the other HTCS in PUL75, is the primary transcription activator of these PUL genes in response to HG. This is consistent with previous results in which deletion of bt4111 greatly impaired the growth on HG while deletion of bt4124 showed no apparent growth defect, but rather a slightly faster growth (16).

  However, the regulatory effect of BT4124RAD has not been observed for bt4118 and bt4121, which are located in the downstream half of PUL75. This could be due to the masking effect of a global aTc response. Tetracycline resistance is widespread in Bacteroides (50) and it has been shown that a small noncoding RNA involved in tetracycline sensitivity belongs to a large set of small RNA regulators impacted by stress conditions (51). It would not be surprising that expression of many stress-related genes could also be broadly affected by the tetracycline derivative aTc, which may limit the use of the aTc-inducible expression system for future transcriptional regulation studies. Nevertheless, the upstream and downstream sets of PUL75 genes appear differently regulated by aTc and the active BT4124RAD. The downstream set of PUL75 genes (BT4117-BT4124) is initially predicted to be a separate PUL named as predicted PUL73 according to PULDB (52). Many genes in this region, such as bt4119 encoding a pectin lyase and bt4121 encoding the transporter SusC, are not essential for growth on HG, while their counterparts bt4116 and bt4114 in the upper half of PUL75 are required for HG utilization (17). Moreover, there are conflicting transcriptomic data on the expression of this set of genes, with HG-responsive increase in one dataset (16) and no change in the other based on Theta-Base, the transcriptome database for B. theta (53). Therefore, the role of BT4117-BT4124 in HG utilization is uncertain and the corresponding glycan may need to be redefined. The absence of regulation by BT4124RAD further reflects differences in the regulation and metabolic functions of these genes.

  Despite the lack of knowledge of the activating glycans, BT4124 appears to be involved in the cross-regulation of multiple PULs related to pectin utilization. Pectin is one of the most complex polysaccharides containing multiple glycan components, and is known for its prebiotic potential to modulate the gut microbial community (54, 55). Cross-feeding of pectin breakdown metabolites from the primary pectin degrader, such as Bacteroides, to other bacteria impacts the composition as well as the metabolic outcome of gut microbiota (17, 55, 56). Our results show that many PULs associated with the main components of pectin are regulated by BT4124. Expression of susC genes from HG-PUL75, RGI-PUL77(BT4145-BT4183), RGII-PUL89 (BT0977-BT1030), arabinan-PUL7 (BT0348-BT0369), and galactan-PUL (BT4667-BT4773) are all down-regulated due to expression of the active BT4124RAD. Such sweeping repression of all these PULs may reflect a major cellular change triggered by stress, nutrient abundance, or other unknown environmental factors (Fig. 6E). This cross-regulation can be direct, as evidenced by the identified binding sites in PUL75, or indirect, potentially through the regulation of other global or local transcription regulators. With the active BT4124RAD, it becomes possible in the future to delineate different regulatory interactions by mapping its binding sites across the genome. Furthermore, repression of these PULs could also result from hierarchical regulation by a high-priority carbohydrate. Application of this universal activation strategy to other HTCSs could provide a mechanistic explanation for the hierarchical glycan utilization observed in Bacteroides.


## Experimental procedures

### Strains and growth conditions
  Strains, plasmids, and primers used in this study are listed in Tables S3 and S4. E. coli strain DH5α was used for cloning and BL21(DE3) for protein production. E. coli was grown aerobically at 37 °C in LB medium, with 100 μg/ml carbenicillin when needed. All B. theta strains were grown anaerobically at 37 °C in brain heart infusion (BHI) medium within a GasPak EZ container (BD). BHI medium was supplemented with 5 mg/L hemin, 1 g/L cysteine, 1 mg/L vitamin K3, 4 mg/L FeSO4·7H2O, 2 g/L NaHCO3, and 200 μg/ml gentamicin. For conjugation and gene deletion, 5% horse blood was added to BHI agar plates. When necessary for selection, 12.5 μg/ml (agar plates) or 25 μg/ml (liquid medium) erythromycin was used.

  A suicide plasmid pBT11-4124del was created with golden gate assembly of multiple PCR fragments (Table S4) containing the backbone of the suicide vector pLGB13 (50) and sequences flanking bt4124 for homologous recombination. Donor strain S17-1 λpir with pBT11-4124del was mixed with B. theta VPI-5462 for conjugation and subsequent selection of the gene deletion mutant Δbt4124 (RUB001) as described (50). To integrate the active mutated RR allele into B. theta, the integrative cloning vector pRG-BT04 was first constructed. It contains the backbone of the integration plasmid (57), an aTc-inducible promoter with tet operators, the gene encoding the TetR regulator, two BsaI cloning sites, a C-terminal HA tag and a transcription terminator. BT4124RAD was cloned into pRG-BT04 to generate pRG-BT04-4124RAD that is integrated at the attB site of the B. theta chromosome following the reported protocol to create RUB002 (57). All plasmids were verified by sequencing and strains were confirmed by colony PCR.


### Recombinant protein purification
  RR components of HTCS proteins, BT4124R and BT4124RAD, were purified as described previously (20). Briefly, lysates from corresponding cultures induced by IPTG were subjected to multistep purification chromatography using the following columns: HisTrap FF, anion exchange HiTrap Q, HiTrap Blue, and Superdex 75 pg 26/600 size-exclusion (all Cytiva). Proteins were eluted in 50 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, and 5 mM beta-mercaptoethanol, and concentrated using Amicon Ultra 15-ml centrifugal filters (MilliporeSigma, 10 kDa MW cut-off) for crystallization trials.


### Crystal structure determination
  Initial crystals of BT4124R were obtained from the MSCG2 (Anatrac) broad screen, and when optimization trials did not increase the crystal quality, microseeding with the MSCG2 broad screen was done, and subsequent crystals were harvested and frozen for screening and data collection. Data were collected on the Stanford Synchrotron Radiation Lightsource (SSRL) beamline 9-2 from a crystal grown in 0.15 M DL-malic acid, 20% PEG 3350. Crystals were cryo-protected in 15% glycerol. Data were processed using XDS (58) and then scaled and merged with Aimless in the CCP4 suite (59). The structure was initially determined by molecular replacement with the AlphaFold model used as the starting model input into PHASER (60). Subsequent refinement was done in phenix.refine in PHENIX (61) with rebuilding between rounds of refinement in COOT (62).


### Emulation of protein conformation dynamics with BioEmu
  BioEmu (31) in ColabFold (63) was used to explore the conformational dynamics of BT4124R and BT4124RAD. A total of 4000 structure samples were generated for each protein and analyzed by MDAnalysis (64). Only backbone atoms were emulated; side chain reconstruction and structural relaxation were not performed. Root mean square fluctuations (RMSFs) were calculated for individual domains by aligning all structures to the REC and DBD domains, respectively. Due to high RMSF values at the termini and the interdomain loop, RMSF values were capped at the 90th percentile for better illustration of local conformational flexibility.

  To analyze the distribution of conformations, RMSDs between the emulated structures and the crystal structure were computed to evaluate the probability density of the “closed” conformations with low RMSD values to the crystal structure and the “opened” ones with large RMSDs. A second dimension, the dihedral angle depicting the relative position of the DBD domain with respect to the REC domain, was used to define the probability landscape of different emulated conformations. The dihedral angle is based on the center of mass for four arbitrary groups of backbone atoms: the conserved cysteine C1262, the conserved histidine H1263, four consecutive residues at α5 of the REC domain (residue 1308–1311) and the entire DBD domain (residue 1344 - end). Kernel density estimation was used to estimate the probability density of emulated structures.


### AUC and gel filtration
  Oligomeric states of RR proteins were determined at the indicated concentrations in Tris buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgSO4, and 5 mM beta-mercaptoethanol). For phosphorylated BT4124R (BT4124R-p), ammonium hydrogen phosphoramidate (65) was added to a final concentration of 50 mM followed by 45 to 60 min of incubation to allow for sufficient phosphorylation. Protein samples were analyzed by either gel filtration or AUC. Size-exclusion chromatography was performed at RT using a Superdex 75 increase 10/300 Gl column with a sample volume of 500 μl and a protein concentration of ∼70 μM. A standard curve was generated using a series of MW standard proteins, including bovine serum albumin (BSA, 66.4 kDa), chicken ovalbumin (44.3 kDa), trypsin inhibitor (20.1 kDa), and RNase A (13.7 kDa). Apparent MW was computed from the peak elution volume based on the standard curve.

  Sedimentation velocity experiments were conducted at 4 °C in a Beckman Optima XL-I analytical ultracentrifuge (Beckman Coulter), with a speed of 45,000 rpm and the radial absorbance measured at 280 nm. All protein samples were normalized to the absorbance of either 0.45 (30 μM) or 0.15 (10 μM). For BT4124R-p, phosphoramidate remained in the solution to maintain phosphorylation. Sedimentation velocity data were fitted using the continuous sedimentation coefficient c(s) model in SEDFIT (66). The friction ratio was allowed to be optimized during the fitting, and the fitted friction ratio was used for iterative fitting until the value converged and RMSD minimized.


### DNA-binding assays
  EMSAs were performed as described previously (20) to assess the DNA-binding activities of BT4124 RR proteins. Fluorescein-labeled DNA fragments were generated by PCR using corresponding primers (Table S4) to yield the Full, Up, and Dn regions of the bt4114 promoter. If necessary, proteins were phosphorylated using 50 mM phosphoramidate prior to the DNA-binding reaction. Proteins of different concentrations were mixed with ∼2.5 ng/μl of DNA in binding buffer (Tris 50 mM, pH 7.6, 200 mM NaCl, 2.5% glycerol, 2 mM MgCl2, and 0.5 mM DTT) in the presence of 0.1 μg/μl BSA and 10 ng/μl of salmon sperm DNA, followed by 30 min of incubation and subsequent electrophoresis with 5% TBE gels. DNA bands were visualized with an Azure 600 imager (Azure Biosystems). DNA band intensities were quantified using ImageJ and the percent of bound DNA was calculated as the percentage of the DNA band intensity of bound DNA to the total DNA intensity of each lane. Binding data were fitted with the Hill equation to illustrate the binding curve.

  DNase I footprint assays were carried out using similar fluorescent DNA fragments generated with the HTCSup-6FAM primer (Table S4) containing the fluorescein at the 5′-end distant from the start codon of bt4114 or bt4108. A reversely labeled DNA with fluorescein near the start of bt4114 was created to minimize bias and validate results. DNase I footprint assays were only performed for unphosphorylated proteins because the effects of the highly reactive phosphoramidate on DNase activity are largely unknown. Based on the protocol for footprinting with capillary electrophoresis (35, 36), DNase I digestion was carried out in binding buffer in the presence of 0.1 μg/μl BSA with no competitor DNA. A 30-μl mixture of ∼8.4 ng/μl of DNA with 20 μM protein or an equal volume of Tris buffer for the no protein control, was treated with 0.02 units/μl of DNase I (New England Biolabs), and stopped after 1.5 min of incubation by adding EDTA to a concentration of 167 mM. DNA fragments were then purified with the DNA clean & concentrator Magbead kit (Zymo Research), eluted in 30 μl H2O and sent for fragment analysis (Azenta).

  DNase digestion patterns were visualized using Peak Scanner (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A spiked-in size standard GS500 Liz and the size of undigested DNA were used to establish the base pair sizes of individual peaks. Peak intensities in the presence and absence of protein were used to calculate ratios and identify the protected DNA regions. Ratios of peak intensities across the entire resolved DNA length were smoothed by the Fast Fourier Transform filter to filter out high-frequency noise and generate the trend line. Regions of decreased ratios, or the “valleys” of the smoothed trend line representing the potential protected regions, were picked by calculating the second derivatives, and all the non-valley data points were used to derive the baseline and SD. All ratios were normalized according to the baseline, and data points below 2 SDs of the baseline were considered as the protected protein-binding regions. Hypersensitive sites were manually selected as points with ratios high above those in the vicinity within the protected region. Sequences of six half-sites in protected regions in bt4114 and bt4108 promoters were input into MEME (45) to identify the DNA-binding logo. RegPrecise, a database for prokaryotic transcriptional regulons reconstructed by comparative genomics, documents 26 predicted full sites from different Bacteroides species (34). This yields 52 half-sites to derive the binding motif, which was used to scan the B. theta genome for clusters of binding sites with MCAST in the MEME suite (44).


### AlphaFold prediction of a protein-DNA complex
  Considering a DNA binding complex with two protein monomers, two copies of BT4124-DBD sequences and a double-stranded DNA sequence were used for structure prediction with AlphaFold3 (37). Predictions with two DNA sequences of different lengths were compared to evaluate the success of structure prediction. One sequence of 44 bp (AAAACAAAAAATACTATCGTAAAAACAAATATTACACATCCCAC), designated as “short”, contains the full protected site with two half sites, while the other, with a length of 100 bp, designated as “long”, contains an additional 56 bp upstream of the “short” DNA from the bt4114 promoter. Each AlphaFold prediction yielded five top-ranked structures, and six independent predictions were performed to give 30 predicted structures. The top 10 structures ranked by the ranking score and interface predicted template modeling score were selected for further analyses. Structural properties, such as the minor groove width, of predicted DNA structures were computed using the web X3DNA server (67). The top-ranked conserved protein-DNA complex was used as input for DNA binding site prediction with DeepPBS (43).


### Gene expression analyses with quantitative PCR (qPCR)
  To investigate which genes are potentially regulated by the active BT4124 variant, RUB002 with the aTc-inducible BT4124RAD integrated into Δbt4124 was grown in the presence or absence of 1000 ng/ml aTc. WT B. theta cells were grown simultaneously as a control. Fresh cell cultures were harvested when OD600 was 0.35 to 0.8. Pelleted cells were lysed, followed by electrophoresis and immunoblotting to confirm protein expression, or placed into Tri-reagent before RNA purification using the Direct-zol RNA miniprep kit (Zymo research). Western blots were probed with 1:2000 mouse HA-Tag antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) and 1:10,000 Alexa Fluor 680 anti-Mouse IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Blots were imaged with an Azure 600 imager (Azure Biosystems) and antibody specificity was confirmed with purified HA-tagged proteins as well as WT samples without HA-tagged proteins. Purified RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA using the high-capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit (Applied Biosystem). Real-time qPCR was performed in a QuantStudio 3 PCR system (Applied Biosystem) with Luna Universal qPCR master mix (New England Biolabs) using primers targeting genes of interest (Table S4). Transcript levels were normalized to 16S ribosomal RNA, and the fold changes in expression levels were computed by comparing the normalized transcript levels in aTc-induced samples over uninduced samples.
