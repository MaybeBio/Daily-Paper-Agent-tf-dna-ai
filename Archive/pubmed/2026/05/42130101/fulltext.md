# Decoding Asthma Therapy: The Role of VEGFA and HIF1A in Yanghe Pingchuan Granule's Mechanism


## ABSTRACT
  This study integrated network pharmacology, bioinformatics, and experimental validation to investigate the glycolysis‐related targets of Yanghe Pingchuan granule (YPG) in asthma therapy. Active ingredients of YPG were screened via TCMSP, with targets identified through UniProt. Asthma‐associated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were extracted from GEO datasets, whereas asthma‐ and glycolysis‐related targets were mined from GeneCards, OMIM, TTD, DrugBank and MSigDB. Venn analysis identified three key genes (GSTP1, VEGFA and HIF1A) linked to HIF‐1 and VEGF signalling pathways. Molecular docking confirmed strong binding between core YPG compounds (quercetin, kaempferol and luteolin) and these targets. ROC analysis demonstrated high diagnostic value of VEGFA and HIF1A for asthma. In vivo validation showed that YPG significantly downregulated GSTP1, VEGFA and HIF1A expression in asthmatic rats. Our findings establish VEGFA and HIF1A as pivotal glycolysis‐associated targets in asthma, indicating that YPG exerts anti‐asthma effects by modulating these critical regulators.


## Introduction
  Asthma is a chronic inflammatory disease of the airways that usually manifests as wheezing, shortness of breath, chest tightness and coughing. Asthma is a common and serious global health problem affecting approximately 300 million people and inducing about 1000 deaths per day [1]. Currently, asthma is generally treated with inhaled glucocorticoids to reduce inflammation or β2 agonists to relieve bronchospasm. Unfortunately, the frequent use of short‐acting β2 agonists increases the mortality of asthma [2], whereas the use of inhaled glucocorticoids is limited by individual differences in hormone sensitivity and side effects [3]. Therefore, it is a top priority for medical research to probe the pathogenesis of asthma and develop new therapeutic strategies.

  With the rise of metabolomics, researchers have discovered that metabolic reprogramming is significantly associated with inflammation and that targeting glycolysis can be utilised as an anti‐inflammatory strategy [4, 5]. Glycolysis is the anaerobic breakdown of glucose to produce pyruvate, lactate and a small amount of adenosine triphosphate, which requires the participation of a series of enzymes, such as hexokinase (HK), phosphofructokinase and pyruvate kinase muscle isozyme (PKM) [6]. Importantly, several studies have unveiled the association between increased glycolysis and asthma. For example, a prior study revealed that aerobic glycolysis was significantly increased in human bronchial epithelial cell and mouse models of house dust mite (HDM)‐induced asthma [7]. Through RNA sequencing of bronchial epithelial cells from asthmatic patients, Wijsman et al. observed that genes related to the glycolysis pathway were down‐regulated in bronchial epithelial cells from asthma patients after bronchial thermoplasty [8]. The research by Qian et al. uncovered that glycolysis‐related proteins HK1, HK2 and lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) were up‐regulated in the lung tissues of mice with HDM‐induced allergic asthma and the primary nasal epithelial cells of asthmatic patients and that LDHA knockdown reduced lactate levels and airway inflammation in lung tissues from asthmatic mice, suggesting the potential involvement of glycolysis in asthma pathogenesis as an important pro‐inflammatory signal [9]. Another study found that PM2.5 exposure enhanced glycolysis rates and HK2 and LDHA protein expression, further exacerbating airway inflammation in asthmatic mice [10]. In addition, PKM2, a key enzyme in the glycolysis pathway, was detected as highly expressed in sputum from asthmatic patients and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid from HDM‐exposed mice [11]. Collectively, glycolysis is essential for the pathogenesis of asthma, and targeting glycolysis‐related enzymes may be a new anti‐inflammatory therapeutic strategy for asthma.

  Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) has historically been used in the treatment of asthma for its significant efficacy and minimal side effects. Accordingly, given the high incidence and low control rate of asthma, it is particularly vital to strengthen the research and application of TCM in the treatment of asthma. Yanghe Pingchuan granule (YPG) is a potent TCM prescription refined by Professor Guojun Hu, a nationally famous veteran TCM practitioner in China, during long‐term diagnosis and treatment of asthma and has been used in the clinical treatment of asthma for more than 10 years. The group has carried out a series of clinical studies and ex vivo and in vivo experimental validation of the formula, and has demonstrated the clinical efficacy of the formula and the relevant molecular mechanisms of the interventions [12, 13, 14]. Nevertheless, no studies have reported whether YPG can ameliorate airway inflammation in asthma through the glycolysis pathway. TCM compounds have a wide range of targets because of their complex compositions. Of note, network pharmacology shows promising applications in addressing the mechanism of action of active ingredients in TCM, and network pharmacology combined with bioinformatics can empower the investigation of disease intervention mechanisms from a broader perspective. Therefore, this study deeply analysed the potential targets and the possible mechanism of action of YPG in the intervention of glycolysis in asthma by integrating network pharmacology, bioinformatics and experimental validation, thus providing new treatment strategies for asthma and opening up new avenues for the modernisation research and development of TCM.


## Materials and Methods

### Screening of Active Ingredients and Corresponding Targets of YPG
  The chemical constituents of nine herbs in YPG, namely Ephedrae Herba, Schisandrae Chinensis Fructus, Morindae Officinalis Radix, Rehmanniae Radix Praeparata, Inulae Flos, Sinapis Semen, Lepidii Semen, Platycodonis Radix and Angelicae Sinensis Radix, were retrieved in the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology Database and Analysis Platform (TCMSP; https://tcmspw.com/tcmsp.php/; accessed on 21 May 2024) [15]. Next, active ingredients were preliminarily screened based on oral bioavailability (OB) ≥ 30% and drug‐likeness (DL) ≥ 0.18 in the parameters of absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion to obtain active compounds and their protein targets, and the known targets of unpredicted active compounds were supplemented according to published literature. After screening, protein target information was standardised by converting the protein targets of the compounds to gene names in the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/; accessed on 30 May 2024) [16] with Homo sapiens and ‘reviewed’ as screening conditions.


### Screening of Asthma‐Related Targets
  Asthma‐related datasets were attained per the following criteria: (1) Datasets from the gene expression omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/; accessed on 15 June 2024) of the National Center for Biotechnology Information; (2) datasets obtained with ‘asthma’ as the keyword; (3) datasets obtained with Homo sapiens as the species; (4) datasets with the research type ‘expression profiling by array’; (5) datasets with the sample type of induced sputum cells; (6) datasets containing control and asthma groups and (7) datasets with a sample size of 20 or more per group. Ultimately, the asthma‐related dataset, GSE76262, obtained a [HT_HG‐U133_Plus_PM] affymetrix HT HG‐U133+ PM array plate from the GPL13158 platform and comprised 114 samples, including 21 control samples and 93 severe asthma samples. For data preprocessing, raw expression data were background‐corrected, normalised and summarised using standard affymetrix preprocessing procedures. Batch effects were adjusted using the limma package in R software. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the limma package, with the screening criteria set as |logFC| > 0.58 and adjusted p‐value (p.adj) < 0.01. Subsequently, asthma‐related targets were mined in databases including GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/; accessed on 15 June 2024) [17], Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM; https://omim.org/; accessed on 15 June 2024) [18], Therapeutic Target Database (TTD; http://db.idrblab.net/ttd/; accessed on 15 June 2024) [19], and DrugBank (https://go.drugbank.com/; accessed on 15 June 2024) [20]. In the GeneCards database, a higher Relevance score represents a closer association between the target and the disease. Accordingly, targets were set as Relevance score ≥ 1.76. Finally, targets in the four databases were merged to obtain all asthma‐related targets.


### Screening of Glycolysis‐Related Genes
  Glycolysis‐related genes were searched in MSigDB (https://www.gsea‐msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/; accessed on 15 June 2024) [21] and GeneCards (screening criteria: ‘protein‐coding’ and ‘Relevance score > 1) databases with the keyword ‘glycolysis’, followed by duplicate removal.


### Key Genes Involved in the Intervention of Glycolysis in Asthma by YPG
  The targets of YPG, DEGs, asthma‐related targets and glycolysis‐related genes were uploaded to the Xiantao Academic Website (https://www.xiantaozi.com/; accessed on 17 June 2024) to draw the Venn diagrams, thus visualising and obtaining key genes involved in the intervention of glycolysis in asthma by YPG.


### Functional Enrichment Analyses
  The gene ontology (GO) database integrates findings from the Biological Models Database Group and other biological studies and can analyse massive amounts of genetic information in terms of cellular composition (CC), molecular function (MF) and biological process (BP) items. The Kyoto encyclopaedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) database is a database for systematic analysis of gene function, including up‐to‐date annotations of gene function. Therefore, the obtained key genes were subjected to GO and KEGG enrichment analyses with the Xiantao Academic Website. This was done through the clusterProfiler package (v4.4.4) of R software (v4.2.1), identifying significantly enriched functional entries based on the hypergeometric distribution test, correcting for multiple hypothesis testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, and correcting for a false discovery rate < 0.05 defined as statistically significant.


### Regulatory Networks of mRNA‐miRNAs and mRNA‐TFs of Key Genes
  The key gene‐miRNA interaction network was constructed with the miRWalk database (http://mirwalk.umm.uni‐heidelberg.de/; accessed on 20 June 2024) [22] based on the following criteria: sore > 0.9; 3′ untranslated region as the binding region of target genes; the corresponding miRNAs available in TargetScan, miRDB and miRTarBase databases, therefore predicting the relationship between key genes and miRNAs. The target TFs of the key genes in asthma were predicted using the TRRUST version2 database (http://www.grnpedia.org/trrust/; accessed on 20 June 2024) [23], with ‘Human’ as the species. mRNA–miRNA and mRNA–TF interaction networks were visualised with Cytoscape v3.8.2.


### Molecular Docking
  Molecular docking was used to verify the binding of YPG components and targets. The 2D structural energy of the main active compounds of the top three YPG (Quercetin, Kaempferol, Luteolin) were minimised using Chem3D (Version 20.0.0.41) software and saved in ‘.mol2’ format. The UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/) was searched for glutathione S‐transferase Pi 1 (GSTP1), vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA), and hypoxia‐inducible factor 1A (HIF1A) gene IDs. Based on the target IDs, the 3D structures of the three targets were retrieved from the PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org/) in ‘.pdb’ format. Water molecules were removed and hydrogen atoms were added to the targets using PyMOL (Version 3.0.4) software. AutoDockTools (Version 1.5.7) software was used to converted the compounds and target proteins into’. Pdbqt’ format for molecular docking. The binding energy of −5 kcal/mol was used as the criteria to determine that the proteins achieve good binding to the compounds [24].


### Expression and Diagnostic Model of Key Genes
  Obtain expression level data of key genes in the GSE76262 dataset from the GEO database. Analysis was performed using the Xiantao Academic Website, constructing receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves to evaluate the diagnostic performance of each gene. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) and its 95% confidence interval were calculated to assess the diagnostic performance and statistical reliability of the selected genes. Validation was simultaneously performed using the GSE148004 dataset.


### Construction of the LASSO Combined Diagnostic Model
  To further enhance the diagnostic value of the three key genes (VEGFA, HIF1A and GSTP1), a least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) logistic regression model was constructed using the Xiantao Academic Website. The expression profiles of the three genes in the GSE76262 dataset were used as input features, with asthma patients defined as the positive group (1) and healthy controls as the negative group (0).

  Ten‐fold cross‐validation was performed to select the optimal penalty parameter λ, which was determined based on the minimum binomial deviance criterion (λ.min). The LASSO algorithm assigned non‐zero coefficients to the three genes, and a combined diagnostic risk score was calculated for each sample using the formula:Risk Score = (β1 × VEGFA expression) + (β2 × HIF1A expression) + (β3 × GSTP1 expression) where β1, β2 and β3 represent the LASSO‐derived coefficients for each gene. ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of the combined model and individual genes in the training dataset GSE76262, with AUC calculated to quantify diagnostic efficacy.


### Animal Treatment
  Eighteen healthy male Sprague–Dawley rats (specific pathogen‐free grade; aged 7−8 weeks; weighing 200−220 g) were purchased from Zhejiang Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co. Ltd. (Zhejiang, China; SCXK (Zhejiang) 2019‐0001). The rats were housed in the Laboratory Animal Center of Anhui University of Chinese Medicine under the conditions of a light/dark cycle of 12 h, indoor temperature of 22 ± 2°C, and humidity of 55 ± 5%, with free access to food and water. All animal experiment procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Anhui University of Chinese Medicine in Hefei, Anhui, China on April 15, 2024 (Ethics Approval No. AHUCM‐rats‐2023128). Rats were randomly allocated into normal, model, and YPG groups, with 6 rats in each group. The classical animal model of asthma was established with the method modified from the relevant literature [25]. The modelling procedure consisted of 2 weeks of sensitisation and 4 weeks of stimulation. In detail, 1 mL of 10% ovalbumin (OVA) solutions were prepared, that is, sterile antigen mixture solutions containing 100 mg of OVA and 100 mg of aluminium hydroxide. The solutions were intraperitoneally given to rats on days 1 and 8, respectively, for sensitisation. From day 15, the rats were placed in an incompletely closed glass nebuliser box and were stimulated by inhalation of 1% nebulised OVA for 30 min once a day for a total of 4 weeks. Rats in the normal group were given normal saline. The gavage dose of YPG for rats was calculated according to the equivalent value ratio of the body surface area between humans and animals in Pharmacology Experimental Methodology (People's Health Publishing House) edited by Xu Shuyun. Afterwards, the intervention dose of YPG was selected as 15.48 g·kg−1 according to the efficacy of our previous experiments, and YPG was administered by gavage at 1 mL/100 g. From day 15, rats in the YPG group were gavaged with YPG once a day, and rats in the normal and model groups were gavaged with an equal amount of normal saline for 4 weeks. The asthma model was successfully replicated upon the appearance of obvious respiratory and systemic symptoms, such as nodding breathing, shortness of breath, dysphoria, yellow fur and scratching of ears and cheeks. 24 h after the last stimulation, rats in each group were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 3% pentobarbital sodium (30 mg·kg−1) in a sterile environment. Under deep anaesthesia, euthanasia was performed, and subsequently, abdominal aortic blood and lung tissues were harvested.


### Drugs and Reagents
  The compositions of YPG: Ephedrae Herba 6 g, Schisandrae Chinensis Fructus 6 g, Morindae Officinalis Radix 10 g, Rehmanniae Radix Praeparata 15 g, Inulae Flos 9 g, Sinapis Semen 6 g, Lepidii Semen 10 g, Platycodonis Radix 10 g and Angelicae Sinensis Radix 10 g. YPG was uniformly purchased from the Preparation Centre of the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui University of Chinese Medicine in the form of decoction‐free compound granules (21050059). OVA was bought from Beijing Lvshengyuan Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China; AP0028) and aluminium hydroxide was provided by Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China; 20190704).


### Main Reagents and Instruments
  The used reagents and instruments included goat anti‐mouse immunoglobulin G (IgG), goat anti‐rabbit IgG and glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) antibodies (Zs‐BIO; 249760203, 247860317, 200040908), VEGFA antibodies (Bioss, Beijing, China; AF11182863P), GSTP1 and HIF1A antibodies (Abcam, Cambridge, UK; GR3100421–7 and GR204055‐13), and D‐lactate kits (Wuhan ColorfulGene Biological Technology Co. Ltd., Wuhan, China; 20240610). Ultrasonic nebuliser (Guangdong Yuehua Medical Equipment Company); microscope (OLYMPUS); electrophoresis instrument, electrophoresis tank, film transfer instrument (Shanghai Tianneng Science & Technology Co. Ltd. (Tanon), models: EPS300, VE‐180 and VE‐186) and automatic exposure instrument (Shanghai Peiqing Science & Technology Co. Ltd., model: JS‐1070P).


### Haematoxylin and Eosin (HE) Staining
  Right lung tissues were fixed in 10% formaldehyde for 24 h, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin and prepared into 3−4 μm sections, followed by haematoxylin staining and eosin staining. The sections were dehydrated in ethanol, cleared in xylene and then sealed. The pathological changes of the rat lung tissues were microscopically observed.


### Enzyme‐Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)
  After the rats were anesthetized, blood was drawn from the abdominal aorta and then coagulated naturally for 10−20 min at room temperature, followed by about 20 min of centrifugation at 3000 r/min. The supernatant was carefully harvested, and the level of lactate in rat serum was detected with ELISA.


### Western Blotting
  Right lung tissues (approximately 0.1 g) were lysed with 600 μL of RIPA cell lysis solutions containing 0.6 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min. The supernatant containing total proteins was attained. After sodium dodecyl sulfate‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) gels were prepared, the collected protein samples were fully denatured and added with 5 × SDS‐PAGE protein loading buffers at 1:4. Electrophoresis was performed at a constant pressure of 80 v and a constant current for 1 h to transfer proteins onto the PVDF membranes. Thereafter, the membranes were rinsed for 5 min to wash away the transfer solutions and blocked with 5% skimmed milk powder on a shaker for 2 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation with primary antibodies (dilution ratios: VEGFA antibody, 1:1000; GAPDH antibody, 1:1000; GSTP1 antibody, 1:5000; HIF1A antibody, 1:2000; HK2 antibody, 1:1000 and PFK‐1 antibody, 1:1000) and three washes with PBS with Tween 20 (PBST; 10 min each). Next, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase‐labelled secondary antibodies (1:20,000) for 1.2 h and subjected to three washes with PBST (10 min each). The ECL kit was used to detect proteins, and the bands were analysed with Image J software.


### Statistical Analysis
  The graphing and statistical analysis of bioinformatics data were conducted with the Xiantao Academic Website, an online bioinformatics analysis tool developed based on R language. Experimental data were analysed with SPSS21.0. All quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). Data with normal distribution and homogeneity of variance were compared among multiple groups with one‐way analysis of variance. p < 0.05 denoted statistically significant differences.


## Results

### Acquisition of Active Ingredients and Corresponding Targets of YPG
  According to screening results, there were 23, 12, 2, 20, 8, 2, 19, 3, and 7 active ingredients of Ephedrae Herba, Lepidii Semen, Rehmanniae Radix Praeparata, Morindae Officinalis Radix, Schisandrae Chinensis Fructus, Angelicae Sinensis Radix, Inulae Flos, Sinapis Semen and Platycodonis Radix, respectively, including quercetin, kaempferol, luteolin and beta‐sitosterol. Table 1 lists the top 10 active ingredients of YPG from the perspective of degree values and their target information. Notably, quercetin, kaempferol and luteolin were common components of Ephedrae Herba, Descurainiae Semen and Inulae Flos. The targets of all active ingredients were searched, yielding 480 targets for Ephedrae Herba, 301 targets for Lepidii Semen, 34 targets for Rehmanniae Radix Praeparata, 138 targets for Morindae Officinalis Radix, 26 targets for Schisandrae Chinensis Fructus, 65 targets for Angelicae Sinensis Radix, 430 targets for Inulae Flos, 17 targets for Sinapis Semen and 91 targets for Platycodonis Radix. A total of 233 targets were acquired after merging and de‐duplication.


### Results of Asthma‐Related Targets
  The dataset GSE76262 was downloaded from the GEO database and subjected to calibration prior to data analysis (Figure 1A,B). The limma package was used for batch correction and DEG screening, and the screening criteria were |logFC| > 0.58 and P. adj < 0.01. DEGs were identified by comparing the severe asthma group and the control group. A total of 940 DEGs were harvested, including 289 up‐regulated genes (the red dots on the right side) and 651 down‐regulated genes (the blue dots on the left side). The volcano plot is displayed in Figure 1C, and the expression heatmap of the top 20 DEGs ranked high and low between asthmatic patients and healthy individuals is presented in Figure 1D. Moreover, 1126, 43, 147 and 44 asthma‐related targets were obtained from GeneCards, OMIM, TTD and DrugBank databases, respectively. Ultimately, 1257 asthma‐related targets were yielded after merging and de‐duplication.


### Results of Glycolysis‐Related Genes
  A total of 850 and 1104 glycolysis‐related genes were screened out from the MSigDB and GeneCards databases. Through merging and de‐duplication, 1703 genes were eventually attained.


### Key Genes of YPG in the Intervention of Glycolysis in Asthma
  The targets of YPG, DEGs, asthma‐related targets and glycolysis‐related genes were constructed to a Venn diagram (Figure 2A), yielding 3 key genes, namely GSTP1, VEGFA and HIF1A. The expression heatmap of these key genes in GSE76262 is shown in Figure 2B.


### Functional Enrichment Analysis Results
  To further ascertain the possible mechanisms underlying the intervention of glycolysis in asthma by YPG, we performed GO and KEGG enrichment analyses on the key genes. According to GO analysis results, the key genes were enriched in BPs such as regulation of transcription from RNA polymerase II promoter in response to hypoxia, vascular endothelial growth factor receptor pathway and cellular response to hypoxia, in CCs including secretory granule lumen, cytoplasmic vesicle lumen and vesicle lumen, and in MFs such as sulfur compound binding, vascular endothelial growth factor receptor binding and glutathione peroxidase activity. As found in KEGG analysis results, the key genes were enriched in the Kaposi sarcoma‐associated herpesvirus infection, HIF‐1 pathway and VEGF pathway (Figure 2C,D).


### Construction of Key Gene‐miRNA and Key Gene‐TF Networks
  To comprehensively and accurately clarify the regulatory relationships between mRNAs and miRNAs and between mRNAs and TFs in asthma, we analysed the mRNA–miRNA network of GSTP1, VEGFA and HIF1A using the miRWalk database and predicted their target TFs using the TRRUST database. Subsequently, mRNA–miRNA and mRNA–TF interaction networks were visualised with Cytoscape v3.8.2.

  From the mRNA–miRNA network, the three key genes could be targeted by a total of 17 miRNAs in asthma, including 14 miRNAs for VEGFA, 3 miRNAs for HIF1A, and 0 miRNAs for GSTP1 (Figure 3A). From the mRNA–TFs network, all of the three key genes could target TFs in asthma, totalling 88 TFs (Figure 3B).


### Molecular Docking Results
  GSTP1, VEGFA, and HIF1A were identified as key target molecules potentially involved in the intervention of YPG in asthma‐related glycolysis. To preliminarily explore the potential binding capability of YPG components with these targets, molecular docking analysis was performed (Figure 4). The top three major active components in YPG were docked with the key target proteins, and detailed results are listed in Table 2. The molecular docking results showed that Quercetin, Kaempferol and Luteolin could potentially bind stably to GSTP1, VEGFA and HIF1A (Figure 4A–I), with receptor–ligand binding energies all less than −5 kcal/mol. These results provide supportive evidence for potential interactions between YPG and its candidate targets.


### Diagnostic Significance of Key Genes for Asthma
  We evaluated the diagnostic value of the three key genes by constructing ROC curves in two independent asthma expression datasets. In GSE76262, VEGFA and HIF1A were significantly up‐regulated in patients (p < 0.001) while GSTP1 was down‐regulated (p < 0.001) (Figure 5A–C). ROC analysis yielded AUC = 0.782 (95% CI 0.681–0.884) for GSTP1, 0.806 (0.706–0.906) for HIF1A, and 0.811 (0.721–0.900) for VEGFA (Figure 5D–F). In the independent validation set GSE148004, the AUCs were: GSTP1 = 0.679 (95% CI 0.391–0.967), HIF1A = 0.753 (95% CI 0.487–1.000), and VEGFA = 0.790 (95% CI 0.558–1.000) (Figure 5G–I), which are consistent with the training cohort. Overall, HIF1A and VEGFA demonstrated good diagnostic performance for asthma across both datasets, whereas GSTP1 shows only lower diagnostic performance in the validation set.


### Construction and Validation of the LASSO‐Based Combined Diagnostic Model
  To further improve the diagnostic efficiency of the three key genes, a LASSO logistic regression model was constructed based on their expression profiles in the GSE76262 training set. As shown in Figure 6A, the coefficient trajectories of VEGFA, HIF1A and GSTP1 were plotted against log (λ), demonstrating that all three genes retained non‐zero coefficients at the optimal λ value, confirming their independent contribution to the diagnostic model. Ten‐fold cross‐validation was used to select the optimal λ parameter (Figure 6B). The left vertical dashed line indicates λ.min (the λ value corresponding to the minimum binomial deviance), which was selected to construct the final model. The LASSO model assigned positive coefficients to VEGFA and HIF1A, and a negative coefficient to GSTP1, which was fully consistent with their expression patterns in asthma patients (upregulation of VEGFA/HIF1A and downregulation of GSTP1). ROC curve analysis was performed to compare the diagnostic performance of individual genes and the combined LASSO risk score. As shown in Figure 6C, the LASSO‐derived combined risk score achieved an AUC of 0.795 in the GSE76262 dataset, which was slightly higher than that of VEGFA (AUC = 0.794) and HIF1A (AUC = 0.774), and significantly higher than that of GSTP1 (AUC = 0.740). These results indicate that the combination of the three key biomarkers synergistically improves the diagnostic performance for asthma, verifying the clinical application potential of the three‐gene signature.


### Validation of Key Genes in Asthmatic Rats

#### Effects of YPG on Pathological Changes in Lung Tissues of Asthmatic Rats
  HE staining demonstrated that in the normal group, the bronchial structure of rats was intact and visible, with no inflammatory cell infiltration in all layers of the bronchial wall and surrounding tissues. In the model group, the structure of the airway wall was severely damaged, with the presence of massive inflammatory cells and the formation of mucus plugs from inflammatory exudates, which obstructed the airway lumen to cause obvious stenosis. Compared with the model group, the YPG group showed clear and visible wall structures and markedly reduced degrees of inflammatory cell infiltration in the bronchial tubes and the surrounding area (Figure 7).


#### Effect of YPG on Serum Levels of Lactate in Asthmatic Rats
  As exhibited in ELISA results (Table 3), the serum level of lactate was remarkably higher in the model group than in the normal group (p < 0.01) and prominently lower in the YPG group than in the model group (p < 0.01).


#### Effect of YPG on GSTP1, HIF1A, VEGFA, HK2 and PFK‐1 Protein Expression in Lung Tissues From Asthmatic Rats
  Western blotting results showed that the protein expression of GSTP1, HIF1A, VEGFA, HK2 and PFK‐1 in rat lung tissues was significantly higher in the model group than in the normal group (all p < 0.001) and markedly lower in the YPG group than in the model group (all p < 0.05) (Figure 8A–G, Table 4).


## Discussion
  Asthma is a highly heterogeneous chronic airway inflammatory disease that has become a major global public‐health challenge [1]. Elucidating its pathogenesis is a key avenue for improving clinical outcomes. TCM has accumulated extensive clinical experience in asthma treatment [26, 27, 28] and offers unique advantages in holistic regulation and syndrome‐differentiated therapy. YPG is a commonly used herbal formula for asthma, developed and clinically applied by the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui University of Chinese Medicine. Metabolomics studies have shown that the glycolytic process is closely linked to airway inflammation in asthma, and targeting glycolysis can markedly suppress inflammatory responses [4]. Therefore, this study integrates network pharmacology and bioinformatics to explore the potential targets and molecular mechanisms by which YPG intervenes in the glycolytic pathway of asthma, providing a theoretical basis for subsequent experimental validation and clinical translation.

  Network pharmacology, as a new strategy for drug development, can reveal the drug‐component‐target‐disease relationships from a holistic perspective [29]. In this study, network pharmacology analysis identified the main active constituents of YPG as quercetin, kaempferol, luteolin and beta‐sitosterol, among others. Quercetin, kaempferol and luteolin are common components of Ephedrae Herba, Lepidii Semen and Flos Inulae. Our team previously found from the ‘TCM Inheritance Support System’ software show that Professor Hu Guojun used Ephedrae Herba combined Flos Inulae up to 150 times and Ephedrae Herba and Lepidii Semen up to 118 times for asthma treatment [30], further underscoring the potential value of these three herbs. Literature reports indicate that quercetin can ameliorate airway inflammation and remodelling in asthmatic mice by inhibiting the TGF‐β1/Smad pathway [31]. Our previous work has confirmed that YPG improves airway remodelling in rats via the same pathway [13]. Kaempferol exhibits multiple bioactivities, including anti‐inflammatory, antioxidant and immunomodulatory effects [32]. Beta‐sitosterol also demonstrates anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant properties [33]. In summary, YPG may exert comprehensive therapeutic effects on asthma through the synergistic actions of these active constituents, modulating inflammatory responses, reducing oxidative stress, improving mucus secretion and decreasing airway hyper‐responsiveness. However, pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic studies of YPG have not yet been conducted; future work should identify and quantitatively evaluate its active components to advance its application in precision asthma therapy.

  TCM formulae, with their multi‐component and multi‐target synergistic actions, can achieve ‘one drug multiple targets’ and ‘multiple drugs same target’ within a complex regulatory network. By integrating network pharmacology and bioinformatics analyses, we systematically identified GSTP1, VEGFA and HIF1A as key targets of YPG in the glycolytic pathway of asthma. Functional enrichment analysis showed that the HIF‐1 signalling pathway and the VEGF signalling pathway were significantly enriched during asthma‐related glycolysis, suggesting that YPG may reduce glycolytic activity and ameliorate airway inflammation through these two pathways. Further miRNA and TF predictions and network construction revealed the regulatory layers governing these key genes, laying the groundwork for subsequent validation at the transcriptional and post‐transcriptional levels. Molecular docking results indicated that the main active compounds quercetin, kaempferol and luteolin exhibited strong binding affinities to GSTP1, VEGFA and HIF1A. Based on these targets, we constructed an asthma diagnostic model. In the GSE76262 dataset, the area under the AUC for GSTP1, VEGFA and HIF1A were 0.782, 0.811 and 0.806, respectively. In the GSE148004 validation set, the AUCs were 0.679, 0.790 and 0.753, respectively. These findings provide a theoretical basis for the molecular mechanisms by which YPG treats asthma.

  To validate the predictions obtained from network pharmacology and bioinformatics, we performed animal experiments. Western blotting analysis showed that the protein levels of HIF1A and VEGFA were markedly up‐regulated in the lung tissues of asthmatic model rats. After YPG intervention, the expression of both proteins decreased significantly, consistent with the gene‐expression trends observed in the public datasets GSE76262 and GSE148004. Notably, GSTP1 protein expression was significantly increased in lung tissues of asthmatic rats, which was inconsistent with its downregulated trend in the human GSE76262 dataset. This divergence may be attributed to multiple biological and methodological factors. First, the sample sources differ substantially. The GSE76262 dataset was derived from human induced sputum, whereas our experimental data were obtained from rat lung tissue. Cellular composition and microenvironmental differences between sputum and lung tissue may lead to distinct expression patterns of GSTP1. Second, GSTP1 is a key enzyme involved in antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory responses. Under asthma‐related inflammatory and oxidative stress, GSTP1 expression may be upregulated in a compensatory manner to counteract cellular injury. Such a stress‐induced response at the protein level may not be fully consistent with the transcriptional profile identified from the human dataset. Third, species differences and limitations of animal models should also be considered. Animal models cannot fully replicate the complex heterogeneity and pathogenesis of human asthma, which may also contribute to the inconsistent expression of GSTP1. Taken together, the discrepancy between bioinformatics prediction and experimental validation highlights the complexity of asthma pathogenesis. Further clinical studies are warranted to clarify the expression pattern and regulatory mechanism of GSTP1 in human asthma. Lactate is the final product of aerobic glycolysis [34]. To further strengthen the mechanistic interpretation, we additionally detected the protein expression of key glycolytic rate‐limiting enzymes HK2 and PFK‐1. The results showed that the expression levels of HK2 and PFK‐1 were significantly elevated in the model group and notably reduced after YPG treatment. Meanwhile, ELISA measurement of serum lactate levels confirmed that lactate was significantly increased in the model group and markedly reduced in the YPG group. These findings further support that YPG exerts anti‐inflammatory and anti‐asthmatic effects at least partially by suppressing abnormal glycolysis.

  Based on the above network screening and experimental validation, we further elucidated the specific regulatory mechanisms of GSTP1, VEGFA and HIF1A in asthma‐related glycolysis and the possible intervention pathways of YPG. HIF1A (HIF‐1α) is a core regulator of metabolic reprogramming; it up‐regulates key glycolytic enzymes (HK2, LDHA and GLUT1) and promotes glycolysis [35, 36, 37]. In OVA‐induced asthma models, HIF1A activation leads to enhanced glycolysis and elevated lactate [38]. Formaldehyde induces macrophage inflammation through HIF1A‐dependent glycolytic reprogramming, aggravating allergic asthma [39]. House dust mite induced glycolysis also depends on HIF1A, acting via the miR‐223/VHL/HIF1A axis to promote inflammatory cytokine release and increase glycolysis in airway epithelial cells [7]; inhibition of HIF1A down‐regulates glycolysis‐related genes and weakens lung Group 2 innate lymphoid cell function, thereby alleviating airway inflammation [40]. Moreover, HIF1A‐driven glycolysis stabilises GATA3 expression in Th2 cells, boosting IL‐5 production and worsening asthma inflammation [41]. VEGFA's pathogenic role in asthma has been confirmed by multiple studies. Yang L.J. et al. found that in OVA‐induced asthmatic mice, VEGFA aggravates airway inflammation and oxidative stress by activating the NF‐κB pathway [42]. Lin J. et al. further demonstrated that VEGFA knockdown reverses LPS‐induced macrophage glycolysis enhancement, thereby reducing inflammatory responses [43]. Additionally, the VEGFA/PI3K/AKT axis up‐regulates key glycolytic genes such as HK2 and PKM2, promoting glycolysis [44]. Zhang W. et al. showed that PKM2 forms a transcriptional complex with NF‐κB, up‐regulating VEGFA expression and further promoting glycolysis [45]. Meanwhile, YPG has been reported to inhibit the PI3K/AKT signalling pathway and improve asthma airway inflammation [46], and also to suppress the IKK/IκB/NF‐κB pathway, decreasing IKKβ, IκBα and p‐NF‐κB p65 protein levels, accompanied by reduced VEGFA and VEGFR2 protein expression [14]. Based on these findings, we hypothesise that YPG blocks the IKK‐IκBα‐p65 axis, inactivating NF‐κB, thereby weakening the formation of the PKM2‐NF‐κB complex, leading to down‐regulation of VEGFA transcription. This subsequently inhibits the VEGFA/PI3K/AKT axis, ultimately reducing the expression of glycolytic enzymes such as HK2 and PKM2, decreasing glycolytic flux and lactate production and thus alleviating airway inflammation. GSTP1 is an important antioxidant enzyme in the lung that can modulate post‐translational modifications of PKM2, promoting asthma‐related glycolysis [11]. GSTP1 polymorphisms reduce enzyme activity, weaken reactive oxygen species clearance, drive oxidative‐stress‐induced up‐regulation of glycolysis and increase asthma susceptibility [47]. In summary, YPG may regulate asthma glycolysis through three complementary pathways: (i) Inhibit HIF‐1α signalling, lowering transcription of key enzymes such as HK2, GLUT1 and LDHA; (ii) down‐regulate the VEGFA/PI3K/AKT axis, block the NF‐κB‐VEGFA positive feedback loop and suppress HK2 and PKM2 expression; and (iii) mitigate oxidative stress and correct abnormal GSTP1 up‐regulation, thereby overall reducing lactate production and relieving airway inflammation.

  This study systematically elucidated the potential molecular mechanisms by which YPG intervenes in asthma‐related glycolysis, offering new clues for its clinical application. Nevertheless, the following limitations remain: molecular docking and Western blotting analysis verified the expression changes of key targets, quantitative measurements of the direct binding affinities between active compounds and their targets, as well as functional assays assessing enzyme inhibition or the degree of signalling‐pathway activation, are still lacking.


## Conclusion
  The present study reveals that YPG may be a key target for regulating asthma‐related glycolysis by targeting VEGFA and HIF1A. Future studies could explore how YPG precisely regulates the expression of these proteins and the specific impact of this regulation on the pathological process of asthma. More in vivo and in vitro experiments will be carried out to verify the effects of YPG on other key enzymes and metabolites of the glycolytic pathway, and to analyse the mechanism of anti‐inflammatory effects of YPG from a metabolic perspective.
