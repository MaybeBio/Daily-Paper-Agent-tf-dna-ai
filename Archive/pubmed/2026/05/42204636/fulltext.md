# Integrative single-cell and bulk analyses reveal ATF4-associated immune suppression and WNT/β-catenin signaling in osteosarcoma


## Background
  Osteosarcoma (OS) is highly heterogeneous and often exhibits an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, contributing to poor outcomes. The cell-type-resolved role of the ATF/CREB stress-response network, particularly ATF4, remains unclear in OS.


## Methods
  We integrated public OS single-cell RNA-seq and bulk transcriptome cohorts. Single-cell analyses included malignant program discovery (cNMF), regulon inference (SCENIC), and cell-cell communication (CellChat). Bulk cohorts (TARGET and GEO) were used to build and validate a prognostic model. ATF4-associated immune features were assessed using ESTIMATE, immune deconvolution, and TIDE-related metrics. WNT/β-catenin activity was evaluated by gene-set scoring. ATF4-related drugs were screened using DSigDB/Enrichr and assessed by docking. Sorafenib was selected for molecular dynamics simulation. ATF4 function was validated by siRNA knockdown (MG63, U2OS) and overexpression (HOS).


## Results
  A stress-associated malignant program showed enriched ATF/CREB activity and prominent signaling interactions. Intersecting its markers with ATF/CREB genes identified ATF3/ATF4/CREB5, which were used to construct an ATF/CREB-associated risk score (ACS) that stratified survival in TARGET and external cohorts. ATF4 showed the most consistent adverse prognostic association and remained an independent factor in multivariate analyses. ATF4-high tumors were associated with a more immunosuppressive profile and stronger WNT/β-catenin signaling, and these features tracked with the ACS. Among the compounds tested in silico, sorafenib ranked highest in docking, and subsequent molecular dynamics simulations suggested that the ATF4-sorafenib interaction can remain stable. In cell-based assays, silencing ATF4 curtailed proliferation, colony formation, and migration, accompanied by reduced β-catenin, CyclinD1, and c-MYC. By contrast, ATF4 overexpression led to the opposite pattern.


## Conclusion
  Our results place ATF4 at the intersection of stress-related tumor programs, immune suppression, and WNT/β-catenin activation in OS, and this pattern is associated with poorer clinical outcomes. The ACS offers a practical way to stratify risk, and sorafenib warrants further evaluation as a potential ATF4-oriented therapeutic lead.

## Introduction
  Osteosarcoma (OS) is the most common primary malignant bone tumor in children and adolescents. Although treatment has improved, long-term survival is still limited, mainly because pulmonary metastasis and therapy resistance remain major challenges [1, 2]. Population-based studies further suggest that survival gains from multi-agent chemotherapy plus surgery have become modest in recent decades, especially in patients with metastatic or recurrent disease [3, 4]. These clinical realities have drawn increasing attention to tumor-intrinsic programs and the tumor microenvironment (TME) as key determinants of disease progression and treatment response.

  The OS microenvironment is strongly shaped by the bone niche and often shows clear immune dysfunction. Prior reviews and cohort studies describe a complex cellular ecosystem in which myeloid populations, T/NK cells, stromal cells, and endothelial cells contribute to immune evasion, angiogenesis, and metastatic spread, which may partly explain the limited benefit of immunotherapy in unselected OS cohorts [5]. At the same time, bulk transcriptomic data average signals across mixed cell populations and may miss rare but functionally important cell states. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) helps address this limitation by resolving cellular heterogeneity in OS, distinguishing malignant cells from stromal/immune populations, and revealing cell-cell communication patterns that are difficult to capture in bulk data [6, 7]. This creates an opportunity to identify tumor programs in which intrinsic stress responses are linked to an immunosuppressive microenvironment.

  OS progression is regulated by a complex molecular landscape involving stress-responsive transcriptional programs, oncogenic signaling pathways, and therapy-resistance networks. Recent studies have emphasized the growing importance of molecularly targeted approaches in osteosarcoma treatment and the need to identify key regulatory nodes with translational potential. Among these, the activating transcription factor (ATF)/cAMP response element-binding (CREB) axis represents a major component of cellular stress adaptation [8, 9]. Within this network, ATF4 acts as a central effector of the integrated stress response (ISR), coordinating transcriptional programs involved in metabolic rewiring, stress tolerance, and phenotypic plasticity [10]. Accumulating evidence indicates that diverse regulatory mechanisms, including non-coding RNA-associated pathways, contribute to OS growth and chemoresistance [11, 12]. Notably, ATF4 has attracted increasing attention in OS because it has been reported to be upregulated in tumor tissues and experimental models, where it promotes proliferation, migration, and metastasis [13, 14]. Beyond these tumor-intrinsic effects, growing evidence also suggests that ATF4 participates in immune regulation. For instance, a recent study published in Immunity showed that sustained ATF4 activation under hypoxia can promote CD8⁺ T-cell dysfunction, pointing to a potential mechanistic link between stress signaling and impaired anti-tumor immunity [15]. Collectively, these findings support the notion that ATF4-associated signaling plays an important role in osteosarcoma progression and provide a strong rationale for further investigating its biological function in this disease.

  Tumor-intrinsic WNT/β-catenin signaling is another pathway repeatedly implicated in immune exclusion, particularly through suppression of chemokine programs required for dendritic cell recruitment and CD8+ T-cell priming [16, 17]. In OS, WNT pathway activity has been tied to aggressive phenotypes and metastatic potential, yet how stress-responsive transcriptional circuits, especially the ATF/CREB family, interface with WNT signaling and immune suppression in specific malignant subpopulations remains insufficiently defined.

  In this study, we integrated scRNA-seq and bulk transcriptomic cohorts to map ATF/CREB family activity within the OS ecosystem and to identify tumor programs associated with adverse outcomes. We further constructed and validated a three-gene prognostic model derived from ATF3, ATF4 and CREB5, examined links between ATF4 and an immunosuppressive phenotype, and explored WNT/β-catenin pathway involvement at both cohort and cellular levels. Finally, leveraging drug-signature resources and structural modeling, we prioritized OS-relevant candidates with predicted ATF4 binding and performed molecular dynamics simulation for the ATF4-sorafenib complex, followed by functional validation of ATF4 in OS cell lines. This study was designed to clarify how ATF4-centered stress programs relate to immune suppression and poor prognosis in OS, and to evaluate whether these findings can inform therapeutic targeting.


## Methods

### Public datasets
  OS bulk transcriptome and clinical data were obtained from the TARGET cohort and GEO datasets (GSE39058, GSE21257, and GSE39508). An immunotherapy-treated cohort (IMvigor210) was used for external validation of immunotherapy-related associations. A public OS scRNA-seq dataset (GSE162454) was also obtained from GEO. All datasets were processed according to the corresponding platform specifications.


### Single-cell RNA-seq analysis
  Single-cell RNA-seq data were analyzed in R using Seurat. Cells expressing fewer than 200 genes or more than 8,000 genes, as well as cells with mitochondrial transcript proportions greater than 20%, were excluded. Potential doublets were removed using DoubletFinder. After normalization, dimensionality reduction was performed by PCA, and the top 30 principal components were used for clustering and UMAP visualization. Batch effects were corrected using Harmony. Cell clusters were identified by shared nearest neighbor graph–based clustering (resolution = 0.5) and annotated according to canonical marker genes. An ATF/CREB/BATF family gene set (Supplementary Table S1) was scored using Seurat’s module-scoring approach. Malignant osteosarcoma cells were identified using CopyKAT and subset for downstream analyses. Tumor programs were identified by cNMF. Candidate ranks were compared by stability and reconstruction error, and k = 5 was selected based on robustness, model fit, and biological interpretability. Differential expression analysis was performed in Seurat using FindAllMarkers, with significance defined as adjusted P < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| > 1. Pathway and gene-set analyses were conducted using GSVA with MSigDB reference gene sets. Trajectory analysis was performed using Monocle2 (DDRTree). Regulon analysis was performed using SCENIC, and cell-cell communication was analyzed using CellChat, focusing on WNT and BMP signaling. Cell identities were assigned by manual inspection of canonical marker genes, including ALPL, RUNX2, and IBSP for osteoblastic OS cells. COL1A1, ACTA2, and VIM for CAFs. LYZ and CD68 for myeloid cells. CD2, CD3D, GNLY, and NKG7 for NK/T cells. CD79A and MS4A1 for B cells. IGHG1 and MZB1 for plasmocytes. ACP5 and CTSK for osteoclasts. and PLVAP, EGFL7, and CD34 for endothelial cells [7]. In addition, CopyKAT was used to infer large-scale CNVs and distinguish aneuploid malignant tumor cells from diploid non-malignant cells, thereby further validating the identification of malignant osteoblastic OS cells.


### Prognostic modeling and survival analyses
  Intersection of C1 program marker genes with the ATF/CREB family identified ATF3, ATF4, and CREB5, which were used as candidate features for prognostic model construction. Following a previously reported machine learning-based integrative strategy, multiple candidate algorithm combinations were trained in the TARGET cohort and evaluated using a leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) framework [18]. Model performance was assessed by the concordance index (C-index), and the optimal model was defined as the algorithm combination with the highest average cross-validated C-index. The selected model was then used to construct the ATF/CREB-related signature (ACS), and patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups according to the median ACS value in the TARGET cohort. The same formula derived from the training cohort was then applied to the external validation cohorts (GSE39058 and GSE21257). Patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups using median cutoff. Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival analysis, Cox regression, and time-dependent ROC analyses were performed to assess prognostic performance, and the model was validated in GSE39058 and GSE21257. Single-gene survival analyses for ATF3, ATF4, and CREB5 were conducted in TARGET and external cohorts.


### Clinical utility and immune microenvironment analyses
  Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed in TARGET to evaluate whether ATF4 provided independent prognostic value after adjustment for clinicopathological variables. We assessed potential clinical utility using decision curve analysis and nomogram calibration. Non-linear associations were explored using restricted cubic splines, and the proportional hazards assumption was examined using Schoenfeld residuals. Immune infiltration was estimated using ssGSEA and compared between ATF4-high and ATF4-low groups. StromalScore, ImmuneScore, ESTIMATEScore, and tumor purity were calculated using ESTIMATE. Immunotherapy-related metrics, including TIDE, MSI score, immune checkpoint gene expression, and ssGSEA-based immune function signatures were evaluated between ATF4 groups. In IMvigor210, we grouped samples by ATF4 expression and then compared response patterns and survival outcomes between groups.


### Cell culture and functional assays
  Human OS cell lines (U2OS, MG63, and HOS) were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. For ATF4 knockdown experiments, U2OS and MG63 cells were transfected with two independent siRNAs targeting ATF4 (siATF4#1 and siATF4#2) or a non-targeting siRNA control (siControl). For ATF4 overexpression experiments, HOS cells were transfected with an ATF4 expression plasmid or the corresponding empty vector. For rescue experiments, U2OS and MG63 cells were co-transfected with siATF4 and an ATF4 expression construct. Protein expression was analyzed by western blotting using tubulin as the loading control, and band intensity was quantified by densitometry normalized to tubulin. Cell proliferation was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay by measuring OD450 at the indicated time points (0–72 h), and proliferative activity was further assessed by EdU incorporation assay. Clonogenic potential was determined by colony formation assay. Cell migratory ability was assessed using Transwell migration chambers. WNT/β-catenin pathway activity was evaluated by measuring the protein expression of β-catenin, CyclinD1, and c-MYC. All in vitro experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates.


### Statistical analysis
  All statistical analyses were performed using R and GraphPad Prism. For transcriptomic analyses, comparisons between two groups, including differences in pathway scores, immune-related signatures, and other continuous variables, were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test unless otherwise indicated. Comparisons among three or more groups were performed using one-way ANOVA for normally distributed data or the Kruskal-Wallis test for non-parametric data. Correlations between continuous variables were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation. Survival differences between groups were evaluated using the Kaplan-Meier method with the log-rank test, and prognostic associations were further assessed using univariate and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression, with results reported as hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For in vitro assays, comparisons between two groups were analyzed using Student’s t-test, whereas comparisons among three groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparison test, as appropriate. Quantitative data are presented as mean ± SD from three independent biological replicates, unless otherwise stated. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.


## Results

### Cell atlas and malignant transcriptional programs in osteosarcoma
  Figure 1 summarizes the cellular landscape of the OS tumor microenvironment and the distribution of ATF/CREB family-related transcriptional programs. UMAP clustering separated malignant osteoblastic OS cells from multiple stromal and immune populations, including cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), endothelial cells, myeloid cells, NK/T cells, B cells, plasmocytes, and osteoclasts. Cell identities were supported by canonical marker expression (Fig. 1A, B). When we examined a composite ATF/CREB/BATF signature across cell types, its activity varied markedly. The strongest enrichment was seen in CAFs and myeloid cells, followed by osteoblastic tumor cells and endothelial cells (Fig. 1C). Consistent with this pattern, dot plots showed that immune cells mainly expressed BATF family members, whereas tumor and stromal compartments had higher expression of ATF and CREB transcription factors (Fig. 1D). We then focused on malignant cells. Rank-selection analysis supported k = 5 as the optimal cNMF solution, as this value retained maximal stability while achieving lower reconstruction error than smaller-rank solutions (Figure S1). cNMF analysis separated them into five programs (C1-C5), each with distinct transcriptional features (Fig. 1E, F). Pathway enrichment linked these five programs to distinct biological states: stress and unfolded protein response signaling (C1), extracellular matrix organization (C2), osteogenic differentiation (C3), cell cycle/mitosis (C4), and immune-related processes (C5) (Fig. 1G). Pseudotime analysis placed the programs at different positions along a branched differentiation trajectory, which is consistent with dynamic transcriptional reprogramming during tumor progression (Fig. 1H-M).

## Regulon specificity and intercellular signaling of ATF-associated malignant programs
  Figure 2 summarizes pathway activity, candidate transcriptional regulators, and cell-cell communication across the five tumor subclusters. Pathway scoring showed clear differences among the subclusters. The stress-associated C1 cluster had high p53 and hypoxia signaling, whereas the osteogenic C3 cluster showed stronger WNT and TGF-β pathway activity (Fig. 2A). SCENIC analysis further identified cluster-biased regulons. ATF2 and TP53 were among the top regulators in C1, cell-cycle regulators such as E2F and FOXM1 were enriched in C4, and NF-κB/IRF regulons were most prominent in the immune-related C5 program (Fig. 2B-F). CellChat analysis indicated that C1 had the strongest outgoing signaling activity, while the other tumor subclusters more often acted as signal receivers (Fig. 2G). This pattern was also evident in the network and chord plots, where C1 appeared as a major source of WNT and BMP signaling and communicated broadly with other tumor subclusters and stromal cells (Fig. 2H-M). Ligand-receptor analysis further identified multiple WNT and BMP ligand-receptor pairs originating from C1, supporting its role as a major signaling hub in the OS microenvironment (Fig. 2N).

### Prognostic significance of the ATF/CREB-associated C1 tumor program
  To develop a clinically applicable prognostic signature linked to the C1 tumor program, we first intersected C1 marker genes with the ATF/CREB family and identified three overlapping transcription factors, ATF3, ATF4, and CREB5 (Fig. 3A). These three genes were then used as candidate features for subsequent prognostic model construction. We next evaluated multiple machine-learning algorithm combinations to establish the ATF/CREB-related signature (ACS) in the OS cohort under a unified LOOCV framework. Model performance was compared using the C-index, and the best-performing model was selected according to the highest average cross-validated C-index. The selected model was then used to derive the ACS for subsequent prognostic stratification and external validation (Fig. 3B). Patients were stratified into high- and low-ACS groups according to the median ACS. Kaplan-Meier (KM) analysis demonstrated significantly poorer survival in the high-risk group in the TARGET cohort. Time-dependent ROC analysis further indicated favorable predictive accuracy, with AUCs of 0.799, 0.904, and 0.958 at 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively (Fig. 3C). To assess robustness, the same model and cutoff strategy were applied to two independent validation cohorts. In GSE39058, the risk score remained significantly associated with overall survival, with time-dependent AUCs of 0.773, 0.695, and 0.918 at 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively (Fig. 3D). In GSE21257, patients classified as high risk also showed significantly worse survival, and the ROC analysis yielded an AUC of 0.882 at 3 years (Fig. 3E). Across the validation cohorts, the ATF3-ATF4-CREB5 signature retained prognostic value, supporting its robustness in OS.

### Single-gene prognostic value of ATF3, ATF4, and CREB5 across OS cohorts
  Given that ATF3, ATF4, and CREB5 were selected for prognostic model construction, we next assessed the prognostic relevance and predictive performance of each gene individually. In the TARGET cohort, KM analysis showed that high ATF4 expression was significantly associated with worse survival, whereas ATF3 and CREB5 did not display significant survival differences between high- and low-expression groups (Fig. 4A). Time-dependent ROC analyses further supported the superior prognostic discrimination of ATF4 compared with the other two genes in TARGET, with AUCs of 0.733 (1-year), 0.715 (3-year), and 0.725 (5-year), while ATF3 showed limited predictive ability and CREB5 exhibited poor performance (Fig. 4B). External validation confirmed the cohort-consistent association of ATF4 with adverse outcomes. In GSE39508, high ATF4 expression remained significantly associated with worse survival, while ATF3 and CREB5 were not significant (Fig. 4C). In GSE21257, both ATF4 and ATF3 showed significant associations with survival, whereas CREB5 was not significant (Fig. 4D). Taken together, ATF4 showed the most consistent prognostic association among the three genes across datasets, which led us to focus on ATF4 in the subsequent mechanistic analyses.

### Independent prognostic value and clinical utility assessment of ATF4 in TARGET cohort
  Building on the cross-cohort results for ATF4, we asked whether ATF4 adds prognostic information beyond routine clinicopathological factors in the TARGET cohort. In univariate Cox analysis, higher ATF4 expression was significantly associated with worse overall survival (HR = 1.008, 95% CI: 1.000-1.016, P = 0.046), whereas age, sex, and primary status were not significant (Fig. 5A). After adjustment for these variables in multivariable Cox models, ATF4 remained significant (HR = 1.013, 95% CI: 1.004–1.023, P = 0.006), indicating that its prognostic effect was not fully explained by standard clinical factors (Fig. 5B). We next used decision curve analysis (DCA) to estimate potential clinical benefit at 1, 3, and 5 years. Across a wide range of threshold probabilities, models including ATF4 showed greater net benefit than the “treat-all” and “treat-none” strategies, and performed similarly to or better than models using clinical variables alone (Fig. 5C-E). Nomogram calibration curves showed good agreement between predicted and observed survival probabilities at multiple time points (Fig. 5F). We also evaluated the functional form of ATF4 and checked model assumptions. Restricted cubic spline analysis suggested a non-linear relationship between ATF4 expression and hazard (Fig. 5G). Schoenfeld residual testing showed a borderline deviation from the proportional hazards assumption for ATF4 (Fig. 5H), indicating that possible time-varying effects should be considered in later modeling.

### Association of ATF4 with immune infiltration, immune functional states, and immunotherapy-related metrics
  Given the independent prognostic value of ATF4 (Fig. 5), we next investigated whether ATF4 expression was associated with an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Immune deconvolution analyses showed that ATF4-high tumors exhibited altered immune infiltration patterns compared with ATF4-low tumors (Fig. 6A, B). Consistently, ESTIMATE analysis indicated that the ATF4-high group displayed lower ImmuneScore, StromalScore, and ESTIMATEScore, together with higher tumor purity (Fig. 6C), supporting reduced immune/stromal components in ATF4-high tumors. To further characterize immune suppression/escape features, we assessed immunotherapy-related metrics. The ATF4-high group showed higher TIDE score and significant differences in TME-related scores and predicted responder proportion (Fig. 6D), along with differences in TIDE-derived dysfunction/exclusion components and MSI score (Fig. 6E). In parallel, multiple inhibitory immune checkpoint/immunosuppressive genes were significantly different between ATF4-high and ATF4-low groups (Fig. 6F), and ssGSEA-based immune functional signatures related to immune regulation/checkpoint activity also differed accordingly (Fig. 6G). Importantly, in the immunotherapy-treated IMvigor210 cohort, ATF4 stratification was associated with different response distributions (Fig. 6H), and ATF4-high cases showed significantly worse survival (Fig. 6I). Overall, ATF4-high tumors displayed transcriptomic and immune-inference features consistent with features of immune suppression and immune evasion, in line with the poorer survival observed in this group.

### ATF4-targeting drug prioritization and molecular dynamics validation
  Because ATF4 was linked to both prognosis and immune suppression in OS, we next tested whether clinically relevant OS drugs might directly interact with ATF4. We used this analysis to identify a practical starting point for targeting the ATF4 axis. To this end, we performed molecular docking for four OS-relevant drugs, including niclosamide, pyrvinium, nitazoxanide, and sorafenib. Docking analyses suggested that all four compounds could be accommodated within the predicted ATF4 binding pocket, forming hydrogen-bond and hydrophobic interactions. Sorafenib had the best docking score among the four compounds (BE = -7.2 kcal/mol), outperforming pyrvinium (BE = -6.1 kcal/mol) and showing stronger predicted binding than niclosamide (BE = -5.1 kcal/mol) or nitazoxanide (BE = -5.0 kcal/mol) (Fig. 7A-D). On this basis, we selected sorafenib for molecular dynamics simulations to examine whether the interaction remains stable under dynamic conditions. Molecular dynamics simulations of the ATF4-sorafenib complex indicated that, after an initial equilibration phase, the system reached a relatively stable conformational state, as reflected by the RMSD and radius of gyration (Rg) trajectories (Fig. 7E, F). SASA decreased early and then fluctuated within a narrower range (Fig. 7G). RMSF analysis showed that larger fluctuations were mainly confined to flexible regions, while the putative binding region remained comparatively stable (Fig. 7H). Hydrogen-bond tracking also showed persistent ATF4-sorafenib hydrogen bonding during much of the simulation (Fig. 7I). The free-energy landscape was dominated by a low-energy basin (Fig. 7J), consistent with a preferred conformational state. MM/PBSA analysis supported favorable overall binding, with nonpolar/van der Waals interactions contributing most of the stabilizing effect. Per-residue decomposition further identified residues with notable contributions to sorafenib stabilization within the binding pocket (Fig. 7K, L). Together, these in silico results prioritized sorafenib as the leading candidate, with favorable predicted ATF4 binding and stable simulation behavior. Experimental validation is still needed to confirm direct ATF4-drug interaction and to determine whether this interaction can be therapeutically leveraged in OS.

### Association of ACS risk and ATF4 with WNT/β-catenin signaling
  To examine signaling differences associated with the ACS-defined high-risk state, we analyzed WNT/β-catenin pathway activity and key downstream readouts. Compared with the ACS-low group, the ACS-high group had higher WNT pathway scores and increased expression of canonical WNT target genes, including CyclinD1, c-MYC, and β-catenin (Fig. 8A). WNT pathway activity was positively correlated with ACS, and ACS also correlated positively with CyclinD1, c-MYC, and β-catenin expression (Fig. 8B), indicating coordinated upregulation of WNT/β-catenin signaling in high-risk tumors. Enrichment analysis likewise highlighted WNT-related pathways and β-catenin-associated components, together with adhesion/junction terms (Fig. 8C), supporting activation of WNT/β-catenin-linked programs in the high-risk setting.

  We then assessed whether these patterns were related to ATF4, which emerged as the most consistent prognostic marker. WNT pathway activity was higher in the ATF4-high group (Fig. 8D), and ATF4 expression was positively correlated with WNT pathway activity (Fig. 8E). Correlation heatmap analysis further showed significant positive associations between ATF4 and multiple WNT/β-catenin-related molecules (Fig. 8F). Overall, both the ACS high-risk phenotype and ATF4-high status were associated with increased WNT/β-catenin signaling.

### ATF4 knockdown suppresses OS cell proliferation and migration, accompanied by attenuation of WNT/β-catenin signaling
  We first examined ATF4 expression in several OS cell lines. ATF4 protein was detectable in all tested lines and was higher in U2OS and MG63, so these two lines were used for the loss-of-function experiments (Fig. 9A). ATF4 was knocked down with two independent siRNAs (siATF4#1 and siATF4#2), and western blotting confirmed efficient silencing in both U2OS and MG63 compared with siControl cells (Fig. 9B, C).

  CCK-8 assays showed that ATF4 knockdown reduced cell growth over time in both cell lines (Fig. 9D, E). Colony formation assays also showed reduced clonogenic capacity, with fewer colonies and lower colony counts after ATF4 silencing (Fig. 9F, G). In addition, EdU incorporation assays further confirmed that ATF4 depletion suppressed proliferative activity in U2OS and MG63 cells (Fig. 9H, I). We next assessed cell migration using Transwell assays. In both U2OS and MG63 cells, ATF4 knockdown significantly reduced the number of migrated cells compared with the control group (Fig. 9J, L). To connect these phenotypic changes with the transcriptomic findings, we further examined key components of the WNT/β-catenin pathway. Western blot analysis showed that ATF4 silencing reduced β-catenin protein expression and downregulated its downstream targets CyclinD1 and c-MYC in both U2OS and MG63 cells (Fig. 9K, M). Taken together, these results indicate that loss of ATF4 suppresses OS cell proliferation, clonogenicity, and migration, accompanied by attenuation of WNT/β-catenin signaling.

### ATF4 overexpression in HOS cells promotes proliferation, migration, and WNT/β-catenin signaling
  To complement the knockdown data, we performed gain-of-function experiments in HOS cells, which exhibited relatively low basal ATF4 expression. Western blotting confirmed successful ATF4 overexpression in HOS cells transfected with the ATF4 expression construct compared with vector controls (Fig. 10A). CCK-8 assays showed that ATF4 overexpression significantly increased HOS cell growth over time (Fig. 10B). Consistently, EdU incorporation assays demonstrated enhanced proliferative activity in oe-ATF4 cells compared with vector-transfected controls (Fig. 10C). Colony formation assays further showed that ATF4 overexpression markedly increased clonogenic capacity, as reflected by more colonies and higher colony counts (Fig. 10D). We next evaluated migratory ability using Transwell assays. ATF4-overexpressing HOS cells exhibited significantly increased migration capacity, with more cells than the vector control group (Fig. 10E). To further link these phenotypic changes to the transcriptomic findings, we examined key components of the WNT/β-catenin pathway. Western blot analysis showed that ATF4 overexpression increased β-catenin protein levels and upregulated the downstream effectors CyclinD1 and c-MYC (Fig. 10F). Taken together, these gain-of-function results are consistent with the knockdown findings and support a role for ATF4 in promoting OS cell proliferation, clonogenicity, and migration, accompanied by activation of WNT/β-catenin signaling.

### ATF4 re-expression partially rescues the inhibitory effects of ATF4 knockdown on OS cell clonogenicity and migration
  To further strengthen the causal link between ATF4 depletion and the observed phenotypes, we performed rescue experiments in U2OS and MG63 cells by re-expressing ATF4 after siRNA-mediated knockdown. Western blotting showed that ATF4 protein levels were markedly reduced by siATF4 and were partially restored after co-transfection with the ATF4 overexpression construct in both U2OS and MG63 cells (Fig. S2A, B). Functionally, colony formation assays showed that ATF4 knockdown significantly reduced clonogenic capacity in both U2OS and MG63 cells, whereas ATF4 re-expression partially rescued this effect, resulting in increased colony numbers compared with the siATF4 group (Fig. S2C, E). Similarly, Transwell migration assays demonstrated that ATF4 silencing markedly impaired the migratory ability of both cell lines, while re-expression of ATF4 significantly restored migration relative to the knockdown group (Fig. S2D, F).


## Discussion
  We integrated public bulk OS cohorts with single-cell transcriptomic data to evaluate ATF/CREB-family activity at cell-type resolution. Across datasets, higher ATF/CREB activity—particularly ATF4—was linked to malignant heterogeneity, an immunosuppressive microenvironment, and poorer clinical outcomes. Using malignant-cell programs derived from the single-cell data, we also constructed a compact three-gene signature (ATF3/ATF4/CREB5) that stratified prognosis in multiple independent cohorts. Clinical outcomes in OS remain poor, especially in metastatic and relapsed disease [19]. Immunotherapy has likewise produced limited and inconsistent benefit in unselected OS populations [20, 21]. For this reason, the OS immune microenvironment is not just background context but a central part of the disease biology. Previous studies have shown that the bone niche often contains suppressive myeloid programs and weak cytotoxic immune infiltration [22]. Our cohort-level findings are consistent with this picture and point to a role for tumor-intrinsic stress programs in shaping immune escape.

  ATF4 is a major effector of the ISR [23]. Through ISR signaling, cells adapt to hypoxia, nutrient limitation, oxidative stress, and proteotoxic stress by remodeling metabolism and transcription [24]. In cancer, these adaptive responses can become sustained and incorporated into the malignant state rather than remaining transient. Prior work has identified the eIF2α-ATF4 axis as a central route linking stress sensing to survival programs [25, 26]. In OS, ATF4 has also been implicated in tumor progression. For example, Zeng and colleagues reported that ATF4 promotes OS progression and metastasis through crosstalk with MTA1/HDAC1 [27]. Our functional data are in line with this: silencing ATF4 in MG63 and U2OS cells reduced proliferation, clonogenicity, and migration, whereas ectopic ATF4 expression in HOS cells increased these behaviors.

  One of the clearest findings in our cohort analyses was the association between high ATF4 expression and an immunosuppressive phenotype. ATF4-high tumors showed patterns consistent with immune dysfunction and immune exclusion, and they also had poorer survival. This is broadly consistent with recent work linking persistent ATF4 activity to impaired anti-tumor immunity. For example, a recent Immunity study showed that chronic ATF4 activation under intratumoral hypoxia drives mitochondrial oxidative stress, loss of viability, and dysfunction in CD8⁺ T cells [15]. However, the immune effects of ATF4 appear to be highly context-dependent rather than uniformly deleterious or beneficial. Other studies have suggested that, under certain stress conditions, ATF4 induction may help sustain CD8⁺ T-cell survival and activity, presumably by supporting adaptive metabolic programs during transient stress [28]. Taken together, these apparently divergent findings may reflect differences in the duration and intensity of ISR activation, as well as the cellular compartment in which ATF4 is engaged. In this framework, acute or adaptive ATF4 signaling may help immune cells cope with metabolic stress, whereas chronic ATF4 activation in a hypoxic tumor microenvironment may favor T-cell dysfunction and immune escape. In OS, our data provide cohort-level evidence that ATF4-high status is associated with an immunosuppressive phenotype. However, they do not distinguish whether this pattern is driven primarily by tumor-intrinsic ATF4, immune-cell-intrinsic ATF4, or both. This issue warrants direct mechanistic investigation in future studies.

  We also observed a consistent relationship between ATF4 and WNT/β-catenin signaling. This pathway has established roles in immune exclusion across cancers, and tumor-intrinsic β-catenin activation can weaken anti-tumor immunity by limiting dendritic-cell recruitment and impairing T-cell priming [16, 29]. In OS, WNT/β-catenin signaling has also been associated with aggressive behavior and metastasis [30]. In our study, WNT pathway activity was higher in both the ATF4-high group and the ACS high-risk group, and this increase was accompanied by elevated β-catenin, CyclinD1, and c-MYC. These data do not prove a direct causal link, but they are compatible with a model in which ATF4-high tumors tend to adopt a WNT/β-catenin-active state that is compatible with both malignant progression and immune suppression.

  To assess translational potential, we combined drug-signature enrichment with structural modeling to prioritize compounds for follow-up testing. Enrichr and DSigDB are commonly used to connect gene sets with perturbagen signatures and to generate testable drug hypotheses [31–33]. From the enrichment output, we selected four OS-relevant candidates for further analysis: niclosamide, pyrvinium, nitazoxanide, and sorafenib. Previous studies have reported anti-OS activity for niclosamide and pyrvinium, with links to WNT signaling, and nitazoxanide has also been shown to inhibit OS growth and metastasis through AKT/mTOR and WNT/β-catenin pathways [34, 35].Sorafenib also has clinical support in relapsed or unresectable high-grade OS, including a phase II trial and a non-randomized phase II study in combination with everolimus [36, 37]. In our docking analysis, sorafenib showed the most favorable predicted binding energy among the four candidates and was therefore selected for molecular dynamics simulation. The simulation suggested a relatively stable interaction pattern over the analyzed time window. Even so, these computational results should be treated as prioritization evidence rather than proof of direct target engagement. Biophysical binding assays and cell-based target-validation experiments are still needed to determine whether ATF4 is a bona fide sorafenib-interacting protein in relevant OS settings.

  Several limitations should be noted. First, the cohort-level analyses were largely retrospective and based on public datasets, and thus prospective validation is still required. Second, although single-cell RNA sequencing improved the resolution of tumor and microenvironmental programs, it could not fully capture spatial organization, which will require spatial transcriptomic or multiplex imaging approaches. Third, our functional experiments support a role for ATF4 in osteosarcoma cell growth and migration, but they do not yet define the upstream triggers of ATF4 activation or the precise mechanisms connecting ATF4 with WNT/β-catenin signaling in different cellular compartments. In addition, the structural analyses are hypothesis-generating and require direct experimental validation. Finally, although our transcriptomic and immune-inference analyses consistently linked ATF4-high status to an immunosuppressive phenotype, these findings remain correlative. Because no in vivo immunocompetent model was included, we cannot conclude that ATF4 directly drives immune escape in osteosarcoma. Future studies using ATF4 perturbation in immunocompetent mouse models will be needed to establish causality and further define the underlying mechanisms.


## Conclusion
  In summary, our findings suggest that ATF4-high OS is associated with an immunosuppressive phenotype and poor prognosis, accompanied by activation of WNT/β-catenin signaling and a potential therapeutic association with OS-relevant drugs. These results provide a rationale for future studies to determine whether targeting the ATF4/ISR axis—potentially in combination with strategies modulating WNT signaling and immune checkpoints—may improve outcomes in molecularly defined subsets of OS.
