# Molecular mechanism leading to human coronary atherosclerosis assessed by proteomic analysis and RNA sequences


## Background and Aims
  Atherosclerosis results from cellular and extracellular changes in the arterial wall, preceded by molecular shifts that initiate disease and drive tissue conversion, yet these changes are not yet fully described. More data are needed concerning these early changes in the coronary artery molecular landscape that signify the initiation of atherosclerosis and the subsequent tissue pheno-conversion to atherosclerotic plaque. This report summarizes results from a large biorepository of human coronary artery tissue, applying state-of-the-art omics technology, advanced data analytic methods, and an arterial organoid model system to predict molecular dynamics and identify potential regulatory mechanisms that could interrupt molecular changes that contribute to the earliest stages of disease pathogenesis. The long-term goal of this effort is to identify and develop new therapies to further mitigate the persistently high burden of clinical coronary disease.


## Methods
  Mass spectrometry-based proteomic analysis and RNA sequencing (RNASeq) were used to analyse proximal coronary arterial samples from young adults who died of trauma with no ante mortem suspicion of coronary disease [n = 322, mean age (range): 34.1 years (15–59); sex: M-239, F-83; race: W-218, B-88, other-16]. Despite the absence of clinical disease, 56% of samples had morphologic evidence of pre-clinical atherosclerosis. Analyses of the proteomic data (n = 1900 proteins) using state-of-the-art dimensionality reduction and deconvolution techniques generated an estimate of molecular disease progression (e.g. pseudo-time) and identified selected proteomic latent features (LFs) (i.e. large groups of co-ordinated proteins) associated with its initiation and progression. Computational genomics, machine learning models, and multi-omic network mapping of these proteomic LFs and associated mRNA gene transcripts suggested potential transcriptional regulators which were subsequently confirmed in publicly available single-cell coronary artery data. The effects of one of the leading regulatory transcription factors (TFs), MLXIPL, predicted to regulate two LFs, were further validated in a human arterial cell organoid model system.


## Results
  Four proteomic LFs, composed of n = 100 signature proteins/LF, exhibited distinct patterns with respect to disease progression [false discovery rate (FDR) P < .01]. These LFs illuminate the earliest changes in the arterial proteome during tissue pheno-conversion from normal coronary artery to atherosclerotic plaque, including dramatic declines in mitochondrial energy biosynthesis proteins, evidence of vascular unit activation (including pericytes), and neurovascular and neuroimmune modulation (all FDR P < .01). These early changes preceded the expected immune cell recruitment and innate immune response characteristic of atherosclerotic plaque formation. Analysis of transcriptional regulatory networks identified from RNASeq data highlighted both known and novel TFs and master regulators of LF proteins that may drive the initial and early stages of disease progression. Publicly available single-cell RNASeq data from normal and atherosclerotic coronary arteries validated the LFs and several of their likely master transcriptional regulators (all P < .01); and manipulation of the levels of one of top regulatory TFs, MLXIPL, in human arterial cell organoids resulted in the expected changes in expression of the proteins associated with its two targeted LFs (P = .0003 and P < .00001, respectively).


## Conclusions
  The unique nature of this human coronary biorepository with samples ranging from entirely normal to mature pre-clinical atherosclerotic plaque facilitated prediction of molecular disease progression and identification of several potential transcriptional regulators for further evaluation as potential novel targets to interrupt early initiation and progression of atherosclerotic coronary disease.


## Introduction
  Atherosclerosis is the consequence of complex changes in the cellular and extracellular composition of the arterial wall. These changes are preceded by alterations in the regional molecular landscape that signify the initiation of atherosclerosis and are responsible for the tissue pheno-conversion to follow. A deeper understanding of these early changes in the molecular landscape, and identification of novel strategies to interrupt them, remains a top priority in vascular biology because of the persistent global burden and clinical heterogeneity of ischaemic heart disease.1 This is especially true for coronary atherosclerosis, one of the most important manifestations of human atherosclerotic vascular disease.

  Current understanding of the temporal dynamics in the human coronary molecular landscape and their implications for the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis remains limited for three reasons. First, much of current understanding is generalized from model systems (e.g. murine, rabbit, etc.)2,3 and/or anatomic distributions (e.g. aortic, carotid, femoral)2–5 that may not fully represent human coronary pathophysiology.6,7 Second, most molecular profiling data from actual human atherosclerosis are limited to small numbers of subjects, non-coronary arterial samples, or tissue exclusively representing terminal stages of the disease.8–10 Third, few studies have combined transcriptional and proteomic profiling from the same samples or capitalized on newer concepts in high-dimensional data analytics designed to more completely characterize the co-ordinated dynamics of the hundreds to thousands of genes and proteins that define the arterial molecular landscape.

  To address these gaps, both coronary and aortic tissue were collected from autopsies of young adults who died of trauma with no ante-mortem suspicion of atherosclerotic vascular disease,11 and these tissues were interrogated with mass spectrometry (MS)-based proteomics and RNA sequencing (RNASeq). In the current report, in-depth molecular characterization of n = 397 proximal left coronary arteries representing the full range of atherosclerosis disease progression from completely normal to advanced atherosclerotic plaque is presented. Because of the potentially large number of proteins involved in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis, specialized dimensional reduction (i.e. deconvolution) and pattern recognition (i.e. manifold learning) tools were used to map the proteomic landscape across the range of human coronary tissues phenotypes from normal to disease. This map of the ‘pseudo-temporal’ progression of disease was then used to identify unique groups of proteins, collectively referred to as ‘latent features’ (LFs), whose changes are most informative about the stages of disease progression, and examined the RNA data to identify potential regulatory molecules [e.g. transcription factors (TFs)] that may drive these changes. The same deconvolution technique, developed by this group,12–14 was also used previously to study vascular tissue dynamics in human aortic tissues.15,16 The association of the top TFs with atherosclerosis was validated in external single-cell RNASeq databases, and the expected effects of one of the most promising transcription factors on expression of proteomic LFs linked to early disease initiation were confirmed in human arterial cell organoids.

  Collectively, these data provide important insights into the regulation of the molecular features associated with the earliest stages of initiation and progression of coronary atherosclerosis. If validated in further in vitro, pre-clinical and clinical testing, the regulatory targets identified here could represent new class(es) of therapies designed to prevent or treat premature coronary atherosclerosis.


## Methods
  The overall study design is illustrated in Figure 1 and detailed methods are provided in the Supplementary data online. Briefly, human arterial samples, including left main and proximal left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery segments, were acquired from male and female medico-legal cases in the Jefferson Parrish Coroner’s Office (New Orleans, LA, USA) under a protocol approved by the Louisiana State University (LSU) institutional review board. Post-mortem interval (PMI) was calculated as the minutes between the officially recorded time of death and the subsequently recorded time of autopsy, during which time the deceased study subjects were kept in a refrigerated environment. All samples were graded by a vascular pathologist for per cent intimal surface area involvement of fatty streaks (FS), fibrous plaques (FP), and complicated or calcified lesions and subsequently frozen at −80C. Selected arterial sections underwent protein extraction (n = 291), or protein plus RNA extraction (n = 106), and the molecular extracts were analysed using data-independent acquisition MS proteomics and mRNA sequencing. The raw proteomics and RNASeq data, and associated metadata, are available in the MassIVE and the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) data repositories, respectively (see Supplementary data online, Table S1). Extreme sample and individual peptide/protein outliers were removed, followed by normalization, batch corrections, and imputation. Quality control (QC) performance metrics and results of pre-processing for proteomic (see Supplementary data online, Figure S1A–G), including the identification of two outlier samples for removal using probabilistic principal components analysis (e.g. PCA; Supplementary data online, Figure S1F), and transcriptomic (see Supplementary data online, Figure S3) data are provided in the Supplementary data online. The relationship between PMI and proteomic and transcriptomic data quality indicators was assessed on all samples and is shown in the Supplementary data online, Figure S5.

  External validation of the proteomic LFs associated with different stages of disease progression and their candidate regulatory TFs was performed using publicly available single-cell transcriptomics data downloaded from the Plaqueview data repository.17 Two human datasets were accessed, one representing coronary artery samples with significant atherosclerotic lesions (n = 8)18 and a subset of another dataset with coronary arteries collected from failing hearts at the time of transplant which were not overtly atherosclerotic (n = 5) and thus serve here as controls.19 Since the normal coronary artery dataset had significantly more cells relative to the coronary artery disease (CAD) dataset, a random down-sampling was performed in Seurat20 to equalize the datasets to roughly 10 000 cells each. We then utilized the built-in Seurat merging and integration functions to produce a combined single-cell dataset object and performed clustering and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) dimensionality reduction to identify putative cell-type clusters. Markers for cell types published in the original studies as well as typical canonical cell-type markers [e.g. PECAM1 for endothelial cells (ECs), MYH11 for smooth muscle cells (SMCs), TREM2 for lipid-laden macrophages, etc.] were used to assign cell types to the UMAP clusters (see Supplementary data online, Figure S3). The integrated, clustered dataset was used to investigate cell-type specific disease expression patterns of the LFs and putative regulatory TFs identified from bulk tissue proteomic and RNASeq data (see below).


### Statistical analyses
  Tests for association of individual proteins with pathologist-derived tissue phenotype classification, as well as the continuous pseudo-temporal measure of molecular progression of disease (see below), were performed using weighted-ANOVA or weighted-linear regression models with a Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted P-value ≤.01 (SAS and JMP, Raleigh, NC, USA). For low abundant proteins (missingness >50%) proteins were encoded as present (1) or absent (0) and tested for association using the χ2 test. One-dimensional Potential of Heat diffusion for Affinity-based Transition Embedding dimensionality reduction (PHATE),21 a manifold learning tool, was used to generate a pseudo-temporal measure of molecular progression of samples, from most normal to most severely diseased, based on the proteomic landscape. Separately, unsupervised convex analysis of mixtures (CAM)13 deconvolution of the same proteomic data was used to identify the proteomic LFs (e.g. subsets of proteins with unique expression patterns indicative of co-regulation, see additional details above and in the Supplementary data online, Methods) and their top signature proteins, defined as the 100 proteins that were most strongly and uniquely aligned with a given LF (see Supplementary data online for details). For each sample, a single quantitative value representing the expression of each LF was generated by averaging the expression of its 100 signature proteins in that sample. In the case of the transcriptomic and single-cell datasets, the mRNA counts for the genes corresponding the LF signature proteins were used. Ontology mapping and enrichment analysis of LF signature proteins, including cell-type specific expression and Reactome pathway enrichment analyses, were performed using Web-based Cell Specific Enrichment Analysis (WebCSEA),22 ToppGene Suite,23 and Metascape.24

  All identified mRNA transcripts were queried against the Human Transcription Factor Database25 to identify a final list of 1385 quantified TFs. To investigate transcriptional regulatory mechanisms contributing to the proteomic LF dynamics, negative binomial regression was used to identify TFs whose mRNA expression was uniquely and significantly correlated with expression of each of the four atherosclerosis-associated proteomic LFs, and the individual LF-specific signature genes (SGs) and proteins. The lists of provisionally identified master regulators for each LF were further reduced using (i) computational regulatory genomics prediction database analysis (ISMARA26) to limit candidate TFs to those with proven binding motifs in the promoters of one or more LF-specific SGs and whose association with the individual features were directionally consistent with the overall relationship between TF expression and all genes with mappable promoter region binding sites for the same TF, and (ii) boosted trees and bootstrap forest machine learning models to select TFs independently associated with overall expression of each LF.27 The machine learning methods were employed to accommodate potentially complex non-linear relationships between expression TFs and their target LF(s). As a final step, provisional LF-specific master regulators were only retained if they also exhibited a similar relationship with expression of the same LFs in the external single-cell RNASeq dataset derived from atherosclerotic and normal human coronary arteries described above. The resulting list of TFs for each LF was used as input for construction of multi-layered gene regulatory networks (TF → SGmRNA → SGprotein, where ‘SG’ stands for ‘signature gene’)28 to identify and visualize the TFs independently associated with the LF SGs, and their cognate proteins.

  Data visualizations and other conventional statistical analyses were created or performed using JMP or Morpheus (https://software.broadinstitute.org/morpheus).


### Validation experiments
  Human arterial organoids were generated from a combination of SMC (40%), aortic fibroblast (40%), coronary ECs (8%), monocytes (11%), and microvascular pericytes (1%). After ∼3 days of co-culture, the self-organized spheroids were infected with one of three adenoviral vectors (AAV): Green Fluorescent Protein (GFP)-expressing control (GFP); exogenous MLXIPL-overexpression (OE); or an siRNA knockdown construct targeted against the MLXIPL gene (KD). Cellular composition of organoids was confirmed by immunohistochemistry (IHC), and successful targeting of MLXIPL gene expression was confirmed by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). Mass spectrometry proteomic analysis was performed to confirm LF expression and the effect of MLXIPL manipulation on LF-specific signature proteins. Since MLXIPL was only confidently identified in the OE samples of this discovery liquid chromatography (LC) MS proteomics experiment, a more sensitive targeted parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) acquisition was run on a separate aliquot of the same trypsin-digested lysate that confidently quantified three specific MLXIPL peptides from every organoid sample. Detailed methods on organoid generation, AAV infection, and MS are provided in the Supplementary data online.


## Results

### Study samples
  Left main and proximal LAD coronary artery segments were collected from 322 autopsies [mean age (range): 34.1 years (15–59); sex: M-239, F-83; race: W-218, B-88, other-16]. In 75 cases, both left main and proximal LAD samples were available, producing 397 anatomically distinct proximal coronary segments for study. Clinical characteristics, distributed by lesion classification, are shown in the Supplementary data online, Figure S4A. Twenty-nine per cent (n = 117) of the samples had FS, 27% (n = 108) had raised FP, 3% (n = 11) had calcification, and 1% (n = 5) had evidence of a complex (ulcerated) atherosclerotic lesion on some portion of the intimal surface (see Supplementary data online, Figure S4B). The distribution of sample types was not statistically different by PMI (P = .16; see Supplementary data online, Figure S5A). Weak and non-significant associations were observed between PMI and quality indicators of proteomic data (see Supplementary data online, Figure S5B) and a handful of weak, but statistically significant, negative associations between PMI and quality indicators of RNA (see Supplementary data online, Figure S5C); however, overall median intensities per sample for RNA or proteins were not significantly associated with PMI.


### Association of arterial proteins with pathologist-defined disease status
  After data pre-processing, 1900 proteins with <50% missingness were available for analysis in all 397 LAD samples (see Supplementary data online, Table S2). Of these, 831 proteins were associated with pathologist-defined disease status (FDR P-value <.01 in at least one model; Supplementary data online, Table S3). In a separate analysis of n = 294 low abundant proteins (>50% missingness), an additional 86 proteins were also associated with disease status (see Supplementary data online, Table S4). Among these 917 disease-associated proteins, 383 (41.7%) were not previously linked to ‘atherosclerosis’, ‘coronary artery disease’, or ‘heart disease’ in GENECARD, Uniprot, the Human Proteins Atlas, and PubMed (see Supplementary data online, Table S5, for gene ontology (GO) terms and tissue specificity for these novel atherosclerosis-associated proteins). The large percentage of measured arterial proteins associated with coronary atherosclerosis (917/2194 = 42%) emphasizes the extensive changes in the arterial proteomic landscape during early stages of atherosclerotic lesion development and highlights the large gaps in existing literature on the human coronary proteome.

  Database mapping of cell-type markers indicated that the sources for these disease-associated proteins include all major arterial cell types including the vascular unit (ECs, pericytes, and SMCs), the adventitia and inflammatory cells. Importantly, functional pathway enrichment analysis of these disease-associated proteins highlighted several pathways not typically associated with the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis including nervous system development and axon guidance, neutrophil degranulation, and cellular respiration (see Supplementary data online, Figure S6A–C).


### Molecular pseudo-temporal disease progression and prediction of proteomic molecular subtypes (e.g. latent features)
  To better estimate the dynamics of the arterial molecular landscape with respect to disease progression, a manifold learning approach based on non-linear principals (PHATE21) was applied to construct a metric of ‘pseudo-time’ (Figure 2A), using as input the atherosclerosis-associated proteins (n = 831 proteins and n = 397 samples) described above. The association between this molecular pseudo-time measure of disease progression and the simple presence or absence of any coronary lesion (FS or FP) was assessed. As expected, after adjustment for age and sex, the odds ratio (OR) for lesion vs non-lesion associated with a 1 standard (std) difference in PHATE was 2.67 (P < 1 × 10−11), offering preliminary validation for this proteomic definition of disease progression. This association was not confounded by proteomic digestion batch (see Supplementary data online, Figure S7A) and was robust to random sub-sampling of the proteins used to compute the PHATE score (see Supplementary data online, Figure S7B).

  Importantly, the molecular definition of progression was also correlated with age in both males and females (R2: M: 0.09. F: 0.22, P < .0001 for each; Figure 2C). Interestingly, females reached the same level of molecular disease progression at consistently older age than their male counterparts, shifted by ∼5 years through age 30 and up to 10 years by the fourth decade of life. These data are consistent with well-established relationships between age, sex, and atherosclerotic disease risk and offer further evidence of the practical validity of this measure of disease progression. They also suggest opportunities to study the tissue proteomic basis for the effects of these and other cardiovascular risk factors. Notably, after adjustment for age and sex, there was no residual statistically significant relationship between the measure of pre-clinical molecular disease progression and race/ethnicity (white vs non-white, P = .45); although it remains possible that there are molecular contributors to disease progression that arise at a later age than observed in these study subjects or due to social or environmental factors that come into play once clinical manifestations emerge (e.g. differences in access to care, etc.).29

  Next, unsupervised deconvolution (CAM) was performed to identify subsets of co-regulated proteins that might be uniquely informative about stages of disease progression and the specific cell types responsible for expression of these proteins. CAM analysis identified seven distinct proteomic LFs (see Supplementary data online, Figure S7) and their corresponding signature proteins (Figure 2D and E, and Supplementary data online, Table S6). The average expression of the top 100 signature proteins for each LF was used to quantify the LFs’ overall expression. The expression of four of the identified seven proteomic LFs (LF2, LF4, LF6, and LF7) was significantly correlated with molecular progression of disease (R2: LF2: 0.22, LF4: 0.71, LF6: 0.17, LF7: 0.11; FDR P < .0001 each; Figure 2F and G). In the subset of samples with both protein and RNASeq data (n = 106; Supplementary data online, Table S7), the average expression of mRNA transcripts of the genes encoding LF2, LF4, and LF7 signature proteins were generally well correlated with the average expression of the corresponding signature proteins themselves (see Supplementary data online, Figure S8), providing further validation of the distinct and molecularly coherent nature of these LFs. In contrast, there was poor correlation between the averages of LF6 signature proteins and their corresponding mRNAs, suggesting additional post-transcriptional mechanisms contributing to the concentration of these signature proteins in coronary arterial tissue. Importantly, adjustment for PMI had no significant impact on the estimate of pseudo-temporal disease progression or the relationship between the proteomic LFs with this molecular disease progression score (see Supplementary data online, Figure S9A). Furthermore, the association of LF with and pseudo-temporal disease progression was not different by sex (see Supplementary data online, Figure S9B).

  The expression of two of the LFs, LF6 (green) and LF2 (red), declines early in the pseudo-temporal projections of atherosclerotic disease progression. The signature proteins for LF6 were enriched with mitochondrial proteins involved with β-oxidation and oxidative phosphorylation (e.g. ACAA2, multiple cytochrome-c oxidase subunits,30) particularly in pericytes and vascular SMCs (VSMCs) (Figure 3A and B). LF2 signature proteins are recognized for their role in cell–cell and cell–matrix interactions including L1CAM—a neural adhesion molecule with effects on axon migration and growth as well as tumour metastasis in various non-nervous system cancer cell types.31,32 In human vascular tissues, these LF2 SGs are enriched for expression in adventitial fibroblasts and stromal cells.

  The early changes in LF2 and LF6 were followed by a dramatic increase in LF4 (blue) composed of the expected inflammatory proteins expressed by lipid-laden macrophages and other antigen-presenting cells—including CD14 and CD163 expressing cells, among many others. The final LF, LF7, had a biphasic pattern with an initial increase, followed by a dramatic decrease in expression from the most normal to the most abnormal arterial samples. This LF is characterized by VSMC proteins, including several indicative of a contractile phenotype (e.g. CNN1 and SMTN33), and its biphasic response is consistent with early VSMC proliferation and migratory phenotype34 followed by pheno-conversion to a macrophage-like phenotype in more well-established atherosclerotic lesions.35,36 Results of the full Reactome functional enrichment analysis of all the athero-associated LF signature proteins are provided in the Supplementary data online, Tables S8–S11.


### Independent confirmation of the human coronary latent feature dynamics and cell-type specificity
  To confirm the general patterns of association between the proteomic LFs and disease status, the average expression of the mRNA species was computed for each of the LF signature proteins in cells from the atherosclerotic and normal coronary artery samples from Wirka et al.18 and Hu et al.,19 respectively. In general, the initially observed association of LF expression with disease was confirmed in the single-cell RNASeq data, providing independent validation of their informativeness with respect to human coronary atherosclerosis (see Supplementary data online, Figure S10). The aggregated signal for the SGs for LFs LF2, LF4, LF6, and LF7 was projected onto the clustered UMAPs of the integrated single-cell dataset and compared cell-specific LF expression between normal and CAD samples (Figure 4A–E). This analysis illuminated several interesting observations concerning cell-type specific expression of LF markers in normal and CAD samples. For instance, LF2 was significantly expressed in adventitial nerve and fibroblasts, as well as ECs; and while LF2 was decreased overall in the CAD samples, its expression was increased in the low abundant nerve cells—providing a possible explanation of L1CAM as a leading signature protein in this LF. In another interesting juxtaposition, LF7 expression was dominated by expression in SMCs and pericytes; but while SGs for this LF were dramatically decreased in SMCs from CAD samples, they were increased in pericytes which were also significantly more numerous in the CAD samples. Likewise, the largely inflammatory LF4 SGs were dramatically up-regulated in the lipid-laden macrophages, plasma cells, and B-cells in the CAD samples, although there was a broad-based increase in expression of these genes in most resident cell types in the artery wall—suggesting multi-cellular contributions to the inflammatory states widely shown in the literature to occur in the later stages of disease progression. In contrast, the LF6 genes responsible for energy substrate oxidation and cellular respiration which were, unsurprisingly, constitutively expressed by most cell types were broadly depressed in CAD samples compared with cells from normal coronary samples—with the singular exception of lipid-laden macrophages.


### Identification of potential latent feature-specific regulatory transcription factors
  In a next series of analyses, TFs from bulk RNAseq data that had significant correlations with proteomic LFs LF2, LF4, LF6, or LF7 and their individual signature proteins were identified (see Supplementary data online, Figure S11A–D and Tables S12–S16). Subsequently these lists were narrowed to a smaller subset with the highest probability of being regulatory by requiring the TFs to also display (i) strong correlation between TF expression and LF SG expression in bulk RNASeq data (Figures 5A, 6A, 7A, and 8A); (ii) strong correlation between TF expression and ISMARA predicted TF activity in bulk RNASeq data (Figures 5B, 6B, 7B, and 8B); (iii) strong and consistent correlation between TF expression and LF marker gene expression in the external single-cell validation dataset (Figures 5C, 6C, 7C, and 8C, and Supplementary data online, Figure S12) . This resulted in n = 4 TFs for LF6, n = 5 TFs for LF2 and LF4, and n = 7 TFs for LF7. Importantly, none of these TFs were identified as LF signature proteins in the proteomic analysis.

  Projection of the expression of these TFs onto single-cell clusters indicated that among the top LF2 regulators (MLXIPL, FOXO4, SOX9, TEAD1, and SREBF2), two appear to be predominantly expressed in fibroblasts (MLXIPL and SOX9), while the other three are more global in expression across several cell types (Figure 5D). For LF4, four of the five top potentially regulatory TFs (RUNX2, RUNX3, FOXM1, and SPI1) were clearly dominant in immune cells, while the fifth (SP100) was widely expressed across all cell types (Figure 6D). For LF6, the regulatory TFs exhibited enhanced expression in fibroblasts (MXLIPL), combined fibroblasts and SMCs (MESP1), ECs (TAL1), or were non-specific to most cell types (TCF4) (Figure 7D). Finally, for LF7 regulatory TFs, expression of all five top candidates (ZNF711, TEAD3, SRF, PGR, and FOXC2) was strong among SMC subtypes with some additional expression in fibroblasts, ECs, and macrophages for SRF (Figure 8D). To understand the relationships between regulatory TFs and subsets of the LF signature genes and proteins, differential dependent networks were constructed to display associations between regulatory TFs and the individual LF mRNA and proteins for each LF (Figures 5E, 6E, 7E, and 8E). These analyses of independent human coronary artery tissue samples offer additional validation of the potentially important roles of several TFs previously linked to vascular tissue or cellular phenotypes in model systems (discussed below), while also identifying numerous novel candidates for further evaluation as potentially actionable therapeutic targets.


### In vitro validation of LF2 and LF6 regulatory transcription factor MLXIPL
  In a final set of experiments, the TF MLXIPL, which was positively associated with both LF2 and LF6, was selected for in vitro validation of its putative regulatory role. To model the human arterial context, arterial organoids comprised of five major cells of the vessel wall (e.g. endothelial, smooth muscle, fibroblasts, pericytes, and macrophages; Figure 9A) were generated. Organoids were found to have good viability and express markers for key cell types (see Supplementary data online, Figure S13). Significant up-regulation and down-regulation of MLXIPL was confirmed by qPCR after adenoviral infection with MLXIPL overexpressing (OE) and knockdown (KD) virus (or GFP control virus; Figure 9B). Targeted parallel reaction monitoring proteomic analysis identified similar and expected patterns for differential protein abundance of MLXIPL across groups (Figure 9C). Global proteomic analysis identified 80%–90% of the LFs signature proteins originally defined in the human arterial tissue (see Supplementary data online, Figure S14) and more importantly, significant association was observed between MLXIPL levels and both LF2 and LF6 signature proteins (Figure 9D and E, respectively), confirming association between this TF and its putatively regulated target LF proteins in an independent, human organoid model system.


## Discussion
  In this report proteomic and transcriptomic data obtained from human coronary arteries of a large number of young and middle-aged adults (n = 322) with no ante mortem suspicion of coronary disease were used to characterize the molecular pheno-conversion from normal coronary arteries to atherosclerotic plaques. Through unsupervised deconvolution, four dominant LFs defined by hundreds of proteins were identified and found to be informative of different stages in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis. These LFs provide a more granular view of disease pathogenesis and highlight the potential role of mitochondrial dynamics, pericytes, and neurovascular contributions in the initiation and early disease progression as well as the evolution of the inflammatory phase of coronary atherosclerosis. Transcriptomic analyses identified potentially important transcriptional master regulators that are attractive targets for future mechanistic studies to prevent the initiation or interrupt the earliest stages of disease progression. Importantly, the generalizability of the LF and regulatory TF dynamics were validated in separate, publicly available datasets of single-cell transcriptomic data from CAD and normal human coronary arteries, and further validated a lead regulatory target, MLXIPL, in a human arterial cell organoid model. Collectively, these results, combined with the now publicly available data resources generated by this work, provide novel and highly relevant information concerning the initiation and progression of human coronary atherosclerosis.

  Several groups have contributed significantly to understanding the molecular architecture of human arterial tissue in atherosclerosis, including the Athero-Express Biobank,37 the Stockholm-Tartu Atherosclerosis Reverse Network Engineering Task (STARNET),38 the Biobank of Karolinska Endarterectomies (BiKE) GSE21545,39 the Stanford Heart Transplant program,40 the Mayr Lab at King’s College London,41 the Progression of Early Subclinical Atherosclerosis42 study, and several other studies with smaller numbers of samples or subjects.31,5,43 Most of this work has focused on transcriptional profiling (bulk and/or single-cell) of end-stage atherosclerosis from carotid endarterectomy samples. Although the Stanford group has analysed coronary (not peripheral) arteries from heart transplant donors and recipients, while the Mayr Lab has performed proteomic analysis of numerous carotid endarterectomy samples. The present study complements this work by providing both transcriptomic and proteomic data from proximal coronary arteries across the full range of disease severity—from normal to pre-clinical plaque, including some complex or calcified lesions. This range helps in the construction of a molecular pseudo-temporal trajectory from normal to advanced atherosclerosis, while the large number of samples with intermediate levels of disease severity provide a unique window on the molecular transitions that occur with the initiation and early stages of disease progression. These data are expected to highlight viable targets to prevent or interrupt early disease. As some tissue-derived proteins are secreted into the blood, some of these targets may also be examined as potential biomarkers in cohorts focused on early or sub-clinical atherosclerosis42,44 in order to derive much needed informative biomarkers for early atherosclerosis.45

  In a recent and directly comparable study to date, Theofilatos et al.41 profiled the proteome of human carotid endarterectomy samples from 320 patients including 419 specimens, comparing peripheral tissue to plaque core, and asymptomatic to symptomatic plaques. Several top plaque-associated proteins were also identified in this carotid work, including cathepsins, periostin, TIMP1, and numerous inflammatory/immune-related proteins such as CD14. Theofilatos and colleagues also identified modules of proteins associated with disease state. Among these, SMC contractile proteins were inversely associated, and immune/inflammatory proteins were positively associated, consistent with LF7 and LF4 results. Interestingly, there were no mitochondrial (LF6) or adventitial/neuroimmune (LF2) signatures identified in their late-stage carotid proteomic study, a difference attributable to variations in analytical approaches, inclusion of earlier stage lesions in this study, and ontology of the vascular region studied. Similar proteomic differences between LAD and the distal aorta11 have been previously observed. Overall, these findings both corroborate established and emerging biology, while introducing novel molecular insights focused on the earliest stages of initiation and progression of disease—stages that are especially relevant for prevention or early interruption of coronary atherosclerosis.

  One clear example of both corroborating and novel insights is LF6, which decreases quickly during the earliest stages of molecular disease progression and is dominated by mitochondrial proteins related to oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid metabolism. The observation of reduced mitochondrial abundance or activity in human atherosclerosis is consistent with previous report11 and results from other groups.46 However, the new data reported here provide additional temporal specificity in humans supporting extensive animal model and in vitro evidence that mitochondrial dysfunction is likely an initiating event that precedes atherosclerotic lesion formation.47 A regulatory role for the TF MLXIPL was validated in vitro as at least one key regulator for the changes in proteomic LF6 as well as LF2 that occurred with disease progression. MLXIPL is known as a carbohydrate sensor and regulator of glucose and lipid metabolism.48 Mutations in MLXIPL have been directly linked to CAD49 and a broad list of Genome Wide Association Study (GWAS) traits conferring CAD risk.50 While some of these studies have shown associations with circulating lipids and cholesterol as one mechanism for conferred risk, the data presented here show clear expression of MLXIPL in coronary artery aortic tissue, suggesting additional possible involvement at the level of the arterial wall. Mechanistically, MLXIPL has been shown to bind to intracellular lipid droplets, which sequesters this TF from the nucleus and prevents it from regulating expression of numerous metabolic enzymes.51 These data, in combination with these prior studies, inform a novel hypothesis that an early cellular response to excessive lipid uptake and/or energy surplus by arterial cells results in formation of intracellular lipid droplets and loss of MLXIPL transcriptional activity. Targeting of MLXIPL and strategies to maintain its transcriptional activity during early atherogenesis are warranted to determine its potential as a clinical therapeutic.

  A major strength and novel approach in this study design is the sampling of relatively young individuals (almost exclusively <60 years) at time of autopsy due to non-cardiovascular causes of death (e.g. trauma, overdose, etc.). This resulted in a wide range of pre-clinical coronary tissue phenotypes which were leveraged in order to generate the pseudo-time estimate of disease progression. Furthermore, while study subjects were maintained in a cool environment between death and time of autopsy, variability in the PMI could be an important confounder of the observed associations between proteins or RNAs and disease severity. Fortunately, in this study subjects, the length of PMI (∼50% < 12 h) was distributed evenly across pathological specimen categories with no statistical difference in PMI by lesion status. Additionally, the associations of individual mRNAs, proteins, and the proteomic LFs with disease progression were sustained after statistically controlling for PMI—a result that was further supported by evidence of similar associations in the external single-cell RNA dataset. Nevertheless, it cannot be ruled out that there are other important regulatory proteins or RNAs that would only be observable in pre-mortem or immediately post-mortem tissue samples.

  Some additional limitations in the current work should also be considered. First, these data are observational, and while supported by robust sample sizes for post-mortem human tissue studies and biological alignment with prior work, verifying disease drivers will ultimately require functional assays and experimental data. Second, few studies focus on human coronary arteries, with even fewer including non-atherosclerotic coronary controls. While the primary discovery samples included a large number of normal coronary samples, the normal control samples for the single-cell validation study were from failing hearts at the time of transplant. The degree to which these are fully representative of true ‘control’ cellular states is unknown, though the analysis aligns with well-recognized CAD vs control features, such as immune involvement and diminished SMC contractility. Third, some proteins and transcripts likely influencing atherosclerosis remain undetected due to assay and sample limitations. Finally, our focus on subjects and samples with pre-clinical disease precludes any inferences about progression to clinical cardiac events. Latent feature signature proteins strongly associated with molecular disease progression that can also be sampled in the blood would be one potential means to bridge this gap.

  In conclusion, these data provide a comprehensive description of the molecular landscape in human coronary arteries and identify important groups of proteins, and their likely transcriptional regulators, that comprise a putative molecularly defined estimate of the early pheno-conversion from normal artery to atherosclerotic plaque. These proteomic LFs and their candidate regulatory TFs, if further validated, represent attractive candidates as novel therapeutic targets. These descriptive results specifically emphasize the potentially important role of mitochondrial dynamics, pericytes, and neurovascular activation in the initiation and earliest stages of disease progression. More broadly, these results highlight the potential value of the application of recent advances in omics and data analytic techniques to human arterial tissues samples to reveal promising new opportunities to treat or prevent atherosclerotic vascular disease.
