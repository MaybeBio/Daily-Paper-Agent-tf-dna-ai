# Segmentation and classification of hippocampal subregions using multi-task generative adversarial networks


## Abstract
  Accurate segmentation and identification of hippocampal subregions are essential for understanding spatial memory, neuronal plasticity, and disease-related alterations in brain architecture. While fluorescent immunohistochemistry (IHC) enables detailed visualization of subregion-specific molecular markers, automated segmentation and classification remain challenging due to staining variability, morphological complexity, and low signal-to-noise ratios. Moreover, the absence of benchmark datasets has hindered the development of computational approaches for the automatic segmentation and identification of hippocampal regions in histological images, which are crucial for streamlining downstream analyses. To address these limitations, we introduce a novel multiplexed murine hippocampal dataset containing images stained with cFos, NeuN, and either ΔFosB or GAD67, capturing neuronal activity, structural features, and plasticity-associated signals. In parallel, we propose a multitask UNet-based generative adversarial network (MT-UGAN) that simultaneously performs segmentation and classification of hippocampal subregions from murine IHC images. The model leverages a UNet-based GAN architecture with a shared encoder, allowing for feature reuse across both segmentation and classification tasks. Together, the dataset and MT-UGAN establish the first integrated system for automated hippocampal subregion analysis. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed MT-UGAN significantly outperforms conventional single-task models in both region-wise segmentation and classification performance, achieving a Dice score of 0.82 and classification accuracy of 91.0%. By unifying subregion segmentation and identification, this framework provides a scalable and interpretable solution for automated hippocampal analysis, thereby facilitating downstream analyses in neuroimaging research. To democratize our funding and support further research, we made the curated hippocampal subregion datasets and the proposed MT-UGAN model as a standalone tool publicly available at: https://github.com/MLBC-lab/MT-UGAN.


## Introduction
  The hippocampus is a vital structure within the brain, known for its involvement in memory processing, spatial navigation, and emotional regulation. Disfunction in this region has been implicated in a variety of neurological and psychiatric disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease, epilepsy, and depression1–3. Anatomically, the hippocampus is divided into distinct subregions, namely dentate gyrus (DG), Cornu Ammonis 1 (CA1), and Cornu Ammonis 3 (CA3), each with specialized functions within the hippocampal circuitry. DG contributes to adult neurogenesis, CA3 is central to spatiotemporal memory processing and pattern completion, and CA1 is critical for encoding topological memory and integrating spatial representations4,5.

  The structural organization of hippocampal subregions, including DG, CA1, and CA3, is largely conserved between murine models and humans, with comparable roles in memory encoding, pattern separation, and retrieval. As a result, murine hippocampal studies are widely used as a foundation for understanding human brain function and neurological disorders6.

  Accurate delineation and identification of hippocampal subregions in histological brain images are essential for characterizing region-specific alterations associated with neurodevelopment, aging, and disease progression7. In experimental settings, this is typically performed manually, a process that is time-consuming and labor-intensive. Fluorescent immunohistochemistry (IHC) enables high-resolution visualization of neuronal structures and molecular markers. Antibodies such as cFos (neuronal activity), NeuN (neuronal identity), ΔFosB (chronic activity and plasticity), and GAD67 (inhibitory neurotransmission) are commonly used to visualize distinct molecular and functional features within hippocampal tissue8,9. These spatial expression patterns, in conjunction with anatomical landmarks, assist in delineating subregions such as DG, CA1, and CA3. Despite these advantages, automated segmentation of hippocampal subregions from IHC images remains challenging due to variability in staining intensity, morphological complexity of subregion boundaries, and low signal-to-noise ratios inherent to fluorescence imaging10,11.

  Deep learning has substantially advanced biomedical image analysis, with convolutional neural networks (CNNs) achieving strong performance in segmentation and classification tasks across a wide range of imaging modalities12. Encoder-decoder models like U-Net and its successors have become standard for segmenting complex anatomical structures13,14. Meanwhile, transfer learning-based classifiers have shown excellent results in histopathological tasks such as disease grading, tissue classification, and cell-type identification15,16. Beyond these standalone approaches, generative adversarial networks (GANs) have shown promise in histological image segmentation, where adversarial training enhances boundary localization and promotes structural realism in complex tissue environments17–19. However, most existing models treat segmentation and classification as independent tasks, missing the opportunity to leverage their shared spatial and contextual information.

  While segmentation and classification are often addressed separately, multitask learning (MTL) has emerged as an effective strategy for jointly optimizing related tasks by enabling shared representation learning. In biomedical imaging, MTL has been successfully applied to improve both performance and generalization in applications such as lesion segmentation and disease classification, anatomical labeling, and biomarker prediction20–22. These approaches leverage the complementary nature of tasks to provide richer feature supervision, reduce overfitting, and improve model robustness, particularly in limited or heterogeneous data settings.

  To address these limitations, we propose a multitask generative adversarial network that jointly performs segmentation and identification of hippocampal subregions from IHC images in an end-to-end fashion. We focus exclusively on an extended multiplexed IHC dataset composed of murine brain sections stained with cFos, NeuN, and either ΔFosB or GAD67, which captures structural, neuronal activity-related, and plasticity-associated signals. By jointly optimizing pixel-wise segmentation and subregion classification, the proposed model leverages shared spatial features to enhance both anatomical accuracy and label consistency, particularly in the presence of heterogeneous or weakly stained data.

  The key contribution of our study can be summarized as follows:

  We introduce a multiplexed IHC dataset comprising 660 manually annotated images stained with cFos, NeuN, and either ΔFosB or GAD67, facilitating joint segmentation and identification of hippocampal subregions.We propose a novel multitask generative adversarial network (MT-GAN) that jointly performs hippocampal subregion segmentation and classification in a unified framework, eliminating the need for post-hoc region annotation.We demonstrate that MT-GAN outperforms single-task baselines in both segmentation and classification metrics, validating the effectiveness of multitask learning for histological analysis tasks.

  By unifying segmentation and subregion identification into a single model, this work advances automated analysis of histological brain images and facilitates scalable, reproducible region-specific investigations in neuroscience. To democratize our findings and support downstream research, we have made the collected hippocampal dataset and the proposed MT-UGAN model as a standalone tool publicly available at: https://github.com/MLBC-lab/MT-UGAN.


## Methodology
  In this section, we discuss the dataset preparation process and our unified GAN framework combining a semantic segmentation and classification module. Then, we introduce the loss functions used for optimizing the network to achieve the optimal results.


### Benchmark dataset
  Murine tissue images were collected from Rutgers University-Camden and the Joint Health Science Building following approved ethical guidelines. Immunohistochemistry was performed as previously described23. We collected data from 53 adult (10–14-week-old) C57BL/6 mice (27 females, 26 males). The dataset curated for this study consists of high-resolution IHC tissue images with manually annotated hippocampal regions annotated by three domain experts using Fiji ImageJ, which serve as ground truth segmentation masks. To reduce variations in staining protocols, preprocessing steps, including normalization, histogram equalization, and stain normalization, were applied.

  The multiplexed dataset includes two staining protocols: one combining cFos, NeuN, and ΔFosB; the other combining cFos, NeuN, and GAD67, introducing anatomical and functional diversity. cFos marks neuronal activity, NeuN marks neuronal identity, ΔFosB reflects chronic activity and plasticity, and GAD67 labels inhibitory projections. These markers offer spatial and molecular cues that support the delineation of hippocampal subregions (DG, CA1, CA3).

  A total of 660 images were collected and annotated, containing 387 DG, 93 CA1, and 180 CA3 regions. The dataset was split into training, validation, and test sets using stratified sampling to preserve class distribution. In addition to the multiplexed staining datasets (cFos+NeuN + ΔFosB and cFos+NeuN+GAD67), we curated and annotated two single-stain hippocampal datasets (cFos and NeuN) to further evaluate model generalizability. Each single-stain dataset comprised 275 annotated images (157 DG, 118 CA3).

  Each image was resized to 256 × 256 and normalized to the [0, 1] range by dividing pixel intensities by 255.0. Corresponding binary masks were also resized using nearest-neighbor interpolation and normalized similarly. To improve generalization and robustness to morphological and staining variations, data augmentation was applied to each training sample, including horizontal flipping (probability = 0.5), 90° rotation, random rotation within ± 10° (p = 0.3), and brightness/contrast adjustments (p = 0.2), and elastic deformation (p = 0.3). All datasets developed in this study, including the single-stain collections, will be publicly released to support future benchmarking and reproducibility.


### Model architecture
  Multi-task learning-based approaches enhance individual task performance by jointly optimizing related objectives and leveraging shared feature representations24,25. Especially in the field of image processing, each image contains rich geometric and semantic information. Therefore, multitask learning has been successfully applied to computer vision tasks such as semantic segmentation, depth estimation, and medical image denoising25–28.

  Fig. 1Model architecture of MT-UGAN pipeline, illustrating the U-Net–based generator, region-aware discriminator, and multi-task outputs for simultaneous segmentation and classification.

  Motivated by these successes, we introduce MT-UGAN, a unified multitask generative adversarial framework for simultaneous segmentation and classification of hippocampal subregions. As illustrated in Fig. 1, MT-UGAN consists of two main components: a generator and a discriminator19. The proposed multitask generative adversarial network comprises a shared encoder for segmentation-classification in the generator and a spatially aware discriminator, designed to jointly perform hippocampal subregion segmentation and anatomical region classification. The generator is based on a modified U-Net architecture with an auxiliary classification head, while the discriminator adopts a U-Net-like design to provide spatially resolved adversarial supervision.


#### The generator
  The generator in MT-UGAN jointly performs pixel-wise segmentation and subregion classification. The segmentation module carries out pixel-wise classification in the form of image-to-image translation29. The features derived from the bottleneck layer were used to classify the regions.

  The generator follows an encoder-decoder structure based on U-Net, extended with a parallel classification pathway. The encoder consists of four downsampling blocks, each composed of two sequential convolutional layers (3 × 3 kernel, stride 1, padding 1) followed by batch normalization and LeakyReLU activation. Max pooling layers (2 × 2) are applied after each block to reduce spatial resolution. The bottleneck comprises two convolutional layers with 1024 channels.

  The decoder mirrors the encoder structure with four upsampling stages, each utilizing a transposed convolution (2 × 2, stride 2) for resolution restoration. The upsampled features are concatenated with the corresponding encoder features via skip connections and passed through two convolutional layers, followed by batch normalization and LeakyReLU activation. The final decoder output is processed through a 1 × 1 convolution and sigmoid activation to produce a binary segmentation mask.

  The classification head is derived from the bottleneck feature map, which is pooled using adaptive average pooling to a spatial resolution of 1 × 1, followed by a dropout layer (p = 0.3) and a fully connected layer to predict subregion class probabilities over three categories (DG, CA1, CA3). The output is used to supervise classification using weighted focal loss.


#### The discriminator
  The discriminator employs a U-Net-like architecture that takes binary segmentation masks as input and outputs a spatial probability map referred to as a spatial realism map, indicating region-wise anatomical plausibility. The encoder path comprises three downsampling blocks with convolutional layers (3 × 3), LeakyReLU activation, and max pooling. The bottleneck layer expands feature representation to 512 channels.

  The decoder consists of three upsampling blocks, each beginning with a transposed convolution followed by skip connection fusion with the corresponding encoder features. These fused features pass through paired convolutional layers to reconstruct the spatial fidelity map. A final 1 × 1 convolution followed by a sigmoid activation yields the discriminator output.

### Post processing
  The output of our segmentation head was then passed through a filtering phase to reduce erroneous predictions. To improve the structural fidelity of the predicted segmentation masks, we applied a post-processing step using connected components analysis (CCA). Raw probability maps from the generator were binarized, followed by 8-connectivity component analysis with area filtering, which enhanced anatomical plausibility. To eliminate noise and spurious fragments, isolated components smaller than 300 pixels were removed using an empirically determined threshold based on evaluations across a 5-400 pixel range. This filtering strategy enhanced region cohesion and structural consistency without necessitating model retraining, in line with contemporary artifact reduction techniques employed in neuroimaging segmentation pipelines. To ensure a fair comparison, identical post-processing steps were applied to the outputs of all segmentation models.


### Training protocol
  The generator and discriminator were jointly trained in an adversarial setting using separate Adam optimizers. The generator used a learning rate of 5 × 10− 4, while the discriminator used a lower learning rate of 1 × 10− 6 to ensure stable adversarial feedback. To address the differing complexities of the two tasks, we employed a warm-up phase wherein the network was trained solely for the segmentation task for the first 10 epochs, providing a stable initialization for pixel-wise predictions.

  Following this phase, multitask training was initiated, jointly optimizing both segmentation and classification using the full MT-UGAN framework. Training proceeded for a maximum of 500 epochs with early stopping based on the validation Dice score. The model exhibiting the highest validation performance was selected for final evaluation. All experiments were implemented in PyTorch and conducted on an NVIDIA RTX A6000 48 GB GPU.


### Evaluation metrics
  We evaluated both segmentation quality and subregion classification performance using a comprehensive set of metrics widely adopted in medical imaging benchmarks32,33. To assess anatomical segmentation quality, we report Dice Similarity Coefficient (Dice), Intersection over Union (IoU), Precision, and Recall. Dice and IoU quantify region-level overlap between predicted and ground truth masks. Precision and Recall measure over- and under-segmentation tendencies and are fundamental to performance analysis in modern segmentation frameworks34. All metrics are computed per sample and averaged across the test set.

  For subregion classification (DG, CA1, CA3), we evaluate overall Accuracy along with class-wise Precision, Recall, and F1-score. Accuracy reflects the proportion of correctly predicted samples, while the per-class metrics assess the model’s ability to distinguish individual hippocampal subregions. Together, these metrics provide a holistic evaluation of both segmentation fidelity and classification robustness.

## Results and discussion
  We evaluate the proposed MT-UGAN model against a range of state-of-the-art segmentation and classification architectures under three evaluation settings: (i) region-wise segmentation accuracy, (ii) subregion classification on an independent test set, and (iii) 5-fold cross-validation.

  To assess segmentation performance, we compared MT-UGAN with six widely used segmentation architectures: UNet13, UNet++35, AttentionUNet36, ResUNet++14, DeepLabV337 with ResNet 101 backbone, and SegFormer38 using a comprehensive standardized evaluation framework.

  For evaluating classification performance, we compared MT-UGAN against a diverse set of baselines. This included traditional machine learning classifiers such as Random Forest (RF)39 and Support Vector Machine (SVM)40, a standard convolutional neural network (CNN)41, and advanced transfer learning architectures including ResNet-5042, EfficientNet43, MobileNetV344, ConvNeXt45, ConvNeXt v246, and EdgeNeXt47.

  MT-UGAN demonstrates strong performance on both segmentation and classification tasks, consistently outperforming its single-task counterparts. We present a detailed discussion of these results in the following subsections.


### Segmentation performance
  We evaluated the segmentation performance of MT-UGAN on the proposed benchmark dataset through both quantitative metrics and qualitative inspection. To ensure a comprehensive comparison, we benchmarked MT-UGAN against six established single-task segmentation architectures: UNet, UNet++, AttentionUNet, ResUNet++, DeepLabV3, and SegFormer using the standardized evaluation metrics described in Sect.  2.5. Across all segmentation metrics, MT-UGAN consistently outperformed baseline models, demonstrating the effectiveness of multitask learning in improving segmentation accuracy.

  As presented in Table 1, MT-UGAN achieved the highest Dice score (0.82) and IoU score (0.72), surpassing all baseline models, which reached Dice scores of 0.81 and IoU scores of 0.71. MT-UGAN also achieved the highest recall (0.86) and matched or exceeded the precision (0.80) of all other models. These findings underscore the advantages of integrating segmentation and classification within a unified framework, enabling MT-UGAN to leverage complementary task-specific cues and achieve superior anatomical delineation through shared representation learning.

  Figure 2 illustrates the qualitative segmentation results of MT-UGAN in comparison to the baseline models across various staining conditions. MT-UGAN demonstrates robust performance across subregions and staining modalities, maintaining precise boundaries even in challenging samples. Notably, in cases where multiple hippocampal subregions were present in an image but not explicitly annotated, MT-UGAN accurately delineated all present structures, whereas baseline models exhibited incomplete or imprecise segmentations, highlighting MT-UGAN’s ability to generalize beyond annotated training examples and produce complete, anatomically coherent segmentations.

### Cross-validation analysis
  Table 3 presents the mean classification performance across five cross-validation folds. As shown in this table, MT-UGAN achieved consistent accuracy (88%) and maintained high class-wise F1-scores across DG, CA1, and CA3, outperforming competing models, particularly in recall and F1-score for CA3. These findings confirm the robustness and generalization capability of MT-UGAN, especially in class-imbalanced and anatomically complex settings. Moreover, other benchmarking models failed to maintain consistent classification performance across folds, often exhibiting significant deviations from their average metrics (2-7.5%). In contrast, MT-UGAN consistently achieved a classification accuracy of 0.88 with a standard deviation of only 0.008, indicating high reliability and stability.

### Ablation study and sensitivity analysis

#### Model component analysis
  To evaluate the contribution of individual components of the proposed framework, we conducted an ablation study examining (i) the effect of adversarial training and (ii) the impact of joint multi-task learning compared to single-task (ST) training. The results are summarized in Table 5.

  In the single-task setting, incorporating adversarial training improves segmentation performance, with Dice increasing from 0.79 to 0.81. This indicates that adversarial supervision promotes more accurate and structurally consistent segmentation outputs even in the absence of multi-task learning.

  Table 5Ablation study evaluating the impact of multi-task learning and adversarial training on segmentation and classification performance.

  More substantial improvements are observed when transitioning from single-task to multi-task learning. While segmentation performance shows a modest increase (Dice from 0.81 to 0.82), classification performance improves significantly. In particular, the multi-task model without adversarial training (MT-NoGAN) achieves an accuracy of 0.87, compared to 0.61 for the corresponding single-task classification model. This demonstrates that shared representation learning between segmentation and classification provides strong structural and contextual priors that enhance discriminative performance.

  The addition of adversarial training in the multi-task setting (MT-UGAN) further improves both segmentation and classification performance, achieving a Dice score of 0.82 and classification accuracy of 0.91. While the gain in segmentation is modest, the improvement in classification performance (from 0.87 to 0.91) suggests that adversarial supervision contributes to learning more consistent and biologically meaningful feature representations.

  Notably, MT-UGAN achieves these classification gains while maintaining competitive segmentation performance relative to strong single-task baselines. Overall, these results indicate that multi-task learning is the primary driver of performance improvements, particularly for classification, while adversarial training provides complementary refinement in both segmentation quality and predictive consistency.


#### Channel contribution analysis
  To evaluate the contribution of individual staining channels, we conducted additional experiments using single-channel inputs (cFos, ΔFosB/GAD67, and NeuN), as well as the combined multi-channel input. The results are summarized in Table 6.

However, these signals provide complementary contextual information when combined with structural staining. In addition, these functional markers are commonly used to capture neuronal activity and molecular responses in downstream analyses, further supporting the practical relevance of incorporating multiplexed staining in real-world applications.

  Importantly, the combined multi-channel input achieves the best overall performance (Dice: 0.81), along with improvements in IoU and overall segmentation consistency. These results suggest that while NeuN provides essential structural information, the additional channels contribute complementary contextual and biological signals that improve model robustness.

  Notably, classification accuracy remains comparable across configurations, indicating that additional channels enhance contextual representation without introducing instability. Overall, these findings support the use of multiplexed staining, where structural and functional markers jointly enhance both anatomical delineation and contextual understanding of tissue organization.

### Discussion and limitations
  The experimental findings demonstrate that MT-UGAN delivers robust performance in both segmentation and classification tasks, surpassing segmentation-specific and classification-specific baselines (Fig. 5). To further assess its generalizability, we evaluated the model on two single-stain datasets. MT-UGAN achieved a Dice score of 0.76 with 93% accuracy on the cFos dataset and a Dice score of 0.81 with 91% accuracy on the NeuN dataset, highlighting adaptability across both functional and structural markers.

  Fig. 5Comparison of model performance across evaluation metrics. (a) Line plot of segmentation performance (Dice, IoU, Precision, Recall) across baseline models and the proposed MT-UGAN. (b) Radar plot summarizing model-wise performance across all classification metrics, showing relative strengths and weaknesses of each architecture. MT-UGAN demonstrates consistently high performance across segmentation and classification metrics compared to competing models.

  By unifying both tasks within a single architecture, MT-UGAN facilitates effective feature reuse and benefits from shared representations. The adversarial discriminator contributes additional spatial supervision, promoting anatomically coherent segmentations. Moreover, the classification head leverages context-aware features extracted by the segmentation pathway, enhancing the model’s ability to distinguish between closely related hippocampal subregions. Together, these elements underscore the promise of multitask adversarial learning for histological image analysis, where structural delineation and region identification are inherently interdependent.

  Despite the strong performance of MT-UGAN, several limitations remain. First, the relatively small dataset size constrains the model’s ability to fully capture inter-subject variability. Increasing the dataset size would likely enhance both segmentation and classification performance, enabling the development of a more robust and scalable framework for downstream neuroanatomical analysis. Second, although the model was trained on multiplexed IHC images stained with distinct markers (cFos, NeuN, ΔFosB, GAD67), all samples were acquired under controlled conditions using a uniform imaging pipeline. Although we used two different staining protocols, the lack of diversification in the dataset limits the model’s generalizability to variations in experimental design, tissue preparation, staining protocols, and imaging equipment. Therefore, the generalization capability of the proposed model should be interpreted within the context of similar acquisition settings. Third, the present study focuses exclusively on three hippocampal subregions. Expanding the framework to include additional anatomical structures or other brain regions may introduce new challenges related to structural heterogeneity and annotation complexity.

  In future iterations of this work, we aim to explore domain adaptation techniques to improve robustness across heterogeneous datasets and evaluate model performance on out-of-distribution samples. We also aim to extend the subregion taxonomy and investigate the applicability of MT-UGAN to other neuroanatomical domains. Such directions are essential to enhance the generalizability, scalability, and translational impact of multitask GAN-based approaches in biomedical image analysis.


## Conclusion
  In this study, we addressed the pressing challenge of automated hippocampal subregion segmentation and classification from IHC images by introducing MT-UGAN, a multitask generative adversarial network that jointly optimizes both objectives. Leveraging a shared encoder and task-specific heads, MT-UGAN benefits from cross-task supervision to enhance both anatomical boundary delineation and subregion-level classification.

  Additionally, to facilitate evaluation and enable further research, we introduced a multiplexed hippocampal IHC dataset featuring diverse staining protocols. On this benchmark, MT-UGAN achieved a Dice score of 0.82 for segmentation and a classification accuracy of 91.0%, outperforming several strong single-task baselines. Furthermore, evaluation on two independent single-stain datasets (cFos and NeuN) confirmed the model’s robustness in a reduced two-class setting, underscoring its adaptability across staining protocols.

  By integrating segmentation and classification into a unified, end-to-end trainable architecture, our approach provides a scalable and interpretable solution for downstream applications in neuroscience, including circuit mapping, plasticity analysis, and disease progression modeling. Future directions will explore domain adaptation and transformer-based extensions to support cross-cohort generalization and broader applicability in neuroimaging pipelines.
