# Quantitative coupling of clonal CNV evolution and spatially restricted malignant states identifies MFGE8 as a candidate late-state-associated target in breast cancer


## Background
  Breast cancer progression reflects heterogeneous malignant states shaped by clonal evolution and microenvironmental selection, yet how genomic instability quantitatively couples to transcriptional state transitions and spatial niche organization remains unclear.


## Methods
  We integrated single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics across ER-positive, HER2-positive, and triple-negative tumors, combined with CNV inference, branched trajectory analysis, transcription factor regulon modeling, cell–cell communication inference, and spatial niche mapping. Trajectory-derived evolutionary gene programs were translated to bulk-tumor stratification and multicohort prognostic modeling. MFGE8 was further evaluated as a therapeutic target using structure-based virtual screening with docking and molecular dynamics refinement. Functional validation of MFGE8 was performed using knockdown, overexpression, rescue, and in vitro phenotypic assays.


## Results
  Malignant epithelial cells formed a branched three-state trajectory that was strongly coupled to CNV-defined clonal architecture, with the tightest coupling in triple-negative tumors. Regulon analyses revealed coordinated transcription factor rewiring along progression, converging on a late State 3 program enriched for extracellular matrix and adhesion signaling. Spatial transcriptomics localized State 3 to a discrete invasive-front niche characterized by stromal-dominant ligand–receptor interactions. MFGE8 emerged as a State 3–biased output associated with LHX2 activity, and in silico MFGE8 perturbation induced transcriptional rollback from the late-state branch. Structure-guided screening prioritized candidate MFGE8 binders with stable interaction dynamics. Evolutionary gene programs enabled robust bulk stratification and supported a multicohort-validated 10-gene prognostic model associated with genomic instability patterns and immunotherapy-related signatures.


## Conclusions
  CNV-linked clonal evolution and spatial niche restriction are associated with a late malignant program and prioritize MFGE8 as a candidate late-state-associated therapeutic target in breast cancer.

## Introduction
  Breast cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality among women worldwide, largely because it is not a single disease but a collection of biologically distinct entities with diverse clinical behaviors and therapeutic vulnerabilities [1]. Conventional classification into ER-positive, HER2-positive, and triple-negative (TN) subtypes has improved treatment selection, yet substantial inter-patient variability persists within each subtype, including differences in progression, metastatic potential, and treatment resistance [2]. A central driver of this variability is intratumoral heterogeneity, where malignant epithelial cells occupy multiple transcriptional states and clonal lineages, and where the tumor microenvironment (TME) provides dynamic stromal and immune cues that can promote selection, adaptation, and immune escape [3].

  Over the past decade, high-throughput profiling has greatly advanced our understanding of breast cancer biology, but key challenges remain in linking tumor evolution to the cellular ecosystem in which it unfolds [4]. Bulk transcriptomic studies have enabled molecular subtyping and prognostic modeling, yet they average signals across cell types and cannot resolve how malignant states interact with immune and stromal compartments [5]. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has addressed this limitation by mapping cellular composition and identifying malignant programs, including proliferative, lineage-associated, and stress-response states [6]. In parallel, copy-number variation (CNV) inference from scRNA-seq has been increasingly used to distinguish malignant from non-malignant cells and to approximate clonal structure [7]. However, many studies still treat transcriptional states, clonal CNV architecture, regulatory programs, and microenvironmental signaling as partially disconnected layers, limiting mechanistic insight into how evolutionary progression is stabilized and spatially organized in situ.

  Spatial transcriptomics provides an additional dimension by retaining tissue context, enabling interrogation of whether specific malignant states concentrate within defined anatomic regions such as the invasive front, and whether localized niches align with distinct stromal and immune configurations [8]. Despite rapid progress, there remains a need for integrative frameworks that (i) compare subtype-dependent ecosystem composition at single-cell resolution, (ii) connect CNV-linked clonal structure to continuous transcriptional trajectories, (iii) identify the regulatory logic that stabilizes trajectory-associated malignant programs, and (iv) quantify how microenvironmental ligand–receptor signaling converges on particular epithelial states and manifests as spatially restricted niches [9]. Bridging these levels is also essential for translation, because evolution-associated programs may encode prognostic information and shape immunotherapy responsiveness through their coupling to immune contexture and genomic instability [10].

  We established an integrated single-cell and spatial transcriptomic framework to characterize evolutionary heterogeneity across ER+, HER2+, and triple-negative breast cancers. By integrating epithelial CNV inference with trajectory analysis, clonal architecture was linked to dynamic transcriptional states and subtype-specific evolutionary patterns were evaluated. Regulon activity and cell–cell communication analyses were performed to delineate progression-associated regulatory programs and microenvironmental signaling, and spatial transcriptomics was used to localize trajectory-related states and niche interactions in situ. For clinical translation, breast cancer evolutionary gene signatures were derived to stratify tumors, and a parsimonious multi-cohort prognostic model was constructed with immunogenomic associations. The paper outlines the ecosystem atlas and malignant state map, clonal–trajectory integration, regulatory and communication analyses, spatial validation, and breast cancer evolutionary gene signature-based classification with clinical evaluation.


## Materials and methods

### Study design and data sources
  This study integrated public single-cell and bulk cohorts to characterize BCEG-associated malignant programs and their clinical relevance. scRNA-seq data were obtained from GEO (GSE161529), including breast tumors spanning ER-positive, HER2-positive, and triple-negative subtypes, and were analyzed with subtype-aware stratification [11]. Bulk multi-omics and clinical analyses were anchored in TCGA-BRCA, incorporating transcriptome profiles together with somatic mutation data, GISTIC-derived copy-number alterations, and DNA methylation (Illumina 450 K) linked to available clinicopathologic variables. External multi-center validation was performed using multiple independent GEO cohorts (GSE202203, GSE96058, GSE20685, GSE58812, GSE131769, GSE86166, GSE88770, and GSE21653), and tumor–normal expression comparisons used GTEx as the normal reference when needed [12]. Immunotherapy-related inference leveraged TIDE and external evaluation in the anti–PD-L1 cohort [13, 14]. Functional dependency and therapeutic context were supported using DepMap with CCLE copy-number data and curated cancer-gene annotations from OncoKB, while candidate compound screening used a DrugBank-derived small-molecule library [15, 16].


### CNV inference and clonal/evolutionary visualization
  Large-scale CNV profiles were inferred from scRNA-seq expression patterns using inferCNV in R. Putative malignant epithelial cells were evaluated against a diploid reference population defined by T cells, with optional downsampling applied to reduce computational burden and avoid over-representation of abundant identities. CNV inference was performed with scaling and denoising enabled, using a conservative expression cutoff of 0.1 and noise filtering threshold of 0.12. In addition to a global analysis, CNV landscapes were reconstructed separately within ER-positive, HER2-positive, and triple-negative subsets to enable subtype-level comparisons of chromosomal alteration patterns. CNV-informed clonal structure was summarized using phylogeny-style visualization in R, with clonal trees rendered using uphyloplot2 and further annotated/visualized using ggtree to contextualize malignant transcriptional states within inferred evolutionary architectures.


### Trajectory inference and pseudotime analysis of malignant progression
  To model continuous malignant-state transitions, trajectory inference was first performed in R using Monocle2 [17]. To standardize runtime and memory usage, a representative epithelial subset was used when needed (up to 5,000 cells). Cells were embedded into a learned low-dimensional manifold capturing progressive and branching structures, enabling assignment to trajectory-defined states and ordering along pseudotime.

  As an orthogonal validation, the same malignant epithelial compartment was additionally analyzed using Slingshot, which infers lineage structure from reduced-dimensional embeddings and cluster relationships. The concordance between Monocle2 and Slingshot was evaluated at the level of overall branching topology and progression structure. Trajectory results were used to map discrete epithelial subclusters onto continuous progression paths and to support downstream coupling analyses with CNV-defined groups.


### Gene regulatory network inference and regulon activity analysis
  To infer transcription factor–centered regulatory programs while reducing sparsity, metacell aggregation was conducted in R using a k-nearest-neighbor strategy, requiring a minimum of 10 cells per metacell with neighborhood size set to 10. Metacell expression profiles were merged across groups and filtered to retain genes expressed in at least five metacells. Data were exported in loom-compatible formats using SCopeLoomR for downstream regulon inference.

  Regulatory network reconstruction and regulon activity estimation were performed using the SCENIC ecosystem, combining R-based preprocessing with Python-based execution using pySCENIC and standard numerical/data dependencies (commonly including numpy, pandas, scipy, loompy, and arboreto). Motif-ranking resources were used to constrain candidate target sets to motif-supported TF–gene relationships. Regulon specificity and group-biased activity patterns were subsequently summarized to identify state- and subtype-associated regulatory programs.


### In silico single-cell knockout perturbation analysis
  To evaluate candidate regulators within a selected malignant state (trajectory-defined State 3), network-based in silico knockout perturbation analyses were performed in R using scTenifoldKnk [18]. Cells were downsampled to a fixed size (up to 5,000) for consistent computational load. Perturbations were simulated for candidate genes individually and jointly, and network-level changes were quantified to identify downstream genes and pathways most shared or most specifically disrupted. State-restricted differential expression was computed in Seurat to contextualize perturbation targets relative to State 3 transcriptional signatures.

### Spatial transcriptomics morphology-informed domain identification and spatial deconvolution
  Spatial transcriptomics analyses were performed to localize malignant epithelial states within tissue architecture and to evaluate enrichment at tumor boundary regions. Morphology-aware spatial domain discovery and boundary delineation were performed in R using Cottrazm, with clustering resolution set to 1.5 to capture fine-grained spatial domains [19]. When morphology-aware analyses required Python-based tooling, interoperability was enabled through reticulate in R, and stlearn (Python) was used for complementary morphology-informed spatial modeling and visualization support.

  To infer cell-type composition at spatial locations, reference-based spatial deconvolution was performed using spacexr (RCTD) in R [20]. Feature selection was constrained to the middle quantile range (5th–95th percentile) to reduce the influence of extreme features. Spatial associations between epithelial states and anatomical regions were evaluated using permutation-based testing to obtain empirical P values. Spatial neighborhood structure and autocorrelation-related utilities were supported using spdep.


### In silico drug screening, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics simulation
  A structure-based virtual screening workflow was used to identify candidate small molecules targeting MFGE8. Ligands were curated and prefiltered in Python using RDKit with pandas-based processing. Ligand preparation (3D coordinate generation and energy minimization) was performed using Open Babel under the MMFF94 force field, with protonation adjusted to physiological pH (7.4). Binding pocket detection was performed using fpocket to define candidate docking sites. Molecular docking was carried out using AutoDock Vina with exhaustiveness set to 8 and up to 10 binding modes retained per ligand [21]. Docking boxes were parameterized around pocket centers using a uniform cubic search space (26 Å per dimension). A pilot docking step was used to confirm that predicted affinities fell within an expected range (approximately − 6 to − 12 kcal/mol) before scaling to the full compound set. To improve robustness of hit selection, candidates were re-evaluated under alternative docking configurations, including modest box-size variation (22–24 Å) and optional alternative scoring within the Vina framework. Final candidates were prioritized based on consistency across pockets/configurations and strong predicted binding.

  Protein–ligand complex stability was assessed by all-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations using GROMACS [22]. Production simulations were run for 100 ns with a 2 fs time step under periodic boundary conditions. Long-range electrostatics were treated using particle mesh Ewald (PME), and short-range Coulomb and van der Waals interactions used a 1.0 nm cutoff under the Verlet cutoff scheme. Temperature was maintained at 300 K using the velocity-rescaling thermostat with separate coupling groups for protein and non-protein components, and pressure was controlled at 1 bar using the Parrinello–Rahman barostat under isotropic coupling (compressibility 4.5 × 10− 5 bar − 1). Trajectories and energies were saved every 5,000 steps to balance temporal resolution and storage.

  Trajectory preprocessing included periodic boundary correction, centering, and least-squares fitting to the protein backbone prior to analysis. Structural stability and flexibility were quantified using backbone RMSD, ligand RMSD (relative to backbone-fitted trajectories), radius of gyration, and Cα RMSF, computed using standard GROMACS analysis utilities. Protein–ligand proximity was assessed via minimum distance time series, and hydrogen-bond dynamics were evaluated where topology compatibility allowed. Solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) was computed for protein, ligand, and pocket-associated regions after discarding an initial equilibration interval (first 30 ns) to focus on stabilized dynamics. A free energy landscape (FEL) was constructed based on principal component analysis of fitted Cα motions, using the first two principal components to estimate a 2D conformational landscape via Boltzmann inversion of projected density. Residue–ligand contacts were additionally summarized in Python using MDAnalysis, defining contacts by a 0.45 nm distance cutoff; figures were generated using matplotlib and interactive summaries were produced using plotly/htmlwidgets.


### In-house RNA-seq cohort for BCEG validation
  An independent in-house bulk RNA-seq cohort was generated for external validation of the BCEG model. A total of 158 breast cancer specimens were collected at Guizhou Provincial People’s Hospital between March 2025 and December 2025 and subjected to RNA extraction and RNA-seq profiling following standard procedures. The resulting transcriptomic data were used for BCEG model validation in downstream analyses. The RNA-seq dataset has been deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under accession GSE323973.


### Multiplex immunofluorescence staining
  Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) breast tumor tissues were sectioned according to the pre-defined orientation and quantity requirements. Sections were mounted on glass slides and baked at 60 °C for 2–4 h to enhance adhesion. Deparaffinization was performed by sequential immersion in xylene (three changes, 5 min each; additional cycles were applied when paraffin removal was incomplete), followed by rehydration through graded ethanol (100% twice for 5 min each, 95% for 5 min, 70% for 5 min) and a final rinse in distilled water for 5 min.

  Antigen retrieval was carried out by immersing slides in retrieval buffer and heating in a microwave oven. After retrieval, slides were cooled to room temperature and rinsed in running tap water for 5 min. To confine reagents to the tissue area, a hydrophobic barrier was drawn around the section at approximately 0.5 mm from the tissue edge. Slides were washed in PBST three times (5 min each). Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched by incubating sections with 3% H₂O₂ in methanol at room temperature, followed by PBST washes (three times).

  Non-specific binding sites were blocked at room temperature using an immunofluorescence blocking solution (or the kit-provided blocking buffer). Primary antibodies were diluted according to the manufacturers’ instructions and incubated either at room temperature for a short incubation or overnight at 4 °C. After PBST washes (three times, 5 min each), secondary antibodies were applied according to the corresponding antibody system instructions, followed by PBST washes. Fluorophore development was performed by incubating sections with fluorescent dye reagents prepared by diluting fluorophore and signal amplification reagents at the recommended ratio, then washing with PBST (three times, 5 min each) to remove unbound fluorophore [23].

  For multiplex staining, antibody stripping was performed between cycles to enable sequential detection of multiple markers on the same section. Stripping was achieved either by applying an antibody stripping solution followed by PBST washes, or by repeating the antigen retrieval step (microwave-based retrieval), cooling to room temperature, and washing in PBST. Steps for peroxidase quenching and blocking were not repeated during subsequent cycles unless required. The staining/stripping cycle was repeated until all markers in the panel were completed. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI at room temperature, followed by PBST washes (three times, 5 min each). Slides were mounted with an antifade mounting medium and coverslipped for fluorescence imaging.

  Two antibody panels were used: Panel 1: Pan-cytokeratin (PanCK; abs123684), CD3 (ab16669), CD19 (ab134114), CD68 (abs171440), and CD56 (CST99746). Panel 2: PanCK (abs123684), CD8 (ab316778), FOXP3 (CST98377), PD-1 (CST86163), and PD-L1 (CST13684).


### In vitro functional validation and dual-luciferase reporter assay
  MCF7 and BT549 breast cancer cell lines were used for in vitro functional validation. After MFGE8 knockdown, overexpression, or rescue, cell proliferation was assessed by CCK-8 assay, whereas migratory and invasive capacities were evaluated by wound-healing and Transwell assays. In parallel, promoter-level support for the predicted LHX2–MFGE8 relationship was examined by dual-luciferase reporter assay using wild-type and motif-mutant MFGE8 promoter constructs in MCF7 and BT549 cells. Detailed experimental conditions are provided in the Supplementary Methods.


### Statistical analysis
  All statistical analyses were performed in R unless otherwise specified. Continuous variables were summarized as median or mean ± standard deviation as appropriate, and categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages. Two-group comparisons of continuous variables were conducted using two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, and multi-group comparisons used Kruskal–Wallis tests followed by post hoc pairwise Wilcoxon tests when needed. Associations between categorical variables were evaluated using Chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests depending on expected cell counts. Correlations were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation. Survival analyses were conducted using Kaplan–Meier estimation with log-rank testing, and Cox proportional hazards regression was used for univariate and multivariable modeling; hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. For predictive model evaluation, concordance index (C-index) was used as the primary discrimination metric, and prediction error was assessed when applicable. Permutation-based procedures reported empirical P values derived from repeated label randomization under the null hypothesis. Unless otherwise stated, all tests were two-sided and P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure where relevant.


## Results

### Single-cell ecosystem atlas of breast cancer identifies subtype-dependent microenvironmental composition and epithelial transcriptional heterogeneity
  We established a single-cell transcriptomic framework to interrogate breast cancer evolutionary heterogeneity across ER-positive, HER2-positive, and triple-negative tumors, following a standardized workflow for tissue processing, quality control, integration, clustering, and downstream analyses (Fig. 1A). In the integrated low-dimensional embedding, cells from the three molecular subtypes broadly overlapped but also formed subtype-enriched neighborhoods, suggesting shared core transcriptional programs accompanied by subtype-associated shifts in cellular states (Fig. 1B). Marker-guided annotation resolved the tumor ecosystem into major lineages, including epithelial cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, macrophages, T cells, B cells, plasma cells, plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDC), and mast cells (Fig. 1C). Canonical lineage markers supported these assignments: EPCAM and KRT18 for epithelial cells, PECAM1 and VWF for endothelial cells, COL1A1 and PDGFRA for fibroblasts, CD68 and CSF1R for macrophages, CD3D and CD3E for T cells, CD38 and IGHG1 for plasma cells, IRF8 for pDC, and TPSAB1 and KIT for mast cells (Fig. 1D).

  At the patient level, cells from different individuals were well represented across the integrated manifold, indicating effective cross-sample integration while preserving patient-enriched neighborhoods (Figure S1A). Unsupervised clustering identified multiple transcriptional clusters spanning malignant and microenvironmental compartments (Figure S1B), and cell count summaries confirmed epithelial cells as the dominant population relative to immune and stromal lineages (Figure S1C). Quantification of cellular composition across patients revealed pronounced inter-tumoral heterogeneity: epithelial cells dominated most samples, while the relative abundance of immune and stromal compartments varied widely among individuals (Fig. 1E). When stratified by lineage, subtype contributions differed markedly, indicating subtype-dependent remodeling of the tumor microenvironment (Fig. 1F). For example, endothelial cells were enriched in HER2-positive tumors (HER2 61.9% vs. ER 29.3% and TN 8.8%), plasma cells were strongly skewed toward HER2-positive tumors (HER2 79.9%), and pDC were predominantly derived from ER-positive tumors (ER 67.3%). In contrast, epithelial cells were contributed by all three subtypes (TN 41.2%, HER2 26.8%, ER 32.0%), consistent with tumor-intrinsic programs being shared yet differentially represented across clinical groups (Fig. 1F).

  Given that tumor evolution is primarily encoded within malignant epithelial populations, we further resolved epithelial diversity by subclustering the epithelial compartment into 14 transcriptionally distinct epithelial clusters (0–13) (Figure S1D). The distribution of patients and molecular subtypes across epithelial subclusters was uneven, with several subclusters showing patient- and/or subtype-biased representation, consistent with heterogeneous tumor-cell state composition across tumors (Figure S1E). Differential expression analysis across epithelial subclusters revealed distinct gene programs, including epithelial lineage and differentiation markers (e.g., EPCAM- and keratin-associated genes), proliferation/cell-cycle signatures (e.g., PCNA, HMGB2, CENPF, UBE2S), subtype-linked oncogenic expression patterns (including ERBB2-enriched signals in selected subclusters), and interferon/stress-associated programs (e.g., ISG15 and IFI-family genes) (Figure S1F). These analyses establish a patient-resolved atlas and an epithelial state map that support subsequent CNV-based evolutionary analyses.


### CNV-derived clonal architecture tracks subtype-specific epithelial trajectories
  To distinguish malignant epithelial populations from non-malignant cells and to reconstruct subtype-linked evolutionary patterns, we inferred large-scale CNV profiles from single-cell transcriptomes. Epithelial subclusters displayed markedly elevated CNV scores compared with T cells, supporting their malignant identity and revealing heterogeneous aneuploidy across epithelial states (Fig. 2A). At genome scale, CNV heatmaps showed structured chromosomal gains and losses across epithelial cells (Figure S2A), and subtype-stratified visualization highlighted distinct CNV landscapes for ER, HER2, and TN tumors (Figure S2B–D). CNV-based clonal architectures reconstructed across subtypes exhibited branched evolutionary patterns with subtype-associated chromosomal alteration features (Fig. 2B). Consistently, genome-wide comparisons further emphasized differences in the distribution and magnitude of CNV events across subtypes (Fig. 2C).

  To integrate genomic variation with transcriptional state dynamics, we reconstructed epithelial trajectories and ordered cells along pseudotime. The inferred trajectory exhibited a branched structure and a continuous pseudotime axis, indicating gradual progression with discrete branch points (Fig. 2D). Mapping discrete trajectory states identified three major states occupying distinct regions of the trajectory (Fig. 2E). To assess whether this structure was robust to the choice of trajectory algorithm, we re-analyzed the same malignant epithelial compartment using Slingshot. The Slingshot result reproduced a comparable branched progression architecture with a shared trunk and bifurcating lineages (Figure S2E), supporting that the major trajectory organization underlying the three-state framework was not specific to Monocle2. These results strengthen the interpretation of States 1–3 as a reproducible trajectory-supported malignant transcriptional framework. Subtype mapping further revealed biased occupancy of the trajectory architecture by TN, HER2, and ER tumors (Figure S2G), and state-level visualization highlighted clear separation of state programs around branch points (Figure S2H). In parallel, CytoTRACE2 scores varied along the trajectory, supporting a gradient of cellular potential aligned with pseudotime progression (Figure S2F).

  To determine whether CNV-defined clonal groupings aligned with transcriptional progression. Projecting CNV-derived groups (CNVgroup) onto the trajectory manifold showed that CNVgroups were non-randomly distributed along the trajectory (Fig. 2F). This pattern was reinforced by the pseudotime distribution of CNVgroups, which displayed structured shifts in CNVgroup composition along progression (Figure S2I). Direct comparison between trajectory states and CNVgroups revealed strong coupling between transcriptional states and CNV-defined clonal structure (Fig. 2G), supported by both contingency-based statistics (Chi-square p < 1e − 16; Cramér’s V = 0.841, 95% CI 0.834–0.847) and information-theoretic dependence (mutual information = 0.7538; permutation p < 0.0009999) (Fig. 2G). Notably, the strength of coupling varied by subtype, with the highest subtype-specific association in TN (Cramér’s V ≈ 0.745) compared with ER (≈ 0.253) and HER2 (≈ 0.155) (Figure S2J).

  we identified state-differential gene programs along the trajectory and evaluated their overlap with breast cancer evolutionary genes (BCEG). Intersection analysis indicated a substantial BCEG component within the state-differential signatures (844 genes), alongside additional state-associated gene sets with smaller intersection sizes (Fig. 2I), providing a focused gene set for subsequent evolutionary and functional analyses. To further assess whether the three trajectory-defined epithelial states reflected clinically relevant malignant programs rather than dataset-specific computational partitions, we projected State 1/2/3 programs onto TCGA-BRCA bulk transcriptomes using deconvolution analysis. Survival analysis revealed significant prognostic differences across the inferred state groups, with the State 3-enriched group showing the poorest survival outcome (Fig. 2J). These findings support the interpretation that State 3 represents an externally reproducible and clinically relevant late malignant transcriptional program at the patient level.


### Transcription factor regulatory programs define state-specific epithelial modules and dynamic activation along progression
  To characterize the regulatory logic underlying the three epithelial states identified along the trajectory, we inferred transcription factor (TF) regulon activities and compared their state-specific patterns. Differential regulon activity analysis revealed distinct TF activity landscapes across states, with multiple regulons showing strong state bias and forming coherent activity modules (Fig. 3A). Module-level averaging further confirmed that state identity was accompanied by coordinated shifts in regulon activity rather than isolated TF changes, indicating that each state was supported by a characteristic regulatory program (Figure S3A).

Representative TF-centered subnetworks illustrated how state-preferential regulators connected to distinct target gene sets across the trajectory-defined states (Fig. 3C), supporting the interpretation that epithelial progression involves systematic rewiring of regulatory control.

  we assessed how these regulators changed along epithelial progression. Multiple representative TF activities exhibited gradual, structured variation along pseudotime (Fig. 3D), and mapping their activity scores onto the trajectory revealed state- and branch-associated activation patterns consistent with the state assignments (Fig. 3E). These data delineate a modular TF-regulatory architecture that stratifies epithelial cells into the three trajectory states and provides candidate regulators for subtype- and state-linked evolutionary transitions.


### Cell–cell communication analysis highlights microenvironmental signaling converging on the epithelial State 3 program
  To determine how the tumor microenvironment may shape epithelial state transitions, we inferred intercellular communication networks across immune, stromal, and epithelial compartments. The global interaction map revealed extensive bidirectional signaling among epithelial states (Epi_State1–3) and multiple microenvironmental populations, with pronounced connectivity between stromal/immune senders and epithelial receivers (Fig. 4A; Figure S4B). Quantitative comparison of signaling roles showed that fibroblasts and endothelial cells contributed the strongest outgoing communication strength, indicating that these stromal populations were major signal sources within the ecosystem (Fig. 4B). In contrast, macrophages displayed the highest incoming communication strength, followed by pDC and T cells, suggesting that myeloid and immune compartments were prominent signal recipients in this network (Fig. 4B). Epithelial states exhibited intermediate incoming and outgoing strengths, consistent with both receiving microenvironmental cues and broadcasting signals to surrounding cells (Fig. 4B; Figure S4A).

(B) Bar plots summarizing outgoing (sender) and incoming (receiver) communication strength across cell populations. (C) Dot plot of significant ligand–receptor interactions highlighting pathways involving Epi_State3. (F) Chord diagram illustrating representative molecule-level ligand–receptor interactions linking microenvironmental populations to epithelial states, highlighting collagen- and adhesion-related axes targeting Epi_State3

  We focused on Epi_State3, which occupied a distinct branch of the epithelial trajectory, to examine whether it was associated with a specific communication context. The ligand–receptor dot plot highlighted a concentrated set of significant interactions targeting Epi_State3 from multiple cell types, with particularly prominent contributions from fibroblasts and endothelial cells (Fig. 4C). Ranking of incoming pathways to Epi_State3 further indicated that extracellular matrix and adhesion-related signaling dominated the top inputs, with the COLLAGEN axis representing a major pathway across several sender populations (Fig. 4D). In addition to collagen-associated signaling, Epi_State3 received recurrent inputs involving THBS, FN1, and CD99, while macrophage-derived signals additionally included GALECTIN and SPP1-associated communication, suggesting layered stromal and myeloid regulation of this epithelial program (Fig. 4D).

  A pathway-level heatmap comparing incoming signals to Epi_State3 across sender populations confirmed that collagen/ECM-related signaling formed a shared core input from endothelial cells and fibroblasts, while additional pathways (e.g., FN1- and THBS-associated signaling) were differentially emphasized across immune and stromal sources (Fig. 4E). At the molecular level, a chord diagram summarized representative ligand–receptor pairs mediating these interactions and illustrated that Epi_State3 was coupled to a dense set of ECM and adhesion molecules, including multiple COL family members (e.g., COL1A1/COL1A2, COL6A1/COL6A2/COL6A3) and FN1, as well as CD99-linked interactions, consistent with an epithelial state embedded in strong matrix- and contact-dependent communication (Fig. 4F). These results support a communication model in which stromal populations act as dominant signal emitters and provide ECM/adhesion-centric inputs that preferentially converge on the Epi_State3 program.


### Spatial transcriptomics resolves tumor architecture and a State 3–centered niche in situ
  Spatial transcriptomic profiling revealed coherent transcriptional domains across the tissue section, with unsupervised clustering delineating discrete spatial regions that reflected compartmentalized tumor and surrounding programs (Fig. 5A). Boundary-aware region calling using Cottrazm further refined tissue organization by projecting CNV-defined groups onto the section and identifying sharp transitions between malignant and non-malignant territories (Fig. 5B). This boundary structure was supported by CNV score comparisons across the Cottrazm-derived CNV labels, which showed marked differences in inferred aneuploidy burden (Kruskal–Wallis p < 2.2e − 16) and reinforced spatial segregation of high-CNV tumor regions from low-CNV normal-like areas (Fig. 5C). In the same Cottrazm framework, a NormalScore provided an additional malignant–normal contrast, differing significantly across spatial identities (Kruskal–Wallis p < 2.2e − 16) (Figure S5A). Consistent with these spatial partitions, low-dimensional embedding of spots preserved the major compartment structure (Figure S5B), and independent Cottrazm region analysis reproduced macro-area boundaries aligned with tumor-dominant versus non-tumor zones (Figure S5C).

  With these Cottrazm-defined boundaries as a spatial scaffold, we annotated macroscopic zones corresponding to the invasive front, tumor core, and non-tumor regions (Fig. 5D) and mapped epithelial state scores onto the section. State 1–3 scores displayed non-uniform localization, with State 3 concentrated in discrete spatial areas rather than diffusely distributed across the tumor (Fig. 5E). Spatial cell-type score maps further indicated that immune and stromal populations were not evenly distributed but instead formed localized patterns across the same architecture, providing an ecosystem context for state-enriched regions (Figure S5D). Focusing on the invasive front, neighbor enrichment analysis showed that State 3–high spots were embedded within a characteristic local microenvironment, with systematic shifts in neighboring cell-type abundance compared with State 3–low areas (Fig. 5F). Finally, spatial ligand–receptor mapping suggested a State 3–centered signaling axis at the transcriptomic level. MFGE8 expression was predominantly contributed by Epi_State3 spots, and inferred interaction scores for MFGE8–ITGAV and MFGE8–PDGFRB formed localized hotspots overlapping State 3–enriched regions (Fig. 5G). These findings are consistent with a region-restricted invasive-front niche model but do not establish protein-level interaction in situ.


### MFGE8 is a State 3-associated functional effector linked to malignant behavior, with promoter-level support from LHX2
  We prioritized candidate downstream outputs of the LHX2-associated program and evaluated MFGE8 as a state 3–biased effector. MFGE8 expression mapped strongly onto the state 3 region on the trajectory manifold and was significantly upregulated in state 3 relative to states 1 and 2 (Fig. 6A–B), supporting MFGE8 as a prominent molecular output associated with the state 3 program.

  Fig. 6MFGE8 is a State 3-associated functional effector linked to malignant behavior. (A) Trajectory feature maps showing expression of MFGE8 and DDIT4 aligned with the state 3 branch. (B) Violin plots comparing MFGE8 and DDIT4 expression across epithelial States 1–3 with statistical significance. (C) In silico perturbation projections showing the trajectory redistribution (“rollback”) of state 3 cells after virtual knockout of DDIT4, MFGE8, or both. (D) Immunoblot validation of MFGE8 knockdown, overexpression, and rescue across experimental groups. (E) CCK-8 assays showing that MFGE8 depletion suppresses cell proliferation in BT549, whereas MFGE8 overexpression promotes growth and rescue restores proliferative capacity. (F) Transwell migration and invasion assays showing that MFGE8 modulates migratory and invasive behavior in BT549. (G) Wound-healing assays further confirming the effect of MFGE8 on BT549 cell migration

  We performed in silico perturbation to test whether MFGE8 contributed to maintenance of the state 3 transcriptional identity (Figure S6A-C). Virtual knockout of MFGE8 induced a pronounced redistribution away from the state 3 branch on the manifold, consistent with rollback toward earlier transcriptional space (Fig. 6C). In contrast, perturbation of an alternative candidate (DDIT4) produced a weaker shift, and the combined perturbation did not exceed the effect of MFGE8 alone, prioritizing MFGE8 as the strongest candidate effector associated with maintenance of the State 3 program in this analytical framework (Fig. 6C).

  To further characterize the downstream footprint of MFGE8, analysis of tumor cell line dependencies associated with MFGE8 amplification revealed a distinct set of genes with differential dependency patterns in MFGE8-amplified versus non-amplified contexts (Figure S6D), and functional enrichment highlighted RNA processing/splicing-related processes among the most significantly represented categories (Figure S6E). MFGE8-associated ligand–receptor pairs involving receptors such as ITGAV/ITGB5 and PDGFRB were detected in relevant interacting populations (Figure S6F), and integration of regulatory and signaling components yielded a directed schema linking LHX2 to MFGE8 and downstream receptor-mediated signaling nodes, integrative regulatory–signaling framework linking state 3-biased transcriptional regulation to microenvironment-facing outputs (Figure S6G). To experimentally assess whether the predicted LHX2–MFGE8 relationship had promoter-level support, we performed a dual-luciferase reporter assay using wild-type and motif-mutant MFGE8 promoter constructs. LHX2 significantly increased the activity of the wild-type MFGE8 promoter, whereas this effect was markedly attenuated in the mutant construct (Figure S6H). These findings support the interpretation that MFGE8 may be transcriptionally responsive to LHX2-associated regulatory input, consistent with the regulon-based inference.

  To directly test whether MFGE8 influences malignant cellular behavior, we performed MFGE8 knockdown, overexpression, and rescue experiments. Immunoblotting confirmed effective modulation of MFGE8 protein levels across the experimental groups (Fig. 6D). Functionally, MFGE8 depletion suppressed cell proliferation, whereas MFGE8 overexpression promoted growth and rescue restored proliferative capacity in CCK-8 assays (Fig. 6E). Similarly, Transwell migration/invasion and wound-healing assays showed that MFGE8 knockdown impaired migratory and invasive behavior, whereas MFGE8 overexpression enhanced these phenotypes and rescue restored them to varying degrees (Fig. 6F–G; Figure S6I–J). Together, these findings provide direct in vitro evidence that MFGE8 is not merely a State 3-associated marker, but a functional effector linked to late-state-associated malignant behavior.


### Structure-guided virtual screening identifies candidate small molecules with stable MFGE8 binding in molecular dynamics simulations
  To explore potential pharmacological strategies targeting MFGE8, we implemented a structure-guided in silico pipeline integrating library curation, pocket prediction, docking-based screening, and molecular dynamics (MD) refinement (Fig. 7A). A total of 14,191 compounds were downloaded from DrugBank and standardized using RDKit. After applying structural and physicochemical filters, 9,774 compounds were removed as unsuitable for downstream screening, and the remaining 4,417 molecules were retained for virtual screening. Binding-site prediction was performed using fpocket, and the top five ranked pockets were selected as docking targets. Docking across these pockets produced a ranked list of candidate ligands, from which five compounds (DB03571, DB07041, DB11652, DB20089, and DB21611) were advanced to short MD prescreening (Fig. 7A).

  Fig. 7Virtual screening and molecular dynamics refinement nominate small-molecule candidates with stable MFGE8 binding. (A) Schematic of the virtual screening and refinement workflow, including ligand library preparation, pocket identification, docking, robustness evaluation, and MD simulation. (B) Representative docking pose of the top-ranked compound in the MFGE8 binding pocket and detailed view of key residue interactions (e.g., ASN-36, SER-40, GLN-41, LEU-313). (C) RMSD trajectories for protein and ligand across the MD simulation. (D) Time-resolved protein–ligand hydrogen bond counts. (E) Protein residue RMSF (Cα) profiling. (F) Protein radius of gyration over time. (G) Time course of protein–ligand atomic contacts within the specified distance cutoff. (H) Time course of contacting protein residues within the cutoff. (I) Per-residue contact probability across MD frames, highlighting residues with persistent interactions

  Docking analysis placed candidate ligands into a defined MFGE8 pocket and suggested stabilizing contacts with surrounding residues (Fig. 7B). To prioritize a ligand for long-timescale refinement, we performed 10 ns MD simulations for the five candidates and compared early stability metrics across complexes (Figure S7). This prescreening identified DB20089 as the ligand with the most stable binding behavior, which was then selected for extended MD evaluation.

  DB20089 was subsequently subjected to a 100 ns MD simulation to assess binding stability and the associated MFGE8 structural dynamics. The protein–ligand complex reached a stable regime after initial equilibration, and RMSD traces for both the protein backbone and ligand remained within a constrained range during the remainder of the trajectory (Fig. 7C). Hydrogen bond analysis showed sustained intermolecular hydrogen bonding, consistent with durable binding-site engagement (Fig. 7D). Residue-level flexibility profiling (RMSF) indicated that backbone fluctuations were largely restricted to limited regions without global destabilization (Fig. 7E). The radius of gyration remained stable, supporting preservation of overall protein compactness in the bound state (Fig. 7F). Contact-based analyses further supported persistent binding: the number of protein–ligand atomic contacts increased during equilibration and remained high thereafter (Fig. 7G), and the number of contacting MFGE8 residues likewise stabilized, indicating a sustained interaction interface (Fig. 7H). Per-residue contact probability highlighted a subset of residues with high interaction frequency across frames, suggesting a consistent contact pattern for DB20089 rather than transient binding (Fig. 7I).


### BCEG-based stratification defines two breast cancer classes with distinct immune contexture, clinical features, and pathway activities
  To translate breast cancer evolutionary genes (BCEG) into clinically informative stratification, we clustered bulk tumors using the BCEG expression matrix and identified two robust classes (C1 and C2) (Figure S8A). Kaplan–Meier analysis showed a significant survival difference between the two classes (p = 0.029), indicating that BCEG-based classification captured prognostically relevant heterogeneity (Figure S8B). Consistent with divergent clinical behavior, C1 and C2 exhibited distinct distributions of standard clinicopathological variables, including hormone receptor status (ER/PR), HER2 status, pathological stage, and TNM categories, as well as differences in PAM50 molecular subtype composition (Figure S8C). These results support BCEG signatures as a compact basis for patient stratification beyond conventional subtype labels.

  We interrogated biological characteristics underlying this separation. Breast cancer functional signature scoring indicated that C2 displayed higher proliferation-associated signals, whereas C1 was relatively enriched for hormone-related programs (Figure S8D). Concordantly, oncogenic signaling scores showed marked class differences across multiple pathways, with C2 displaying stronger cell-cycle–linked activity and distinct patterns across WNT/TP53 and other signaling axes compared with C1 (Figure S8E). At the transcriptional regulator level, a curated TF panel further distinguished the classes, with C1 showing higher expression of luminal lineage regulators (e.g., ESR1/FOXA1/GATA3) and C2 showing relative enrichment of basal-like/lineage-plasticity-associated regulators (Figure S8F). In parallel, pathway-level metabolic programs also differed between C1 and C2, indicating coordinated rewiring of tumor metabolism between BCEG-defined classes (Figure S8G). These findings suggest that BCEG-based grouping reflects broad divergence in tumor-intrinsic regulatory circuitry and pathway utilization.

  Because evolutionary dynamics may be tightly coupled to immune selection, we evaluated immune microenvironmental features of the two classes. Global ESTIMATE scoring revealed no significant difference in overall ESTIMATE score (p = 0.84), but both immune and stromal components differed significantly between C1 and C2 (ImmuneScore p = 4.1e − 06; StromalScore p = 1.2e − 11), indicating class-specific remodeling of immune and stromal infiltration (Figure S9A). Cell-type deconvolution further demonstrated systematic differences across lymphoid and myeloid lineages, including multiple T-cell subsets, B-cell compartments, NK cells, macrophage polarization states, dendritic cells, and mast cell activation states (Figure S9B). In addition, immune subtype composition differed between the classes, consistent with BCEG classes capturing distinct immune ecosystem states rather than only tumor-intrinsic differences (Figure S9E).

  To connect these patterns to immune function, we compared immunomodulatory gene modules and immune pathway scores. Ligand–receptor and immune regulator heatmaps highlighted broad differences across co-stimulatory/co-inhibitory axes, cytokine/chemokine signaling, antigen presentation, and other immune programs between C1 and C2 (Figure S9C). Signature scoring further showed that C2 exhibited higher levels across several immune-related programs, including antigen processing/presentation and immune checkpoint-related metrics (e.g., ICR), alongside differences in BCR/TCR-associated signals and interferon-related activity (Figure S9D). These results indicate that BCEG-based stratification delineates breast cancer classes with coordinated divergence in tumor signaling, lineage programs, and immune contexture, providing a framework to interpret evolution-associated transcriptional states in relation to clinical phenotype.


### A BCEG-derived prognostic signature was optimized across multi-cohort machine learning and stratified outcomes and clinical phenotypes
  To translate breast cancer evolutionary genes (BCEG) into a clinically actionable risk predictor, we constructed a prognostic model using a stepwise feature-selection framework. Starting from the BCEG set (n = 844), we intersected this list with differentially expressed genes and previously defined prognostic genes, yielding a compact candidate pool for model development (Fig. 8A). These candidates were mapped to genomic locations and directionality of expression change, providing an overview of their distribution and potential contribution to risk stratification (Fig. 8B).

  Fig. 8A BCEG-derived multi-cohort prognostic model identifies a robust 10-gene signature for risk stratification. (A) Overlap of breast cancer evolutionary genes (BCEG) with differentially expressed genes and prognostic genes, yielding a reduced candidate set for model training. (B) Genomic and expression overview of candidate genes used for model construction. (C) Schematic of model development using 10 algorithms combined into 117 model configurations, trained on TCGA-BRCA and validated across multiple external cohorts. (D) Heatmap of C-index performance across model configurations and cohorts, showing mean C-index in training and validation. (E) Coefficient plot for the final 10-gene signature.   We performed systematic model training using 10 algorithms assembled into 117 algorithmic combinations, with TCGA-BRCA as the training cohort and multiple independent cohorts as external validation sets (Fig. 8C). Model performance was evaluated by concordance index (C-index) across cohorts, revealing clear performance differences among algorithmic strategies (Fig. 8D). Based on mean C-index consistency across training and validation cohorts, we selected the top-performing approach and derived a final parsimonious signature consisting of 10 genes (PROM2, GYPC, PSMD14, ECHDC2, ERRFI1, SIAH2, CACYBP, UBE2T, CELSR2, and JUN) with corresponding coefficients (Fig. 8E).

  Using this signature, patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups. In the TCGA-BRCA training set, the high-risk group showed significantly poorer overall survival compared with the low-risk group (log-rank p = 0.0023) (Fig. 8F). Risk groups also differed in clinical composition, with significant differences in vital status (p = 0.008) and broadly comparable distributions across stage and TNM categories in this cohort-level summary (Fig. 8G), supporting that the signature captured outcome-relevant heterogeneity while not simply recapitulating a single clinicopathological variable.

  To assess robustness and generalizability, we evaluated the prognostic effect across multiple external cohorts. Meta-analysis of univariate Cox regression demonstrated a consistent association between the risk score and survival across centers, with pooled hazard ratios indicating reproducible prognostic separation under both fixed- and random-effects models (Figure S10A). Time-dependent ROC analyses further supported predictive accuracy at 1, 3, and 5 years across cohorts (Figure S10B). In addition, comparative benchmarking against previously published breast cancer signatures showed that this BCEG-derived model achieved competitive performance across external datasets (Figure S10C). Consistently, Kaplan–Meier analyses in each validation cohort reproduced significant survival separation between high- and low-risk groups (Figure S11), confirming the portability of the signature across independent populations.


### BCEG score stratification is associated with distinct genomic instability patterns and predicts differential immunotherapy responsiveness
  To investigate the biological basis of BCEG score stratification, we compared multi-omic features between the high-score and low-score groups in TCGA-BRCA. Genome-wide alteration profiling revealed clear differences in mutation and copy-number landscapes between the two groups (Fig. 9A). Consistent with increased genomic instability, the low-score group exhibited a higher tumor mutational burden (TMB) than the high-score group (p = 0.00027) (Fig. 9B). Copy-number comparisons further demonstrated significantly elevated arm-level gains and losses in the low-score group (arm gain p = 0.00027; arm loss p = 0.0073), indicating broader chromosomal instability accompanying the low BCEG score class in this cohort (Fig. 9C).

(J–M) Representative multiplex immunofluorescence images from the in-house breast cancer cohort, showing marker staining patterns in high-score (J, L) and low-score (K, M) tumors (scale bars, 50 μm)

  We examined the relationship between BCEG score and immune checkpoint biology. Correlation analysis showed that BCEG score was broadly associated with the expression of multiple immune checkpoint genes, spanning both inhibitory and stimulatory regulators (Fig. 9D). In line with these checkpoint-linked patterns, TIDE-based analyses indicated significant differences between the high-score and low-score groups in immune evasion–related metrics, including the composite TIDE score (p = 0.0019) and dysfunction score (p = 0.0058), while exclusion showed no clear separation (p = 0.0751) (Fig. 9E). When extending prediction to specific immune checkpoint blockade settings, the two score groups also differed in their inferred response profiles across multiple therapeutic scenarios, including anti–CTLA-4 and anti–PD-1/PD-L1-related contexts (Fig. 9F), supporting that BCEG score captures clinically relevant immune-response heterogeneity.

  To provide external evidence in an immunotherapy-treated population, we evaluated the score in the IMvigor210 cohort. BCEG score stratification was associated with clinical response categories (CR/PR versus SD/PD) (Fig. 9G). The distribution of response states differed between high-score and low-score groups (Fig. 9H), and survival analysis demonstrated significant outcome separation (log-rank p = 0.0011) (Fig. 9I), supporting the utility of BCEG score for immunotherapy-associated prognosis.

  To validate immune context differences in real-world breast cancer specimens, we analyzed an independent in-house cohort (> 100 tumors) profiled by RNA-seq and performed multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF). Representative high-score samples displayed a distinct immune marker pattern compared with low-score samples (Fig. 9J–M), with tumor (panCK) and immune markers (including CD68, CD19, CD56, CD3, PD-1, PD-L1, FOXP3, and CD8) showing differential abundance and spatial distribution across score groups. These analyses link BCEG score to tumor genomic features, immune checkpoint programs, and predicted as well as observed immunotherapy-associated phenotypes.


## Discussion
  This study establishes an integrated single-cell and spatial transcriptomic framework to dissect breast cancer evolutionary heterogeneity across ER-positive, HER2-positive, and triple-negative tumors. By combining malignant-cell CNV inference, trajectory reconstruction, TF regulatory modeling, cell–cell communication analysis, spatial niche mapping, and downstream translational modeling, our results support a unified view in which subtype-associated ecosystems and epithelial state dynamics are closely linked to genomic instability, regulatory rewiring, and microenvironment-facing ligand outputs.

  At single-cell resolution, breast cancers from different clinical subtypes shared core epithelial programs but differed markedly in lineage composition and ecosystem architecture. In particular, the enrichment of endothelial and plasma cell compartments in HER2-positive tumors and the predominance of pDC in ER-positive tumors suggest that subtype labels capture not only tumor-intrinsic biology but also systematic differences in stromal and immune organization [5, 24, 25]. Within malignant epithelial cells, transcriptional heterogeneity could be organized along a branched progression framework rather than as a set of isolated clusters, with distinct lineage/differentiation, proliferative, ERBB2-linked, and interferon/stress-associated programs represented across subclusters [26]. The strong concordance between CNV-defined groups and trajectory states further supports an association between clonal architecture and malignant-state organization [27]. Notably, this coupling was strongest in triple-negative tumors, suggesting tighter alignment between CNV-linked clonal evolution and transcriptional progression in TN disease than in ER-positive or HER2-positive tumors, where greater plasticity may weaken clone–state correspondence [28].

  The TF regulon analysis further characterizes the regulatory programs associated with epithelial state transitions. Rather than isolated TF changes, we observed modular, state-preferential TF activity programs with strong within-module connectivity, consistent with coordinated regulatory neighborhoods that may buffer or reinforce state identity. The gradual, structured variation of representative TF activities along pseudotime supports a model of progressive regulatory rewiring, where branch points correspond to shifts in dominant regulatory modules [29]. This modular architecture provides plausible mechanistic entry points for interventions aimed at redirecting malignant trajectories: targeting an upstream regulator or a key module hub could, in principle, shift the system away from late-trajectory programs that may be associated with invasive behavior or therapy resistance [30].

  A notable finding of this study is that the epithelial State 3 program is embedded within a distinctive stromal and spatial context. Communication analyses indicate that fibroblasts and endothelial cells act as dominant signal emitters, with ECM/adhesion-related pathways, including collagen-associated axes, THBS, and FN1, among the strongest inferred inputs to State 3. Additional macrophage-derived signals, such as GALECTIN- and SPP1-associated communication, suggest that myeloid populations may further shape this late epithelial program [31]. Spatial transcriptomics extends this observation by showing that State 3 is enriched in discrete tissue regions, particularly an invasive-front-associated niche with characteristic neighbor enrichment, supporting the interpretation that late malignant programs are spatially organized rather than uniformly distributed across the tumor [32]. Together, these transcript-level findings are consistent with a model in which State 3 is preferentially maintained in a matrix-rich microenvironment where stromal and immune inputs converge. The spatial overlap between MFGE8-enriched Epi_State3 spots, receptor-associated transcriptomic signals, and inferred interaction hotspots further supports a State 3-centered signaling axis at the transcriptomic level, although direct protein-level validation remains to be established [33, 34].

  Within this late-state context, MFGE8 emerged as a particularly relevant candidate effector. The increase of LHX2 activity along pseudotime and its preference toward the State 3 branch suggest that LHX2 may participate in shaping late-trajectory transcriptional identity, whereas MFGE8 represents a microenvironment-facing output associated with this program [35]. In silico perturbation prioritized MFGE8 over alternative candidates by showing a stronger rollback-associated effect, and the revised study further strengthens its relevance through direct in vitro validation demonstrating that MFGE8 modulates proliferation, migration, and invasion. These findings support MFGE8 as a functionally relevant State 3-associated effector, rather than merely a marker of late progression. Prior breast cancer studies are also consistent with a context-dependent role for MFGE8: it has been linked to triple-negative breast cancer, adverse prognosis, and aggressive phenotypes, while also showing subtype- and isoform-dependent complexity [36–41]. In this context, our study extends the existing literature by placing MFGE8 within a trajectory-supported late malignant program, linking it to invasive-front-associated spatial localization and to an LHX2-associated regulatory context. At the same time, the current evidence is most appropriately interpreted as supporting a candidate regulatory and signaling module, rather than a fully demonstrated direct mechanism.

  The translational extensions of our work suggest that evolution-associated gene programs can be leveraged for prognostic and therapeutic inference. By defining BCEG from state-differential programs and using them to stratify bulk tumors, we identified two robust classes with distinct survival, pathway activity, lineage-regulator patterns, and immune/stromal infiltration features. This supports the idea that single-cell–derived evolutionary signatures can bridge tumor-intrinsic state dynamics and patient-level clinical behavior [42]. The subsequent construction of a 10-gene prognostic model across multi-cohort machine learning and external validation indicates that a compact BCEG-derived signature can capture outcome-relevant heterogeneity in a way that generalizes across centers. Importantly, the association of BCEG score stratification with genomic instability patterns (TMB and arm-level CNV differences) suggests that the score may reflect the balance between mutational processes and chromosomal instability, both of which can influence immune recognition and therapeutic response [43].

  Consistent with the close interplay between evolution and immune selection, BCEG score groups showed broad associations with immune checkpoint gene expression and differences in TIDE-derived immune evasion metrics, as well as predicted response differences across checkpoint blockade scenarios. The observation that BCEG score stratifies response categories and survival in an external immunotherapy-treated cohort provides additional support that the evolutionary program captured by BCEG is not purely prognostic but may relate to immunotherapy responsiveness [44]. The in-house RNA-seq and multiplex immunofluorescence analyses further reinforce that these score-defined groups correspond to tangible differences in immune composition and checkpoint marker distribution in real tumor specimens. Together, these findings suggest that evolutionary state programs may imprint both tumor cell-intrinsic vulnerabilities and immune ecosystem configurations, offering a path toward integrated risk assessment and therapeutic prioritization [45].

  Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, CNV inference from scRNA-seq provides an approximation of genomic alterations and may be influenced by expression variability, stromal contamination, or reference selection; therefore, orthogonal genomic validation would strengthen claims about clonal structure [46]. Second, pseudotime and trajectory models infer progression from transcriptomic similarity and cannot definitively establish temporal ordering; longitudinal sampling or lineage tracing is needed to confirm inferred transitions, particularly around branch points [47]. Third, cell–cell communication analyses rely on curated ligand–receptor databases and expression-based assumptions that may not capture post-transcriptional regulation or spatial constraints; integrating proteomic measurements or functional assays would refine the inferred signaling networks [48]. Fourth, although the study includes in vitro functional validation showing that MFGE8 modulates proliferation, migration, and invasion, the rollback phenotype itself remains inferred from computational perturbation and should be further tested using orthogonal experimental systems, ideally including in vivo or spatially resolved validation. Finally, the BCEG-derived prognostic and immunotherapy associations, while supported by external validation and an in-house cohort, may still be confounded by treatment heterogeneity and cohort-specific biases; prospective evaluation and harmonized clinical metadata will be important for clinical translation.

  Future studies could build on these findings in several directions. Mechanistically, dissecting how LHX2 is activated along progression and how it interfaces with chromatin state or signaling inputs could clarify upstream drivers of the State 3 program. Functionally, testing whether MFGE8 blockade disrupts ECM/adhesion signaling feedback loops, alters invasive-front organization, or reshapes immune infiltration would directly probe the niche-coupled model suggested by our spatial analyses. Clinically, integrating BCEG scores with established subtype classification, spatial immune phenotypes, and genomic instability measures may yield composite predictors that better guide immunotherapy or combination strategies. Finally, expanding spatial sampling across multiple tumors and subtypes would determine whether the State 3 niche and MFGE8-centered signaling axis represent a generalizable invasive-front program or a context-dependent phenomenon.


## Conclusions
  Overall, our results support a model in which breast cancer evolution involves coordinated CNV-linked clonal structure, modular TF regulatory rewiring, and microenvironmental signaling that converges on a spatially localized late epithelial state. The LHX2–MFGE8 axis emerges as a candidate regulatory-output module that may stabilize this niche-associated program, while BCEG-derived stratification and prognostic modeling connect evolutionary state biology to patient-level outcomes and immunotherapy-related phenotypes.
