# Offspring genetic diversity regulates rearing experiences that predict differential susceptibility to Chd8 haploinsufficiency


## Abstract
  Loss-of-function mutations in the autism-associated CHD8 gene are highly penetrant for trait and behavioral abnormalities in children, but there is substantial clinical heterogeneity in the occurrence and extent of disruptions between individuals. Using a large genetic reference panel of mice, we recently showed that this heterogeneity is, in part, regulated by genetic background. Here, we hypothesize that genetic background may also influence early life experiences, further shaping individual susceptibility to neurodevelopmental disorders. To test this, systematic observations were conducted to track behaviors during the rearing of genetically diverse Collaborative Cross offspring raised by genetically identical dams. Biostatistical and machine-learning analyses of the data reveal robust strain-dependent differences in both pup and maternal behaviors that significantly predict sex-specific alterations in body and brain weights, as well as social, anxiety-like, and cognitive trait disruptions following weaning due to Chd8 haploinsufficiency. These results suggest that phenotypic variability in disease models arises from an interaction between inherited mutations and genetically influenced early-life environments. This work highlights the value of incorporating genetic diversity into model systems to better understand the origins of heterogeneity in neurodevelopmental disorders.

  


## Introduction
  CHD8 haploinsufficiency is highly penetrant for an autism spectrum disorder (ASD) diagnosis, intellectual disability disorder (IDD), and macrocephaly, along with other, less penetrant co-occurring symptoms1–3. There is, however, documented broad heterogeneity in the phenotypic manifestation of CHD8 haploinsufficiency between impacted individuals, including in the occurrence and severity of highly penetrant traits and co-occurring trait disruptions1,4,5. There is a knowledge gap in understanding the mechanisms that contribute to individual differences in the occurrence and severity of trait disruptions due to CHD8 haploinsufficiency, as well as other single-gene mutations in neurodevelopmental disorder (NDD) risk genes. Understanding these mechanisms is important to improve precision interventions that yield functional improvements. Research in humans has indicated that genetic background contributes to a phenotypic spectrum of trait disruptions in ASD6,7. Likewise, we and others have demonstrated the impact of controlled variation of genetic background on brain structure and function by using genetic reference panels (GRPs). We recently leveraged genetically diverse, recombinant inbred GRP mouse strains with Chd8 haploinsufficiency and found heterogeneity in the occurrence, severity, and combination of trait disruptions across strain and sex groups that exhibited similarities to the heterogeneity reported in clinical populations8. Therefore, evidence points to the genetic background as one factor that mediates heterogeneous outcomes in CHD8 haploinsufficiency. There is little understanding, however, of the impact of genetic background in regulating individual differences in the early postnatal environment experienced by offspring that may contribute to variable phenotypic outcomes.

  In rodent models, variation in early-life experiences, particularly in parental care, profoundly shapes offspring development. For example, manipulating environmental resources, such as limiting bedding and nesting, alters the frequency and patterns of dam provisioning, including attending to offspring and leaving the nest9–11. This variability and unpredictability in dam nest exits directly affects offspring's physical growth as well as metabolic, neural, and behavioral development11–16. Naturally occurring differences in maternal licking and grooming and arched-back nursing also shape offspring brain development and stress reactivity17. The presence of additional caregivers, including the father, can also modify early-life experiences, adult traits, and underlying neurochemical systems18–21. Importantly, genetic variation in offspring can also influence both their own developmental behaviors and maternal responses they elicit, which in turn may impact early-life experiences and developmental outcomes22–25. Despite the recognized complexity of gene and environment factors, most studies of early-life influences on NDDs use a single inbred strain, limiting the ability to capture the diversity of early social environments found in natural populations and their impact on NDD outcomes. While understudied from a genetic perspective, evidence from human and animal studies consistently underscores the critical role of variation in parent-offspring interactions in shaping lifelong expression of biological and behavioral phenotypes.

  Here, we aimed to determine the extent to which offspring genetic background contributes to variation in the preweaning litter environment in a genetically diverse murine model of Chd8 haploinsufficiency, and the impact of that variation on Chd8-driven trait outcomes across strains. To determine if the genetic background of the offspring impacts maternal and pup behaviors, genetically identical B6 dams, heterozygous for Chd8 (Chd8+/−), were observed for maternal behavior towards their WT and Chd8+/− offspring from 16 genetically diverse and inbred mouse strains. Moreover, we measured the age at which developmental milestones were first observed across strains. Finally, we investigated whether maternal and pup behaviors across a subset of offspring were associated with both typical trait development as well as trait disruptions due to Chd8 haploinsufficiency. Results demonstrate that offspring genetic background regulates individual differences in maternal-offspring interactions and preweaning experiences with large to moderate effect sizes. Remarkably, these differences are highly predictive of differential susceptibility to Chd8 haploinsufficiency. Our results highlight the contribution of genetic background to both developmental heterogeneity and differential susceptibility to neurodevelopmental disruptions by modulating early-life environmental factors.


## Results

### Offspring genetic background shapes individual differences in litter survival, B6-Chd8+/− maternal care, and nest quality

Sires were consistently separated from pregnant dams approximately 1 week prior to litter birth. Pregnant females were checked daily for litter births, and the day of birth was designated as postnatal day (P) 0. From P1 until weaning at P21, litters were observed at approximately the same time for one hour per day at five-minute intervals during the light phase. During observations, the frequency of dam and pup behaviors for each litter was spot-checked and scored by a researcher blind to the strain of the offspring (Fig. 1A). Each spot check consisted of recording one tally mark for each behavior displayed. Coat colors overlapped across multiple strains, preventing identification of offspring strain from appearance and thereby maintaining effective blinding during scoring.   Nest quality scores varied over postnatal development, with nest quality decreasing as offspring age increased (Fig. 1B). The frequency of B6-Chd8+/− dam behaviors toward the combined strain population of litters varied by measure and postnatal day (Supplementary Fig. 1E–L). The frequency of B6-Chd8+/− dam behaviors observed across P1–21 was reduced statistically to factors that captured most of the behavioral variance via principal component analysis (PCA). Two principal components (PCs) accounted for 66% of the total variance (Fig. 1C). PC 1 represented dam-centered behaviors with the highest loadings for out-of-the-nest (0.78), eating (0.83), and running (0.45) frequencies, which were negatively related to passive nursing (-0.83) and resting (−0.82). PC 2 represented maternal care, with the highest loadings for active nursing (0.90), licking and grooming (0.85), and nest building (0.52), and was more modestly negatively related to passive nursing (−0.47) and resting (−0.51). Regression scores derived from the two PCs were calculated for each litter. PC 1 increased and PC 2 decreased across preweaning development, intersecting and reversing direction relative to each other by P8 (Fig. 1D).

  In the combined strain population, litter size significantly correlated with multiple dam measures across P1–21 with small to large effect sizes (Supplementary Fig. 1O). The largest correlation coefficients reflected a negative association with nest quality scores (r = −0.52) and positive associations with dam out-of-the-nest (r = 0.48) and eating (r = 0.48) frequencies. The percentage of males per litter was positively associated with nest quality scores, but with a small effect size (r = 0.22). The percentage of Chd8+/− pups per litter did not correlate with any measures.

  There was a significant effect of offspring strain on litter survival at weaning (X2 = 34.72, p < 0.01). Follow-up comparisons with Bonferroni-corrected p-values revealed that B6-Chd8+/− dams that gave birth to B6-B6 litters had a lower proportion of litters that survived to weaning compared to all other strains (Fig. 1E). Offspring strain had no significant effect on litter size, sex ratio, or the percentage of Chd8+/− pups per litter (Supplementary Fig. 2A–C). The mean litter size for each strain ranged from 5.2 to 7.6 pups.

  To determine if offspring strain impacts B6-Chd8+/− dam behaviors, B6-Chd8+/− dam trait frequencies across offspring strain groups were analyzed with Linear Mixed Models (LMMs) with litter size, the percentage of males and Chd8+/− pups per litter, and dam parity covaried to control for variance in dam behaviors due to these factors. Because the combination of dam behaviors changes over time prior to weaning, including B6-Chd8+/− dam behaviors (Fig. 1D), and offspring undergo biological and behavioral changes across specific developmental epochs, we binned the data into separate postnatal weeks (PNWs). Most litters for each strain were observed for five days per postnatal week (Supplementary Data 1). Litters were required to have at least three observation days within a given week to be included in the time-course analysis. Bonferroni-corrected post hoc comparisons were conducted to determine which offspring strain groups differed from each other on measures that showed significant strain differences. These are fully detailed in Supplementary Data 2, with effect size estimates highlighting the magnitude of strain differences.

  For B6-Chd8+/− dams PC 1 and PC 2 scores, there were no differences across offspring strains during PNWs 1, 2, and 3 (Supplementary Fig. 2D, E). There were also no differences in individual B6-Chd8+/− dam behaviors during PNW 1 (Supplementary Fig. 2F, G). However, during PNW 2, B6-Chd8+/− dams’ total nursing (F15, 105 = 1.926, p < 0.05), out-of-the-nest (F15, 105 = 3.109, p < 0.001), and eating (F15, 104 = 2.058, p < 0.05) frequencies differed as a function of offspring strain (Fig. 1F). During PNW 3, total nursing (F15, 104 = 2.982, p < 0.001) and nest building (F15, 100 = 3.616, p < 0.001) frequencies differed across offspring strains (Fig. 1G).

  To determine if B6-Chd8+/− dam behaviors differ across offspring strains across the entire P1–21 duration, a subset of litters that were observed for at least two postnatal days during PNW 1, 2, and 3, were analyzed for differences across offspring strain groups. Most litters had 15 total observation days across P1–21 (Supplementary Data 1). Offspring strain impacted B6-Chd8+/− dams total nursing (F15, 98 = 2.284, p < 0.01), licking and grooming (F15, 99 = 2.074, p < 0.05), out-of-the-nest (F15, 99 = 1.918, p < 0.05), and eating (F15, 99 = 1.94, p < 0.05) frequencies across P1–21 (Fig. 1H, I). Eating frequencies across P1–21 did not survive Bonferroni-corrected post hoc strain comparisons. Passive nursing, active nursing, nest building, resting, running, and dam PC scores did not differ as a function of offspring strain during the P1–21 period (Fig. 1H, I and Supplementary Fig. 2L, M). The heritability of B6-Chd8+/− maternal behaviors during the P1–21 period was estimated by quantifying the proportion of variance in dam behaviors explained by genetic differences among their offspring, after controlling for litter composition (Fig. 1H, I). Among these behaviors, total nursing (0.4), licking and grooming (0.3), out-of-the-nest (0.4), and eating (0.4) frequencies showed moderate heritability, while all other behaviors exhibited low heritability (H2 ≤ 0.2). Moderate heritability for key maternal behaviors, including total nursing, licking and grooming, out of the nest, and eating, further suggests these behaviors are sensitive to offspring genetics in B6-Chd8+/− dams.

  Nest quality scores differed by offspring strains across each of the weeks (PNW 1: F15, 99 = 1.886, p < 0.05; PNW 2: F15, 93 = 3.470, p < 0.001; PNW 3: F15, 97 = 3.999, p < 0.001; Fig. 1J). The data indicates that nest quality scores are sensitive to offspring strain during all PNWs, while the behavioral frequencies of B6-Chd8+/− dams are more sensitive to offspring strain during later PNWs. The results are consistent with there being time-dependent differences in the influence of genetic background on specific dam behaviors.

  Figure 1K–M highlights examples of strains that differ from each other with large effect sizes. B6-Chd8+/− dams with B6-CC12 offspring had more total nursing frequencies compared to dams with B6-CC25 offspring (Fig. 1K; P1–21 d = 2.57). In addition, B6-Chd8+/− dams with B6-CC10 offspring were observed to leave the nest more frequently compared to dams with B6-B6 offspring (Fig. 1L; P1–21 d = 1.38). Moreover, litters with B6-CC1 offspring had reduced nest quality scores compared to litters with B6-CC22 offspring (Fig. 1M; P1–21 d = 2.50). These data demonstrate that offspring strain can impact B6-Chd8+/− maternal care and nest quality throughout preweaning development, with some strains eliciting large effect size differences.

  We next wondered whether the durations and patterns of B6-Chd8+/− dam’s behavior might differ as a function of offspring strain. To test this, the frequency and duration of maternal behaviors of B6-Chd8+/− dams with B6-B6 and B6-CC12 offspring were quantified and compared at select days spanning the 3-week preweaning period (P4, P5, P11, P12, P18, and P19). We selected these two strains for expanded observations because they were being used in separate experiments investigating strain differences in social behavior development, enabling coordinated follow-up work. B6-Chd8+/− dam behavioral durations were scored in real time for three 5-min durations spaced 15 min apart. Behavioral observations consisted of 5-min scoring durations every 15 min for 1 h. Nest quality was scored in addition to the duration the dam spent out of the nest, licking and grooming pups, total nursing, and nest building, as well as the frequency of nest exits by the dam. Differences in dam behaviors were analyzed using LMM’s that included all postnatal days simultaneously, along with litter composition covariates, and tested for a main interaction effect of strain by day. Significant interactions were subsequently followed up with Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc comparisons to determine which days strains differed on. The data revealed substantial variation in the durations of maternal behaviors at P4 (Fig. 2). B6-Chd8+/− dams with B6-CC12 offspring had increased out-of-the-nest durations (F11, 23.335 = 2.787, p < 0.05; d = 1.1) compared to dams with B6-B6 offspring on P4 (Fig. 2B). In addition, B6-Chd8+/− dams with B6-CC12 offspring had decreased licking and grooming (F11, 24.437 = 4.438, p < 0.001; d = 0.8) and nursing (F11, 23.824 = 2.345, p < 0.05; d = 1.1) durations on P4, compared to dams with B6-B6 offspring (Fig. 2C, D). There were no significant differences in the time B6-Chd8+/− dams with B6-CC12 versus B6-B6 offspring spent nest building, but B6-CC12 litters had increased nest quality scores on P19 compared to dams with B6-B6 offspring (F11, 16.251 = 6.05, p < 0.001; d = 1.1; Fig. 2E, F). The difference in nest quality at P19 (3.5 vs. 2.7) reflected notable variation in nest structure, including flatter, less developed nests in B6-B6 litters compared to the more cup-shaped nests with multiple built-up sides observed in B6-B6 litters.   Maternal and pup behaviors were significantly correlated, particularly activity behaviors during PNW 3 (Supplementary Fig. 5). Dam out-of-the-nest and eating behavior frequencies correlated with pup activity, including pup crawling/running, autogrooming, licking and grooming, eating solid food, digging, and eyes open, with mostly small to medium effect sizes. The largest effect size correlations show a positive correlation between dam out of the nest with pup autogrooming (R2 = 0.32) and pup eyes open (R2 = 0.25).

  To determine if pup behaviors regulated dam behaviors distinct from litter composition, hierarchical linear regression was conducted across dam behaviors during PNW 3 when increases in pup activity levels emerged. In Step 1, litter size and the percentage of males and Chd8+/− littermates were entered as predictors. In Step 2, PNW 3 pup PC 1 activity scores were added to the models. Pup PC 1 scores predicted dam total nursing, passive nursing, out-of-the-nest, and resting frequencies as well as dam PC 1 scores with medium to large effect sizes (standardized beta |0.3–0.5|; Fig. 3N). In the final models, pup PC 1 scores emerged as a significant predictor of maternal behaviors while litter size was no longer significant, suggesting that pup behavior explains variance in dam behavior beyond the effects of litter size. Litter size remained a significant predictor of dam PC 1 scores but with reduced effect size in the final model after accounting for pup PC 1. These data indicate that pup activity levels during PNW 3 account for unique variance in these dam measures distinct from litter size.

  We then determined whether dam behaviors predicted offspring postweaning traits after controlling for litter composition covariates. Three sets of models were run: one using dam PC 1 scores as the predictor, one using dam PC 2 scores, and a third model included all individual dam behaviors simultaneously. Dam PC scores and individual dam behaviors were significant but different predictors of offspring postweaning traits in WT and Chd8+/− males and females (Supplementary Fig. 6 and Fig. 4C). ## Discussion
  Using a robust model of murine genetic diversity, the study reveals that heritable genetic variability of the offspring impacted specific features of maternal care, offspring developmental milestones, and the frequency of expressed behaviors. The results show that genetic diversity in B6-CC strains impacted development and early-life experiences, providing an important representation of individual differences in the early-life environment in genetically diverse populations. Moreover, strain-specific variability in dam, pup, and litter measures predicted Chd8+/− strain effect sizes for trait disruptions after weaning. These data suggest that genetic variants regulating NDD strain susceptibility to Chd8 mutations also regulate individual differences in early-life experiences. By incorporating an advanced analysis that included a range of behavioral and compositional factors, greater accuracy was achieved for predicting phenotypic disruptions associated with Chd8 mutations across diverse genetic backgrounds. These findings have important implications for modeling human neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs). Although progress has been made in understanding the heritability of NDD risk, more detailed insights are needed to understand the specific roles of genes and the environment in driving developmental outcomes. Most preclinical models of NDDs rely on studying trait alterations in a single inbred strain, which can limit the study of gene X environment interactions, particularly during sensitive periods of development, and restricts the applicability of findings to genetically diverse human populations. Incorporating both genetic and environmental heterogeneity into preclinical models offers a more realistic representation of the well-known heterogeneity of NDD risk and clinical impact. The current study demonstrates the potential to uncover biological mechanisms that are challenging to determine in single-strain models.

  Here, we demonstrate that maternal behavior is influenced by the genetic background of the offspring. Notably, fewer B6-B6 litters born to B6-Chd8+/− dams survived to weaning compared to B6-CC litters. The higher survival rates observed in the F1 B6-CC litters are consistent with the phenomenon of hybrid vigor. However, our findings may extend beyond a general hybrid vigor effect. Consistent with our findings, previous studies have also shown that pup survivability in the B6 strain is reduced when the Chd8 mutation is maternally inherited, and pups are reared by a Chd8+/− dam, in contrast to paternal inheritance and rearing by a WT dam25,32. This pattern indicates that B6-Chd8 litters are specifically susceptible to litter death when the dam carries the Chd8 mutation. We have previously reported that B6-Chd8+/− dams exhibit altered maternal behaviors towards litters of the same genetic background, compared to B6-WT dams25. B6-B6 pups may be particularly sensitive to these changes in B6-Chd8+/− maternal care, while B6-CC offspring may be more resilient to these alterations. Thus, the improved survival observed in the F1 B6-CC crosses likely reflects an interaction between hybrid vigor and developmental vulnerability to B6-Chd8+/− dams, rather than a global fitness advantage of F1 litters alone.

  The data revealed a continuous variation in dam and pup behavioral frequencies across offspring strains. Pup traits generally had higher heritability compared to dam traits, suggesting that heritable genetic contributions account for a larger proportion of variance in pup phenotypes, whereas B6-Chd8+/− dam behaviors are more shaped by contextual influences such as the behavior of their offspring and litter composition. Differences in B6-Chd8+/− dam behaviors across offspring strains first emerged during PNW 2 when the levels of many pup activities increased, suggesting that dam behavior is influenced by individual differences in the behavior of the offspring. Offspring strains that were represented on the extremes of the trait continuums had large effect size differences in specific aspects of the early-life environment and at specific developmental epochs, including in nest quality and total nursing. Different litter observation strategies identified the same factors that were dissimilar across offspring strains, yet different in the developmental stage of occurrence. The data suggests that behavioral durations are more representative of the early postnatal days prior to PNW 1, whereas frequency checks are sufficient to capture the dynamic litter environment during later postnatal weeks when pups and the dam are more active.

  Nest quality scores also differed across offspring strains during PNWs 1, 2, and 3, while nest-building behaviors by the dam differed across offspring strains during PNW 3. Nest quality is impacted by the activity of the pups, which varies across strains, and thus, dams’ nest-building behaviors may be sensitive to changes in late nest quality when pups become more active. In earlier postnatal weeks, however, additional factors such as strain differences in body weight may contribute to nest quality variation. Nest quality at this stage is particularly critical for providing a source of insulation during development and thermoregulation, critical for body weight gain33. B6-CC10 litters had lower nest quality at PNW 1 and beyond, as well as reduced activity levels and more frequent instances of dams being out of the nest, compared to other strains. We have previously shown that B6-CC10 Chd8+/− males are uniquely resilient to Chd8-driven body weight loss and are the only strain and sex group out of 33 studied to show significantly increased adolescent body weights compared to WT8. Together, these findings suggest that reduced nest quality scores in B6-CC10 litters may reflect a distinct metabolic profile of the B6-CC10 strain, which could protect males from body weight loss due to Chd8 haploinsufficiency. Consistent with these differences, B6-CC10 pups also were observed to begin eating solid food approximately 4 days later compared to 3 other strains. In contrast to males, B6-CC10 Chd8+/− females have reduced adolescent and adult body weights compared to WT. In the present study, we found that nest quality scores predict strain effect sizes for adolescent body weights in females: higher nest quality was associated with smaller strain reductions in female body weight. Therefore, nest quality differences in B6-CC10 litters may be shaped by both the distinct metabolic demands of the strain and diverging male and female developmental needs.

  Variability in the frequency and duration of dam arched-back nursing, out-of-the-nest, and licking and grooming behaviors has been shown to regulate brain development and behavior of the offspring in other rodent models9–12,17. Similarly, we found that differences in out of the nest, licking and grooming, and nursing are factors that impact the outcomes of offspring in a model of Chd8 haploinsufficiency. The data suggest that early-life experiences can impact both brain structural and behavioral trait alterations due to Chd8 haploinsufficiency. For example, strain differences in the frequency of the dam licking and grooming her pups across P1–21 were predictive of macrocephaly effect sizes in males and females. The negative correlation suggests that lower levels of licking and grooming by the dam are associated with larger effect sizes for macrocephaly across strains. This may occur by reducing the resilience that develops through maternal-pup interactions. Moreover, there were sex and early environment-specific influences on the Chd8+/− effect size of DSI aggression in strains. For example, total nursing frequency was univariately predictive of DSI aggression strain effect sizes in males, whereas the percentage of male littermates was univariately predictive of DSI aggression in females. Machine learning modeling also revealed that when the interaction between traits was considered, the percentage of male littermates and active nursing were influential predictors of DSI aggression effect sizes in both males and females, with magnitude differences across sex.

  Although there were no strain differences in the size, sex ratio, or the proportion of Chd8+/− offspring per litter, these variables were statistically controlled in all analyses examining maternal and pup behaviors. Importantly, the study design also allowed for directly investigating sibling interactions, which we suggest may be an additional variable that impacts development and adult outcomes. Notably, the number and the percentage of male and Chd8+/− siblings were predictive of DSI aggression and sniffing differently in adult WT and Chd8+/− males and females. The number and percentage of male and Chd8+/− siblings in the litter are expected to alter the rearing environment, considering the impacts sex and Chd8 haploinsufficiency have on regulating adult phenotypes. Consistent with this, we found that higher proportions of male and Chd8+/− littermates predicted greater DSI sniffing and aggression in Chd8+/− females within the combined strain population. Litter size and the percentage of male and Chd8+/− littermates also predicted strain Chd8 effect sizes, including for fear acquisition, DSI aggression, and DOF distance in females. These findings indicate that litter composition, including co-housing Chd8+/− mice with genetically similar siblings, can modulate behavioral outcomes due to Chd8 haploinsufficiency. Our findings align with a previous study in B6 mice that found that varying the ratio of co-housed Chd8+/− mice postweaning impacted social and anxiety-like behaviors, brain activity, and the microbiome34.

  The data also revealed that litter size accounted for significant variability in some B6-Chd8+/− dam behaviors in the combined offspring strain population. However, regression models revealed that pup PC 1 scores, which represent pup activity levels, accounted for variability in specific dam traits beyond litter size. Moreover, in the combined strain population, B6-Chd8+/− dam behavior accounted for greater variance in many postweaning traits compared to litter size.

  While our regression analyses show that early-life litter variables significantly predict postweaning Chd8-driven outcomes, these associations do not establish causality. Rather, they suggest that early-life environment may interact with or modulate the expression of Chd8-driven phenotypes, highlighting the importance of considering developmental context in future mechanistic studies. The results support the hypothesis that genetic mutations not only impact offspring phenotypes but also influence key interactions during sensitive periods of development, including those between offspring and the dam and between siblings, in relation to genetic background. The direct effects of genetic mutations and environmental context together can interact to influence the development of complex behaviors. The presented results establish a basis for future studies to test causal inference across strains identified in this study, with methods such as cross-fostering, environmental enrichment, or litter composition manipulations.

  The large-scale litter observation study of 16 strains across P1–21 posed challenges associated with breeding, housing, and the inclusion of both WT and Chd8 haploinsufficient genotypes. Follow-up studies can aim to improve certain study design aspects detailed here. The present study measured dam and pup behaviors during the light phase of the diurnal cycle when mice are less active. Therefore, it is possible that differences across groups were minimized in comparison to observations made during the dark phase, when mice are more active. It is important to understand differences in dam and pup behaviors as a function of genetic background across both the light and dark phase, and future work should include observations, using a reverse light:dark cycle, to fully understand how rearing experience may differ and contribute to outcomes in NDD models.

  Across offspring strain groups, the litter sizes exhibited a modest range, and the variability in litter size was not statistically significant across strains. Litter size was covaried to control for differences in this environmental variable that could influence the statistical results of strain differences. Although litter size did predict some dam behaviors in the combined strain population, pup activity levels accounted for a larger proportion of variability in dam behaviors. Future studies may choose to more tightly control or experimentally manipulate litter size depending on the specific research questions.

  The order of litter birth for the dam also varied in this study. More than 70% of litters were from first-time dams, and we covaried for dam parity in statistical analyses to reduce bias from this potential confounder. However, future studies can aim to fully eliminate this variable by using only first-time or second-time dams. While including known confounders as covariates reduces bias, statistical control alone cannot always eliminate all potential confounding influences. Thus, our results should be interpreted in the context of these limitations. Furthermore, since dams were paired with CC sires of different genetic backgrounds for breeding, differences in dam behaviors observed across offspring strains may be influenced by prepartum social housing effects, representing a potential source of variation independent of offspring genotype.

  Our litter observations sampled snapshots of behavioral frequencies over one hour per day. We recognize that while the data collection strategy used was sufficient to capture differences in dam and pup behaviors with large effect sizes as a function of offspring strain, a lack of differences in behaviors that were observed to occur less frequently may be due to the sampling rate or the number of litters observed per strain. In this context, it is important to note that a central goal of the study was to identify robust, strain-specific behavioral differences. Therefore, we selected the Bonferroni correction as a conservative approach to minimize the likelihood of false positives. Considering the large number of pairwise comparisons and the exploratory nature of the analyses, we prioritized specificity over sensitivity so that identified strain differences would have a higher probability of reflecting accurately the biologically meaningful effects that can be studied in more detail in follow-up experiments. We acknowledge that this approach may be overly conservative, and that future hypothesis-driven studies may use less conservative FDR-based methods.

  In summary, our results highlight the contribution of genetic background to both developmental heterogeneity and differential susceptibility to neurodevelopmental disruptions by modulating early-life environmental factors. Individual differences in dam and pup behaviors, driven by offspring genetic background, predicted strain differences in the magnitude of trait disruptions between WT and Chd8+/− mice. The data show that specific early-life factors in mice, including litter composition variables, pup activity, and dam behaviors, exert large and interpretable effects across multiple developmental and behavioral domains in trait-specific ways, revealing both shared and dissociable influences on later outcomes across sex. The data emphasize the complex nature of trait variation and highlight the critical role of both maternal and sibling dynamics in predicting outcomes driven by Chd8 haploinsufficiency. Litter size, littermate sex, and genotype ratios are not commonly reported in basic research studies investigating the biological regulation of behavioral or developmental traits. However, these variables are straightforward to measure and are typically already collected during colony management. The present study demonstrates that incorporating such measures can provide meaningful insight into early-life mechanisms underlying trait outcomes and enhance the reproducibility of NDD model studies across laboratories.

  Our results also highlight specific measures, such as dam nest exits, licking and grooming, and nursing occurrence, as potentially influential in shaping offspring outcomes. The spot-checking approach detailed in this study is practical to implement across postnatal days and was sufficient to detect these behaviors as sensitive to pup activity levels and predictive of offspring adult outcomes. Furthermore, the strain-specific developmental trajectories identified here can help guide strain selection for future investigations. For example, pups from the B6-CC10 strain did not display climbing, jumping, or digging during preweaning development, while pups from the B6-CC25 strain exhibited the highest activity levels among the strains assessed, suggesting that these strains may be particularly well suited for studies investigating mechanisms underlying locomotor development.

  Here, the focus was on CHD8 heterozygosity. It will be important to determine the generalizability of our findings by determining if these differences generalize to WT dams and how differences in early-life experiences may modify adult phenotypes and susceptibility to other causal NDD gene mutations. The data provide a framework for designing studies to understand how genetically diverse individuals differ in responses to early-life environments and how this contributes to the emergence of NDD clinical phenotypes. Preclinical models that capture the heterogeneity of genetic and environmental risk factors for NDDs offer opportunities to model individualized experiences and gain valuable insights into underlying mechanisms that are not exhibited in single-strain models. Moreover, considering the added prevalence of rare causal mutations in humans, systems genetics approaches in murine models of NDDs are especially valuable for achieving the power necessary to inform biological mechanisms in genetically diverse populations – an approach that attempts to incorporate the biological and environmental realities that impact phenotypic outcomes.


## Methods

### Animals
  C57BL/6-Chd8em1Fezh/TabbJ (MGI ID 6693434), referred to here as B6-Chd8+/−, were descendants of mice originally received from Dr. Feng Zhang (MIT) in 2019. This B6-Chd8 mouse line was generated through Cas9-mediated germline editing followed by germline transmission and inheritance. The B6-Chd8+/− mice used in this study descend from a single founder with germline transmission of a loss-of-function Chd8 allele containing a 7-nucleotide deletion in exon 1, resulting in a 50% reduction in CHD8 protein expression at embryonic day 18 compared to WT littermates26. The line was maintained by breeding B6-Chd8+/− males with C57BL/6J (B6) females (Jackson Labs, Stock No. 000664). This B6-Chd8+/− line has recently been deposited at The Jackson Laboratory (Strain No. 040087). To generate experimental subjects, B6-Chd8+/− dams were bred with B6 males and males from 15 Collaborative Cross (CC) strains, all obtained from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME), at 6–8 weeks of age and allowed to habituate to the colony for at least one week before breeding. Strains included CC001/Unc (Stock No. 21238), CC002/Unc (Stock No. 21236), CC007/Unc (Stock No. 29625), CC010/GeniUnc (Stock No. 21889), CC012/GeniUnc (Stock No. 28409), CC013/GeniUnc (Stock No. 21892), CC016/GeniUnc (Stock No. 24684), CC022/GeniUnc (Stock No. 25424), CC024/GeniUnc (Stock No. 21891), CC025/GeniUnc (Stock No. 18857), CC028/GeniUnc (Stock No. 25126), CC032/GeniUnc (Stock No. 20946), CC044/Unc (Stock No. 26426), CC057/Unc (Stock No. 24683), CC075/Unc (Stock No. 27293), and the reference strain C57BL/6J (Stock No. 000664). CC strains were chosen based on health data provided for CC strains so that strains noted to have reduced survivability or health challenges were avoided. Notably, the sire was removed from the home cage approximately 1 week prior to litter births to control for individual differences in paternal care. All mice were weaned at P21 and genotyped for Chd8 with validated in-house genotyping protocols using approximately 1–1.5 mm long tail snips collected at weaning. At the end of the study, mice were euthanized by isoflurane overdose in a bell jar followed by decapitation. At euthanasia, additional tail samples were harvested, and subjects were genotyped for a second time to confirm the Chd8 genotype. Litter observations were conducted in three cohorts over 4 years. The first and second cohorts included 4 and 12 strains, respectively, that were different between the cohorts. See Supplementary Data 1 for cohort assignment per strain. Litter observations on these strains measured frequency behaviors every 5-min for 1 h across P1–21. Mice in cohorts one and two were housed in the Ray R. Irani vivarium at the University of Southern California (USC) main campus from 2019 to 2021. Cohort 3 consisted of 2 strains, and litter observations on these strains measured out-of-the-nest frequency and out-of-the-nest, total nursing, licking and grooming, and nest-building duration behaviors during P4, P5, P11, P12, P18, and P18. Litters in Cohort 3 were observed for 5-min durations every 15-min for 1-h, resulting in 15 min of total observation per postnatal day. Mice in Cohort 3 were housed in the vivarium of The Saban Research Institute at Children’s Hospital Los Angeles (CHLA) in 2023. All mice were housed in standard ventilated cages on a 12 h light/dark cycle with lights on at 6:00 AM in a temperature (20-22 °C) and humidity (40–60%) controlled room with ad libitum access to standard rodent chow and filtered water. All mice were weaned at P21 and housed with 2–5 same-sex cagemates with preferential housing of littermates. Litter size and the percentage of males and the percentage of Chd8+/− littermates were recorded at weaning because handling of the pups preweaning adds an experimental variable that may impact dam and pup behaviors differently across experimental groups. A subset of subjects was tested for traits in adulthood, including body weight at weaning, adolescence, and adulthood, and at euthanasia (i.e., terminal weight), dark open field (DOF) activity, direct social interaction (DSI), bright open field (BOF) activity, and fear conditioning. Brain weights were also measured for these subjects. Details regarding behavioral testing and results have been described in ref. 8. All experimental procedures were approved by the USC and CHLA Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees under protocols #11844-CR011 and #430, respectively. In addition, all experimental procedures followed the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals by the National Institutes of Health. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.


### Litter observations
  All litter observations were conducted in a quiet suite, adjacent to the main housing room, and dedicated to mouse behavior observations. At least one hour prior to the start of observations, cages with litters were transferred from the main colony to standard tables so that there was an unobstructed view of all cage sides by the research observer. The hour prior to observation also ensured time for the dam and litter to acclimate and negate any potential stress to the new environment perception early on. Multiple cages were observed per day, and the cage order was counterbalanced each day. Multiple strains with litters of different ages were observed each day. The BORIS35 software (v.7.10.5) was utilized during the observation time to track multiple behaviors, their frequencies, and duration for Cohort 3. The research observer was blind to the litter ID and strain during litter observations. At the end of each observation, the strain and litter ID were recorded corresponding to the cage order. All litter observations were conducted during the light cycle between 7:00 AM and 10:00 AM.

  Pregnant dams were checked daily for litter births, and the first day a litter was born was designated as P0. Litter observations were based on established literature showing that multiple brief observation periods are sufficient to identify differences in rodent maternal care over preweaning development19,36–40. For Cohort 1 and Cohort 2, litter observations consisted of recording twelve snapshots of behavior conducted at 5-min intervals over a one-hour period. Tally marks corresponding to observed behaviors were recorded during each spot check. Measured dam behaviors observed included passive nursing, active nursing, licking and grooming, nest building, out-of-the-nest, resting, running, and eating frequencies19,37–40. Passive nursing consisted of the dam resting with pups located ventrally to the dam. Active nursing included pups located ventral to the dam while the dam was engaged in an active behavior such as licking and grooming, eating, drinking, autogrooming, and/or nest building. Passive and active nursing frequencies were also summed to analyze group differences in total nursing frequencies. Behavior of pups in each litter was also recorded and included pup crawling/running out of the nest, out of the nest, autogrooming, licking and grooming, eating solid food, nest building, jumping, digging, and climbing. Licking and grooming were recorded when a pup was observed to lick and groom the sibling or the dam. We were not able to differentiate individual pups in the litter, and therefore, a behavior was recorded during each spot check if at least one pup in the litter displayed that behavior, and only one tally mark was recorded for that behavior. In addition, the day on which developmental milestones were first observed was recorded for each strain and included eye opening, eating solid food, jumping, digging, and climbing.

  For Cohort 3, the duration of litter behaviors and the frequency of nest exits were recorded for two offspring strain groups that were also observed in Cohort 2 (B6-B6 and B6-CC12). During P4, P5, P11, P12, P18, and P19 observations for each cage took place over one hour and involved a researcher live-scoring maternal behaviors for three 5-min observation periods spaced fifteen minutes apart. Researchers were blind to the strain of the offspring while scoring. Maternal activity for three 5-min epochs was then added together for each observation day, and the total duration and frequencies of maternal care were analyzed across offspring strain groups.


### Postweaning behavioral tests and weights
  A subset of subjects included in the litter observations was also tested in adulthood for locomotor, social, anxiety-like, and fear-learning and memory behaviors. Detailed methods and results are reported in ref. 8, including the impact of strain and Chd8 genotype across these behaviors. Briefly, behavioral tests were conducted during the light cycle after at least 45 min of habituation to the testing room. Developmental body weights were recorded at weaning (P21), adolescence (P35), adulthood (mean = P125 ± 14), and at euthanasia with terminal brain weights (mean = P192 ± 13). Researchers were blind to the Chd8 genotype during testing and weight collection, and the strain testing order was randomized. Behavioral tests were separated by at least one week within subjects. Baseline activity levels were assessed with the Dark Open Field (DOF) test. The test began after subjects were placed in a dark Plexiglass testing arena for 30 min. Activity levels of the freely moving subjects were recorded with infrared sensors. Next, subjects were tested for social behaviors towards a same-sex conspecific in the Direct Social Interaction (DSI) test. In this test, subjects were placed in a rectangular Plexiglass testing chamber to habituate for 10 min. Then, a sex-matched B6 juvenile was placed into the testing arena. The subject’s behavior toward the juvenile stimulus was recorded for 6-min, including sniffing and aggression. Next, anxiety-like behaviors were tested in the Bright Open Field (BOF) test. In the BOF test, subjects were placed into the center of a brightly illuminated Plexiglass chamber. The mouse’s distance traveled along the perimeter and in the center of the box was recorded during the 30-min test using infrared sensors. The percentage distance traveled in the center of the box was the main outcome measure. To assess learning and memory, cued fear conditioning was conducted in a standard fear conditioning system (Med Associates) with stainless steel walls and floor bars enclosed in a sound attenuating chamber. Tests were conducted in the dark with infrared light to record freezing activity using a front-facing infrared camera. The fear conditioning test began with habituation to the testing chamber for 30-min on day 1, training to 5 presentations of a 5 kHz, 90 dB, 30-s tone co-terminating with a mild 0.5 mA 2 s food shock on day 2 (fear acquisition), cued fear memory to the same tone without the shock on day 3 (fear expression), and memory extinction on day 4 (fear extinction). On day 3, textured clear-plastic walls and smooth white-plastic floor inserts provided a novel context. On day 4, the extinction of fear memory was tested in the same manner as on day 3. Fear acquisition on day 2 was measured by subtracting the percentage of time the subject spent freezing to the first tone presentation from the last tone presentation. Fear expression on day 3 was measured by averaging the percent freezing during the first three presentations of the tone in a novel environment. Fear extinction on day 4 was measured by subtracting fear expression scores from the average percent freezing to the last three tone presentations on day 4.


### Statistics and reproducibility
  Dam behavior was observed from all offspring strain groups for Cohorts 1 and 2 for each day from P1–21, and the full data are presented in the figures of dam behaviors across each day. The number of litters per strain included in the study were based off the number of litters needed to generate n = 8 mice per group of strain, Chd8 genotype and sex at weaning for adult behavioral testing. Analyses that tested group differences in behaviors across P1–21 included average behavioral frequencies for litters that had at least 2 postnatal day observations during each postnatal week (PNW) to meet enough litters for statistical analyses. Most litters had at least 3 observation days per PNW (see Supplementary Data 1). Analyses that tested group differences in behaviors across PNWs included average frequencies of dam behaviors per litter for litters that had at least 3 postnatal day (PND) observations per PNW. The total number of observations, as well as the number of litters, included in the analyses of specific postnatal periods is listed in Supplementary Fig. 1A and Supplementary Data 1. Tally marks totaling spot checks for each behavior and litter were summed for each postnatal day and used for data analysis for Cohorts 1 and 2. The average percent frequency of each behavior for each litter was then calculated across each time bin and analyzed for differences across offspring strain groups.

  Strain differences in the percent of litters that survived to weaning were analyzed with Chi-squared tests. Strain differences in litter size, the percentage of males, and the percentage of Chd8+/− offspring were analyzed with one-way ANOVA. Broad-sense heritability was estimated for B6-Chd8+/− dam behaviors using one-way ANOVA for each trait to determine the proportion of variance accounted for by offspring strain compared with the total phenotypic variance. Similarly, heritability for pup traits was estimated by calculating the proportion of variance attributable to differences across strains relative to the phenotypic variance observed within each strain. Principal component (PC) analyses were conducted on dams’ behavioral frequencies standardized trait values (z-scores) with a rotated component matrix, Varimax rotation, and Kaiser normalization. Regression scores were calculated based on each PC.

  Strain differences in dam and pup behaviors and principal component scores were analyzed with linear mixed effects models (LMM) with litter size, dam parity, the percentage of males, and the percentage of Chd8+/− offspring per litter covaried as these variables can impact dam behaviors and the frequency with which pup behaviors were observed. Prior to analyses, extreme outliers, defined in SPSS as values more than 3 times the first to third interquartile range, were removed if only one outlier was identified per group, and a maximum of one outlier was removed per group. LMMs are robust to deviations from homogeneity of variance and are appropriate for repeated-measures behavioral data that can contain variance across groups or time points. Nonetheless, the variance structure was inspected for the primary variables of interest. Nest quality scores, dam passive nursing, active nursing, total nursing, out of the nest, eating, PC 1, and PC2 scores were normally distributed across the genetically diverse offspring population. Dam lick and groom, nest build, and run were approximately normally distributed, with a slight skew to the right (skewness = 0.54, 0.57, and 0.59, respectively). Pup PC 1 scores were moderately skewed to the right (skewness = 1.2). For dam and pup behaviors that showed significant differences across offspring strain groups, post hoc tests were Bonferroni-corrected for multiple comparisons.

  Spearman’s correlation coefficients were calculated to analyze relations between litter composition variables, dam behaviors, pup behaviors, adult behavioral traits, and adult Cohen’s D effect size estimates per strain previously reported8. Cohen’s D effect sizes were calculated by subtracting the mean trait values between groups and then dividing by the pooled standard deviation (SD). Cohen’s D effect sizes were calculated as follows (Mean1-Mean2)/√(((n1-1)*SD12 + (n2-1)*SD22)/(n1 + n2-2)). Regression analyses were conducted in SPSS on standardized variables (z-scores) using linear and multivariate models that included specified covariates. We used a common rule of thumb to categorize Cohen’s D effect sizes (d) as small (d < 0.2), medium (d = 0.2–0.4), and large (d ≥ 0.8). Correlation coefficients were categorized as large effect sizes if at least 25% of the variation in one variable (r ≥ 0.5) was statistically associated with variation in another variable. In addition, we categorized standardized beta coefficients ≥ 0.5 as large effects, indicating that when one predictor increases by one standard deviation, the outcome increases by 0.5 standard deviations while holding all other predictors constant.

  A machine learning decision tree regression analysis was conducted in Python with the scikit-learn package. Decision trees are valuable for understanding the interactions among independent variables and their combined impact on predicting a dependent variable, often yielding greater accuracy than simple linear models. The decision tree regression model was designed to capture nonlinear and non-parametric relationships among the variables and their influence on trait outcomes. The model in this case is configured with a maximum depth of 5 and a minimum of 4 samples per leaf to balance complexity and generalizability. We then used SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) interpretation to further understand the relationships in the decision tree models by quantifying the relative contribution of each litter observation variable to the Chd8+/− effect size31. SHAP values interpret how individual variables influence the deviation from the expected value (average of the dependent variable) to the final predicted value, offering a transparent framework for interpreting a more complicated model, such as a decision tree. The SHAP package in Python was used for generating SHAP values and graphs. Figures and graphs were constructed with SPSS (v29.0.2.0), Python, Prism (v10.4.1), and BioRender. The main research question, key design features, and analyses were planned prior to initiating the study; however, this protocol was not preregistered in a public repository.
