# Cannabichromeorcin targets cathepsin L to alleviate oxidative stress–Driven airway inflammation in severe asthma


## Background
  Severe asthma (SA) is characterized by corticosteroid insensitivity, persistent airway inflammation, and excessive oxidative stress, underscoring an urgent unmet therapeutic need. Meroterpenoids derived from Rhododendron capitatum Maxim have demonstrated potent anti-inflammatory properties; however, their therapeutic potential in SA has not been elucidated.


## Methods
  A meroterpenoid-rich fraction was isolated from R. capitatum Maxim, and cannabichromeorcin (CBCO) was identified as its most abundant constituent. The therapeutic effects of CBCO were evaluated in murine models of SA induced by toluene diisocyanate or by combined house dust mite and lipopolysaccharide exposure. Transcriptomic profiling, TargetNet-based target prediction, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics simulations were employed to identify pharmacological targets. Clinical relevance was examined using transcriptomic datasets from the Unbiased BIOmarkers in Prediction of REspiratory Disease Outcomes project (U-BIOPRED) and validated in an independent clinical cohort.


## Results
  Pretreatment with CBCO ameliorated airway inflammation and oxidative stress in murine models of SA. An integrative approach identified cathepsin L (CTSL) as a redox-sensitive pharmacological target of CBCO. Pretreatment with CBCO inhibited CTSL, thereby reducing the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps containing MMP8, and attenuating the activation of the unfolded protein response (UPR). Pharmacological inhibition of CTSL, MMP8, or the UPR recapitulated the protective effects of CBCO. In vitro, CBCO preserved airway epithelial barrier integrity, reduced reactive oxygen species accumulation, and suppressed alarmin release. Clinically, transcriptomic analyses from the U-BIOPRED cohort revealed elevated MMP8 and DDIT3 expression in sputum and blood samples from patients with SA. In our independent validation cohort, serum MMP8 and DDIT3 levels were increased in SA. Receiver operating characteristic analyses confirmed serum MMP8 and DDIT3 as predictive biomarkers for SA.


## Conclusion
  CBCO conferred protection against the development of SA by targeting CTSL to suppress NET formation, UPR activation, and downstream oxidative stress. MMP8 and DDIT3 emerged as promising biomarkers for predicting disease severity.


## Introduction
  Asthma affects more than 300 million individuals worldwide, with severe asthma (SA) representing approximately 5–10% of cases but contributing disproportionately to morbidity, mortality, and healthcare expenditures [1,2]. This phenotype is characterized by a poor response to corticosteroid therapy, frequent exacerbations, and an accelerated decline in lung function [3]. Among the inflammatory phenotypes of SA, the eosinophilic subtype has been extensively targeted by various biologic agents, resulting in substantial clinical improvements [4]. In contrast, the neutrophilic and mixed-granulocytic phenotypes—primarily associated with interleukin-17 (IL-17), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and oxidative stress—still lack effective targeted therapies [5]. Increasing evidence indicates that these mixed inflammatory patterns reflect a redox-driven pathological process in SA, characterized by excessive oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and steroid resistance [6,7].

  Our group previously established and characterized a murine model of toluene diisocyanate (TDI)-induced asthma that reproduces the hallmark features of SA, including steroid resistance, airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR), mixed-granulocytic airway inflammation, airway remodeling, and mitochondrial dysfunction within the airways [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. Using this model, we and others have demonstrated the pivotal role of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) in amplifying airway inflammation and promoting steroid resistance [12]. NETs are web-like structures composed of extracellular DNA (eDNA), histones, and granular proteins such as NE, myeloperoxidase (MPO), and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), which, upon release, damage the airway epithelium and perpetuate inflammation [13]. Although several NET components have been investigated, the specific contribution of matrix metalloproteinase-8 (MMP8) to the pathogenesis of SA remains poorly defined, representing a potential target for therapeutic intervention.

  The search for novel agents capable of modulating oxidative stress, NETosis, and neutrophilic inflammation prompted our investigation of natural products. We previously identified the Rhododendron genus as a rich source of structurally diverse and biologically active meroterpenoids [14]. Among these, Rhododendron capitatum Maxim, an evergreen shrub traditionally used in Tibetan medicine to treat inflammation, gastric disorders, and cough [15,16], has emerged as a particularly promising candidate. Nevertheless, its active constituents and their potential therapeutic effects in SA remain largely unexplored.

  To systematically identify the molecular target of the active fraction derived from R. capitatum Maxim, we employed an integrative approach that combined transcriptomic analysis with computational prediction. This strategy enabled us to move beyond conventional anti-inflammatory screening and directly identify the key molecular target underlying its bioactivity. Cathepsin L (CTSL)—a lysosomal protease involved in extracellular matrix degradation, epithelial barrier disruption, and immune cell activation [17,18]—was identified as the sole overlapping target between the predicted targets of the active compound and the differentially expressed genes in our asthma model. Although dysregulated CTSL activity has been associated with several chronic airway diseases [19,20], its role in SA and its potential linkage to oxidative stress and NET formation have not yet been elucidated.

  Therefore, this study aimed to isolate the active meroterpenoid constituents from R. capitatum Maxim, identify the principal bioactive compound, and elucidate its mechanistic role in mitigating the development of SA. Particular emphasis was placed on the CTSL–MMP8/UPR axis, its potential association with oxidative stress, and the translational relevance of these findings.


## Methods

### Extraction and isolation procedure of Rhododendron capitatum maxim
  The aerial parts (mainly stems and leaves, 20.0 kg) of Rhododendron capitatum Maxim were extracted three times by heating under reflux with 70% aqueous EtOH. The resulting extract was then partitioned five times with EtOAc. The EtOAc soluble fraction (2.7 kg) was eluted with dichloromethane (100%) and MeOH (100%) and eluted by silica gel column chromatography (200-300 μm) to give three final fractions Fr. A, Fr. B and Fr. C. The Fr. A (424.8 g) was separated by MCI gel column chromatography (MCI gel CHP 20P, 75-150 μm; Mitsubishi Chemical, Japan) using a gradient elution of MeOH/H2O (60:40 to 100:0, v/v) to afford four subfractions (Fr. a-Fr. d). Fr. c (148.3 g) was further chromatographed on an MCI column with a MeOH/H2O gradient (70:30 to 100:0, v/v) to yield six fractions (Fr. 1-Fr. 6). Fr. 3 (3.0 g) was fractionated on a Sephadex LH-20 gel column (25-100 μm) using 100% MeOH as the mobile phase at a flow rate of 2.0 mL/min. A total of 100 fractions (5.0 mL each) were collected and combined based on thin layer chromatography (TLC) profiles to give two subfractions, Fr. 3.1 and Fr. 3.2 (2.1 g). Fr. 3.2 was purified by semi-preparative HPLC (Shimadzu LC-20AT system; column: Zorbax SB-C18, 250 mm × 9.4 mm i.d., 5 μm) with MeOH–H2O (90:10, containing 0.05% HCOOH) as the eluent at a flow rate of 3.0 mL/min. At a retention time (tR) of 20.0 min, compound 1 (Cannabichromeorcin, 67.0 mg) [21] was obtained. Fr. 4 (23.7 g) was separated and purified by silica gel column chromatography using a gradient elution system of PE/EtOAc with volume ratios of 200:1, 150:1, 100:1, 50:1, 40:1, 30:1, 20:1, 10:1, 5:1, and 1:1 (v/v) sequentially. Based on the elution profile, Fr. 4.1-Fr. 4.3 were obtained. Fr. 4.1 (0.4 g) was separated into three subfractions (Fr. 4.1.1-4.1.3) on preparative thin-layer chromatography (PTLC) using a gradient of PE/EtOAc (5:1, v/v). Subfraction. Fr. 4.1.3 (78.6 mg) was further purified by semi-preparative HPLC (MeOH–H2O, 88:12, v/v, containing 0.05% formic acid) to yield compound 2 (Rubiginosins G, 28.0 mg, tR = 15.6 min) [22]. Fr. 4.2 (2.0 g) was first separated by gel column chromatography (eluted with 100% methanol) to yield two fractions: Fr. 4.2.1 (0.5 g) and Fr. 4.2.2 (1.5 g). Fr. 4.2.1 was purified by semi-preparative HPLC (MeOH–H2O, 85:15, v/v, containing 0.05% HCOOH, flow rate 3.0 mL/min) to afford compound 3 (Nyingchinoid F, 32.0 mg, tR = 21.6 min) [23] and compound 4 (Ranhuadujuanine B, 7.3 mg, tR = 16.0 min) [21]. Fr. 4.2.2 was purified by semi-preparative HPLC (MeCN–H2O, 78:22, v/v, containing 0.05% HCOOH, flow rate 3.0 mL/min) to yield compound 5 [24] (Grifolin, 7.0 mg, tR = 16.7 min). Fr. 4.3 was subsequently refined by a gel column chromatography using MeOH/H2O (100:0, v/v) to afford Fr. 4.3.1-Fr. 4.3.2 (2.7 g). Compounds 6 (Ranhuadujuanine D, 7.3 mg, tR = 22.5 min) [21] were afforded using semi-preparative HPLC (MeCN–H2O, 70:30, v/v, containing 0.05% HCOOH, flow rate 3.0 mL/min). Relevant chemical data are provided in Figs. S1–S12. 1H and 13C NMR spectra were recorded on Bruker AVANCE III 400 MHz and 600 MHz spectrometers (Bruker, Germany) using tetramethylsilane (TMS) as an internal standard. Chemical shifts are reported in parts per million (ppm). Rotary evaporation was performed using an EYELAN-1001D-W instrument (Tokyo Rikakikai, Japan).


### Clinical trial
  Participants were recruited from the respiratory clinic of Nanfang Hospital and Dalian municipal friendship hospital. The hospital’s Ethics Committee granted approval for this study under approval number NFEC-2023–259 and LL-2025-033-02. Before being enrolled in the study, each participant signed the informed consent. In total, 172 individuals were progressively included in the trial after receiving an asthma diagnosis in accordance with the Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) 2024 guidelines. Respiratory tract infections and respiratory disease flare-ups within 4 weeks of the initial research visit were both grounds for exclusion. Severe asthma patients are classified according to GINA guidelines, who belong to 4–5 GINA steps. As control, 68 healthy individuals without a history of lung conditions were included with normal spirometry results. Standardized processes were used to induce and process sputum as described in our previous study [12]. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects and that the experiments conformed to the principles set out in the WMA Declaration of Helsinki and the Department of Health and Human Services Belmont Report.


### Animal
  6 weeks-old male Balb/c mice were gained from Southern Medical University. All mice were kept in an specific pathogen-free (SPF) laboratory with a 12-h dark/light cycle at a temperature of 23 ± 2 °C and humidity range of 40 - 70%. Mice were continuously given irradiated food and sterilized water. All mice were randomly housed in separate cages, and interventions and analyses were conducted at the same time point. The experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Subjects Committee of Nanfang Hospital and followed the Principles of Laboratory Animal Care.


### Establishment of the TDI-induced mice asthma model
  110 mice were averagely randomized to 11 groups by simple randomization. A mixture of 2 vol of acetone and 3 vol of olive oil was used as the vehicle to dissolve TDI for cutaneous sensitization, and 1 vol of acetone and 4 vol of olive oil was utilized for airway challenge. The procedure of establishing animal models was described in our previous reporting [7]. CBCO (1, 2, 4 or 6 mg/kg, intratracheally), TDI + Z-Phe-Tyr-CHO (Z-FY-CHO, 5 mg/kg, intratracheally), MMP8 inhibitor-1 (20 mg/kg, intratracheally), ATF4-IN-1 (5 mg/kg, intratracheally), Dexamethasone (1 mg/kg, intratracheally) and Tunicamycin (1 mg/kg, intratracheally) dissolved in DMSO and saline were given to the mice immediately before each sensitization and airway challenge. The dosages for in vivo and in vitro experiments were determined with reference to previous studies and adjusted according to differences in administration routes to ensure safety, and relatively low doses were selected to avoid excessive pharmacological effects [12,[25], [26], [27], [28], [29], [30], [31]]. Sham mice were given the same amount of vehicle.


### Establishment of the HDM/LPS-induced mice asthma model
  60 mice were averagely randomized to 5 groups. Mice were sensitized and challenged with 20 μg HDM i.n. on day 0, day 7, days 14-18 and days 21-25. LPS (10μg/mice) was treated intratracheally on day 21. The mice in control group were sensitized and challenged by inhaling saline. CBCO (2 mg/kg, intratracheally), TDI + Z-FY-CHO (5 mg/kg, intratracheally) ATF4-IN-1 (5 mg/kg, intratracheally) and MMP8 inhibitor-1 (20 mg/kg, intratracheally) dissolved in DMSO and saline were given to the mice immediately before each sensitization and airway challenge.


### Sample size calculation
  Sample size was determined based on previous similar studies [7] and power analysis to detect significant differences in airway inflammation. The final sample size will be larger than the number required for calculation to meet different experimental needs.


### Blinding
  Group allocation was concealed during the allocation process and the conduct of the experiment. Investigators responsible for outcome assessment and data analysis were blinded to the group assignments to minimize bias.


### Preparation of TDI-human serum albumin (HSA) conjugates
  In accordance with our reported procedure [32], TDI-human serum albumin (HSA) conjugates were prepared.


### Cell culture and treatment
  Primary human airway epithelial cells (HAECs) were obtained from ATCC. They were expanded in PneumaCult™-Ex Plus Medium. Once reaching 85% convergence in tissue culture flasks (NEST Science Co.Ltd, Shanghai, China), the cells were passaged and seeded into suitable culture plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/cm2. The dispersed cells were then passaged at the same plating density into further T25 flasks or seeded on apical chambers of 12 mm Transwell (0.4 μm Pore Polyester Membrane Inserts, Costar). Within the inserts, cells were incubated at 37 °C until 100% confluence was reached (2∼4 days). Upon reaching confluence, the medium for expansion was removed from the inserts and washed with D-PBS. Then the PneumaCult™-ALI Maintenance Medium was added to the basal chamber only, leaving the apical chamber empty, to create an air-liquid interface (ALI) for the cells. The PneumaCult-ALI protocol for Maintenance Phase was then followed for 28 days. Cells within five passages were used for our experiments. Cells were harvested for protein extraction and supernatant in the basal chamber was collected 24 h post exposure. A test for mycoplasma contamination should be performed prior to each cell respassaging and sample harvesting to ensure the absence of contamination.


### Assessment of airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR)
  Mice was endotracheal intubated and placed in a chamber to measure airway responsiveness to methacholine on day 25. To achieve this, lung resistance (RL) was gauged in response to escalating dosages (3.125, 6.25, 12.5 and 25 mg/mL) of methacholine through ultrasonic nebulization on anesthetized and mechanically ventilated mice. After every nebulization process, measurements of RL were taken at 5 min intervals until a plateau phase was attained. The results were presented as a percentage of the RL value at baseline for each methacholine concentration.


### Analysis of serum
  After resting at room temperature for an hour, the blood samples were centrifuged (3000 g, 20 min), and the supernatant fluid was collected and kept at −80 °C. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, we evaluated total serum IgE and MMP8 via ELISA kits.


### Analysis of bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluids
  The number of cells in BAL fluid was calculated via cell counter (Count-star, Shanghai, China). Then, the BAL fluid was centrifuged (1000 g, 10 min). According to the manufacturer’s recommendations, the supernatant was frozen (−80 °C) for subsequent Luminex multiplex assay (IL-1β, IL-4, IL-5, IL-17A, IL-13, TNF-α). To classify different cells, cytospin samples were made from cell precipitation after centrifugation and stained using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). A total of 200 cells from each sample were counted to determine the number of macrophages, eosinophils, neutrophils, and lymphocytes.


### Pulmonary histopathological examination
  The left lungs of the mice were separated, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, then dehydrated, and paraffin embedded. Lung tissue sections (4 μm) were cut and stained with periodic acid-Schiff (PAS), H&E, Masson’s trichrome and Immunofluorescence. Briefly, two criteria were scored to document the pulmonary inflammation: peribronchial inflammation and perivascular inflammation. A value of 0 was adjudged when no inflammation was detectable, a value of 1 for occasional cuffing with inflammatory cells, a value of 2 for most bronchi or vessels surrounded by thin layer (one to ten cells) of inflammatory cells and a value of 3 when most bronchi or vessels were surrounded by a thick layer (more than ten cells) of inflammatory cells [33]. As 8–10 tissue sections per mouse were scored, inflammation scores could be expressed as a mean value and could be compared between groups. Sections were assigned a random code to blind the examiner to the identity of each specimen.


### Quantitative PCR (qPCR)
  In accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, total RNA was extracted with the use of the Total RNA Extraction Reagent. Using 2 μg of RNA in 20 μl reaction buffer of HiScript® IV All-in-one RT SuperMix Perfect for qPCR kits and random primers,the first strand of complementary DNA was generated by sequentially heating the mixture to 37 °C for 15 min and 85 °C for 5 s. Real-time PCR was performed using a ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix. All primer sequences were listed in Table S1.


### Detection of cell survival rate
  Cell Counting Kit-8 (Beyotime, China) was utilized for the detection of cell survival rate in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol.


### Detection of cell permeability
  Cell permeability was detected by FITC-labeled Dextran (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to manufacturer’s protocols.


### Analysis of datasets from Gene Expression Omnibus
  The datasets used in this study are sourced from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), with the downloaded data in MINiML format. The detailed processing procedure can be found in the method description on the dataset selection page. Statistical analysis was conducted using R software, version v4.0.3. Results were considered statistically significant when the p-value was less than 0.05.


### Detection of eDNA
  The BALF and serum of mice were collected for measurement of eDNA, using Quant-iT PicoGreen DNA reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.


### Measurement of oxidative stress levels
  Total antioxidant capacity (TAC), malonaldehyde (MDA), and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity were assayed using the TAC assay kit, MDA assay kit, and SOD assay kit, respectively, according to the manufacturer’s instructions.


### Molecular docking
  The crystal structure of CTSL was obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 7Z3T). The docking grid box was defined according to the reported active sites S1, S2, S3, S1′, S2′, and S3′ [34]. The compound CBCO, which exists as a neutral molecule under physiological conditions, was retrieved from the PubChem database in the form of a SMILES string. Twenty three-dimensional conformations of CBCO were generated using RDKit and subsequently energy-minimized with OpenBabel.

  Molecular docking of CTSL with each CBCO conformation was performed using AutoDock Vina, generating 20 docking poses per ligand conformation. The resulting docking complexes were ranked based on their binding affinity scores. The optimal binding pose was selected considering both the docking score and detailed interaction analyses, including hydrogen bonding, van der Waals forces, and other non-covalent forces.


### Molecular dynamics simulation
  To further investigate the dynamic stability and interaction behavior of the CTSL-CBCO complex, all-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted using the GROMACS package (version 2022.6) with the AMBER14SB force field [35]. The RESP charges and ligand topology files were generated using Multiwfn in combination with ORCA [36,37], followed by topology conversion with sobtop, while the protein topology was prepared with GROMACS. The complex was solvated in a dodecahedron periodic box containing TIP3P water molecules, and the system was neutralized by adding appropriate counterions.

  Energy minimization was performed using the conjugate gradient algorithm until the maximum force on any atom was reduced below 100.0 k mol−1 nm−1. The minimized system was equilibrated under position restraints for 5 ns to allow proper relaxation of solvent molecules and ions. Subsequently, a 50 ns unrestrained production MD simulation was carried out under isothermal-isobaric (NPT) ensemble conditions at 298.15 K and 1 bar, maintained using the V-rescale thermostat and Parrinello-Rahman barostat, respectively.

  Trajectory data from the production phase were analyzed to calculate the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) and binding free energy using gmx_MMPBSA, in order to evaluate the conformational stability and binding affinity of the CTSL-CBCO complex throughout the simulation. The dynamic behavior of the last 5 ns of the production trajectory was visualized and converted into a video using PyMOL.


### Detection of reactive oxygen species (ROS)
  ROS level in HAECs were measured by DCFH-DA fluorescent probe in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol (Reactive Oxygen Species Assay Kit, Solarbio).


### Detection of autophagy
  Autophagy level in HAECs were measured by monodansylcadaverine (MDC) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol (Autophagy Staining Assay Kit with MDC, Beyotime).


### Transcriptomics
  Total RNA was extracted from lung tissue using the accuracode cracking mixture reagent (Singleron, China). RNA integrity was assessed with the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100. Sequencing libraries were prepared using the Illumina TruSeq kit and sequenced on the Illumina platform. Differentially expressed genes were identified using DESeq2 with adjusted p < 0.05 as the cutoff. Data was stored in BioStudies database (S-BSST2238).


### Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)
  Cells were treated with the indicated compounds or vehicle, harvested, and heated at defined temperatures for 3 min. After cooling, cells were lysed by freeze–thaw cycles, and soluble proteins were collected by centrifugation. Target protein levels were analyzed by immunoblotting to assess changes in thermal stability.


### Statistical analysis
  Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 software. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied, followed by Bonferroni post hoc correction for multiple comparisons. When data did not meet the assumptions of normality, nonparametric tests were applied as appropriate. Correlation analyses were performed using Spearman’s rank correlation due to the non-normal distribution of most clinical parameters. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of candidate biomarkers, and the area under the curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) was calculated. The optimal cutoff value was determined using the Youden index. Two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with Bonferroni’s post hoc test was used to analyze airway hyperresponsiveness curves. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

### Meroterpenoid fraction analysis of R. capitatum maxim

  To determine the minimum effective dose of CBCO, we performed a dose–response survival analysis. Our findings indicated that intratracheal administration of CBCO at 10 mg/kg resulted in no mortality among mice within 14 days (Fig. 1B). We then established a steroid-insensitive asthma model by TDI, as evidenced by previous research [12,38] and our validation (Fig. S13). As shown in Fig. 1C–E, TDI-exposed mice were treated with varying doses of CBCO (1, 2, 4, and 6 mg/kg). Administration of 1 mg/kg CBCO failed to reduce the TDI-induced elevations in immunoglobulin E (IgE), neutrophil and eosinophil counts, and airway inflammation. In contrast, treatment with higher doses of CBCO (2–6 mg/kg) significantly alleviated these pathological changes. Therefore, a dose of 2 mg/kg was selected for subsequent experiments in TDI-exposed mice.


### Transcriptomics analysis of CBCO-treated TDI asthma group and TDI asthma group

As shown in Fig. 2D and Table S2, heatmap analysis demonstrated that CBCO regulated oxidative stress-related genes (Hmox1, Gclc, Gclm, Txnrd1, Akr1b8, Ptgr1), and significantly suppressed the expression of neutrophilic inflammation–related genes (Mmp8, Il1b, Ctss, Ltf, S100a8, S100a9, Anxa1, and Slpi) [[39], [40], [41], [42], [43], [44], [45]], as well as eosinophilic inflammation–related genes (Fcer1g, Ym1, Ccl2, Ccl9, Ccl20, and Timp1) [[46], [47], [48], [49], [50], [51]]. Gene Ontology analysis indicated significant enrichment in pathways related to antioxidant activity, leukocyte chemotaxis, and granulocyte chemotaxis (Fig. 2E and F). Moreover, gene set enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway analysis revealed that differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were predominantly enriched in inflammatory response pathways, including the unfolded protein response (UPR), TNF-α, IL-17, IL-6, and NF-κB signaling pathways (Fig. 2G–J).   These findings highlight a strong interconnection between the UPR and the CTSL–MMP8 axis. Given the lack of specific pharmacological inhibitors targeting DDIT3, we further investigated whether inhibition of its upstream regulator ATF4 could help validate the involvement of this pathway in airway inflammation (Fig. 6D). Pharmacological inhibition of ATF4 using ATF4-IN-1 markedly reduced AHR, serum IgE levels, proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, and IL-17A), oxidative stress, and inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 6E–M). Consistently, histopathological examination demonstrated that ATF4-IN-1 treatment attenuated peribronchial and perivascular inflammatory cell infiltration, mucus hypersecretion, collagen deposition and the levels of NETs components in BALF and serum, CitH3 and MPO combination in lung tissues (Fig. 6N–Q, Fig. S16).

  To determine whether UPR activation is functionally required for the protective effects conferred by MMP8 or CTSL inhibition in TDI-induced asthma, rescue experiments were performed by pharmacologically reactivating UPR signaling with tunicamycin under conditions of MMP8 or CTSL suppression (Fig. S17). Notably, forced activation of UPR signaling partially attenuated the protective effects conferred by MMP8 or CTSL inhibition. Although UPR reactivation robustly induced canonical UPR markers, including Atf4 and Ddit3, it failed to fully restore AHR, airway inflammation and oxidative stress to levels observed in the TDI group. Furthermore, UPR reactivation increased the levels of MMP8 in serum, while exhibited no effect on the expression of CTSL.

  Given that no specific agonist for MMP8 is available and recombinant protein is prone to degradation in the in vivo environment, we performed in vitro experiments using recombinant human MMP8 to stimulate primary human airway epithelial cells (Fig. S18). We found that MMP8 promoted airway epithelial barrier damage and the expression of UPR-related genes, including ATF4 and DDIT3, but had no significant effect on CTSL expression. Notably, targeted inhibition of ATF4 markedly reversed MMP8-induced changes above, whereas inhibition of CTSL had no such effect.

  Collectively, these results demonstrate that pharmacological inhibition of UPR attenuated the development of TDI-induced asthma. Rescue experiments suggest that UPR and MMP8 signaling function as parallel interacting pathways in TDI-induced airway inflammation, whereas CTSL acts upstream of both and coordinately regulates their activation.

## Discussion
  In this study, we uncovered a previously unrecognized redox-regulatory mechanism underlying airway inflammation, wherein CTSL-driven NETs formation and UPR activation amplified oxidative stress, exacerbating epithelial injury and inflammation. We further identified CBCO—an abundant meroterpenoid isolated from R. capitatum Maxim—as a potent modulator of this redox axis. CBCO suppressed NETs- and UPR-associated ROS generation, restored redox homeostasis, and attenuated downstream inflammatory signaling by inhibiting CTSL activity. Through the integration of phytochemistry, multi-omics, computational biology, and translational clinical validation, we delineated a previously unappreciated pathogenic pathway centered on oxidative stress–driven airway inflammation and proposed a novel phytochemical strategy for its modulation. Collectively, our findings position CBCO as a promising lead compound with potential for targeting oxidative stress and inflammatory cascades in SA.

  We successfully isolated six meroterpenoids from the aerial parts of R. capitatum Maxim using a series of chromatographic techniques, with CBCO identified as the most abundant constituent—reported here for the first time in this species. Guided by its yield and structural novelty, CBCO was selected for further evaluation to determine its safety profile and therapeutic potential in neutrophilic airway inflammation. Our findings demonstrated that intratracheal administration of CBCO at a dose of 10 mg/kg was well tolerated, with no mortality observed over 14 days, confirming its suitability for in vivo studies. Notably, pretreatment with CBCO conferred a protective effect against TDI-induced airway inflammation at a concentration of 2 mg/kg, but not at 1 mg/kg, suggesting that 2 mg/kg represents the minimal effective dose for this protective effect, as evidenced by reduced IgE levels, diminished inflammatory cell infiltration, and improved histopathological outcomes.

  A pivotal finding of this study was the integrative identification of CTSL as a molecular target engaged by CBCO, achieved through the combined use of computational prediction, CETSA analysis and transcriptomic analysis. Cathepsins, particularly CTSL, are lysosomal proteases that mediate extracellular matrix degradation, epithelial barrier disruption, and immune cell activation [17,18]. Although dysregulated CTSL activity has been implicated in chronic airway disease [19,20], its precise contribution to SA has remained unclear. Through our integrative analytical approach, CTSL was identified as the sole overlapping target between the predicted CBCO targets and differentially expressed genes. Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations further supported a stable binding interaction between CBCO and CTSL, while in vivo validation demonstrated that CBCO administration significantly suppressed CTSL expression in lung tissue. This inhibition was associated with pronounced reductions in UPR signaling, NET formation, and MMP8 levels, accompanied by decreased ROS accumulation and restoration of redox homeostasis. Collectively, these results establish CTSL as a redox-sensitive upstream regulator of the NET–MMP8 axis and UPR, extending current understanding by linking proteolytic stress to oxidative signaling pathways that sustain neutrophilic inflammation and steroid resistance in SA [12].

  NETs are increasingly recognized as critical amplifiers of neutrophilic inflammation in SA [52,53]. Although NE and MPO have received substantial attention, the contribution of NET-associated MMP8 has been largely overlooked. Our findings clearly demonstrated that MMP8 is abundantly released during NETosis and plays a pivotal role in mediating AHR, inflammatory cytokine production, and structural remodeling. This process was accompanied by excessive ROS generation, indicating that MMP8-driven inflammation is tightly linked to redox dysregulation. Pharmacological inhibition of MMP8 conferred a protective effect against asthma pathology and oxidative stress, supporting its role as a key pathogenic mediator.

  By modulating CTSL-associated signaling and suppressing NETs formation, CBCO effectively downregulated MMP8 expression and restored redox balance, thereby revealing a novel and therapeutically targetable CTSL–MMP8 axis in SA. Collectively, our data position MMP8 not as a passive bystander but as a redox-sensitive effector molecule within NETs that drives airway hyperresponsiveness, tissue remodeling, and persistent inflammation.

  In this study, UPR activation was identified as a central mechanism linking protease-driven signaling to airway inflammation. Transcriptomic analysis revealed strong correlations between UPR-associated transcription factors (Atf4 and Ddit3) and inflammatory genes, indicating transcriptional coupling between endoplasmic reticulum stress, oxidative stress, and inflammation. Notably, UPR activation is inherently redox-sensitive, and excessive ROS production amplifies maladaptive UPR signaling, thereby promoting chronic inflammation [54]. Pharmacological inhibition of ATF4 with ATF4-IN-1 conferred a protective effect against airway hyperresponsiveness, inflammatory cytokine production, immune cell infiltration, and tissue remodeling in SA models.

  Pretreatment with CBCO or inhibition of the CTSL–MMP8 axis significantly decreased oxidative stress and downregulated ATF4/DDIT3 expression. Importantly, suppression of ATF4 also reduced MMP8 expression and NET formation, suggesting crosstalk between the UPR and NET pathways. These findings indicate that UPR activation and NETs formation represent two parallel downstream branches of CTSL signaling, each contributing to oxidative injury—a concept consistent with previous reports [[55], [56], [57]]. This notion is further supported by rescue experiments showing that pharmacological reactivation of UPR only partially reversed the protective effects of MMP8 or CTSL inhibition and increased MMP8 levels without affecting CTSL expression, indicating that UPR is not strictly downstream of MMP8 but can modulate its activity. Moreover, recombinant MMP8 directly induced UPR-related gene expression, while ATF4 inhibition—but not CTSL inhibition—effectively reversed these effects, suggesting that CTSL acts upstream to coordinately regulate both MMP8 and UPR signaling. Collectively, our results suggest that oxidative stress, UPR, and NET activity form a self-reinforcing regulatory network rather than a strict linear signaling cascade, converging on a CTSL–MMP8/UPR regulatory axis that amplifies airway inflammation during the development of SA and represents a key target for protective intervention to limit disease progression.

  Equally important are the epithelial-protective effects of CBCO. In SA, the airway epithelial barrier is profoundly compromised, facilitating allergen penetration and perpetuating chronic inflammation [58]. Our findings demonstrated that CBCO, as well as inhibition of CTSL or ATF4, mitigated TDI–HSA–induced autophagy dysregulation, suppressed the release of epithelial alarmins (IL-33 and TSLP), and restored the expression of tight junction proteins, thereby maintaining epithelial integrity and homeostasis. Because oxidative stress is a principal driver of epithelial barrier disruption [59], the capacity of CBCO to attenuate ROS generation provides a mechanistic basis for its barrier-protective effects.

  Conversely, exposure to recombinant MMP8 protein directly induced epithelial barrier dysfunction, elevated alarmin production, and increased oxidative stress, further supporting a causal relationship between NETs-derived proteases and redox imbalance. These observations offer a coherent mechanistic explanation for how MMP8—a neutrophil-derived protease—can simultaneously drive both neutrophilic and eosinophilic inflammatory phenotypes in SA.

  Asthma is a highly heterogeneous disease, and SA encompasses multiple distinct inflammatory phenotypes. Although recent advances in type 2 biologics have substantially improved the management of eosinophilic SA, effective targeted therapies remain unavailable for the more complex mixed-granulocytic and neutrophilic subtypes [60]. According to the global initiative for Asthma guidelines and the U-BIOPRED consensus [2,61], the diagnosis of SA is primarily defined by airway hyperresponsiveness, symptom control, and treatment response rather than by the underlying inflammatory profile.

  To account for this heterogeneity, we employed two steroid-insensitive murine models with differing inflammatory characteristics and triggers for validation. The consistent protective efficacy of CBCO across both the TDI-induced and HDM/LPS-induced SA models underscores the robustness and translational relevance of our findings. These models represent complementary etiologies of SA: the TDI model simulates chemically induced, mixed-granulocytic, steroid-insensitive asthma [62], whereas the HDM/LPS model recapitulates allergen- and endotoxin-driven mixed-granulocytic inflammation [63]. The consistent protective effects of CBCO across both models indicate that it confers protection against severe asthma through a shared mechanism involving inhibition of the CTSL–MMP8/UPR regulatory axis, accompanied by attenuation of oxidative stress, mucus hypersecretion, and collagen deposition, thereby modulating the multifaceted, redox-driven pathology that characterizes the disease.

  Beyond mechanistic insights, this study provides substantial translational relevance by identifying MMP8 and DDIT3 as robust biomarkers of SA. Analysis of the U-BIOPRED dataset revealed that MMP8 and DDIT3 expression levels were significantly elevated in both sputum and blood samples from patients with SA compared with HCs and those with MMA. Receiver operating characteristic curve analyses confirmed their predictive value, findings that were further validated in our independent clinical cohort. Importantly, serum MMP8 and DDIT3 levels correlated positively with peripheral neutrophil counts and inversely with lung function, underscoring their clinical relevance. These results extend previous observations linking NETs- and UPR-related biomarkers to asthma severity and position MMP8 as a reliable surrogate marker of neutrophilic inflammation.

  Notwithstanding these compelling findings, several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, although no overt toxicity was observed, the comprehensive pharmacokinetic and toxicological profiles of CBCO have yet to be fully characterized—an essential step toward clinical translation. In addition, while our current data support target engagement of CTSL, they do not yet definitively establish direct pharmacological binding or selectivity, and further biophysical validation will be required. Second, the clinical validation of MMP8 and DDIT3 as biomarkers was conducted in a relatively modest cohort. While our data support their potential utility in disease discrimination and severity stratification, the lack of longitudinal sampling and the absence of a CBCO-treated patient cohort preclude conclusions regarding their value in monitoring treatment response or predicting therapeutic efficacy. Larger, multicenter studies with longitudinal designs will be required for further validation. Third, although our mechanistic investigations were supported by in vivo models and primary human airway epithelial cells, the current in vivo design primarily reflects a preventive rather than a therapeutic paradigm, as interventions were administered prior to sensitization/challenge. In addition, human tissue–relevant experimental systems, such as airway organoids or precision-cut lung slices, could provide complementary mechanistic insights and better recapitulate the complex multicellular microenvironment of the human airway. Incorporating these advanced ex vivo models and delayed-treatment designs in future studies would further strengthen the translational relevance of the proposed CTSL–MMP8/UPR regulatory axis.

  In conclusion, this study identifies CBCO as a promising phytotherapeutic candidate for the prevention or modulation of SA-associated inflammation, exerting protective effects through modulation of CTSL-associated redox signaling and its downstream MMP8 and UPR. Our findings highlight CTSL as a key regulator of NETs formation and UPR activation, establish MMP8 and DDIT3 as biomarkers, and provide a robust scientific foundation for the development of phytochemical-based interventions to treat SA. By bridging oxidative stress biology with translational validation, this work offers a compelling rationale for advancing CBCO through preclinical development and underscores the potential value of targeting redox-driven airway inflammation in SA.
