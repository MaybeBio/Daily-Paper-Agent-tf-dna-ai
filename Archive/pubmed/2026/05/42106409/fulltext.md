# CT-TADB predicts TAD boundaries without Hi-C by integrating DNA sequences and epigenomic features


## Abstract
  Topologically associating domains (TADs) are fundamental units of three-dimensional genome organization, and their boundaries play important roles in gene regulation and genomic stability. However, accurate computational prediction of TAD boundaries remains challenging because of the complex interplay between DNA sequence and epigenomic regulatory signals. Here we present CT-TADB, a hybrid CNN–Transformer framework that integrates DNA sequence with histone modification and CTCF binding signals to predict TAD boundaries without requiring Hi-C data. Trained on six human cell lines, CT-TADB achieved AUC values of 0.932–0.950, demonstrating competitive or superior performance relative to existing multi-modal methods. The model maintained stable performance on strictly independent and dataset-specific boundary subsets, and exhibited robust cross-cell-type transferability and cross-species generalization between human and mouse (AUC > 0.80 for human-to-mouse transfer). Feature attribution analysis identified CTCF as the dominant boundary-associated factor, supported by active chromatin marks, and quantitative attention analysis revealed significant long-range CTCF-mediated dependencies (p < 0.001). CT-TADB further identified a clinically validated PITX2-associated TAD boundary linked to cardiac arrhythmia and captured condition-specific boundary dynamics. By leveraging routinely available epigenomic data, CT-TADB provides a practical and complementary framework for investigating chromatin architecture across diverse biological contexts.


## Introduction
  The three-dimensional genome architecture of eukaryotes demonstrates intimate associations with fundamental cellular processes, including gene regulation, transcription, and DNA replication1. To accommodate approximately two meters of chromosomal DNA within the confined nuclear space, the genome undergoes compression spanning several orders of magnitude while maintaining inter-chromosomal accessibility critical for biological functions2. The advent of high-throughput chromatin conformation capture technologies has substantially advanced our understanding of genome-wide chromatin organization3. Of particular significance, Hi-C technology has revealed discrete self-interacting chromatin domains, designated topologically associating domains (TADs), which exhibit markedly enhanced intra-domain interactions compared to inter-domain contacts4. TADs represent fundamental structural units of three-dimensional chromatin architecture and play pivotal roles in orchestrating gene expression5. Disruption of TAD boundary integrity can lead to aberrant expression patterns of specific genes, thereby contributing to disease pathogenesis6. Consequently, comprehensive characterization of TAD boundary properties has emerged as a research priority of considerable significance.

  Early computational approaches for TAD boundary identification established foundational methodologies while simultaneously revealing technological constraints. In 2012, Dixon et al. pioneered an HMM-based model that employed the directionality index (DI) to delineate boundaries of self-interacting genomic regions. This approach calculated directional changes in interaction frequencies between adjacent regions, enabling TAD detection7. While this methodology provided seminal insights into three-dimensional genome organization and established the conceptual framework for TAD prediction, the high cost and limited resolution of early Hi-C experiments presented substantial barriers to precise boundary identification at scale8. Although subsequent advances have substantially improved the practicality and cost-efficiency of low-input Hi-C protocols9,10, as well as computational enhancement and imputation methods for sparse and single-cell Hi-C data11,12, these initial technical constraints motivated the development of complementary computational prediction strategies.

  To overcome the limitations inherent in Hi-C data acquisition, sequence-based computational methods have emerged as promising alternatives. John Henderson and colleagues developed TADBoundaryDetector, a model that synergistically combines convolutional neural networks (CNN) with bidirectional long short-term memory networks (Bi-LSTM) to extract dynamic features from DNA sequences. This approach achieved notable accuracy in predicting short-range boundaries within Drosophila genomes13–15. In parallel, Hao Wu and colleagues introduced StackTADB, which employs stacked ensemble learning architecture incorporating multiple classifiers alongside K-mer-based feature extraction strategies. This model demonstrated robust TAD boundary prediction capabilities in Drosophila systems16. However, StackTADB faces computational challenges when processing short DNA sequences due to feature sparsity, which may compromise predictive accuracy. Furthermore, the model’s optimization specifically for Drosophila genomes limits validation of its applicability and generalizability to other species, thereby constraining broader biological applications4. While these sequence-based methodologies provide valuable alternatives in scenarios lacking high-quality chromatin interaction data, their capacity to capture long-range chromatin interactions remains fundamentally constrained.

  Subsequent advances have leveraged multi-omics data integration to enhance prediction accuracy. Jacqueline Chyr and colleagues developed PredTAD, which utilizes transcription factor binding profiles and histone modification ChIP-seq data to extract comprehensive features from DNA sequences. This model demonstrated particularly strong performance in TAD boundary prediction within breast cancer cell lines17. However, PredTAD may exhibit reduced generalizability when applied to heterogeneous and complex disease genomes, with potential limitations in accuracy and applicability across diverse species or cell types. Additionally, the model’s dependence on high-quality input data necessitates stringent standards for data completeness and consistency. More recently, Yunlong Wang and colleagues introduced pTADs, which efficiently predict both TAD boundaries and their relative strength. This model achieves high prediction accuracy coupled with robust cross-cell-line generalizability without requiring Hi-C data18. Despite these advantages, the gradient boosting tree algorithm underlying pTADs has inherent limitations in feature selection transparency and model interpretability, while lacking capacity for dynamic adaptation.

  Building upon these approaches, recent studies have explored deep learning and attention-based frameworks for chromatin architecture modeling. For example, deepTAD integrates convolutional neural networks with Transformer modules to learn boundary-associated patterns directly from Hi-C contact matrices, enabling explicit modeling of long-range chromatin interactions. Although such models benefit from direct access to three-dimensional contact information, their performance is inherently dependent on the resolution and quality of Hi-C data19. Annotation-guided methods such as preciseTAD further refine boundary localization by leveraging genomic annotation signals; however, their applicability depends on the availability and consistency of curated annotation resources across cell types20. EPI-Trans, a Transformer-based model originally developed for enhancer–promoter interaction prediction, employs self-attention mechanisms to capture long-range genomic dependencies and integrate multi-modal regulatory signals21. Although its primary task differs from TAD boundary prediction, its self-attention architecture and multi-modal input strategy are representative of current methodological trends in 3D genome analysis, making it a meaningful reference for Transformer-based long-range genomic modeling. In contrast, deep learning architectures demonstrate superior capabilities in processing high-dimensional genomic data, enabling dynamic feature modeling and automated feature extraction.

  Here, we present CT-TADB (CNN and Transformer for TAD Boundary prediction), a deep learning framework that integrates DNA sequences with histone modification profiles to predict TAD boundaries. Our approach addresses the fundamental limitation of sequence-only methods by incorporating epigenetic information that reflects chromatin states and regulatory activity. The hybrid CNN-Transformer architecture captures both local sequence motifs through convolutional operations and long-range chromatin interactions via self-attention mechanisms, enabling comprehensive modeling of the multi-scale features governing TAD boundary formation. We demonstrate that CT-TADB achieves superior predictive accuracy compared to existing methods, maintains robust performance across diverse cell types and species, and provides interpretable insights into the epigenetic determinants of chromatin organization. This framework offers a practical and complementary solution for TAD boundary prediction using routine ChIP-seq data, reducing dependence on Hi-C experiments while advancing our understanding of three-dimensional genome architecture.


## Results

### DNA sequence features alone are insufficient for accurate TAD boundary prediction in human Cells
  The comparative analysis included TADBoundaryDetector (originally validated in Drosophila systems and here trained from scratch on human data), CNN, CNN_Dense, StackTADB, and KNN algorithms (Supplementary Fig. 2). All comparison methods were implemented using their respective best-performing architectures and trained independently on the same human datasets under identical experimental conditions to ensure a fair comparison.

  All evaluated models demonstrated limited predictive capability when using only DNA sequences. CT-TADB achieved the highest AUC of 0.653, marginally outperforming CNN_Dense (0.635), TADBoundaryDetector (0.632), and CNN (0.624), while StackTADB and KNN showed even lower performance (0.608 and 0.511, respectively) (Fig. 1A, B; Supplementary Fig. 2).Fig. 1Performance evaluation of CT-TADB.A, B ROC and precision-recall curves for sequence-only models. C Performance improvements across all metrics when integrating histone modification signals with DNA sequences, with GM12878 shown as a representative example. D Comparison of CT-TADB with multiple baseline methods, including traditional machine learning (TAD_Lactuca_MLP, TAD_Lactuca_RF), ensemble-based (StackTADB), recurrent (Bi-LSTM), multi-modal (PredTAD, pTADs), annotation-guided (preciseTAD), and Transformer-based (deepTAD, EPI-Trans) approaches, evaluated under unified experimental conditions across six metrics.

  These findings reveal fundamental differences in TAD boundary determination between mammalian and invertebrate genomes. The consistently poor performance across diverse algorithmic approaches, from traditional machine learning (KNN) to advanced deep learning architectures (CNN-Transformer), indicates that sequence features alone are insufficient for human TAD boundary prediction. This suggests that mammalian TAD boundaries are primarily determined by epigenetic states and chromatin architecture rather than underlying DNA sequences, underscoring the necessity for multi-modal approaches that integrate histone modifications and other epigenetic features.


### Integration of epigenetic features enhances TAD boundary prediction and outperforms baseline models
  Given the limited predictive capacity of sequence-based models, we investigated whether incorporating epigenetic information could capture the regulatory complexity underlying TAD boundary formation. Histone modifications serve as dynamic markers of chromatin states, transcriptional activity, and regulatory element functionality. By integrating these epigenetic signals with DNA sequence features, the CT-TADB framework gains access to critical chromatin conformation information that sequence data alone cannot provide.

  The multi-modal approach yielded substantial performance improvements across all evaluated cell lines under both the proportion-based and chromosome-based partitioning strategies. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 3, ROC and precision-recall (PRC) curves consistently demonstrate enhanced discrimination capability following epigenetic feature integration under both strategies. Under the proportion-based split (Supplementary Fig. 3A), the dual-input model exhibits clear improvements over the single-input model across all cell lines; consistent performance gains are similarly observed under the chromosome-based strategy (Supplementary Fig. 3B), confirming that the improvements are not contingent on the partitioning approach. For illustration, using GM12878 (a widely used cell line) as an example, AUC increased by 0.288 (44.1%), accuracy by 0.284 (47.0%), and MCC by 0.590 (285.0%), with additional improvements in precision (0.302, 50.0%), recall (0.288, 47.8%), and macro-F1 (0.284, 47.1%) (Fig. 1C). Collectively, these improvements across multiple evaluation metrics highlight the substantial enhancement in overall classification performance achieved by the multi-modal model.

  To comprehensively benchmark CT-TADB against representative existing methods, we evaluated a diverse set of baseline approaches spanning traditional machine learning, ensemble learning, recurrent, annotation-guided, and Transformer-based paradigms (Fig. 1D). These include classical machine learning models (TAD_Lactuca_MLP and TAD_Lactuca_RF), a recurrent architecture (Bi-LSTM), an ensemble-based approach (StackTADB), earlier sequence-driven and multi-modal predictors (PredTAD and pTADs), the annotation-guided boundary localization method preciseTAD, and the Transformer-based models deepTAD and EPI-Trans. deepTAD integrates convolutional neural networks with Transformer modules to learn boundary-associated patterns from Hi-C contact matrices, whereas EPI-Trans, originally developed for enhancer–promoter interaction prediction, employs self-attention mechanisms and multi-modal inputs reflecting current methodological trends in 3D genome analysis. All methods were evaluated under identical data splits, experimental conditions, and evaluation metrics.

  As shown in Fig. 1D, CT-TADB achieves the highest AUC (0.941) and consistently competitive or superior performance across all evaluated metrics. Improvements are particularly notable in MCC (0.797) and macro-F1 (0.887), confirming strong and balanced discrimination capability. These results demonstrate that the integration of epigenomic features with DNA sequence enables stable and reliable improvements over both sequence-only models and alternative deep learning approaches spanning diverse architectural paradigms.

  Overall, these results confirm that integrating epigenomic features with DNA sequence substantially enhances TAD boundary prediction relative to sequence-only modeling. Across a comprehensive set of baseline methods spanning traditional machine learning, ensemble learning, recurrent, annotation-guided, and Transformer-based frameworks, CT-TADB demonstrates consistently superior or competitive performance under unified evaluation conditions. The advantages in MCC and macro-F1 are particularly indicative of robust and balanced discrimination capability, supporting the generalizability of the proposed multi-modal framework.

  To further evaluate the flexibility of CT-TADB with respect to input sequence length, we conducted additional experiments using alternative window sizes (1 kb, 5 kb, and 20 kb) while keeping the model architecture and all hyperparameters unchanged. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 4A, B, ROC and PRC curves exhibit highly consistent trends across all tested input lengths. Under finer-resolution settings (1 kb and 5 kb), performance across all metrics remained largely consistent with the 10 kb setting; when the input window was expanded to 20 kb, AUC remained comparable but accuracy, MCC, and macro-F1 showed a moderate decline (Supplementary Fig. 4C). These results indicate that the 10 kb input length represents a practical balance among resolution, contextual coverage, and modeling complexity, rather than a structural constraint of the framework.


### Robust performance of CT-TADB on strictly independent and dataset-specific TAD boundaries
  To rigorously evaluate the generalization capability of CT-TADB, we utilized an independently curated GM12878 dataset derived from the HTAD framework as an external validation set. This dataset was constructed using an interactive web-based annotation strategy integrated with discriminative active learning, representing an annotation methodology fundamentally distinct from that used in the primary training data.   Within the resulting strictly independent test set, TAD boundaries were categorized into two subsets: (i) a common independent subset, consisting of boundaries shared with the training-phase test set; and (ii) a dataset-specific subset, comprising boundaries unique to the external dataset and absent from the training-phase test set (Fig. 2A, C, right panels).

  Considering potential minor positional discrepancies arising from differences in TAD boundary calling procedures, overlap was evaluated under two criteria: a strict definition requiring exact boundary matches, and a relaxed definition permitting positional differences of ≤1 bin.

  Under the strict definition, the strictly independent test set contained 1322 samples, of which only 30 constituted shared (common) boundaries and 1292 were dataset-specific boundaries (Fig. 2A, right panel). CT-TADB achieved excellent performance on the common independent subset (AUC = 0.979, MCC = 0.905, F1-score = 0.950). Crucially, on the substantially larger dataset-specific subset (n = 1292)—representing the non-common TAD boundaries of primary interest—the model maintained strong predictive performance (AUC = 0.915, MCC = 0.784, F1-score = 0.880) (Fig. 2B), indicating robust generalization to novel boundaries generated under a distinct annotation framework.

  Under the relaxed definition (≤1 bin positional tolerance), the strictly independent test set comprised 863 samples, including 93 common independent boundaries and 770 dataset-specific boundaries (Fig. 2C, right panel). CT-TADB achieved AUC = 0.914, MCC = 0.724, and F1-score = 0.840 on the common independent subset, and maintained stable performance on the dataset-specific subset (AUC = 0.915, MCC = 0.796, F1-score = 0.888) (Fig. 2D).

  Across both definitions, dataset-specific boundaries constituted the overwhelming majority of the strictly independent test set (97.6% under the strict definition; 89.2% under the relaxed definition), and CT-TADB consistently preserved strong discriminative ability on these regions. The stable AUC values (≈0.915 under both definitions) and high precision on the dataset-specific subset collectively demonstrate that non-common TAD boundaries predominantly represent true biological signals rather than false positives, and that the model’s generalization is not contingent on shared boundaries but extends robustly to novel TAD boundaries generated under a fundamentally distinct annotation framework.


### CT-TADB exhibits robust cross-cell-line transferability
  To evaluate cross-cell-line generalization under a leakage-free setting, we adopted the hybrid cross-cell-type/cross-chromosome evaluation framework proposed by Schreiber et al.22. Within each cell type, the dataset was partitioned by chromosome, with chr8 designated for validation, chr9 for testing, and all remaining chromosomes for training. For cross-cell-type prediction, models were trained exclusively on the source cell type and evaluated directly on the chr9 test set of the target cell type, ensuring that genomic loci in the target test set were completely unseen during training and thereby preventing potential information leakage.

  Under this stringent framework, CT-TADB maintained consistently high performance across all source–target cell-type combinations (Fig. 3). AUC values ranged from approximately 0.89 to 0.95, with most values concentrated above 0.93. Diagonal entries (within-cell-type predictions) were generally ranked in the upper range of their respective rows, while off-diagonal performance remained highly comparable, indicating stable cross-cell-type transferability. Left: CT-TADB; Right: pTADs. The color bar indicates AUC values ranging from 0.84 to 0.95. Diagonal entries represent within-cell-type performance, while off-diagonal entries represent cross-cell-type transfer. CT-TADB consistently achieves higher and more stable AUC values than pTADs across all cell-line combinations.

  In contrast, pTADs exhibited lower and more variable performance, with AUC values primarily distributed between 0.84 and 0.89. CT-TADB achieved higher AUC values across nearly all source–target combinations, and performance fluctuations across cell-type pairs were more pronounced in pTADs than in CT-TADB.

  Overall, these results demonstrate that under a strict chromosome-wise evaluation strategy designed to eliminate genomic position leakage, CT-TADB preserves high predictive accuracy and stable cross-cell-line generalization. The consistently strong performance across diverse cell-type combinations indicates that the model effectively captures conserved TAD boundary characteristics rather than cell-type-specific or locus-dependent patterns.


### CT-TADB achieves effective cross-species TAD boundary prediction between human and mouse genomes
  To assess the evolutionary conservation of TAD boundary determinants, we performed bidirectional transfer learning experiments between human and mouse genomic datasets. Cross-species transferability was evaluated through reciprocal prediction experiments: human-trained models were applied to mouse TAD boundary prediction, and conversely, mouse-trained models were tested on human cell lines.

  Radar plot visualization revealed asymmetric but robust cross-species performance (Fig. 4A). Using the mouse G1E erythroid progenitor cell line as the initial cross-species benchmark, human-trained models demonstrated strong transferability when predicting mouse TAD boundaries, achieving AUC values of 0.818–0.829 across all six human cell lines, with GM12878 and HUVEC models performing best (AUC = 0.828 and 0.829, respectively). Conversely, mouse-trained models applied to human cell lines yielded lower but still substantial performance, with AUC values of 0.723–0.743. Among human cell lines, NHEK showed the highest receptivity to the mouse-trained model (AUC = 0.743), while GM12878 showed the lowest (AUC = 0.723). This performance asymmetry—human-to-mouse transfer consistently exceeding mouse-to-human transfer by approximately 0.08–0.10 AUC points—likely reflects the greater diversity of epigenomic regulatory patterns represented across the six human cell lines relative to the single mouse cell line.Fig. 4Cross-species TAD boundary prediction between human and mouse genomes.Radar plots display bidirectional AUC performance across six human cell lines. A Bidirectional cross-species transfer using the mouse G1E erythroid progenitor cell line; the outer polygon (purple) represents human-to-mouse transfer and the inner polygon (blue) represents mouse-to-human transfer. B Bidirectional cross-species transfer using the mouse E14 embryonic stem cell line as a higher-quality mammalian benchmark. Human-trained models maintain AUC values above ~0.80 when evaluated on mouse data, while mouse-trained models achieve AUC values above ~0.72 on human cell lines, demonstrating robust and reproducible cross-species generalization.

  To further strengthen the cross-species evaluation, we incorporated mouse embryonic stem cells E14 as a higher-quality and more standardized mammalian benchmark (Fig. 4B). The E14 dataset provides higher-resolution Hi-C data and more canonical mammalian TAD structural characteristics. Models trained on E14 achieved AUC values of 0.806–0.830 when evaluated on human cell lines (Mouse(ES-E14)→Human), representing a notable improvement over G1E-based mouse-to-human transfer. Models trained on human cell lines maintained stable performance when evaluated on E14 (Human→Mouse(ES-E14)), with AUC values of 0.798–0.819. The inclusion of E14 substantially reduced the performance asymmetry observed in the G1E experiments, with both transfer directions achieving highly comparable AUC ranges, confirming consistent and symmetric bidirectional transfer performance. These findings demonstrate that the cross-species generalization of CT-TADB is reproducible under a more standardized mammalian benchmark and does not depend on any single mouse cell line dataset.

  Collectively, cross-species experiments on both G1E and E14 mouse cell lines demonstrate stable and reproducible bidirectional transfer performance between human and mouse genomes. The consistent predictive accuracy across two distinct mouse datasets, representing different cell types and data quality levels, indicates that CT-TADB captures evolutionarily conserved determinants of TAD boundary formation rather than cell-line-specific or dataset-specific patterns. These results establish CT-TADB as a reliable framework for cross-species TAD boundary prediction and support its applicability in settings where species-specific epigenomic training data may be limited or unavailable.


### CT-TADB reveals enrichment of key transcription factor binding motifs at TAD boundaries
  To elucidate sequence determinants of TAD boundary formation, we compared motifs extracted by CT-TADB’s CNN filters with experimentally validated binding motifs from the JASPAR database. The model successfully identified sequence patterns closely resembling known binding motifs for six transcription factors: NFATC1, HIC2, PLAGL1, NFATC4, FoxE, and MEF2A (Fig. 5A).Fig. 5Feature importance analysis.A Comparison of CT-TADB-learned motifs with JASPAR database motifs. B SHAP value distributions showing feature contributions to predictions. C Feature ablation analysis ranking features by their impact on model performance.

  Visual comparison reveals strong concordance between CT-TADB-derived motifs and their JASPAR counterparts. For instance, the model accurately captured the characteristic TGGAAA core sequence of NFATC1, the TGCCC pattern of HIC2, and the poly-G stretch followed by CCA in PLAGL1. The FoxE motif showed the distinctive TGTTT core sequence, while MEF2A displayed its canonical AT-rich binding pattern. Minor variations between JASPAR and CT-TADB motifs likely reflect the specific sequence context of TAD boundaries in the training data.

  These transcription factors encompass diverse regulatory functions: NFATC1 and NFATC4 mediate calcium-dependent signaling pathways essential for immune responses23, MEF2A regulates muscle differentiation and cardiac development24, PLAGL1 functions in growth regulation and tumor suppression, HIC2 participates in developmental processes, and FoxE controls thyroid and ocular development. The successful identification of these factor binding sites suggests that TAD boundaries are enriched for regulatory elements where multiple transcription factors coordinate chromatin organization and gene expression programs.

  It is important to note that these transcription factors are not the primary structural determinants of TAD boundary formation. Rather, their enrichment within boundary sequences reflects the regulatory nature of these regions, which are frequently associated with open chromatin, active promoters, enhancers, and transcriptionally active elements. Previous studies have established that TAD boundaries often coincide with regions enriched for transcription factor binding and transcription initiation signals, suggesting that they function as regulatory hubs in addition to structural partition points25. In this context, the TF motifs identified by CT-TADB likely contribute indirectly to boundary-associated chromatin environments by modulating local transcriptional activity and chromatin accessibility, rather than serving as canonical architectural anchors. Consistent with current models of genome organization, CTCF/cohesin complexes remain the central structural framework governing TAD boundary formation, while the additional transcription factors identified here may participate in shaping the functional and transcriptional regulatory landscape of boundary regions25,26.


### CTCF emerges as the primary determinant of TAD boundary establishment through feature attribution analysis
  To dissect the contributions of sequence and epigenetic features to TAD boundary prediction, we employed complementary feature importance analyses using SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) and systematic ablation experiments.

  SHAP analysis in GM12878 cells revealed a clear hierarchy of feature importance (Fig. 5B). CTCF demonstrated the strongest predictive power with the widest SHAP value distribution extending prominently into the positive range. High CTCF signals (red points) consistently correlated with positive SHAP values, confirming its role as the primary boundary determinant. H2A.Z ranked second, followed by active chromatin marks H3K9ac, H3K4me3, and H3K4me2, all showing predominantly positive contributions. H3K27ac and H3K36me3 exhibited moderate positive effects. Conversely, repressive marks H3K27me3 and H3K9me3 displayed SHAP distributions centered near zero, indicating context-dependent contributions in which SHAP values may be positive in some samples and negative in others, resulting in a mean effect close to zero rather than an absence of influence. In contrast, H3K79me2, H3K4me1, and H4K20me1 showed minimal impact with highly restricted SHAP value ranges, reflecting values consistently concentrated near zero across all samples and suggesting genuinely negligible contributions to model predictions.

  Systematic feature ablation independently validated these findings (Fig. 5C, Supplementary Table 2). CTCF removal caused the largest performance degradation (highest D_value), confirming its essential role27. Surprisingly, H3K9me3 ablation produced the second-largest impact, followed by H2A.Z and H3K4me3. The repressive mark H3K27me3, together with the active marks H3K36me3 and H3K27ac, showed intermediate effects, while H3K9ac, H3K79me2, and H3K4me2 had smaller impacts. Consistent with SHAP analysis, H3K4me1 and H4K20me1 removal yielded minimal performance changes. The apparent discrepancy between the near-zero SHAP mean of H3K9me3 and its substantial ablation impact suggests that this feature may contribute through combinatorial or context-specific interactions that are not fully captured by average directional SHAP values, further highlighting the complementary value of integrating SHAP analysis and ablation experiments for comprehensive feature characterization. Nevertheless, both analytical approaches converge on CTCF as the dominant architectural constraint governing TAD boundary formation, supplemented by active chromatin features that facilitate boundary establishment.

  To further examine the biological relevance of the Transformer’s long-range attention mechanism, we analyzed CTCF-mediated interactions captured in positive test samples. Among 1140 positive test samples, 926 contained at least two CTCF binding sites. Long-distance CTCF pairs exhibited significantly higher interaction scores than distance-controlled random site pairs (***, p < 0.001, Wilcoxon rank-sum test; Supplementary Fig. 5). The average interaction score for the farthest CTCF pairs was 3.99 ± 11.49, whereas random controls showed substantially lower and more tightly concentrated values (−12.95 ± 1.51). At the sample level, 93.7% of qualifying samples displayed stronger interaction scores for the farthest CTCF pair relative to the corresponding random control. These results indicate that the Transformer selectively captures biologically meaningful long-range CTCF–CTCF dependencies rather than distance-driven background signals, providing mechanistic support for its contribution to TAD boundary prediction.


### Biological validation: CT-TADB captures clinically relevant and pathology-associated TAD boundary dynamics
  To validate CT-TADB’s biological relevance, we examined a clinically significant TAD boundary in the 4q25 region (chr4:112,555,060–112,570,371), located approximately 1 Mb downstream of the cardiac developmental gene PITX2. This 15 kb region maintains topological insulation between the PITX2-containing TAD and an adjacent non-coding domain harboring regulatory elements.

  Deletion of this boundary causes pathological TAD fusion and PITX2 misexpression, resulting in autosomal dominant cardiac arrhythmia28. Hi-C data from cardiac muscle cells reveal clear TAD segregation at this locus, with conserved CTCF binding sites maintaining the boundary structure (Fig. 6A). CT-TADB predicted this boundary with high confidence (prediction score = 0.902) and successfully identified the CTCF binding motif, closely matching the JASPAR reference.   To further evaluate CT-TADB under pathological conditions, we examined whether the model can predict TAD boundary loss and gain associated with domain reorganization. Independently annotated TAD boundary datasets for K562 and GM12878 were obtained from the 4DN consortium, and predictions were performed on chromosome 8 regions completely excluded from model training. K562, a leukemia-derived chronic myelogenous leukemia cell line, was used as a proxy for a pathological chromatin state, whereas GM12878 represents a lymphoblastoid cell line derived from a healthy donor. Although these cell lines do not constitute a strictly matched disease–normal pair from the same tissue of origin, the substantial biological and epigenomic differences between them provide a stringent test of the model’s cross-condition generalization capability.

  As shown in Fig. 6B–D, CT-TADB predictions are consistent with condition-specific domain reorganization observed in Hi-C contact maps. In the region chr8:29,000,000–30,000,000 (Fig. 6B), GM12878 displays two distinct TADs separated by a clear boundary, whereas K562 exhibits a single continuous domain, indicating boundary loss. Correspondingly, the predicted boundary probability at chr8:29,525,000–29,530,000 decreases from 0.8951 in GM12878 to 0.0257 in K562, consistent with the Hi-C structural observations.

  Conversely, in the region chr8:55,000,000–56,000,000 (Fig. 6C), K562 exhibits the formation of a new boundary absent in GM12878. At chr8:55,780,000–55,785,000, the predicted probability increases from 0.2879 in GM12878 to 0.6300 in K562. A second boundary gain event is observed in the region chr8:109,000,000–110,000,000 (Fig. 6D), where the predicted probability rises from 0.2683 in GM12878 to 0.9794 in K562.

  Collectively, these results demonstrate that CT-TADB not only identifies static TAD boundaries but also sensitively detects boundary loss and gain events associated with domain reorganization under pathological conditions. The consistent concordance between prediction probabilities and Hi-C structural changes across all three loci indicates robust cross-condition generalization and supports the potential utility of CT-TADB in interpreting disease-associated chromatin architectural alterations, particularly in settings where histone modification profiles are available but Hi-C data are absent.


## Discussion
  Computational approaches relying solely on DNA sequence or single-modality contact features capture limited regulatory context for accurate TAD boundary prediction29. Consistent with this limitation, the sequence-only model achieved an AUC of approximately 0.653, close to random classification. This observation aligns with evidence that robust TAD boundaries are associated with coordinated chromatin activity and transcription factor occupancy rather than sequence motifs alone19,30. By integrating DNA sequence with epigenomic features, CT-TADB achieved substantially improved predictive performance and demonstrated competitive or superior results relative to existing multi-modal methods. Importantly, this performance was maintained under stringent independent evaluation settings, including strictly independent and dataset-specific boundary subsets, indicating that the model’s predictive capacity does not rely on shared genomic loci but generalizes to previously unseen boundary regions.

  CT-TADB demonstrated strong cross-cell-type generalization under the strict cross-chromosome evaluation framework, indicating that the model captures boundary-associated features that are not restricted to cell-type-specific signal patterns but reflect shared architectural principles. In cross-species analysis, the model maintained stable bidirectional transfer between human and mouse. Given that only a subset of TAD boundaries are highly conserved across mammals31, these results suggest that CT-TADB captures conserved architectural features while accommodating species-specific variation. To further probe the evolutionary scope of these learned features, we performed exploratory cross-phyla transfer experiments using Drosophila melanogaster (kc167). Cross-phyla performance was substantially lower than mammalian transfer, with average AUC values of approximately 0.60 for Drosophila-to-human and approximately 0.57 for human-to-Drosophila prediction. This marked reduction is biologically expected, as mammalian TAD boundaries are predominantly governed by CTCF/cohesin-mediated loop extrusion, whereas Drosophila domain formation is more closely associated with transcriptional activity and chromatin state partitioning32. These results delineate the biological boundary of the model’s cross-species generalizability and confirm that the conserved features captured by CT-TADB are primarily relevant within the mammalian CTCF/cohesin architectural framework.

  Feature analysis provided insights into the regulatory context of TAD boundaries. The model identified motifs resembling MEF2A, NFATC4, NFATC1, PLAGL1, HIC2, and FoxE. These motifs should not be interpreted as direct structural determinants of boundary formation; rather, their enrichment likely reflects the transcriptionally active and regulatory nature of boundary regions. SHAP analysis confirmed CTCF as the dominant predictive feature, followed by H2A.Z and active chromatin marks, consistent with established architectural and accessibility-related roles26,33. Quantitative attention analysis further demonstrated that long-distance CTCF site pairs exhibit significantly higher interaction scores than distance-controlled random pairs (p < 0.001), with 93.7% of samples showing stronger distal CTCF interactions. This supports the notion that the Transformer captures biologically meaningful long-range CTCF-mediated dependencies rather than purely local sequence effects. Predicted boundary regions also displayed RNA-binding protein enrichment patterns consistent with genome-wide observations34.

  The clinical relevance of CT-TADB was supported by its accurate prediction (prediction score = 0.902) of the PITX2-associated boundary, where boundary disruption leads to cardiac arrhythmia28. Beyond static boundary identification, additional analyses in distinct cellular contexts further demonstrated the model’s sensitivity to boundary reorganization. In comparisons between GM12878 and the leukemia-derived K562 cell line, CT-TADB captured condition-specific boundary loss and gain events that were concordant with corresponding changes observed in Hi-C contact maps. These findings suggest that the model is capable of identifying both stable disease-relevant boundaries and dynamic chromatin reconfiguration under pathological conditions. Collectively, these results highlight the potential of CT-TADB as a complementary computational tool for prioritizing candidate pathogenic structural alterations in the absence of matched Hi-C data.

  Current limitations include the model’s focus on structural boundary prediction without explicit modeling of functional state dynamics, and limited availability of comprehensive histone modification data in primary tissues. Future computational improvements may involve incorporating genomic foundation models such as Nucleotide Transformer or HyenaDNA35,36 to enhance sequence-level representation learning, as well as integrating single-cell multi-omics data to better capture cell-state-specific boundary variation. From an experimental perspective, adapting the framework to low-input chromatin profiling technologies such as CUT&Tag and CUT&RUN, including emerging nano-CUT&Tag and multimodal single-cell protocols, may substantially broaden its applicability in clinical and primary tissue contexts where conventional ChIP-seq data are limited or unavailable37,38. In addition, extending the cross-phyla analyses to additional invertebrate and non-mammalian vertebrate species may further refine the evolutionary boundaries of chromatin architecture modeling.

  In summary, CT-TADB integrates DNA sequence and epigenomic features to enable robust TAD boundary prediction across cell types and mammalian species. The model demonstrates stable performance under stringent independent evaluation and captures biologically meaningful architectural signals, including CTCF-mediated long-range dependencies. By leveraging routinely available epigenomic data, CT-TADB provides a practical, scalable, and complementary computational framework for investigating chromatin architecture across diverse biological and translational contexts.


## Methods

### Hi-C data processing and TAD identification
  High-resolution Hi-C data were obtained from six human cell lines: IMR90 (human embryonic lung fibroblasts), NHEK (epidermal keratinocytes), K562 (chronic myelogenous leukemia cells), HUVEC (human umbilical vein endothelial cells), HMEC (human mammary epithelial cells), and GM12878 (B-lymphoblastoid cells), sourced from previously published datasets39,40. Contact matrices were processed at 10 kb resolution using HiC-Pro pipeline41, with normalization performed through iterative correction and eigenvector decomposition (ICE). Hierarchical TAD structures were subsequently identified using the OnTAD algorithm, which employs multi-scale boundary scanning and dynamic programming to explicitly model nested and hierarchical chromatin domain organization42. TAD boundaries identified by OnTAD were used as the primary annotation set for model training and evaluation, representing an operational definition within this algorithmic framework rather than an absolute biological ground truth.


### Dataset construction and DNA sequence processing
  Each identified TAD was mapped to its corresponding boundary region, defined as a 10 kb genomic window centered on the TAD boundary. Genomic sequences were extracted from the human reference genome (hg19) and classified as positive samples. To construct a balanced dataset, an equivalent number of non-overlapping genomic regions with identical length were randomly sampled from intergenic regions to serve as negative controls. The resulting dataset was partitioned into training (80%), validation (10%), and test (10%) subsets using stratified random sampling to ensure balanced class distribution. To further assess model performance under a leakage-free evaluation framework, a chromosome-based partitioning strategy was additionally applied, in which chromosome 8 (chr8) was designated as the validation set, chromosome 9 (chr9) as the test set, and all remaining chromosomes as the training set, ensuring complete independence among partitions at the chromosome level. DNA sequences were transformed using one-hot encoding, where adenine (A) = [1,0,0,0], cytosine (C) = [0,1,0,0], guanine (G) = [0,0,1,0], and thymine (T) = [0,0,0,1]. This encoding scheme generated input matrices with dimensions of 10,000 × 4, corresponding to sequence length and nucleotide channels, respectively (Fig. 7).Fig. 7Overview of the CT-TADB framework.The model integrates DNA sequences and histone modification data through three stages: A Data preprocessing—TAD boundaries from Hi-C data are extracted as 10 kb sequences and one-hot encoded, while histone modification signals are aligned and normalized; B Feature extraction—DNA sequences pass through a two-layer CNN with pooling operations, then concatenate with histone features; C Classification—Combined features are processed by a Transformer module with multi-head attention for final boundary prediction.


### Independent validation dataset
  To evaluate model generalizability across different annotation methodologies, an independently curated GM12878 dataset from HTAD was utilized as an external validation set. This dataset was generated through an innovative interactive web annotation framework integrated with discriminative active learning (DAL) methodology43. The construction pipeline commenced with Hi-C data acquisition for the GM12878 cell line from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Subsequently, approximately 140,000 potential TAD candidate regions were identified using the cooler library in conjunction with Dask parallel computing infrastructure to calculate the simplified Directionality Index (sDI). The annotation process employed an interactive web interface built on Django and WebSocket technologies, enabling expert annotators to classify 50 samples per iteration with binary labels (“YES” for true TAD boundaries, “NO” for non-boundaries). The DAL algorithm iteratively selected the most informative samples for annotation, maximizing information gain while minimizing annotation burden. Following 11 annotation rounds, this process yielded 1550 high-confidence annotated samples, comprising 841 positive and 709 negative instances30. This carefully curated dataset provides an independent benchmark for assessing model performance beyond the primary training data.


### Histone modification and transcription factor data processing
  Comprehensive epigenetic profiles were obtained from the ENCODE project database for all six human cell lines. The dataset encompassed ten histone modification marks per cell line: H4K20me1, H3K9me3, H3K9ac, H3K79me2, H3K4me3, H3K4me2, H3K4me1, H3K36me3, H3K27me3, and H3K27ac. These modifications represent diverse regulatory mechanisms spanning promoter activation (H3K4me3), enhancer states (H3K4me1, H3K27ac), transcriptional elongation (H3K36me3, H3K79me2), and heterochromatin formation (H3K9me3, H3K27me3). Additionally, binding profiles for two architectural proteins were integrated: H2A.Z, a histone variant enriched at regulatory regions and nucleosome-depleted sites, and CTCF, a zinc-finger protein critical for establishing chromatin domain boundaries and mediating long-range chromatin interactions. All epigenetic signals were processed and standardized in BigWig format to ensure consistent data representation across analyses.


### Cross-species validation dataset
  To assess model robustness and cross-species transferability, TAD regions from the mouse G1E erythroid progenitor cell line were obtained from the 3D Genome Browser database. TAD boundary data for the mouse embryonic stem cell line E14 (mESC E14, referred to as ES-E14 in the figures; mm10 genome assembly) were downloaded from the 4D Nucleome (4DN) database. For the G1E dataset, TAD regions were first converted into TAD boundary coordinates using the same computational pipeline applied to human data. Subsequently, 10 kb TAD boundary sequences centered on the identified boundary positions were extracted for both datasets, thereby ensuring methodological consistency across species. Positive samples were extracted from annotated TAD boundaries, while negative samples were generated at a 1:1 ratio from non-boundary genomic regions following the stratified sampling protocol described above. Corresponding epigenetic profiles for both cell lines, including the complete panel of histone modification marks and transcription factor binding signals, were retrieved from the ENCODE project database. This cross-species dataset enables rigorous evaluation of model generalizability beyond human-specific genomic contexts.


### Hybrid CNN-transformer framework
  The CT-TADB framework implements a hybrid deep learning architecture that synergistically combines convolutional neural networks with Transformer modules to exploit their complementary computational strengths. The CNN component efficiently extracts local sequence motifs and short-range chromatin features through hierarchical convolution operations, capturing critical boundary-proximal patterns. In parallel, the Transformer module employs multi-head self-attention mechanisms to model long-range chromatin interactions and global genomic dependencies that extend beyond the receptive field of conventional convolutions. This dual-pathway architecture enables simultaneous processing of multi-scale genomic features, addressing the inherent complexity of TAD boundary formation that involves both local sequence determinants and distal regulatory elements. The integrated framework achieves enhanced predictive accuracy by capturing the full spectrum of genomic signals relevant to TAD boundary establishment (Fig. 7, Supplementary Table 1).


### Data integration and preprocessing
  The CT-TADB framework integrates DNA sequence matrices with their corresponding histone modification profiles through parallel data streams. Histone modification signals are precisely aligned to DNA sequence coordinates using genomic interval mapping, ensuring accurate positional correspondence between sequence features and epigenetic marks. Prior to model integration, all histone modification signals undergo min-max normalization, scaling values to the [0,1] range to ensure comparable magnitudes across different histone marks while preserving relative signal intensities. This normalization strategy effectively handles the heterogeneous dynamic ranges inherent in ChIP-seq experiments and maintains consistent input distributions for optimal neural network training.


### Convolutional feature extraction
  The DNA sequence branch employs a hierarchical convolutional architecture comprising two sequential one-dimensional convolutional layers designed for motif detection and feature abstraction. Each convolutional layer incorporates the following components: rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation for non-linear transformation, dropout regularization (rate = 0.3) to prevent overfitting, and max-pooling (kernel size = 2, stride = 2) for spatial dimension reduction and translation invariance. Following the second convolutional block, an additional max-pooling operation (kernel size = 2, stride = 2) is applied, cumulatively reducing the sequence length to one-eighth of the original input dimension. This progressive downsampling strategy generates a compact three-dimensional feature tensor that preserves essential sequence information while facilitating computational efficiency in subsequent processing stages (Supplementary Fig. 1A).


### Multi-modal data fusion
  To enable seamless integration of heterogeneous data modalities, histone modification signals undergo dimensional transformation from their native two-dimensional representation to three-dimensional tensors. This transformation aligns the epigenetic data structure with the CNN-derived feature maps, where the third dimension is expanded to match the number of convolutional kernels employed in the preceding layers. The dimensionally aligned histone modification tensors are subsequently concatenated with the extracted DNA sequence features along the channel dimension, generating a unified multi-modal representation. This concatenation strategy preserves the spatial relationships within each data modality while enabling cross-modal information exchange in subsequent processing stages (Supplementary Fig. 1B, C).


### Transformer-based global feature learning
  The integrated multi-modal features are processed through a Transformer encoder module specifically configured for genomic sequence analysis. The architecture employs multi-head self-attention with the following hyperparameters: embedding dimension of 32, four attention heads for capturing diverse interaction patterns, and a feedforward network dimension of 32. To ensure training stability and gradient flow, the module incorporates residual connections coupled with layer normalization at each sub-layer. Additionally, dropout regularization (rate = 0.1) is applied within the attention mechanism to mitigate overfitting while preserving model expressivity. The Transformer encoder output, containing enriched global contextual information, undergoes dimensional reduction through flattening operations to generate a comprehensive one-dimensional feature representation. This feature vector is subsequently processed through a fully connected classification layer with sigmoid activation to produce binary predictions for TAD boundary identification (Supplementary Fig. 1D).


### Computational complexity and implementation details
  The CT-TADB architecture is intentionally lightweight despite incorporating a Transformer module. The model comprises two 1D convolutional layers followed by a single multi-head self-attention layer (num_heads = 4), with a total of 190,689 trainable parameters (approximately 0.19 M). This compact design substantially reduces the computational overhead typically associated with Transformer-based architectures applied to long genomic sequences, making the model suitable for deployment on standard single-GPU environments.

  Under the default 10 kb input setting with batch size = 16, each training epoch requires approximately 1 min 47 s, and the complete training process takes approximately 1 h. Experiments were conducted on two hardware platforms: NVIDIA A800-SXM4-80GB (CUDA 12.2) and NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 (24 GB GPU memory, CUDA 11.7). Peak GPU memory usage was monitored using nvidia-smi. These results confirm that CT-TADB can be stably trained on a standard single-GPU setup with manageable computational cost.

### Model evaluation metrics
  Model performance was evaluated using six standard metrics: AUC, accuracy, MCC, precision, recall, and macro-F1. AUC quantifies overall discriminative capability across all decision thresholds. Accuracy measures the proportion of correctly classified instances. MCC provides a comprehensive measure of classification quality by taking into account true and false positives and negatives, offering a balanced evaluation of model performance. Precision and recall assess positive predictive reliability and sensitivity, respectively. All metrics are calculated according to their standard definitions as widely used in the machine learning and computational biology literature.
