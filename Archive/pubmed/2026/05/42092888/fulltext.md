# Modulation of tumor-derived exosomes and reprogramming of cancer-associated fibroblasts for colorectal cancer therapy


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12943-026-02661-2.


## Introduction
  Colorectal carcinoma (CRC) has emerged as a globally significant gastrointestinal malignancy, ranking among the most prevalent cancers affecting the digestive system [1]. Contemporary epidemiological data reveal a marked elevation in both diagnosis frequency and disease-related fatalities of CRC, with a tendency toward younger onset [2]. Tumor initiation and progression are closely linked to dynamic remodeling of stromal cell states within the tumor microenvironment (TME) [3]. Studies have found that signaling molecules derived from tumor cells can disrupt the inherent anti-cancer properties of fibroblasts and promote the activation of normal tissue-associated fibroblasts (NAFs) into cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) with tumor-promoting functions [4, 5]. Activated fibroblasts are present in tissues undergoing regeneration, inflammation, and matrix remodeling, and are linked to adverse clinical outcomes in cancer patients [6]. Multiple studies have shown that CAFs can promote tumor initiation and progression through specific mechanisms, including participation in extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling in the TME, tumor angiogenesis, formation and maintenance of cancer stem-like properties, therapeutic resistance, epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and immune regulation processes [7].

  Hypoxia is a typical feature of the TME in solid tumors [8]. Driven by malignant-cell hyperproliferation and aberrant vascular structures, the tumor core region is often in a hypoxic state. This condition activates key regulatory molecules such as hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), driving metabolic reprogramming and enhanced invasiveness of tumor cells [9]. Recent studies have further revealed that hypoxia not only directly affects the behavior of cancer cells intrinsically but can also remotely regulate the function of other cells in the TME by releasing exosomes (Exo) carrying bioactive molecules such as proteins, lipids, and RNAs (e.g., mRNA, lncRNA, and miRNA) [10, 11]. Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are considered potential key molecules in exosome-mediated TME remodeling owing to their tissue-specific expression patterns and their roles in epigenetics, chromatin modification, and competitive endogenous RNA mechanisms [12–14]. Moreover, miRNAs, as key downstream interactors of lncRNAs, occupy a pivotal position in the tumor regulatory network via binding to the 3’UTR of mRNAs, inhibiting their translation or degrading the transcripts [15, 16]. However, how CRC-derived exosomes within the hypoxic microenvironment influence NAF-to-CAF activation and the underlying molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood.

  This study investigates whether and how CRC-derived hypoxic exosomes regulate NAF-to-CAF activation and promote angiogenesis via the lncRNA/miRNA axis. We propose that HIF1A-AS2 modulates tumor exosome function and fibroblast phenotype through the miR-33/HIF-1α axis in CRC. This hypothesis is supported by multiple machine learning algorithms, clinical data, molecular and genomic analyses, functional status profiling, drug response evaluation, trajectory inference, and cell–cell communication studies using bulk multi-omics and single-cell datasets (pan-cancer and in-house), as well as experimental validation.


## Results

### Integrated ML algorithms identified HIF1A-AS2 from hypoxia-induced exosomal lncRNAs
  We identified 11 upregulated lncRNAs in H-Exo from HCT116 and LoVo cells through RNA-seq analysis (Fig. 1A). qRT-PCR confirmed 6 upregulated lncRNAs (Fig. 1B). Through comprehensive ML analysis for six hypoxia-induced exosomal lncRNAs, HIF1A-AS2 and SNHG16 emerged as the most robust prognostic indicators for CRC (Fig. 1C-D). The predictive model found HIF1A-AS2 as the most significant lncRNA for recurrence based on rank (Fig. 1E-F), while other lncRNAs (LINC01128, MUC20-OT1, PTOV1-AS1, SNHG16, and TUG1) did not predict recurrence in DFS (Figures S1-S2). Thus, HIF1A-AS2 emerged as a novel exosomal lncRNA based on the prognostic and predictive models (Fig. 1G). Pan-cancer analyses further showed higher HIF1A-AS2 expression in tumors than in normal tissues, including CRC (Fig. 1H-I).

### Comprehensive pan-cancer multi-omics characterization of exosomal HIF1A-AS2: associations with immunosuppression, genomic alterations, hallmark pathways, and metabolic activation
  Pan-cancer survival analysis utilizing log-rank and Cox methods showed that HIF1A-AS2 significantly predicts poor overall survival, recurrence, and progression across multiple survival metrics (OS, DFS/RFS, DSS, PFI), indicating its potential as a negative prognostic marker in COAD (Figures S3–S4 and Figure S5A). Figure S4E-F shows that there was no significant inverse correlation between HIF1A-AS2 methylation and its expression across most cancers, including CRC. Enrichment analysis of HIF1A-AS2-associated genes suggested involvement of hypoxia and angiogenesis, such as HIF-1 and VEGFA-VEGFR2 signaling (Figure S5E and Figure S6A-E).

  Pan-cancer analysis demonstrated that HIF1A-AS2 expression exhibited a predominantly positive association with genomic and somatic features such as CTA score and neoantigen load, including in CRC (Figure S5B). Figure S5C-D and Figure S6E-H depict pan-cancer positive correlations in exosome signatures and hallmark pathways. In CRC, the association with the exosome signature was modest. Figure S5D and Figure S6G demonstrate positive associations with oncogenic features like hypoxia, invasion, and EMT, Figure S6F shows metabolic pathway enrichment. Figure S6H illustrates TGF-beta signaling enrichment, with broadly consistent patterns across cancers.

  Pan-cancer TME profiling revealed that elevated HIF1A-AS2 levels were related to altered immune infiltration and immunosuppressive features, including in CRC (Figure S7A-D). Notably, high HIF1A-AS2 correlated with enhanced immunosuppressive signatures (e.g., CAFs, MDSCs, TAMs, TANs, and Tregs) and reduced cytotoxic activity (Figure S7A-C). Moreover, among 68 immune-related signatures, TREM1, IL13, wound healing, and TGF-β scores showed the strongest positive correlations (Figure S7D), consistent with an immunosuppressive TME phenotype.

  Drug analysis revealed that HIF1A-AS2 expression across cancer cell lines was primarily linked to sensitivity to most compounds (R<−0.3, P<0.05) (Figure S5F). In addition, VEGFA, a gene implicated in both chemotherapy and radiotherapy responses, exhibited the strongest positive correlation with HIF1A-AS2 (Figure S8).


### Pan-cancer scRNA-seq profiling
  The pan-cancer scRNA-seq dataset (GSE210347) was used. After data processing, filtering, normalization, and annotation, multiple cell types were identified (Figure S9A). UMAP visualization (Figure S10A–B) revealed that HIF1A-AS2 expression was most prominent in myeloid lineages, including monocytes and macrophages. Across various cancer types, tumor cells showed higher HIF1A-AS2 expression than normal or adjacent tissue cells, including in CRC (P<0.01) (Figure S9B and Figure S10C). Expression was high in macrophages (P<0.001) (Figure S10D), with similar results observed in cell-type proportions between HIF1A-AS2-high (47.7%) and -low (17.3%) groups (Figure S9C). GSEA analysis showed that angiogenesis was the most significantly enriched pathway in HIF1A-AS2-high cells in CRC (Figure S9D).

  Pan-cancer ICB scRNA-seq data were annotated into diverse cell types (Figure S9E-F). UMAP density plot showed that HIF1A-AS2 was predominantly expressed in macrophages (Figure S9E and Figure S10E, G). HIF1A-AS2-high cells were enriched for macrophages across favorable and unfavorable treatment groups (log2FC>2; Figure S9G and Figure S10G-H), and in untreated samples, were accompanied by a higher proportion of malignant cells. When comparing HIF1A-AS2 expression across treatment outcomes (Figure S10F), untreated cells exhibited significantly higher HIF1A-AS2 expression in both unfavorable and favorable groups (P<0.001). However, no significant difference in HIF1A-AS2 expression was observed between the unfavorable and favorable groups. Furthermore, bulk ICB data showed no significant difference in HIF1A-AS2 expression between responders and non-responders (Figure S9H). HIF1A-AS2 expression alone did not distinguish clinical benefit and may be influenced by similar cell-type compositions (e.g., macrophage abundance) across outcome groups.


### Clinical significance, molecular characteristics, and functional status associated with exosomal HIF1A-AS2 expression in CRC
  Based on the findings from the pan-cancer analysis, we further evaluated the role of HIF1A-AS2 in CRC. Merged TCGA and GEO data further confirmed that high HIF1A-AS2 expression was still associated with poor OS and DFS in CRC (P<0.01) (Figure S11A). Analysis of immune and molecular subtypes revealed that HIF1A-AS2 expression was highest in the TGF-β–dominant immune subtype (C6), the fibrotic TME subtype, and the CMS1/CMS3 molecular subtypes (characterized by MSI and metabolic dysregulation), consistent with its association with MSI, metabolic activation, and TGF-β signaling observed in the pan-cancer analysis (Figure S11B). Associations between HIF1A-AS2 expression and clinical features were assessed using chi-square and Wilcoxon tests, revealing that elevated HIF1A-AS2 expression was closely linked to advanced T stage (P<0.01) (Figure S11C and Figure S12A).

  GSVA analysis showed that HIF1A-AS2 expression was significantly positively correlated with multiple pro-tumor functional states, including hypoxia, quiescence, stemness, EMT, angiogenesis, vasculogenic mimicry (VM), inflammation, invasion, proliferation, and metastasis (P<0.001) (Figure S11D). Likewise, PROGENy analysis revealed that HIF1A-AS2-high samples exhibited higher inferred activity in multiple cancer-related signaling pathways relative to the low group (Figure S11E).

  The mutational landscape between high and low HIF1A-AS2 expression groups was shown in Figure S11F–G and Figure S12B–F. 21 genes involving in cell adhesion were significantly more frequently mutated in HIF1A-AS2-high tumors (Figure S11F, Figure S12E–F), with co-occurrence interactions (Figure S11G). CNA analysis revealed a higher amplification burden in HIF1A-AS2-high CRC samples (Figure S11H). Overall, these findings are consistent with a more aggressive molecular phenotype and suggest a potential oncogenic role for HIF1A-AS2 in CRC.


### Drug analysis in CRC using cell lines, bulk transcriptomic data, and in-house scRNA-seq data
  Analysis of drug-target canonical pathways demonstrated positive correlations between HIF1A-AS2 expression and neural signaling, tumor-suppressive miRNA targets, MAPK/ERK signaling, and vesicle/ER membrane coat pathways (Figure S13A), consistent with published literature on vesicle-driven tumor angiogenesis [17], hypoxia-induced miR-5100–mediated CAF activation via exosomes [18], and neural signaling-driven CRC carcinogenesis [19]. In the CMAP analysis (Figure S13B), arachidonyltrifluoromethane yielded the most negative connectivity score, suggesting potential to reverse the high HIF1A-AS2 expression CRC. Arachidonyl trifluoromethyl ketone, a selective cPLA2 inhibitor, reduces arachidonic acid release and downstream ERK/Akt signaling, thereby attenuating fibroblast activation and enhancing radiosensitivity [20, 21]. Figure S13C–D demonstrate negative correlations between HIF1A-AS2 expression and multiple compounds in both CTRP and PRISM screens, indicating potential increased drug sensitivity. Notably, three overlapping agents, OSI-027 (PI3K/mTOR signaling), Navitoclax (apoptosis), and Gefitinib (EGFR signaling) were identified in both CTRP and PRISM screens, highlighting their potential efficacy in HIF1A-AS2-high CRC. In addition, using the GDSC2 database, drug analysis (Figure S13E-F, Figure S14A) revealed that HIF1A-AS2 expression correlated with sensitivity to multiple drugs, primarily those targeting EGFR and PI3K/mTOR pathways.

  We further confirmed drug response analysis for HIF1A-AS2 using in-house scRNA-seq data. The scRank analysis revealed cell type-specific effects of HIF1A-AS2, with fibroblasts achieving high rank percentiles and perturbation scores, suggesting that fibroblasts may represent a key cellular target influenced by HIF1A-AS2 (Figure S13G–I and Figure S14B-G). Based on perturbation scores, cells were classified into three types: resistant, intermediate, and sensitive, where resistant cells exhibited markedly elevated HIF1A-AS2 levels compared to sensitive cells (P < 0.001; Figure S13J). Beyondcell analysis identified five compounds inversely correlated with HIF1A-AS2: iodoacetic acid (a GAPDH inhibitor impairing glycolysis and thereby indirectly disrupting metabolism-regulating pathways such as MEK/ERK, Notch, NF-kB, and PI3K/AKT/mTOR) [22, 23] showed the strongest association, followed by etodolac (cyclooxygenase [COX] inhibitor targeting EGFR/p38-MAPK/Sp1-mediated angiogenesis) [24], quinpirole (dopamine D2 receptor agonist), ipratropium (acetylcholine receptor antagonist), and epothilone (microtubule stabilizing agent implicated in apoptosis) [25] (Figure S13H). Together, these analyses identify several agents warranting further preclinical validation as potential therapeutic candidates for HIF1A-AS2-high CRC.


### In-house single-cell RNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics of exosomal HIF1A-AS2 in CRC
  Analysis of the CRC EMTAB8107 scRNA-seq dataset (Figure S15A–C) revealed that HIF1A-AS2 expression was predominantly observed in monocytes/macrophages, and both monocytes/macrophages and malignant clusters exhibited higher levels than fibroblasts (Figure S15D–E). Pathway analysis of HIF1A-AS2-positive cells (Figure S15F–H) showed enrichment of angiogenesis, proteoglycans, and HIF-1 signaling.

  In-house scRNA-seq data from CRC samples were further used to validate the findings. Thirteen cell types were identified based on canonical markers (Figure S16A–B). HIF1A-AS2 expression was high in monocytes and macrophages (Figure S16C, E), consistent with results from the publicly available EMTAB8107 dataset. BayesPrism deconvolution of cell composition indicated that fibroblasts were more abundant than macrophages and monocytes. Moreover, the high HIF1A-AS2 expression group exhibited increased fibroblast infiltration compared to the low-expression group, and high fibroblast abundance was associated with worse DFS in CRC (Figure S16F and Figure S17I). GSEA and pathway analyses (Figure S16G–H; Figure S18A–G) revealed that hypoxia, proliferation, metastasis, inflammation, and TGF-β signaling were significantly upregulated in HIF1A-AS2-high cells. In-house scRNA-seq data revealed significantly increased HIF1A-AS2 expression in the tumor group relative to the normal group (Figure S16D). Spatial transcriptomics further demonstrated that HIF1A-AS2 expression was highest in malignant regions, followed by the tumor boundary and non-malignant areas (Figure S16I–M; Figure S17A-H).

  Analysis of scMetabolism revealed that multiple metabolic pathways, including HIF-1α and PPARγ-mediated regulation of glycolysis as well as glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, displayed robust enrichment in the HIF1A-AS2-high group (Figure S19A–C). Moreover, analysis of TME features showed that macrophage-associated signatures were frequently observed in high-HIF1A-AS2 cells (Figure S19D–F). These analyses suggest that HIF1A-AS2 is mainly enriched in monocytes and macrophages and may be involved in angiogenesis, hypoxia signaling, metabolic reprogramming, and TME remodeling in CRC.


### Trajectory inference and cell–cell communication analysis of exosomal HIF1A-AS2
  Using in-house scRNA-seq data, CytoTRACE-2 inferred fibroblasts as the starting point. Trajectory inference using CytoTRACE2, Slingshot, and Monocle3 (Fig. 2A-D) revealed that cells with high expression of HIF1A-AS2 exhibited late pseudotime states. High HIF1A-AS2 cells exhibited significant upregulation of HIF1A and distinct TF regulatory programs compared to low HIF1A-AS2 cells (Fig. 2F-G). Functional analysis revealed a progressive increase in HIF1A-AS2 expression along the pseudotime trajectory, with elevated expression at the late stages of pseudotime linked to ECM organization, angiogenesis, and fibroblast. Low expression levels in early stages were associated with immune activation, suggesting a potential role for HIF1A-AS2 in processes related to fibroblast activation, ECM remodeling, and vascular niche formation (Fig. 2C-E, H). CellChat-inferred interaction strength was highest in fibroblasts, suggesting they may represent a central communication hub in the HIF1A-AS2–high group (Figure S20E). Cell–cell communication analysis further revealed interactions between fibroblasts and other stromal cells (smooth muscle and neuronal–glial cells) [26] as well as malignant cells, involving multi-ligands via the CD44 receptor (Fig. 2I–L; Figures S20–21). HIF1A-AS2 expression positively correlated with CD44, whose oncogenic role was observed by bulk RNA-seq, scRNA-seq, and ST data (Figures S22–23). These findings are consistent with previous reports of fibroblast–CD44 interactions in bladder carcinoma progression [27]. Collectively, integrated analyses including scRank, cell-cell communication, and trajectory inference suggest a potential role for HIF1A-AS2 in angiogenesis, ECM remodeling, and CAF formation in CRC. To validate these findings, we next conducted functional assays.

### Exosomes derived from colorectal cancer cells under hypoxic conditions potentially promote the transformation of normal fibroblasts into cancer-associated fibroblasts
  To investigate the effects of tumor cell-derived exosomes on fibroblasts under different microenvironments, exosomes were first isolated and characterized from HCT116 and LoVo cells cultured under normoxic (N-Exo) and hypoxic (H-Exo) conditions. Transmission electron microscopy and nanoparticle tracking analysis showed typical exosome morphology and size (Fig. 3A). TCGA CRC data indicated a positive correlation between exosome/hypoxia scores and α-SMA, FAP, and CD63 (Figure S24A). Western blot confirmed exosome isolation with high CD63 and CD81 expression (Fig. 3B). In addition, given that CAFs remodel collagen within the TME, a collagen gel contraction assay demonstrated that H-Exo significantly enhanced the collagen contraction capacity of NAFs (Fig. 3C). High H-Exo scores showed a trend toward poor DFS in CRC (Figure S24B). TCGA CRC data demonstrated a significant positive correlation between H-Exo score and the expression of ACTA2, FAP, and VIM (Figure S24C-D). Immunofluorescence (IF) staining revealed that H-Exo treatment enhanced α-SMA FAP, and Vimentin intensity in NAFs (Fig. 3D), which are classic markers of activated fibroblasts. H-Exo induced significantly higher levels of these markers than N-Exo (Fig. 3E), consistent with qRT-PCR validation (Fig. 3F), indicating a stronger potential of hypoxic exosomes to activate CAFs. Bioinformatic analyses of TCGA CRC data showed that H-Exo score was significantly positively correlated with CAF infiltration as well as proliferation and invasion scores (Fig. 3G-H). Transwell assays further confirmed that H-Exo treatment markedly enhanced fibroblast migration and invasion compared to N-Exo (Fig. 3I-J). These findings suggest that hypoxia-derived exosomes may more effectively promote NAFs toward a tumor-promoting CAF phenotype.

### HIF1A-AS2 is a key lncRNA mediating the transformation of NAFs by colorectal cancer cell-derived exosomes under hypoxia
  Integrative analyses identified HIF1A-AS2 as a potential critical mediator of oncogenesis, exosome, fibroblast–tumor–stromal interactions, and recurrence in CRC. Therefore, we used in vitro and in vivo models to examine its EV-linked effects and potential role in CAF remodeling. Survival analysis showed that high hypoxia and cell migration scores were associated with shorter DFS (Fig. 4A). TGF-β is a potent activator of CAFs. PROGENy analysis of in-house scRNA data revealed higher inferred activity of multiple pathways (e.g., hypoxia, VEGF, JAK–STAT, and TGF-β signaling) in the HIF1A-AS2-high group (Fig. 4B). Moreover, HIF1A-AS2 expression positively correlated with hypoxia, exosome, and H-Exo scores (Fig. 4C). qRT-PCR further confirmed higher HIF1A-AS2 expression in hypoxic CRC cell lines compared to normoxic controls (Fig. 4D) Consistently, HIF1A-AS2 levels were significantly reduced in si-HIF1A-AS2–Exo and markedly elevated in HIF1A-AS2–Exo (Fig. 4E).

  α-SMA and FAP are markers for evaluating the activation status of fibroblasts and their tumor-promoting functions [28]. Analyses of bulk RNA-seq, scRNA-seq, and ST consistently showed that α-SMA (Figures S25–S26) and FAP (Figures S27–S28) were obviously enriched in fibroblasts and associate with oncogenic programs in CRC. IF staining results showed that the expression of α-SMA, FAP, and Vimentin was reduced in NAFs treated with si-HIF1A-AS2-Exo, while HIF1A-AS2-Exo increased their expression (Fig. 4F-G). ### Exosomal HIF1A-AS2 regulates NAFs transformation through the miR-33/HIF-1α axis
  To investigate how exosomal HIF1A-AS2 may modulate CAF formation in CRC progression, candidate miRNAs targeting HIF1A-AS2 were predicted using three independent databases: the Incbasev3 database, the LncBook 2.0 database, and the DEmiRs_PanCancer data. Among these, miR-33a-5p emerged as the intersecting miRNA (Fig. 5A). Analysis of bulk data demonstrated that miR-33 (33a-5p and 33b-5p) exhibited a potential inverse correlation with HIF1A-AS2 and HIF1A (Fig. 5B and Figure S29B, F). Pan-cancer survival analysis indicated that high miR-33 expression predicted improved DFS (Fig. 5C and Figure S29A). Moreover, miR-33a-5p was implicated in multiple biological processes (Figure S29C-D). Sequence alignment further identified complementary binding sites between HIF1A-AS2 and miR-33 (Fig. 5D). Functional state analyses revealed that miR-33 expression was negatively correlated with key oncogenic processes (Fig. 5E and Figure S29E-J), such as angiogenesis, proliferation, invasion, metastasis, hypoxia response, EMT, inflammation, and VM. PROGENy-based pathway activity comparison also showed lower inferred activity of EGFR, JAK–STAT, hypoxia, p53, and TGF-β pathways in the high miR-33 group (Fig. 5F and Figure S29K).

  Next, a NAF+CRC-conditioned medium (CM) model was established. HIF1A-AS2 silencing upregulated miR-33, whereas its overexpression downregulated miR-33 (Fig. 5G), suggesting that HIF1A-AS2 may negatively regulate miR-33. To further validate the miR-33/HIF-1α regulatory axis, subsequent experiments were conducted in this model under hypoxic conditions. Dual-luciferase analysis showed that miR-33 mimic suppressed HIF-1α mRNA expression and 3’-UTR luciferase activity, while miR-33 inhibitor enhanced both (Fig. 5H), supporting that miR-33 negatively regulates HIF-1α by binding to its 3’-UTR. HIF1A-AS2 overexpression enhanced HIF-1α mRNA and protein levels. Importantly, this upregulation was partially rescued by the transfection of miR-33 mimics (Fig. 5I-J). Meanwhile, the downregulation of HIF-1α induced by HIF1A-AS2 silencing was reversed by the transfection of a miR-33 inhibitor (Fig. 5K). Analyses of multiple public datasets revealed the oncogenic functions of HIF-1α in CRC (Figures S30-S31). Together, these findings support a model in which hypoxic CRC-derived exosomal HIF1A-AS2 functions as a miR-33 sponge, relieving repression of HIF-1α and thereby potentially activating downstream pathways that may contribute to reprogramming NAFs into CAFs and promoting angiogenesis (Fig. 5L).


### Hypoxia-derived exosomes exert direct oncogenic and pro-angiogenic effects in CRC
  To disentangle the direct autocrine effects of tumor-derived exosomes from the paracrine effects mediated by the secretome of Exo-educated NAFs, we first investigated the direct impact of H-Exo on CRC cells and endothelial cells. In HCT116 cells, direct treatment with H-Exo markedly promoted proliferation, colony-forming capacity, invasion, and migratory potential compared to the N-Exo group (Figure S32A-C). Bioinformatic analyses indicated a positive association between the H-Exo score and key pathway markers, including HIF1A, MAPK1, NOTCH1, and MMP7/9, as well as angiogenic factors (VEGFA, ANGPT2, and PECAM1 [CD31]) (Figure S32D and H). These results were validated by Western blot and qRT-PCR, which showed that H-Exo treatment upregulated HIF-1α, phosphorylated ERK1/2 (p-ERK), Notch1, and MMP-7 expression (Figure S32E-F). Furthermore, given the central role of angiogenesis in this study, we assessed the direct effects of exosomes on HUVECs. H-Exo treatment significantly promoted tube formation (Figure S32G) and increased the levels of angiogenic factors (VEGF and ANGPT2) and the endothelial marker CD31 (Figure S32I–J). Collectively, these data suggest that H-Exo has an intrinsic capacity to directly promote CRC malignant progression and vascular modulation.


### Hypoxia-derived exosome-induced NAFs promote colorectal cancer progression and angiogenesis
  To further evaluate the impact of hypoxia-induced exosome-treated NAFs on CRC progression, we observed that H-Exo score was positively correlated with fibroblast infiltration, angiogenesis, EMT, inflammation, VM, hypoxia, TGFβ signaling, MAPK, NFkB, and EGFR pathways (P<0.01) (Figure S24E-G), indicating an association between H-Exo abundance and enhanced malignant phenotypes. Consistently, DIA-based secretome proteomics revealed that the LoVo-derived H-Exo group (vs. N-Exo) was enriched for proteins linked to angiogenesis, ECM remodeling, EMT, fibroblast activation, and invasion/metastasis (Fig. 6A-C). In vitro co-culture and tumor formation assays showed that CM from H-Exo-treated NAFs significantly increased CRC cell proliferation and migration (Fig. 6D-E) and enhanced angiogenesis, as evidenced by increased tube formation in HUVECs (Fig. 6F). In a nude mouse xenograft model, CRC tumors co-inoculated with NAFs and treated with H-Exo exhibited the fastest growth and largest volumes (Fig. 6G). Overall, our comprehensive preclinical studies demonstrate that hypoxic CRC-derived exosomes can reprogram NAFs to promote tumor proliferation, migration, invasion, xenograft growth, and angiogenesis.

### Hypoxia-derived exosome–educated NAFs promote CRC malignant phenotypes and angiogenesis via the HIF1A-AS2/miR-33 axis and Notch1/ERK signaling
  We co-cultured CRC cells with conditioned media from NAFs treated with si-HIF1A-AS2 exosomes or miR-33 mimics and assessed proliferation, invasion, and angiogenesis to investigate the functional and mechanistic basis of the HIF1A-AS2/miR-33 axis.

  Trypan blue assay showed that H-Exo-CM significantly promoted tumor cell proliferation compared to CRC cells alone, while si-HIF1A-AS2-Exo or miR-33 mimics transfection inhibited this effect (Fig. 6H). Consistently, colony formation, migration, and invasion assays showed similar trends (Fig. 6I-J). Tube formation assays revealed that H-Exo-CM enhanced angiogenesis, which was reduced under si-HIF1A-AS2-Exo-CM or miR-33-CM treatment (Fig. 6K). Across TCPA (protein) and in-house scRNA-seq (mRNA) data, HIF1A-AS2 expression was positively correlated with NOTCH1, phosphorylated ERK1/2, VEGFA, and MMP7 (Fig. 6L-M). In contrast, miR-33 exhibited inverse correlations with ERK1/2 and MMP7/9 (Fig. 6N and Figure S29I). Integrated analysis of multiple public datasets revealed that VEGFA, MMP7, and MMP9 were associated with angiogenesis and hypoxia pathways in CRC (Figures S33–S38). Western blot analysis showed that H-Exo-CM significantly upregulated VEGF, MMP-7, and MMP-9 in CRC cells, indicating enhanced pro-angiogenic signaling. This effect was suppressed by si-HIF1A-AS2-Exo-CM or miR-33-CM. Moreover, H-Exo-CM activated Notch1 and ERK pathways, which were partially reversed by HIF1A-AS2 silencing or miR-33 overexpression (Fig. 6O and Figure S18C-D). Together, the data indicate that inhibiting exosomal HIF1A-AS2/miR-33 axis suppresses CRC cell proliferation, invasion, and angiogenesis by downregulating VEGF and MMPs and attenuating Notch1 and ERK signaling.

## Discussion
  The dynamic evolution and remodeling of the TME represent core regulatory mechanisms in malignant progression. The TME comprises a highly heterogeneous ecosystem of ECM, soluble mediators, and diverse cellular populations, including tumor cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and immune cells [29]. Complex interactions and signal transduction exist among these components, significantly influencing tumor initiation, growth, invasion, metastasis, and drug resistance [30]. Cancer cells can dynamically interact with stromal cells through multiple pathways, such as secretion of cytokines and exosomes, thereby inducing stromal remodeling to form a microenvironment favorable for tumor survival and progression [31, 32]. Simultaneously, the altered stromal microenvironment can reciprocally act on tumor cells, activating various signaling pathways and inducing epigenetic and metabolic reprogramming, further promoting malignant transformation [33].

  Integrated machine-learning analyses identified exosomal HIF1A-AS2 as a novel prognostic lncRNA in CRC. Pan-cancer bulk and scRNA profiling revealed that elevated HIF1A-AS2 expression was associated with hypoxia, HIF1A signaling, angiogenesis, and immunosuppressive TME components (e.g., CAFs, MDSCs, Tregs), along with genomic alterations and poor survival outcomes. Drug sensitivity screening indicated that HIF1A-AS2–high CRC cells were selectively sensitive to EGFR-, PI3K/mTOR-, and MAPK/ERK-directed inhibitors. In in-house scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomic analyses of CRC, HIF1A-AS2 was correlated with metabolic reprogramming, pseudotime progression, and fibroblast–CD44 interactions. Cell state analysis indicated a progressive increase in HIF1A-AS2 expression along the pseudotime trajectory, transitioning from immune activation at early stages to ECM organization, angiogenesis, and fibroblast programs at later stages. Mechanistically, hypoxic CRC-derived exosomes delivered HIF1A-AS2 to NAFs, inducing CAF phenotypes that enhanced tumor proliferation, invasion, and angiogenesis via the miR-33/HIF-1α axis and Notch1/ERK activation, leading to upregulation of VEGF, MMP-7, and MMP-9. Conversely, silencing HIF1A-AS2 in exosomes or restoring miR-33 prevented CAF reprogramming and TME remodeling, reduced malignant behaviors, and limited vascularization, highlighting exosomal HIF1A-AS2 as a candidate biomarker and therapeutic vulnerability in CRC.

  Exosomes, as nano-sized vesicles secreted by tumor cells, can participate in tumor microenvironment remodeling by serving as carriers for signaling molecules or cargo. Hypoxic microenvironments can alter exosome contents, such as proteins, lipids, mRNAs, miRNAs, and lncRNAs, thereby influencing their biological functions [34, 35]. For instance, hypoxia can upregulate lncRNA HOTAIR expression through epigenetic mechanisms [36]. Highly expressed lncRNA HOTAIR interacts with RAB35, a key regulatory factor in vesicular transport, increasing RAB35 expression and stability, and upregulating SNAP23, a SNARE protein crucial for vesicle-plasma membrane fusion, thereby promoting tumor cell exosome release and consequently tumor progression [37]. Additionally, hypoxia can transcriptionally activate miR-5100 through HIF1α; this miRNA subsequently enters exosomes and is taken up by tumor-associated fibroblasts, promoting their activation and tumor metastasis [18]. The protein composition of exosomes is also affected by hypoxia. Proteomic analyses reveal that hypoxic tumor-derived exosomes are enriched in markers promoting invasion and angiogenesis, such as MMPs, IL-8, and caveolin-1, suggesting an unfavorable prognosis [38].

  This study confirmed that hypoxic CRC-derived exosomes can induce phenotypic transformation of NAFs into CAFs, a process mediated by exosomal lncRNA HIF1A-AS2. RNA-seq analysis and qRT-PCR validation revealed that HIF1A-AS2 is significantly upregulated in exosomes derived from hypoxia-induced CRC cells. Mechanistic studies showed that exosomal HIF1A-AS2 sequesters miR-33, thereby derepressing HIF-1α and triggering a pro-angiogenic program that promotes tumor angiogenesis and progression. Additionally, HIF1A-AS2 can function as a sponge to alleviate the inhibition of tumor-suppressive miRNAs, such as miR-141-3p, miR-146-5p, and miR-129-5p, on their target genes FOXC1, IL-6, and DNMT3A, thereby promoting cell proliferation, invasion, and EMT processes in various tumors [39–41]. In conclusion, an in-depth understanding of exosome-mediated adaptive mechanisms within the hypoxic microenvironment establishes a framework for biomarker discovery and therapeutic targeting, offering new perspectives for precision tumor diagnosis and treatment.

  MMPs and VEGF are intricately linked in the process of tumor angiogenesis. MMPs, particularly neutrophil-derived MMP-9, can promote tumor angiogenesis by degrading the ECM and releasing bound VEGF [42, 43]. VEGF stimulates endothelial cell proliferation and shapes vascular architecture, with tumor cells often being one of its major sources [44, 45]. Interestingly, HIF-1 concurrently induces MMPs and VEGF, leading to synergistic pro-angiogenic effects [46]. Moreover, Notch signaling is a central regulator of tumor vascular morphology and functional maturation. Activated Notch1 not only directly promotes endothelial cell proliferation but also enhances vascular stability and maturation by upregulating the Dll4 ligand and recruiting pericytes [47]. This study revealed that hypoxic exosome-induced NAFs activated the Notch1/ERK pathway in CRC cells, subsequently elevating VEGF, MMP-7, and MMP-9 levels, thereby promoting tumor angiogenesis and progression. These findings implicate Notch1/ERK signaling in CAF-driven tumor promotion. In conclusion, the exosomal HIF1A-AS2/miR-33 axis synergistically promoted tumor angiogenesis and progression at multiple levels by integrating key signaling pathways including HIF-1α, Notch, and ERK, thereby providing a theoretical basis for multidimensional anti-angiogenic therapeutic strategies.

  Notably, under hypoxic conditions, HIF1A-AS2-modified exosomes can promote CAF transformation via the mechanisms described above, thereby enhancing CRC cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and angiogenesis through paracrine effects. To address potential confounding in conditioned-medium experiments (i.e., residual exosomal components and fibroblast-derived secreted factors), we also assessed the direct effects of tumor-derived exosomes, supporting that hypoxia-derived exosomes can directly modulate cancer and endothelial cells, alongside the effects mediated by exosome-educated NAFs (i.e., transformed CAFs). Previous studies have reported that growth factors, cytokines, and chemokines secreted by CAFs can act on adjacent tumor cells, activating multiple signaling pathways and promoting tumor progression [48]. For instance, CAFs can activate Wnt/β-catenin and STAT3 signaling pathways in CRC cells by secreting factors such as CXCL12 and HGF [40, 41]. The xenograft tumor formation experiment in this study confirmed that exosomes carrying si-HIF1A-AS2 or miR-33 mimics can reduce CAFs infiltration in the xenograft, inhibit the levels of VEGF, MVD (CD31 staining), MMP-9, Ki-67, and PCNA, thereby suppressing tumor growth and angiogenesis. These results support hypoxia–exosome lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA axis as a novel regulatory model for tumor-stroma interaction and microenvironment remodeling.

  Exosomal HIF1A-AS2 may modulate drug sensitivity by reprogramming tumor-intrinsic pathways and the stromal milieu. First, dense ECM has been reported to limit intratumoral drug penetration [49]. Consistent with this, we observed that HIF1A-AS2 may drive the conversion of NAFs into CAFs, generating an ECM‑rich stroma in CRC that is likely to attenuate the efficacy of conventional cytotoxic therapies; Second, scMetabolism analyses showed enrichment of glycolysis/gluconeogenesis in the HIF1A-AS2-high state, consistent with evidence that HIF-1α–driven glucose metabolic reprogramming can confer chemoresistance in CRC [50]. Notably, macrophage-derived exosomal lncRNA HISLA has been reported to stabilize HIF-1α, enhance glycolysis, and promote chemoresistance in breast cancer [51]. Third, our results showed that HIF1A-AS2 sponged miR‑33, stabilized HIF‑1α, and activated the Notch1 and ERK signaling pathways, promoting angiogenesis and ECM remodeling and thereby supporting tumor survival during therapy. Collectively, these findings provide evidence that targeting exosomal HIF1A-AS2 may be therapeutically beneficial (either as monotherapy or in combination with EGFR, PI3K–mTOR, and MAPK/ERK pathway inhibitors and other agents), as indicated by drug-sensitivity analyses. Further experimental validation is warranted.

  Several limitations should be considered in this study. First, while exosomal HIF1A-AS2 shows prognostic potential in CRC, establishing its clinical applicability requires prospective validation in larger, independent multicenter cohorts. Second, single-cell transcriptomic profiling was conducted on treatment-naïve CRC specimens, yet comparable data from post-neoadjuvant specimens are lacking. Third, while in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated the potential of targeting the exosomal HIF1A-AS2/HIF1α/miR-33 axis, clinical translation requires rigorous evaluations of safety, efficacy, and dose-response in prospective trials. Fourth, while myeloid lineages exhibited obvious HIF1A-AS2 expression, the present study focused primarily on CAFs and tumor-derived exosomes based on integrated evidence from BayesPrism deconvolution, perturbational scRank analysis, cell-cell communication networks, trajectory inference, and experimental validation. The effects of HIF1A-AS2-mediated EV communication on other stromal compartments and immune lineages (e.g., monocytes/macrophages) remain to be elucidated. Finally, we observed markedly increased levels of multiple lncRNAs in H-Exo compared with N-Exo, consistent with evidence that hypoxia can remodel EV-associated ncRNA cargo [52]. Although these processes may act synergistically to drive the elevated EV lncRNA levels observed under hypoxia, our current analyses are largely limited to EV cargo profiling and therefore cannot determine whether the hypoxia-induced increase in EV lncRNAs is primarily due to upregulation within cells or preferential loading into EVs. Dissecting their relative contributions will require paired quantification of matched donor cells and EVs (with EV particle–normalized RNA measurements), together with EV-sorting and mechanistic assays in future studies.


## Conclusions
  In summary, this study established the HIF1A-AS2/miR-33/HIF-1α axis as a key mediator of H-Exo–induced NAF-to-CAF transformation, linking stromal reprogramming to enhanced tumor progression and angiogenesis. It suggests that hypoxia-induced exosomal lncRNAs may serve as potential new targets for regulating the tumor microenvironment and anti-angiogenic therapy.


## Materials and methods

### Multiple machine learning algorithms for gene selection
  Multiple machine learning (ML) algorithms were employed to identify important genes. Specifically, we utilized 101 combinations derived from 10 ML algorithms to analyze six significant lncRNAs (HIF1A-AS2, LINC01128, MUC20-OT1, PTOV1-AS1, SNHG16, and TUG1) in constructing prognostic models. Additionally, nine ML algorithms were applied to these lncRNAs for binary classification analyses focusing on recurrence prediction. Detailed methodological procedures are provided in the Supplementary Methods section, including Tables S1-S16.


### Pan-cancer TCGA and single-cell RNA sequencing data
  Data collection from 33 cancer types was obtained from the TCGA database, including CRC (COAD and READ). Expression levels of HIF1A-AS2 were quantified using log2(TPM+1) transformed values. A pan-cancer single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset (GSE210347) without therapy information was downloaded for analysis. The detailed procedures are shown in the Supplementary Methods.


### Genomic and immune landscape analysis in pan-cancer
  Thirteen genomic and somatic features, such as neoantigen load, CNV burden, MSI, and TMB etc., were analyzed. Immune cell infiltration levels were assessed using seven immune deconvolution algorithms (Supplementary Methods).


### Exploration of HIF1A-AS2’s role in tumor biology and prognosis in pan-cancer
  Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) were used to investigate HIF1A-AS2’s involvement in exosome-related processes and cancer hallmark pathways. Moreover, the prognostic value of HIF1A-AS2 was assessed across multiple clinical outcomes (Supplementary Methods).


### Interactions between HIF1A-AS2 and miRNAs
  Differentially expressed miRNAs between pan-cancer patient groups stratified by HIF1A-AS2 expression were identified. Potential interactions between HIF1A-AS2 and miRNAs were also predicted using the lncBase v3 and LncBook 2.0 databases. The overlapping miRNAs from these analyses are illustrated in a Venn diagram (Supplementary Methods).


### Pan-cancer analysis of single-cell and bulk RNA-seq data in immune checkpoint blockade therapy
  scRNA-seq data related to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy were sourced from the study by Gondal et al. [53]. In addition, integrated bulk RNA-seq datasets from ICB-treated cancer patients were utilized. These data were used to explore the expression profiles of HIF1A-AS2 in response to ICB therapy (Supplementary Methods).


### GSVA Analysis of functional status in CRC
  GSVA analysis was performed to evaluate 15 distinct functional pathways, such as stemness, invasion, metastasis, proliferation, EMT, angiogenesis, hypoxia, inflammation, and vasculogenic mimicry (VM). We then assessed the association of HIF1A-AS2 expression with these functional statuses (Supplementary Methods).


### Pathway Responsive Genes for Activity Inference (PROGENy) in CRC
  The “progeny” package was utilized to infer activities of 14 cancer-related signaling pathways. Subsequently, the role of HIF1A-AS2 in modulating these pathways was evaluated. Detailed methodology is provided in the Supplementary Methods.


### Drug target canon and drug sensitivity in CRC
  To assess the correlation between HIF1A-AS2 expression and drugTargetCanon in CRC, data from the UCSC Xena database were analyzed, followed by Spearman correlation and heatmap visualization. Potential therapeutic compounds for high HIF1A-AS2 CRC patients were identified using gene expression data and the XSum method.

  Drug sensitivity was analyzed across various databases, including CCLE, GDSC, and CTRP, with Spearman correlation and Wilcoxon tests applied to evaluate drug response. Pathway analysis further identified key molecular pathways influenced by these compounds. The detailed method is available in the Supplementary Methods.


### DNA mutation and CNA analysis in CRC
  DNA mutation and copy number alteration (CNA) data were analyzed to investigate their role in high and low HIF1A-AS2 expression groups (Supplementary Methods).


### Spatial Transcriptomics (ST) in CRC
  ST data were analyzed to assess HIF1A-AS2 expression across CRC tissue regions, with visualization and correlation analysis performed to explore spatial distribution and tumor boundaries.


### Patient specimens and ethical approval
  This study involving treatment-naïve primary CRC tissues was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Shanghai Municipal Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Approval No. 2021SHL-KY-04-02), and written informed consent was obtained from all six samples (Supplementary Methods).


### Cell preparation, scRNA-seq library construction, and sequencing for in-house data
  For the six samples, library quantification was performed using a High Sensitivity DNA Chip (Agilent) on a Bioanalyzer 2100, and the DNA concentration was measured using a Qubit High Sensitivity DNA Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Sequencing was carried out on a NovaSeq Xplus platform (Illumina) with paired-end 150 base-pair (PE150) chemistry at Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The detailed procedures are shown in the Supplementary Methods.


### scRNA-seq data preprocessing and analysis of in-house data
  For the in-house dataset, the preprocessing and procedures were performed using the “Seurat” package, as described in detail in the Supplementary Methods section. This comprehensive workflow included data filtering to remove low-quality cells, normalization, and cell annotation etc.


### Analysis of cancer Hallmarks and metabolic pathways using in-house scRNA-seq data
  Our analyses emphasized cancer-associated signatures, including hallmark gene sets, KEGG pathways, VEGF pathway, Notch1/ERK signaling, as well as oncogene-related signatures. We also evaluated diverse metabolic pathways. Tumor hallmarks and metabolic activities were compared between high and low HIF1A-AS2 expression groups. Comprehensive methodological details are described in the Supplementary Methods.


### Trajectory inference analysis with CytoTRACE2, Slingshot, and monocle3 at our single cell level
  To infer potential cellular differentiation states, the “CytoTRACE2” package was employed to compare groups exhibiting high and low levels of HIF1A-AS2. Furthermore, trajectory analyses related to HIF1A-AS2 expression were conducted using the “Slingshot” and “monocle3” packages, with methodological details provided in the Supplementary Methods.


### Drug response analysis using in-house scRNA-seq data
  To assess drug response at the scRNA-seq level for HIF1A-AS2, the “scRank” and “beyondcell” packages were utilized. Detailed methodologies are provided in the Supplementary Methods section.


### Cell‒cell communication analysis
  To elucidate the intercellular interactions, ligand–receptor pairs and their associated signaling pathways were analyzed using the “CellChat” package (Supplementary Methods).


### Cell culture
  Human colorectal cancer cell lines HCT116 and LoVo were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and routinely cultured in RPMI-1640 high-glucose or F-12 K medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The normoxic culture condition was 21% O2, and hypoxic culture was performed in a sealed incubator with a mixed gas of 1% O2, 5% CO2, and 94% N2 to maintain an oxygen concentration of 1%. Primary NAFs were isolated and cultured from adjacent normal tissues of surgically resected specimens from colorectal cancer patients, following the method described in [54]. Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Jiangsu, China) and cultured in endothelial cell-specific medium EGM-2 MV (Lonza). All cell lines were authenticated by STR analysis and tested for mycoplasma contamination.


### Cell co-culture experiments
  Two co-culture systems were used in this study: NAFs were cultured in medium containing conditioned medium from CRC cell lines (CRC-CM) to induce NAF transformation. The transformed NAFs were used to assess changes in their biological characteristics by detecting various cellular indicators. In addition, NAFs were treated with exosomes from different sources, and the conditioned medium supernatant of NAFs was collected and added to the culture medium of two CRC cell lines, HCT116 and LoVo, to observe the effects of exosomes on CRC cell proliferation and angiogenesis through NAFs and their molecular mechanisms.


### Exosome isolation and identification
  HCT116 and LoVo cells were seeded at 1×10⁶/mL in 10 cm dishes and grown to 70–80% confluency. After 48 h in serum-free culture medium under normoxic or hypoxic conditions, the supernatant underwent sequential centrifugation (300×g, 10 min; 2,000×g, 15 min; 12,000×g, 30 min), 0.22 μm filtration, and ultracentrifugation (100,000×g, 70 min) to isolate exosomes. Resuspended exosomes were quantified by BCA assay, characterized by electron microscopy and nanoparticle tracking, and analyzed (30 µg protein) by Western blot for CD63 and CD81. The isolated exosomes derived from HCT116 and LoVo cells under normoxic conditions were labeled as normoxic exosomes (N-Exo), and those under hypoxic conditions were labeled as H-Exo.


### Exosomal RNA extraction and in-house lncRNA RNA-seq analysis
  Total RNA was extracted from N-Exo and H-Exo using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). RNA quality and quantity were assessed prior to library construction. RNA-seq libraries were prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq platform. Reads were processed to generate a gene-level count matrix, and differential expression between N-Exo and H-Exo was assessed using DESeq2. For visualization, normalized expression values were log2(x+1)-transformed. Differentially expressed lncRNAs were defined as FC≥2.0 and P<0.05, and were annotated using NONCODE, GENCODE, and lncRNAdb. Raw gene expression count data comparing N-Exo and H-Exo samples are provided in Table S1. The most significantly differentially expressed lncRNAs were selected, and their expression levels in N-Exo and H-Exo were validated by qRT-PCR.


### Construction of exosomal HIF1A-AS2 knockdown and overexpression and cell transfection
  The siRNA targeting HIF1A-AS2 and the HIF1A-AS2 overexpression plasmid were designed based on the lncRNA HIF1A-AS2 sequence, synthesized by Shanghai Jima Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd. 48 h after transfection, the cell supernatant was collected, and exosomes derived from the transfected cells were isolated and labeled as si-HIF1A-AS2-Exo, HIF1A-AS2-Exo, and their corresponding negative controls si-NC-Exo and Vector-Exo.


### Invasion and migration assays
  Transwell chambers (8 μm pore size, Corning) were employed to evaluate cellular migratory and invasive capacities. HCT116 cells, LoVo cells, and NAFs were used in the Transwell assays. For migration assay, 5×104 cells in serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chamber, while complete medium containing 10% FBS served as chemoattractant in the lower compartment. Invasion assays were conducted using Matrigel-coated chambers (100 µg/mL, BD Biosciences) with 1×105 cells/chamber. Cells traversing the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and quantified by counting five random fields per chamber under microscopy.


### Collagen gel contraction assay
  To evaluate the contractile capability of fibroblasts, a collagen gel contraction assay was performed, as described in the Supplementary Methods.


### Immunofluorescence (IF) staining
  NAFs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min, and blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against α-SMA (1:200; 14395-1-AP; rabbit; Proteintech), FAP (1:100; 66562; rabbit; Cell Signaling Technology), and Vimentin (1:400; 10366-1-AP; rabbit; Proteintech), followed by Alexa Fluor–conjugated anti-rabbit secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488, 1:500; Alexa Fluor 647, 1:500) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1:1000). Confocal images were acquired and analyzed using ImageJ. Mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was calculated as integrated density (IntDen) divided by the selected area (Area) (MFI=IntDen/Area), where IntDen represents the sum of pixel intensities within the region of interest. Background signal measured in an adjacent area was subtracted prior to MFI calculation. Tissue IF staining for Ki-67 (GB111499-50, Servicebio) and PCNA (GB11010-50, Servicebio) was performed on paraffin-embedded Sect.  (4 μm) using standard immunofluorescence procedures. Images were captured from five randomly selected, non-overlapping fields per sample using a conventional fluorescence microscope with identical exposure settings across groups. Total cell numbers were determined by DAPI nuclear staining, and Ki-67– or PCNA–positive cells were quantified based on specific fluorescence signals. The positive cell rate (%) was calculated as: (Ki-67– or PCNA–positive cells / total DAPI-stained nuclei) × 100%.


### Prediction of HIF1A-AS2 targeting miRNAs
  The miRanda (v3.3a) and RNAhybrid (v2.2) algorithms were used to predict potential miRNA binding sites for HIF1A-AS2, with parameters set as ΔG ≤ -20 kcal/mol and p<0.01. Based on the prediction results, miR-33 was selected as a candidate miRNA for subsequent validation.


### Luciferase reporter assay to verify exosomal HIF1A-AS2 regulation of miR-33 expression
  The luciferase reporter vector psiCHECK-2-miR-33 was constructed by cloning the perfectly complementary sequence of the mature miR-33 sequence into the 3’ untranslated region (3’-UTR) of the Renilla luciferase gene in the psiCHECK-2 plasmid. Different siRNAs (si-Ctrl, si-HIF1A-AS2) or overexpression plasmids (vector, HIF1A-AS2) were transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 according to the grouping, with three replicates per group. The culture supernatants from the differentially treated HCT116 cells were added to each well to induce NAF transformation. Simultaneously, psiCHECK-2-miR-33 was transfected into NAFs using Lipofectamine 3000, with three replicates per group. Renilla and firefly luciferase activities were measured 48 h post-transfection, and their ratio represented miR-33 expression.


### miRNA mimic and inhibitor transfection
  Cells were transfected with 100 nM miRNA mimics or inhibitors using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX in Opti-MEM, following the manufacturer’s instructions. The transfection reagent was added to the mimic or inhibitor solution, mixed gently, and incubated for 15 min to form transfection complexes. The original culture medium in the six-well plate was removed, and the cells were washed twice with PBS. 1700 µl of fresh DMEM culture medium and 300 µl of transfection complexes were added to each well and mixed gently. The cells were cultured in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator for 24–48 h before being used in subsequent experiments.


### Cell proliferation assay
  The effect of exosomes from different sources or conditioned medium from treated NAFs on CRC cell proliferation was assessed using trypan blue staining. HCT116 and LoVo cells (4×10³/well) were treated with 100 µg/mL exosomes or conditioned medium (1:1) in 96-well plates. Cell viability was assessed by trypan blue staining and microscopy. For colony formation, 1,000 cells were seeded in 6-well plates, treated for 14 days, fixed with methanol, stained with crystal violet, and colonies>1 mm were counted.


### Cell scratch assay
  HCT116 and LoVo cells were seeded in 6-well plates. A standardized scratch wound was made on the cell monolayer using a sterile 200 µL pipette tip. Wound healing was monitored and photographed at 0 h and 24 h. The migration distance was quantified by measuring the scratch width at five random points using ImageJ software.


### Tube formation assay
  To assess angiogenesis, pre-chilled Matrigel was added to 96-well plates (50 µL/well) and allowed to polymerize at 37 °C for 1 h [55]. HUVECs (2 × 10⁴ cells/well) were then plated onto the solidified gel and cultured under the indicated conditions. Tubular network formation was photographed, and the number of complete enclosed tube structures was quantified in three randomly selected fields per well.


### Animals
  Four- to six-week-old female BALB/c nude mice were purchased from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. HCT116 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were digested, counted, and resuspended in PBS at a density of 2×10⁶ cells/100 µL. The cells were inoculated subcutaneously in the right axillary region of the nude mice. When the tumors grew to approximately 100 mm³, the mice were randomly divided into four groups (n=6): (1) CRC: HCT116 alone; (2) CRC+NAFs: HCT116 and NAFs co-inoculation; (3) CRC+NAFs+N-Exo: HCT116 and NAFs co-inoculation with intratumoral injection of 100 µg N-Exo; (4) CRC+NAFs+H-Exo: HCT116 and NAFs co-inoculation with intratumoral injection of 100 µg H-Exo. Injections were performed three times per week for a total of four weeks. In addition, HCT116 cells and differentially treated NAFs were co-inoculated subcutaneously and randomly divided into four groups (n=6): (1) Ctrl: CRC alone; (2) NAFs (H-Exo): HCT116 co-inoculated with H-Exo-treated NAFs; (3) NAFs (si-HIF1A-AS2-Exo): HCT116 co-inoculated with si-HIF1A-AS2-Exo-treated NAFs; (4) NAFs (miR-33 mimics): HCT116 co-inoculated with miR-33 mimic-transfected NAFs. Tumor length (L) and width (W) were measured every three days, and tumor volume (V) was calculated as V=L×W²/2. Blood was collected from the orbital venous plexus of anesthetized nude mice, and serum was separated. The nude mice were euthanized, and the entire tumor tissue was removed, weighed, and photographed. Tumor specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and paraffin-embedded, or snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee.


### Tissue and serum detection
  Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed on xenograft tissues to assess the expression of VEGF, MVD (CD31), MMP-9, α-SMA, FAP, and PDGFRβ (see Supplementary Methods). ELISA kits were used to detect the levels of angiogenic factors such as VEGF (Elabscience; Cat. No. E-EL-M1292), bFGF (Cat. No. E-EL-M0170), PDGF (Cat. No. E-EL-M0632), and HGF (Cat. No. E-EL-M3085) in the peripheral serum of mice.


### Quantitative Reverse Transcription PCR (qRT-PCR)
  Total RNA was extracted from cell and tumor tissue samples using TRIzol reagent. After reverse transcription of the extracted RNA into cDNA, the ABIPRISM 7500 system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) was used in conjunction with TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ III (Invitrogen) for quantitative detection of mRNA levels. Relative expression levels of target mRNAs and lncRNAs were normalized to GAPDH, whereas miRNA expression levels were normalized to U6. The specific primers used in the experiments are listed in Table S1.


### Western blot
  Tissues and cells were lysed using RIPA lysis buffer, and total protein was extracted. After quantification with the BCA kit, SDS-PAGE electrophoresis was performed. The proteins were transferred to a membrane and incubated with the corresponding primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. After washing with TBST, the membrane was incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature, followed by ECL detection. Exposure and imaging were performed using a Bio-Rad ChemiDox XR gel imaging system. β-actin or GAPDH was used as the internal reference, and the relative expression intensity of each protein was analyzed using ImageJ software.


### Evaluation of direct exosomal effects on CRC cells and HUVECs
  To assess the direct effects of tumor-derived exosomes independent of NAF-CM, HCT116 cells were treated with N-Exo or H-Exo at the indicated concentration for 48 h, followed by proliferation/colony formation assays, Transwell migration/invasion assays, and Western blot analysis of pathway markers (HIF-1α, NOTCH1, p-ERK, VEGF, MMP-7, and MMP-9). For endothelial assays, HUVECs were treated with N-Exo or H-Exo under the same conditions, and angiogenic phenotypes were evaluated by tube formation assays. Levels of angiogenic factors (e.g., VEGF [Elabscience; Cat. No. E-EL-H0111] and ANGPT2 [Elabscience; Cat. No. E-EL-H0008]) and the endothelial marker CD31 were assessed by ELISA and Western blot, respectively.


### Secretome analysis for H-Exo-CM
  The secretome of H-Exo-CM derived from CRC cells was profiled using DIA-based LC–MS/MS, enabling robust protein identification and quantification (Supplementary Methods and Table S16).


### Statistical analysis
  Statistical significance between two groups was determined using a two-tailed Student’s t-test. Differences among three or more groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Data involving two independent variables were evaluated using two-way ANOVA followed by Šídák’s multiple comparisons test. Analyses were conducted using SPSS 22.0 and GraphPad Prism 9.5. scRNA-seq and additional multi-omics data were analyzed using R software (version 4.3.0) as described in the Supplementary Methods. P values were corrected for multiple testing with the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) method when needed. P<0.05 was considered significant.
