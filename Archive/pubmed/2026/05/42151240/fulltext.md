# Effects of guaiane sesquiterpenoids from the invasive species Ambrosia trifida on inflammatory bowel disease by targeting JAK2/STAT3 signaling


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50021-3.


## Introduction
  The genus Ambrosia belongs to the Compositae family and comprises approximately 50 species, of which Ambrosia trifida is one of the most abundant1. A. trifida, commonly known as giant ragweed, is an annual invasive weed native to North America that inhabits riversides and agricultural lands. The plant invades cultivated fields and reduces crop productivity, causing serious damage to the ecosystem2. It particularly disrupts the growth and development of several crops, including wheat, corn, and soybeans3. Furthermore, the pollen produced by giant ragweed causes diverse allergic responses in humans. In Europe, approximately 13.5 million people are allergic to Ambrosia species, which results in an estimated annual economic loss of €7.4 billion4. A. trifida is believed to have been introduced to South Korea during the Korean War and was listed as one of the most harmful invasive alien plants by the Ministry of the Environment of the Republic of Korea in 19995. Although this plant is generally controlled using herbicides, this approach is costly and likely to cause several environmental problems, such as bioaccumulation. Despite numerous studies focusing on the distribution of this plant and the ecological problems caused by it, investigations into its phytochemical composition have received limited attention. Recently, a biological study of the extract of A. trifida revealed its anti-inflammatory activity6. Chemical investigations of this plant have revealed sesquiterpenes and polyacetylenes7. Sesquiterpenes are of special interest because of their various biological activities, including antitumor, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and antiviral activities8. Notably, hydroxycolorenone, which is a guaiane-type sesquiterpene, exhibits anti-inflammatory activity by inhibiting NO production9. In this study, three sesquiterpenes, including one new guaiane sesquiterpenoid (1) and two stereoisomers of 11-hydroxy-4-guaien-3-one (2 and 3), were isolated from the whole plant of A. trifida, and their structures were analyzed using spectroscopic methods.

  Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), which includes ulcerative colitis and Crohn’s disease, is a chronic intestinal disorder characterized by gastrointestinal inflammation of unknown etiology10. Although the exact cause of IBD remains unknown, the main causes are immunodeficiency and infection. In particular, inflammation caused by these factors destroys intestinal tight junction (TJ) proteins and increases paracellular permeability to microorganisms and antigens, resulting in the loss of transmembrane proteins, such as occludin and claudin11–13. TJ permeability is regulated by the expression of TJ regulatory proteins, such as mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and claudin 4, which regulate the formation of the actin cytoskeleton. In particular, claudin 4 is expressed in luminal epithelial cells with normal barriers and has been reported to promote the recovery of the barriers damaged by inflammation14,15. In addition, scholars have reported that various cytokines, such as IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β, are directly involved in intestinal inflammation. Moreover, increased levels of these cytokines have been observed in experimental colitis models and actual IBD patients16. Various drugs, such as 5-aminosalicylic acid, steroids, and immunosuppressants, have been used to treat IBD. However, the long-term use of these drugs causes serious side effects17. Therefore, research is currently underway to develop relatively safe treatment methods and therapeutic agents with fewer side effects by modulating cytokine responses in intestinal immune and stromal cells through the regulation of Janus kinase and the signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK-STAT) signaling18. In IBD, aberrant activation of the JAK2/STAT3 pathway is persistent. It causes inflammation and damage to the epithelial barrier, and in particular, persistent activation of STAT3 accelerates the expression of inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and tissue damage to the epithelial barrier19,20.

  In this study, we predicted the potential biological activities of three guaiane sesquiterpenes (1–3) for IBD by performing network pharmacology analysis. Based on the identified targets, we investigated the effect of the active compound on the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway. Herein, the isolation, structural identification, and network pharmacology-based biological assessments conducted in this study are presented.

## Results and discussion

### Network pharmacology analysis
  Network pharmacology analysis provides insights into the complex relationships between drugs and diseases by predicting complicated network interactions that affect the therapeutic effects of drugs24. In this study, we employed network pharmacology to investigate whether the isolated compounds were associated with IBD, and the underlying mechanisms involved. Assessment of drug-likeness for guaiane sesquiterpenes (1 − 3) using SwissADME revealed that the selected compounds showed no violations of either Lipinski’s or Veber’s rules (Fig. S28). A total of 258 potential targets of the isolates were obtained from three databases, and after deleting the duplicate targets, a Venn diagram of the intersection targets was created (Fig. S29). The search for IBD targets was performed with GeneCards using the keywords “inflammatory bowel disease” (relevance score ≥ 5). After the deletion of false-positive targets, 674 potential disease-related targets were identified. To analyze overlapping targets for both the compounds and disease, a Venn diagram was created, and 52 common targets were obtained (Fig. 3A), leading to the hypothesis that the isolated compounds may have biological effects on IBD via these potential targets. A corresponding PPI network was obtained for further identification of the core targets after importing the common 52 genes into the STRING database. The minimum required interaction score was set at the highest confidence score (0.09). After removing the unconnected nodes, the PPI network, which contained 31 nodes and 84 edges, was analyzed, and the average node degree of the network was calculated to be 5.42. The final visualization was performed using Cytoscape (Fig. 3B). A total of 31 targets were identified, and the top eight core targets (degree > 8) were STAT3, PTPN11, IL6, JAK1, PIK3CD, EGFR, JAK2, and PIK3CA, which represent the potential targets for the pharmacological activity of the isolated compounds against IBD. The following screening conditions were used: degree value ≥ 9, BC ≥ 0.02, and CC ≥ 0.4. The gene names, BC, CC, and degree values of the potential targets are listed in Table 2.

### Evaluation of effective compounds through screening and cytotoxicity
  To validate the results predicted by network pharmacology and molecular docking analysis, screening was performed to evaluate the efficacy of the three isolated compounds against IBD. Among compounds 1–3, only 2 showed a downregulation effect (approximately 46%) on the major inflammatory cytokine IL-1β of IBD in Caco-2 cells stimulated with T/I) (Fig. 5A). Therefore, cytotoxicity evaluation was performed to confirm the pharmacological activity of 2 on IBD. To evaluate the efficacy of 2 on IBD, the cytotoxicity evaluation of 2 was performed in Caco-2 cells. Caco-2 cells were cultured and treated with 2. Subsequently, the morphological changes and viability of cells were evaluated after 24 h of culture. Within the indicated concentration range, 2 did not show any morphological changes in the cells over a period of 24 h (Fig. 5B). Both the cell counting and cell viability analyses indicated that 2 did not show any toxicity toward Caco-2 cells (Fig. 5C).

### TJ-induced protein regulation and anti-inflammatory effects of compound 2
  In IBD mouse models and patients, significant expression of IL-1β, a major inflammatory cytokine in IBD, is observed26. In addition, IL-6 and TNF-α are known to play an important role in mediating inflammatory responses in IBD. In particular, IL-10 is known to play an important role in suppressing and fighting the inflammation caused by these inflammatory cytokines27. In addition, in this inflammatory response process, the inflammatory cytokines upregulate the expression of myosin light-chain kinase (MLCK), thereby inducing the destruction of intestinal tissue28. In this study, compound 2 significantly reduced the gene expression of il-1β and il-6, which increased with T/I treatment in Caco-2 cells. Furthermore, il-10 expression was significantly increased compared to the T/I alone group, but no clear dose-dependent increase was observed in the 10 µM and 20 µM treatment groups (Fig. 6A). Previous studies have demonstrated that IL-10 does not always exhibit a linear dose-response relationship but rather is regulated context-dependently depending on experimental conditions and the cellular environment, suggesting that feedback control mechanisms may be involved29. This suggests that IL-10 expression regulation may be influenced by a complex regulatory network that cannot be explained solely by concentration dependence. Compound 2 downregulated the expression of IL-1β protein, a major inflammatory cytokine in IBD, while suppressing the expression of MLCK (Fig. 6B). Thus, the results confirmed the inhibitory effect of 2 on the major inflammatory cytokines of IBD in Caco-2 cells stimulated with T/I. In addition, the experiments with TJ proteins CLDN 4 and OCLN revealed that 2 up-regulated the expression of these proteins in a time- and concentration-dependent manner (Figs. 6C and D). Thus, compound 2 shows a therapeutic effect on IBD by regulating TJ as well as inflammatory cytokines.

### Restoration of the damaged actin cytoskeleton structure of tj proteins by compound 2
  In a previous study, we confirmed the anti-inflammatory effects of compound 2 and the regulation and expression of TJ proteins in Caco-2 cells. In this study, we aimed to confirm the effect of 2 on the loss of TJ proteins in T/I-stimulated Caco-2 cells. The results confirmed the restoration of the expression levels of the TJ proteins CLDN4 and OCLN lost by T/I stimulation (Fig. 7A). In addition, by analyzing the gene levels of CLDN4 and OCLN, we found that 2 restored the gene levels lost because of T/I stimulation (Fig. 7B). These TJ proteins play an important role in determining the permeability of the barrier and maintaining the function of the cell wall by inducing an association with the actin cytoskeleton30. The results showed that 2 induced the recovery of the TJ protein CLDN4, which was lost by T/I stimulation, and approximately 61% of the actin cytoskeleton structure lost by T/I was restored (Fig. 7C). These results confirm that 2 is involved not only in regulating inflammation but also in TJ homeostasis. Thus, it plays a key role in inhibiting inflammation, which is a major treatment strategy for IBD, and in restoring the barrier function. The expression of inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF and IL-1β, which are characteristic of IBD patients, has been shown to induce the activation of MLC. MLC is known to regulate intestinal permeability, thereby weakening the intestinal barrier structure. In addition, it is regulated by NF-kB and MAPK signaling pathways30. MAPK signaling is mediated in various cells and is involved in various diseases. Scholars have reported that MAPK activation induces IBD31. Therefore, we investigated whether NF-kB and MAPK signaling pathways mediate between the inflammation suppression and TJ protein regulation effects of 2. We found that compound 2 downregulated the phosphorylation levels of NF-kB and MAPK, which were increased by T/I stimulation in Caco-2 cells (Figs. 7D and E).

### Inhibitory effect of compound 2 on IBD through STAT3/JAK2 regulation
  The target STAT3/JAK2 signaling pathway discovered by network pharmacology was predicted to have the anti-inflammatory and TJ protein regulation effects of compound 2 (Fig. 3). The JAK-STAT pathway has been shown to play an important role in the regulation of cytokine action in several diseases and is directly related to pathology32. Therefore, small-molecule JAK inhibitors, such as tofacitinib and upadacitinib, have been used to treat IBD with proven efficacy; however, they are known to have serious side effects, including cardiovascular deterioration and an increased risk of developing cancer33–35. The results confirmed that 2 downregulates the expression of phosphorylated proteins of the STAT3/JAK2 target used in network pharmacology in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 8A). Furthermore, the anti-inflammatory effects of 2 were compared using AG490, a JAK2 inhibitor, as a positive control. While 2 did not reduce STAT3/JAK2 phosphorylated protein expression as effectively as AG490, it exhibited a significant inhibitory effect compared to T/I stimulators (Fig. S30). Furthermore, the IC50 values ​​of the two substances were measured, and 2 was approximately 130.5 µM, while AG490 was 32.5 µM, demonstrating physiological activity within the IC50 values (Fig. S31). To investigate the effect of 2 on previously demonstrated anti-inflammatory and TJ regulatory proteins by suppressing the expression of STAT3/JAK2 phosphorylated proteins, the signaling pathway was silenced with STAT3 siRNA. We confirmed that the previously shown IL-1β and MLCK protein inhibition effects of 2 were reversed when the signaling pathway was silenced with STAT3 siRNA. In addition, the TJ regulatory proteins CLDN4 and OCLN also reversed the previously shown results (Figs. 8B and C). Furthermore, we examined the effect of 2 on the recovery of CLDN4 and the actin cytoskeleton (lost barrier structure) by STAT3 silencing. As discussed earlier, 2 could recover the actin cytoskeleton lost by T/I. The recovery effect was reduced by approximately 34% after STAT3 silencing (Fig. 8D). These results suggest that the regulation of STAT3/JAK2 signaling induces the anti-inflammatory and TJ protein regulatory effects of 2. Thus, compound 2 could exert IBD regulatory effects on the targets found through actual network pharmacology.

## Conclusion
  The in vitro effects of the isolates 1−3 on IBD were evaluated based on network pharmacology analysis. Among them, (1S,7R,10R)-11-hydroxy-4-guaien-3-one (2) exhibited significant activity, whereas its (1R,7R,10S)-diastereomer (3) was inactive. These results underscore the critical role of stereochemistry in determining biological activity, highlighting the importance of structure–activity relationships in stereoisomeric compounds. These findings suggest that 2 may have the potential to be developed as a new chemical scaffold for IBD treatment. However, further in vivo validation and comprehensive pharmacological studies will be essential to establish its efficacy and safety. Moreover, this study may contribute to establishing a foundation for the beneficial use of this plant, which is known worldwide as an invasive species and causes diverse problems in agricultural fields.


## Materials and methods

### General experimental procedures
  Thin layer chromatography was performed on glass plates pre-coated with silica gel 60 F254 and RP-18 F254 (20 × 20 cm, 200 μm, 60 Å, Merck). Optical rotations were recorded on a Jasco P-2000 automatic digital polarimeter (Jasco, Tokyo, Japan). A UV-1900i ultraviolet–visible (UV-Vis) spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) was used to record the UV-Vis spectra of the specimens. Electronic circular dichroism (ECD) experiments were performed using a J-1700 circular dichroism spectrometer (Jasco, Tokyo, Japan). Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy analysis was performed on a VERTEX 80v spectrometer (Bruker Optics, Germany). Vacuum-liquid chromatography (VLC) was conducted using silica gel (70 − 230 mesh, Merck). Medium-pressure liquid chromatography (MPLC) was performed using a Biotage® Selekt apparatus equipped with a reversed-phase C18 (Biotage® Sfär C18 Duo 100 Å, 30 μm 120 g, Uppsala, Sweden) cartridge. High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) was performed on a Shimadzu HPLC system (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) with Hector M C18 (250 × 10 mm, 5 μm; RStech, Korea) and Thermo Polar Advantage II C18 5 μm (250 × 10 mm; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) columns at a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min. The separated fractions were then detected under UV light at wavelengths of 210, 254, and 365 nm using a Shimadzu SPD-M20A PDA detector. Preparative HPLC was conducted on a Gilson PLC2020 system (Gilson, WI, USA) with Hector M C18 (250 × 21.2 mm, 5 μm; RStech, Korea) and Thermo Polar Advantage II C18 5 μm (250 × 21.2 mm; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) columns at a flow rate of 5–8 mL/min. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) was performed to determine the structure. The 1H- and 13C-NMR spectra were recorded on Bruker Avance Neo 400 & Avance III 600 (1H; 400 and 600 MHz, and 13C; 100 and 125 MHz, Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA) spectrometers. High-resolution electrospray ionization mass spectrometry was performed using a 1290 Infinity II (Agilent, USA) HPLC system and a hybrid quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer (TripleTOF 5600 plus, SCIEX, Canada).


### Plant material
  The whole plant of A. trifida was collected from Yuseong-gu, Deajeon, Republic of Korea (N 36°25’26.33124’’ E 127°24’32.2884’’) in July 2023. Since A. trifida is designated as an invasive alien species by the Ministry of Environment of Korea, no specific permission was required for its collection. Based on the distinctive morphological characteristics, such as palmately divided leaves with 3–5 deep lobes and plant height of 1.2–1.5 m (Figs. S32 and S33), the species was identified as A. trifida by Prof. MinKyun Na. A voucher specimen (CNU202307) was deposited at the Laboratory of Pharmacognosy, College of Pharmacy, Chungnam National University, Daejeon, Korea.


### Extraction and isolation
  The dried whole plants of A. trifida (13.0 kg) were extracted with 95% EtOH (108 L) at room temperature and refluxed at 80 °C with 95% EtOH (98 L) (each for 2 h) to obtain an EtOH extract (600.0 g). The EtOH extract (550.0 g) was suspended in water and successively partitioned with n-hexane. A portion of the H2O extract (320.0 g) was subjected to VLC (20 × 20 cm) and eluted with n-hexane/EtOAc (100:0, 75:25, 50:50, and 25:75; 2 L for each step) and EtOAc/MeOH (100:0, 75:25, 50:50, and 25:75; 4 L for each step; final wash with 2 L of 100% MeOH) to yield five fractions (AT1–AT5). Fraction AT1 (2.0 g) was separated by MPLC (Biotage Sfar C18, 120 g) with a stepwise gradient of MeOH-H2O (5:5, 7:3, 8:2, MeOH 100%) to yield five fractions (AT1.1–AT1.5). Compound 2 (tR = 27.6 min, 34.5 mg) was purified from fraction AT1.4 (454.1 mg) by preparative HPLC [Thermo Polar Advantage II C18 5 μm (250 × 21.2 mm)] using a mobile phase of MeCN/H2O (30:70 for 100 min, 7 mL/min). Fraction AT1.4.4 (150.0 mg) was further separated by preparative HPLC [Hector C18 - M 5 μm (250 × 21.2 mm)] using a mobile phase of MeCN/H2O (32:68 for 80 min, 8 mL/min) to afford 3 (tR = 28.0 min, 108.0 mg). Fraction AT2 (8.0 g) was chromatographed over reversed-phase MPLC (Biotage Sfar C18, 120 g) with a stepwise gradient of MeOH-H2O (5:5, 7:3, 8:2, 10:0, MeOH 100%) to yield five fractions (AT2.1-AT2.5). Fraction AT2.3 (345.9 mg) was separated by preparative HPLC [Phenomenex Biphenyl C18 5 μm (250 × 21.2 mm)] using MeCN/H2O (17:83 for 100 min, 5 mL/min) to obtain fraction AT2.3.1 (12.3 mg). Fraction AT2.3.1 (12.3 mg) was further subjected to preparative HPLC [Hector C18 - M 5 μm (250 × 10.0 mm)] with MeOH/H2O (45:55 for 50 min, 3 mL/min) to isolate 1 (tR = 18.7 min, 3.5 mg).


### ECD calculations
  The absolute configurations of 1 and 2 were determined using time-dependent density functional theory (TDDFT) calculations. The theoretical ECD calculations were performed using the TDDFT method at the B3LYP/6-311 + level in the MeOH solution36. The ECD curves were generated by SpecDis v1.71 software with a half-band of 0.3 eV based on the Boltzmann weighting of each conformer. The experimental ECD spectrum of compounds 1–3 was recorded at a concentration of 0.1 mg/mL.

### Collection of isolated compounds and IBD-related target proteins
  ChemDraw Professional 14.0 was used to draw the stereo-structures of guaiane sesquiterpenes 1−3 and to obtain their SMILES. The SwissADME online service was used to assess the drug-likeness of the isolated compounds based on the Lipinski and Veber rules. SwissTargetPrediction (https://www.swissta.rgetprediction.ch/), polypharmacology browser 2 (PPB2; https://ppb2.gdb.tools/), and Super-Pred (https://prediction.charite.de/) were used to predict the potential targets of these sesquiterpenes. Universal Protein Database (UniProt; https://www.uniprot.org/) was used to transform the target protein name into the gene name. The GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/) was used to identify the potential targets for IBD.


### Screening core genes and hub genes
  The potential targets for IBD were matched with those for the isolated compounds 1−3 using a Venn diagram. Common targets were uploaded to the STRING platform (https://string-db.org) to construct a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network and to identify the core targets. In this study, the species was limited to “Homo sapiens,” the lowest interaction score was set to the highest confidence level (0.09), the discrete targets were hidden, and the remaining parameters remained at the default settings. Subsequently, the PPI network obtained from the STRING database was visualized and further analyzed using Cytoscape v3.10.2. The core genes were screened according to betweenness centrality (BC), closeness centrality (CC), and degree values. Then, a compound-disease-target-pathway visual analysis was performed using Cytoscape software.


### Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathway annotation
  The Bioinformatics (http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/) database was used for GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses to explore the biological functions and pathways of each target further.


### Preparation of protein/ligand for molecular docking
  In the present study, the three-dimensional (3D) structures of STAT3 and JAK2 were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org) with the PDB IDs 6NJS and 2B7A, respectively. Then, the 3D structures of three guaiane sesquiterpenes 1–3 were constructed using ChemBio3D Ultra 14.0 software. The target proteins were prepared by removing the water molecules using PyMOL. The docking parameters were set up using the graphical user interface of AutoDock Tools37. The preparation of the target protein structure involved the addition of hydrogen atoms and formal charges. The number of rotatable bonds and the determination of the root for the ligand were set to default. Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock 4.2. The grid maps for the target proteins embedded in a 3D grid of manually set parameters were generated using the AutoGrid 4 program of AutoDock. The binding energies between the target protein and ligand were calculated by running the AutoDock 4 program using the preset grid maps. The results were visualized using PyMOL and Discovery Studio 2024 Client. The binding energies for various ligand conformations with the target proteins were determined, and the best conformation was chosen based on the binding energy and number of hydrogen bonds that they formed with the protein.


### Cell culture
  Human colorectal adenocarcinoma Caco-2 cells were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, VA, USA). The cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Wellgene, Korea) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (Wellgene, Korea), 1% (v/v) L-glutamine, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.


### Cell viability and confluency
  Caco-2 cells (5 × 104 cells/mL) were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated for 48 h at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. To evaluate the cell viability, the cells were exposed to varying concentrations of compound 2 for 6 h. MTT solution (5 mg/mL) was added to each well (50 µL per well), and the plates were incubated for 4 h. After incubation, the supernatant was removed, and 100 µL of dimethyl sulfoxide was added to dissolve the formazan crystals. The absorbance was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader (Tecan Trading AG, Männedorf, Switzerland). For the confluency analysis, Caco-2 cells (5 × 104 cells/mL cells/well) were seeded in 96-well plates and treated with 2 (concentrations equivalent to the viability assay results) for 24 h. The cell confluency was monitored using an Incucyte® live-cell analysis system (Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany). The confluency data were expressed as percentages relative to the untreated control. All experiments were performed in triplicate to ensure reliability and reproducibility.


### Western blot analysis
  Caco-2 cells were plated in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells/mL and cultured for 48 h. Proteins were extracted using a radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors on ice for 30 min. The lysates were then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C. The protein concentrations were determined using the Bradford assay (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Equal amounts of protein (50 µg) were loaded onto a sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gel, separated, and then transferred onto PVDF membranes (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) nonfat dried milk dissolved in a Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween® 20 detergent (TBST) buffer (10 mM Tris, pH 8.0, and 150 mM NaCl) at room temperature for 1 h. After blocking, the membranes were washed with TBST for 30 min and were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary monoclonal antibodies. The next day, the membranes were washed again with TBST and incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies for 3–4 h. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL Western blotting detection system, and quantification was performed using ImageJ software, followed by statistical analysis.


### Small interfering RNA transfection
  Caco-2 cells were transfected with STAT3 siRNA (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, CA, USA) at a concentration of 30–60 nM using lipofectamine RNAiMAX reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). When the cells reached 50% confluence, a mixture of siRNA and Lipofectamine RNAiMAX reagent was added. The medium was replaced, and 6 h post-transfection, the cells were treated with compound 2, as indicated. This step was followed by stimulation with TNF-α + IFN-γ (T/I) after 24 h.


### Immunofluorescence staining
  The Caco-2 cells were cultured on coverslips in 12-well plates for 48 h. The cells were treated with 2, as indicated, and stimulated with T/I after 6 h. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature, followed by permeabilization with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 min at room temperature. Then, the cells were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin for 1 h and incubated overnight with a primary antibody against CLDN4 (1:1000) at 4 °C. After washing with PBS, the cells were incubated with a secondary antibody conjugated with Alexa Fluor 488 (1:500) for 4 h in the dark at room temperature.


### RT-qPCR
  Caco-2 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well and incubated for 48 h at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. The Caco-2 cells were treated with compound 2 at concentrations ranging from 0 to 40 µM and were then stimulated with T/I for 24 h. The total RNA from the Caco-2 cells treated with 2 was extracted using a TRIzol reagent (Bio Science Technology, Daegu, Republic of Korea) and was then quantified using a NanoDrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The expression levels of each synthesized cDNA were measured using TOPscript™ RT DryMIX (dT18 plus, Enzynomics, Daejeon, Republic of Korea). Subsequently, PCR was performed using a CFX Connect system (Bio-Rad, California, USA). The target markers used were IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, CLDN4, and OCLN, and the primer sequences are listed in Table 4.
