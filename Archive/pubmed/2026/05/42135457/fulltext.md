# Discovery of small molecule inhibitors of liver X receptor for pediatric metabolic disorders by structure based screening, modelling, dft analysis and MD simulation


## Abstract
  Metabolic disorders such as dyslipidemia and cholesterol-related diseases in pediatric populations have become a major concern, highlighting the need for effective therapeutic strategies. Liver X Receptor (LXR) is one of the key factors that control lipid metabolism and can be used as the treatment target. The aim of this study was to discover possible LXR inhibitors to use in pediatric metabolic disorders through an in-silico method, which combines molecular modeling, quantum chemical analysis, and molecular dynamics simulations. Molecular docking was performed to screen compounds from the ChEMBL database and quantum chemical analysis to determine electronic properties of the screened compounds. MD simulations over 100 ns confirmed the stability of the protein–ligand complex, supported by consistent RMSD, RMSF, and hydrogen bonding profiles. DFT analysis further indicated suitable electronic properties for receptor interaction. ADMET predictions suggested favorable pharmacokinetic and toxicity profiles. However, experimental validation is required to confirm the biological activity and therapeutic potential of the identified compounds. This study provides a computational framework for the discovery of LXR inhibitors targeting pediatric metabolic disorders.


## Introduction
  Pediatric metabolic syndrome has become a significant international health issue, representing the coexistence of obesity, dyslipidemia, insulin resistance, and hypertension at an early age. These endocrine–metabolic alterations predispose individuals to, and promote the development of type 2 diabetes, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and cardiovascular disease in adulthood in addition to increasingly manifesting in pediatric endocrine practice as complex and multifactorial disorders requiring mechanism-based rather than symptomatic management1–3. It is in this context that nuclear receptors involved in lipid and glucose homeostasis have become a target of therapeutic attention with the liver X receptors (LXRs, LXRalpha/NR1H3 and LXRb/NR1H2) being particularly interesting because of their central roles in cholesterol efflux, de novo lipogenesis and inflammatory signaling in metabolic tissues including liver, adipose tissue and macrophages4–6. LXRs are endogenous oxysterols and synthetic ligand-activated transcription factors that regulate the expression of genes like ABCA1, ABCG1, SREBP 1c and thus are involved in the coupling of cholesterol management to triglyceride synthesis7. Even though most of the clinical and preclinical studies have been done on adult population, pediatric data reveal that the pathophysiological elements of metabolic syndrome, central obesity, dyslipidemia and compromised fasting glucose are already present in school going children and adolescents globally, with the need to implement early and specific pharmacological measures.

  The first efforts at drug discovery on LXRs focused on agonists to take advantage of their athero-protective and cholesterol lowering effects, but systemic LXR activation was always associated with hepatic steatosis and hypertriglyceridemia, and could not be translated clinically6,8. It has moved the spotlight on to LXR antagonists and inverse agonists that inhibit the expression of lipogenic target genes and retain or decouple positive effects on lipid homeostasis and inflammation6,7. Recent efforts have found potent LXR inverse agonists, including SR9238, SR9243 and newer chemotypes, that bind the ligand binding domain, increase corepressor recruitment and effective down regulation of de novo lipogenesis genes that ameliorates metabolic associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) and dyslipidemia in preclinical models9,10. Parallel programs have identified a broad array of natural products of both terrestrial and marine origin that exist as either LXR agonists or antagonists, and this has been used to widen the chemotypes available, as well as emphasize the structural diversity that can fit into the LXR binding pocket. The structural biology can address several LXR2 and LXR3 ligand binding domain complexes with synthetic ligands to give an atomic level understanding of the agonist and modulator binding modes which can be utilized to design inhibitors11. Despite these developments, the majority of LXR-directed programs have failed to specifically consider the application in pediatrics and the safety issues surrounding the global nuclear receptor modulation, off target endocrine side effects, and long-term metabolic programming have not been adequately discussed in the context of growing children.

  Simultaneously, computational drug design has been progressing fast including the pharmacophore modelling, structure based virtual screening, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations and quantum chemical methods to aid in the identification of hits and optimization of leads in both metabolic and endocrine targets12,13. Artificial intelligence, and machine learning have also enhanced the speed of antidiabetic and metabolic drug discovery as chemical space may quickly be explored and the pharmacokinetics and toxicity profiles of novel drugs predicted, saving time and money14,15. Pharmacophore based virtual screening, docking, and MD have been used in silico to identify agonists or modulators to LXRs, and several campaigns have been used to discover orally effective LXR ligands to metabolic and inflammatory diseases. However, the current computationally-based efforts have focused on agonist discovery, general ligand profiling or adult-orientated indications, and little has been done in terms of comprehensive workflows that integrate pharmacophore based virtual screening, all atom MD, binding free energy estimation, quantum chemical calculations and in silico ADMET/toxicity assessment to design an LXR inhibitor expressly with metabolic disease in mind. This leaves a crucial gap in the rational design of LXR antagonists and inverse agonists, which have not only high binding affinity and selectivity but also have a good safety profile to be used in children with metabolic syndrome, obesity, and early NAFLD6,16.

  The current research has filled this gap by concentrating on in-silico discovery of liver X receptor inhibitors to pediatric metabolic diseases through an integrated approach of molecular modelling, quantum chemical and molecular dynamics methodology. The hypothesis was that virtual screening of various chemical libraries using structure-directed pharmacophore-based screening, followed by rigorous docking, MD and MM/GBSA binding free energy examination would reveal new LXR ligands with antagonist or inverse agonist properties and acceptable predicted pharmacokinetics and toxicities that can be used in pediatrics6,17. Moreover, it was hypothesized that quantum chemical (density functional theory, DFT) modeling would further develop the insight into the important electronic characteristics that regulate LXR binding and aid the segregation of agonistic and inhibitory chemotypes of closely related scaffolds18. The overall aims of this work were thus to (i) derive and test a receptor and ligand-based pharmacophore model of LXR inhibition, (ii) conduct virtual screening to rank candidate inhibitors, (iii) characterize binding modes and dynamics of top hits using molecular docking, MD simulations and MM/GBSA analysis and (iv) determine the in silico ADMET and toxicity profiles of top hits with emphasis on parameters based on long term pediatric use.

  To attain these objectives, high resolution LXR ligand binding domain structures, creation and validation of a pharmacophore model, and pharmacophore mediated virtual screening of small molecule libraries were used as a multi-level computational pipeline to achieve the desired objectives. The top ranked hits were then subjected to MM/GBSA and molecular docking to estimate binding affinities and prioritize compounds to further study, with Quantum chemical calculations at the DFT level done to explore electronic descriptors and interaction energies of the selected compounds, in silico ADMET and toxicity analysis done to predict oral bioavailability, metabolic stability, off target liabilities, and safety endpoints of pediatric metabolic diseases. In addition, large scale MD simulations were conducted to determine complex stability, conformational changes in the receptor activation purpose region as well as the key protein-ligand interactions over time.


## Methodology

### Structure-Based Pharmacophore Generation
  The crystal structure of liver X receptor (LXR) (PDB ID: 6S4N) was obtained from the Protein Data Bank and imported into Maestro19 to be preprocessed. The structure was first optimized by adding missing hydrogen atoms, optimization of protonation states and reorganization of the hydrogen-bond network. This was followed by restrained minimization to minimize any steric clashes without changing the overall conformation of the backbone. AlphaFold220 was employed to model and complete the receptor to deal with any missing loops in the structure. The Phase module was used to create a structure-based pharmacophore model. The ligand-binding domain key residues were manually located based on past studies on LXR-ligand interactions21. The pharmacophoric characteristics of hydrogen bond donors, acceptors, hydrophobic regions, and metal-binding motifs were produced after phase and included in the virtual screening procedure to find potential LXR inhibitors.


### Virtual Screening of Compound Library
  The virtual screening was performed with a compound library of 25,083 small molecules obtained through the ChEMBL database. The compounds were prepared with the Phase database preparation module22 to make sure that the screening was prepared appropriately. This step consisted of the computation of several low-energy conformers of each compound in the Epik module23 that generated no fewer than twenty conformers per molecule to get a complete sampling of the available molecular shapes. It was also done by considering pH-dependent ionization states, which guaranteed proper treatment of compounds in physiological conditions, whereas tautomers that were energetically unfavorable were eliminated. After the preparations of the compounds were made, virtual screening was then conducted based on the pharmacophore model through the LXR structure. Phase module was used to give a match between the compounds and the pharmacophore features such as hydrogen-bond donors, hydrophobic and other important interactions. The compounds were further ranked according to the Phase screen score which measures the fit of the compounds to the pharmacophore model. This score is a composite of several variables such as matching vectors, matching sites, steric complementarity, and root-mean-square- Deviation (RMSD) of aligned features. The hits that had a Phase screen score ≥ 1.4 were subject to further analysis.


### Molecular docking studies
  The crystal structure of liver X receptor (LXR) (PDB ID: 6S4N)24 prepared in the pharmacophore modelling step was further subjected to the preparation of molecular docking by using the Maestro Protein Preparation Wizard. The preparation procedure comprised three stages namely, preprocessing, optimization, and minimization. In preprocessing, missing hydrogen atoms, bond orders, alternative conformations, and removal of non-essential heteroatoms were performed. Protonation and tautomeric forms of ionizable residues were attributed with the use of PROPKA at pH 7.025. Hydrogen bonding pattern was then optimized and restrained energy minimization of the LXR structure was carried out with the help of the OPLS force field to avoid steric clashes26. Receptor grid was created using the co-crystal ligand of the LXR binding site with the grid center coordinates being X = 0.87, Y = 5.65 and Z = 5.39, which was selected to undergo site-specific docking. The hits identified following the screening procedure were synthesized by LigPrep27 with correct stereochemistry, protonation state, and low-energy conformers produced and formed in physiological pH. These ready ligands were docked in the LXR binding site by employing SP mode of Glide tool28. The outcome of docking was evaluated using the glide scores which indicate the affinity of the binding of each compound to the receptor.


### Drug-likeness and toxicity profiles assessment
  The drug-likeness of the selected compounds was assessed using the SwissADME29, which applies standard drug-likeness rules established by Lipinski, Veber, Ghose, and Egan to evaluate the physicochemical properties of the compounds. Major features including molecular weight, topological polar surface area (TPSA), lipophilicity (logP), and the number of hydrogen bond donor/acceptors were identified to make the compounds meet the requirements of an orally active small molecule. Also, the degree to which the compounds conformed to the guidelines of the known categories of small molecule drugs was considered to predict the drug-like potential. In the case of toxicity prediction, the ProTox-II server30 was used to analyze the possible risk of hepatotoxicity, mutagenicity and cardiotoxicity among other safety endpoints. This extensive screening was to determine compounds that had favorable pharmacokinetic properties and satisfactory safety margins to be subjected to further experimental study.


### Computational analysis of electronic properties
  DFT analysis was performed to explore electronic characteristics of the chosen LXR inhibitor candidates. Geometry optimization and frontier molecular orbital (FMO) analysis were done using Gaussian 09 W31 with B3LYP/6–311 + G (d, p) level of theory which is appropriate in the evaluation of the reactivity of small molecules. The overall reactivity indicators were the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy values of the final wavefunctions. The optimized structures and orbital distributions had been visualized with the help of Gauss View 6.0.


### Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations
  To further understand the stability and dynamics of the chosen LXR inhibitors candidates in the receptor binding site, the stability and dynamics of these inhibitors were analyzed using molecular dynamics (MD) simulation of 100 ns with GROMACS32. The protein-ligand complex was prepared using CHARMM-GUI33 and the system was defined for the simulation that included the receptor-ligand complex, solvent and ions by application of CHARMM36 force field34. Potassium and chloride ions were introduced to the simulation box at a concentration of 0.15 M to counter the current flow and to simulate physiological conditions. These ions were randomly introduced into the system so that the ionic strength was appropriate in the simulation. Energy minimization was first applied to the system with the steepest descent method to eliminate any steric clashes. After minimization, equilibration of the system occurred in two steps: position-restrained equilibration to facilitate an appropriate distribution of solvent and ions and production phase at constant temperature and pressure (NPT ensemble) over 100 ns35. Periodic conditions of the boundary were employed in the production phase, and the temperature was fixed at 300 K with Berendsen thermostat36, and pressure was fixed at 1 bar with Parrinello-Rahman barostat37. The analysis of the trajectory of the systems was done by storing the md trajectory every 40 ps time interval.


### Artificial intelligence-based simulation analysis
  Python-based machine learning tools, namely MDAnalysis, Numpy and Scikit-learn were used to analyze the 100 ns MD trajectory of the LXR-receptor complex38,39. Cartesian coordinates of the C-alpha atoms had been obtained, and Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed to reduce the dimensions of the data and identify the major motion components. K-means clustering was used to cluster the first two PCs to determine separate conformational states and in each state a frame nearest to the cluster centroid was used to define the state. Also, density-based clustering was done with DBScan and t-SNE analysis was conducted to represent non-linear relationships in the data. Anomaly scanning was used to detect outlier conformations, and a PCA density map depicting conformational state distribution was also made.


## Results and discussion

### Protein modelling and loop prediction
  Figure 1a demonstrates that the missing loops in the LXR structure could be successfully modelled with AlphaFold2. The comparison of the original structure (green) and the modelled structure (blue) shows that the region of the deleted loop was correctly predicted and only a few structural differences have been observed among the two models. The generated Ramachandran plot (Fig. 1b) was designed to determine the quality of the structure modelled in terms of stereochemistry. The distribution of the phi and psi angles in the plot has been favorable and most of the residues (over 95) fall within the permitted areas, which demonstrated that the loop modeled was well within the anticipated protein geometry40. This implies that there is no critical strain or undesirable steric interaction that is produced by the modelled loop to the structure of the protein. Also, the analysis of errors (Fig. 1c) based on the ERRAT score presented an elevated level of structural quality. It is shown by the plot that most of the error values are less than 5%, which means that the model is very reliable, with only minor deviations in particular areas that do not lie at the center of the structure41. The quality factor of 98.73% in total justifies the usefulness of the modelled loops in future molecular dynamics simulations and other analyses.

### Pharmacophore model development and validation
  The Phase module has been employed to depict the pharmacophore model of LXR with the main characteristics of a ligand binding process such as the presence of hydrogen bond donors, acceptors, and aromatic areas. Figure 2a shows the spatial positioning of these features in the LXR binding site and the respective coordinates are presented in Table 1. The pharmacophore map overlaid onto the LXR receptor, as depicted in Fig. 2b, gives a good visual depiction of the interaction of the major features with the binding site. The color-coded aspects, such as hydrogen bond acceptors and donors and hydrophobic regions, fit the binding pocket, and this proves the model to be relevant to the actual receptor structure. To prove the pharmacophore model, predictive capability was evaluated using several metrics. Figure 2c shows that the pharmacophore is very accurate in identifying true positives, the area under the curve (AUC) value is 0.92, indicating that the model is highly efficient in differentiating between active and inactive compounds42. There are other evaluation metrics like EF1, BEDROC and ROC which also demonstrated good values of (6.7, 0.95, and 0.99, respectively), which additional indicates the strength of the model. The hits (marked with different colors) were successfully superimposed on the key pharmacophore features proving the capability of the model predicting molecules that suit the binding site requirements of the receptor.

### Screen of the ChEMBL database virtual
  The ChEMBL database was screened against the developed pharmacophore model to identify possible LXR inhibitors. A compound was considered as a hit when it overlapped at least four features of the pharmacophore. The compounds obtained after the screening were then ranked on the Phase screen score, which is a combination of the volume score, RMSD site matching and the vector alignment. The score of the pharmacophore and the ligand is measured as a vector score between − 1.0, where the maximum alignment between the pharmacophore and the ligand is −1.0, to 1.0, where the maximum alignment of the two molecules is 1.0. The volume score is a score ranged between 0.0 and 1.0 that measures the overlap between the reference ligand and the aligned ligands with high scores representing a better overlap. To identify hits, a hit identification threshold of 1.4 was established on a Phase screen score and 103 compounds of the ChEMBL database passed this hit identification threshold. These 103 compounds were put on the list of potential hits that should be further analyzed, which implies that they are well aligned to the pharmacophore features.


### Docking interactions and binding studies
  Molecular docking study revealed that the chosen compounds interact with the LXR receptor with the best binding affinities of −10.59 to −11.07 kcal/mol and − 76.80 to −110.08 kcal/mol by the Glide and MMGBSA binding affinities, respectively43. The docking results were summarized in Table 2 which has Glide scores, MMGBSA binding affinities, and the exact residues taking part in hydrogen bond and hydrophobic contacts with each compound. It was revealed that the entire compounds were hydrogen bond to the key residues of LXR binding site which comprise of Ser62, Glu65, Arg103, and Leu114 that are important in biological activity of LXR. Hydrogen bond interactions within 2.5–3.5 Å were considered significant, while distances above this range were interpreted as weaker interactions. An example is that CHEMBL1207018 was hydrogen bonded with Glu65, Ser62, and Arg103 with bond lengths of 2.64 A, 3.63 A and 2.63 A, respectively. Likewise, CHEMBL1207638 was found to have hydrogen bonds with Glu65, Asn23, Arg103, Thr100, and Leu114, and bond lengths of 1.78 to 3.37 A. Hydrophobic interactions were also noticeable as some of the main hydrophobic residues including Phe113, Leu129, Met96, Trp241 and Leu58 were also found to stabilize receptor-ligand complexes. As an example, CHEMBL1207018 exhibited hydrophobic interactions with Ala59, Met96, Phe113, Phe133, and Leu129, which leads to the overall stability of binding. Equally, CHEMBL3558113 has hydrophobic interactions with Phe113, Leu129, Phe55, Leu97, and Met96 which increased binding affinity. The docking outcome indicates that the selected compounds can bind to the LXR receptor in an effective manner and, in this case, form a combination of hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions that stabilize the protein-ligand complex. These results suggest that the compounds are potential drug candidates which require further experimental validation.

### Drug-likeness assessment and toxicity preclinical
  The physicochemical properties of the chosen compounds were compared according to such drug-likeness indicators as molecular weight (MW), hydrogen bond acceptors (HBA), hydrogen bond donors (HBD), topological polar surface area (TPSA), lipophilicity (LogP), gastrointestinal absorption (GIA), blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeability and compliance with the Lipinski rule of five. These properties of each compound are summarized in Table 3. The compounds were usually of good drug-like properties since most of them fit the Lipinski rule of five, which insinuates that they would be orally bioavailable. The MW of the compounds was 378 to 461 which is in the optimal range of small-molecule drugs. The range of TPSA was large (74.25 to 152.11), which was expected of several compounds, such as CHEMBL3558113 and CHEMBL1625580, meaning that they were polar, which could affect the cell membrane permeability. Nevertheless, the LogP values of all compounds fell within the desirable range of oral absorption of 1.34 to 2.78. In the case of GIA (gastrointestinal absorption), all the compounds were anticipated to have high gastrointestinal absorption, which again favors their possibility of having an oral bioavailability. It can be noted that CHEMBL1620601 was expected to possess high BBB permeability, and this could be of interest in treating central nervous system diseases. The compounds were also devoid of PAINS (pan-assay interference compounds) whereby none of the compounds are likely to have non-specific binding behaviors which would invalidate their biological assays efficacy. To determine the safety profiles of the compounds selected, toxicity predictions were made. Table 4 shows the estimated toxicity and the probability of toxicity being hepatotoxic, carcinogenic, immunotoxin, mutagenic and cytotoxic to each compound. Most of them, such as CHEMBL1207018, CHEMBL1207638, CHEMBL1205680, CHEMBL1205813, and CHEMBL1207021, were estimated to fall inactive in the key toxicity groups and there was no significant risk of hepatotoxicity, carcinogenicity, or mutagenicity. These compounds were grouped under toxicity class 4, which was considered with lesser potential toxicity with predicted values of LD50 exceeding 800 mg/kg. Nevertheless, some of the compounds such CHEMBL1625580 and CHEMBL3306087 were toxic. CHEMBL1625580 was reported as potentially immunotoxin (active), whereas CHEMBL3558113 had an LD50 prediction of 300 mg/kg, indicating it belongs to toxicity class 3, and it is more likely to cause toxicity. Nonetheless, their high toxicity risk prediction of most compounds justifies further development of this group of compounds as LXR inhibitors, although most compounds have low toxicity profile.

### DFT analysis
  Calculations of the electronic properties of the chosen compounds, the highest occupied and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (HOMO and LUMO) energies, were carried out using the Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations44. These energy levels are important terms that characterize the chemical reactivity of the compounds and their capacity either to give up electrons or to receive the electrons. The HOMO-LUMO energy gap is significant because it is used to show the stability and the reactivity of a particular molecule (those with smaller energy gaps are more reactive and have more possibilities of chemical reactions). Figure 3; Table 5 indicate that the results of the calculations of HOMO and LUMO energies of the compounds were different, with CHEMBL1207018 having the highest HOMO energy (−6.97 eV) and CHEMBL1207638 the lowest LUMO energy (−2.23 eV). There were 4.99 eV (CHEMBL1207018) and 6.50 eV (CHEMBL1207638) energy gaps, indicating that chemical reactivity and the formation of stable complexes with the receptor were more likely in compounds with smaller HOMO-LUMO gaps, e.g., CHEMBL1207018. The electron affinity and ionization potential were like HOMO and LUMO energies. The more ionized the compound (e.g., CHEMBL1207018 at 6.97 eV) the less likely it is to donate electron, and the stronger the electron affinity, the more likely it is to accept electrons (e.g., CHEMBL1207638 at 2.23 eV). Electronegativity, which is the ability of the molecule to attract electrons, was determined on each compound. The most electronegative molecules (7.96), and the least electronegative molecules (6.83) were CHEMBL1207018 and CHEMBL1620601, respectively. These values reflect the possible reactivity of the compounds in the biological system and those molecules that are more electronegative have more chances of interacting with the receptor. The compounds also differed in the chemical hardness and softness of the compounds that characterize the resistance of the compounds to the flow of electrons and the ease in which the compounds can accept electrons, respectively. Both CHEMBL1207018 and CHEMBL1207638 were found to have moderate values of chemical hardness (5.97 and 5.38, respectively) indicating that both compounds have balanced reactivity profiles. Conversely, the compounds such as CHEMBL1205813 and CHEMBL1207021 had a low value of hardness (6.16 and 5.76), which means that they are more flexible in the ability to donate or accept electrons. Figure 3 is the visual representation of the HOMO and the LUMO of each compound, which shows the characteristics of the electronic state and the energy gaps. These findings support the hypothesis that the compounds with lower HOMO-LUMO gaps (e.g., CHEMBL1207018) can be more active and can be used to interact with LXR. According to this multi-parameter screening strategy, CHEMBL1205680 was chosen to be the target of the detailed MD simulations. The compound exhibited better values of binding free energies, favorable ADMET and toxicity, and stable electronic properties and are thus favorable to obtain later dynamic and energetic analysis.

### Binding mode analysis of CHEMBL1205680
  Although several compounds demonstrated favorable binding characteristics, CHEMBL1205680 was selected as the lead candidate based on a combination of docking scores, MM/GBSA binding energy, and electronic properties. Compared to other top hits such as CHEMBL1207018 and CHEMBL1207638, CHEMBL1205680 exhibited a more balanced profile, including stable binding interactions, favorable HOMO–LUMO energy gap, and acceptable ADMET properties. While some compounds showed slightly higher docking scores, their toxicity profiles or electronic properties were less favorable. This multi-parameter evaluation supports the selection of CHEMBL1205680 as a promising lead for further investigation. Figure 4 presents the binding pose of CHEMBL1205680 within the receptor’s active site, showing key interactions with the critical residues identified during the docking study. The analysis reveals that CHEMBL1205680 binds effectively at the active site of LXR through the formation of multiple hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions. The compound forms hydrogen bonds with Glu65, Ser62, and Arg103, which are essential for the stabilization of the complex. ## Discussion
  In this study, we employed an integrated computational approach to identify potential inhibitors of Liver X Receptor (LXR), a key regulator of lipid metabolism implicated in pediatric metabolic disorders. By combining pharmacophore modeling, virtual screening, molecular docking, density functional theory (DFT) analysis, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, and ADMET prediction, we identified CHEMBL1205680 as a promising candidate for further investigation.

  The pharmacophore-based screening enabled the identification of compounds that satisfy the essential structural and interaction features required for LXR binding. The high enrichment metrics, including AUC, EF, and BEDROC values, indicate that the pharmacophore model effectively distinguishes active-like compounds from inactive ones. This supports the reliability of the screening workflow and its applicability for identifying novel LXR modulators.

  Molecular docking analysis on LXRβ revealed that the top-ranked compounds exhibit favorable binding affinities toward the LXR ligand-binding domain. The use of LXRβ (PDB ID: 6S4N) was based on the availability of a high-resolution structure and its structural similarity to LXRα, suggesting that identified ligands may exhibit cross-isoform activity. Notably, key residues such as Glu65, Ser62, Arg103, and Leu114 were consistently involved in ligand interactions. These residues have been previously reported to play critical roles in ligand recognition and stabilization within the LXR binding pocket. The combination of hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions observed in the docking results suggests that both electrostatic complementarity and hydrophobic packing are essential determinants of ligand binding.

  Among the screened compounds, CHEMBL1205680 demonstrated a balanced profile in terms of binding affinity, interaction stability, and physicochemical properties. Although some compounds exhibited comparable docking scores, their ADMET profiles or electronic properties were less favorable. The selection of CHEMBL1205680 was therefore based on a multi-parameter optimization strategy rather than a single metric, which enhances the robustness of lead identification.

  The DFT analysis provided additional insight into the electronic properties of the selected compounds. The HOMO–LUMO energy gap is a critical parameter reflecting chemical reactivity and stability. CHEMBL1205680 exhibited a moderate energy gap, suggesting a balance between reactivity and stability that may facilitate effective interaction with the receptor. These findings support the notion that electronic descriptors can complement structural analyses in prioritizing candidate molecules.

  Molecular dynamics simulations further validated the stability of the CHEMBL1205680–LXR complex under physiological conditions. The RMSD profile indicated that the complex remained stable throughout the 100 ns simulation, with only minor fluctuations. RMSF analysis revealed that flexibility was primarily localized to loop regions, while residues within the binding site remained stable, indicating persistent ligand interactions. Additionally, stable radius of gyration and solvent-accessible surface area profiles suggest that ligand binding does not disrupt the overall structural integrity of the receptor. The consistent presence of hydrogen bonds throughout the simulation further supports the stability of the complex.

  Despite these promising findings, it is important to note that computational methods primarily evaluate binding affinity and structural compatibility, and do not directly determine the functional activity of a ligand as an agonist or antagonist. In the context of LXR, the distinction between agonists and antagonists is closely related to conformational changes that influence coactivator or corepressor recruitment. While the binding mode of CHEMBL1205680 suggests potential inhibitory behavior, experimental validation is required to confirm its functional role.

  The therapeutic targeting of LXR is particularly challenging due to its dual role in regulating cholesterol efflux and lipogenesis. While LXR agonists have shown beneficial effects on cholesterol metabolism, they are often associated with adverse effects such as hepatic steatosis and hypertriglyceridemia. This has led to increased interest in LXR antagonists and inverse agonists that can suppress lipogenic pathways. In this context, the identification of potential LXR inhibitors, such as CHEMBL1205680, is of significant interest, particularly for pediatric applications where long-term safety is critical.

  From a pediatric perspective, the safety profile of candidate compounds is of paramount importance. Although in silico ADMET predictions suggest favorable pharmacokinetic and toxicity profiles for CHEMBL1205680, these predictions must be interpreted with caution. Nuclear receptors such as LXR are involved in multiple physiological pathways, and off-target effects or long-term metabolic consequences cannot be fully assessed through computational methods alone. Therefore, further experimental studies are necessary to evaluate the safety and efficacy of these compounds in relevant biological systems.

  A key limitation of this study is the absence of experimental validation. While the integration of multiple computational techniques enhances confidence in the predictions, in vitro and in vivo studies are required to confirm the biological activity and therapeutic potential of the identified compounds. Additionally, future studies should explore structure–activity relationships (SAR) to optimize the potency and selectivity of these molecules.

  Overall, this study demonstrates the utility of an integrated computational pipeline for the identification of potential LXR inhibitors. The findings provide a foundation for further experimental validation and optimization, contributing to the development of targeted therapies for metabolic disorders, particularly in pediatric populations.


## Conclusion
  In this study, we combined molecular modeling, quantum chemical analysis, and molecular dynamics simulations to identify Liver X Receptor (LXR) inhibitors in treating pediatric metabolic disorders. Through the combination of these in silico methods, virtual screening, and characterization of compounds, such as CHEMBL1205680, that showed stable interactions with LXR were identified. We find that CHEMBL1205680 retains the stability of the receptor via favorable hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions indicating its potential in modulating LXR activity. This study provides a robust computational framework for the identification of LXR inhibitors. While CHEMBL1205680 demonstrated promising binding and stability profiles, further experimental validation is required to confirm its biological activity and therapeutic potential.
