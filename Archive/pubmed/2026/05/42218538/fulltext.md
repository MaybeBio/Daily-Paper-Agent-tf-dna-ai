# Toosendanin suppresses acute myeloid leukemia by targeting DDX5/c-Myc axis to inhibit protein synthesis


## Background
  Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) remains a therapeutic challenge due to relapse and resistance, driving the need for novel therapeutic strategies. While the natural compound toosendanin (TSN) has shown anti-tumor activity in solid cancers, its mechanism of action and direct molecular targets in AML are unknown.


## Methods
  We utilized an integrated chemical biology approach (affinity pull-down, cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA), drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS) assay, molecular docking) for target deconvolution. Downstream mechanisms were dissected using transcriptomic (RNA-seq), functional assays and genetic rescue experiments. Efficacy and toxicity were evaluated in an MLL-AF9-driven AML mouse model.


## Results
  TSN exhibited potent anti-AML activity at nanomolar concentrations, inhibiting cell proliferation, inducing dual G0/G1 and G2/M phase arrest, and triggering caspase-dependent apoptosis. To uncover the mechanism, we identified the RNA helicase DDX5 as a novel, direct cellular target of TSN. TSN binding promoted DDX5 degradation via both proteasomal and lysosomal pathways, a dual mechanism ensuring its efficient depletion. Subsequent transcriptomic analyses revealed that TSN, via targeting DDX5, impaired the oncogenic c-Myc transcriptional network, leading to suppressed ribosome biogenesis and global protein synthesis. Genetic rescue experiments linked DDX5 to these effects. Importantly, in preclinical AML model, TSN treatment significantly prolonged survival without observable toxicity, confirming its therapeutic potential.


## Conclusions
  Our study delineates a coherent and novel anti-leukemic pathway: TSN suppresses AML by directly targeting DDX5 for degradation, thereby inhibiting the pro-survival c-Myc axis. These findings elucidate the pharmacology of TSN and validate the DDX5/c-Myc axis as a promising therapeutic candidate in AML.

## Introduction
  Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a highly diverse blood cancer characterized by impaired cell death, blocked maturation, and uncontrolled myeloid precursors growth [1]. With the widespread application of multi-omics technologies, an increasing number of key molecular markers and oncogenic drivers of leukemia have been identified [2]. The development of various small-molecule targeted drugs has also significantly expanded, reshaping cancer treatment substantially [3]. However, despite advances in chemotherapy and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, the long-term survival rate for AML patients remains unsatisfactory. Drug resistance and relapse remain major clinical challenges in elderly patients and those with high-risk genetic abnormalities, such as MLL rearrangements and FLT3-ITD [4]. Therefore, exploring effective therapeutic strategies that target novel molecular entities is urgently needed to improve the clinical prognosis of AML.

  In recent years, natural products and their derivatives have garnered increasing attention as important resources for anticancer drug development. Traditional Chinese Medicine, with its multi-target effects and relatively low toxicity, shows unique potential in oncology [5, 6]. Toosendanin (TSN) is a triterpenoid compound derived from the fruit of Melia toosendan Sieb. et Zucc., which is traditionally used as an anthelmintic agent [7]. Accumulating evidence indicates that TSN exhibits significant antitumor activity in various solid tumors, including glioblastoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, and triple-negative breast cancer. Its mechanisms include the induction of apoptosis, cell cycle arrest, and inhibition of tumor metastasis [7–13]. However, in the field of hematological malignancies, despite a few studies reporting that TSN can induce apoptosis in HL-60 cells [14, 15], its potent anti-leukemic efficacy in AML, specific molecular target(s), and downstream signaling pathways remain largely unknown, severely limiting its clinical application. Therefore, identifying and validating the direct target(s) of TSN in AML is crucial for advancing its development as a novel therapeutic agent.

  DEAD-box helicase 5 (DDX5) is a crucial RNA helicase that plays a central role in cell proliferation by regulating multiple aspects of RNA metabolism, including RNA splicing, stability, ribosome biogenesis, and microRNA processing [16, 17]. Studies have shown that DDX5 is aberrantly overexpressed in various malignancies, such as colorectal cancer [18], breast cancer [19], prostate cancer [20], and T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia [21]. It can function as a transcription activator for oncogenes, such as c-Myc and β-catenin, thereby driving tumor initiation and progression [18–21]. Recent research has revealed a dependency of AML cells on DDX5, and inhibiting DDX5 expression effectively suppresses leukemia progression [22], strongly suggesting that DDX5 is a potential therapeutic target in AML.

  In this study, we demonstrated that TSN exerts potent anti-leukemic effects in multiple AML cell lines and murine models. At the mechanistic level, we employed integrated chemical biology approaches to identify DDX5 as a direct target of TSN. We further elucidated that TSN promotes the degradation of DDX5 via both the proteasomal and lysosomal pathways, thereby disrupting its protein stability. Subsequent transcriptomic and functional analyses indicated that TSN-induced downregulation of DDX5 significantly inhibited the c-Myc-driven transcriptional program and ribosome biogenesis, ultimately leading to global suppression of protein synthesis and leukemic cell death. This study delineates the direct targets and molecular mechanisms of TSN in AML, highlighting the critical role of the DDX5/c-Myc axis in maintaining the malignant phenotype of AML and providing a solid theoretical foundation for developing TSN as a novel anti-AML agent that functions by inducing targeted protein degradation.


## Materials and methods

### Cell culture
  Human acute myeloid leukemia (AML) cell lines (MOLM-13, MV4-11, THP-1, and HL-60) and HEK293T cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; https://www.atcc.org/). Primary mouse AML cells were derived from an MLL-AF9-driven leukemia model. AML cell lines were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (C3001, Vivacell, Shanghai, China) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; F103-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S) mixture (C3420, Vivacell). Primary AML cells were cultured in IMDM (C3113, Vivacell) containing 10% FBS, 1% P/S, 10 ng/mL mouse IL-3 (78042, STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, Canada), 10 ng/mL mouse IL-6 (78052, STEMCELL Technologies), 100 ng/mL mouse SCF (78064, STEMCELL Technologies), and 55 µM β-mercaptoethanol (21985023, Gibco, Grand Island, New York, USA). HEK293T cells were cultured in DMEM (C3010, Vivacell) containing 10% FBS and 1% P/S.


### Reagents and antibodies
  TSN was purchased from MedChemExpress (HY-N0263, Shanghai, China). The CCK-8 assay kit was obtained from BIOGROUND (BG0025; Chongqing, China). The EdU-647 Cell Proliferation Detection Kit and HPG-647 Protein Synthesis Assay Kit were purchased from Beyotime (C0081L and P1213S, Shanghai, China). The protein synthesis inhibitor cycloheximide (CHX, S7418), proteasome inhibitor MG132 (S2619), and autophagy inhibitor chloroquine (CQ, S6999) were obtained from Selleck Chemicals (Shanghai, China). Recombinant human DDX5 protein was obtained from CUSABIO (CSB-EP006630HU, Wuhan, China). Primary antibodies used for western blot analysis included: β-actin (AC004, ABclonal, Wuhan, China; 1:1000), glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH, AF1186, Beyotime; 1:1000), β-Tubulin (10068-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China; 1:1000), CDK1 (A22347, ABclonal; 1:1000), CDK2 (A0294, ABclonal; 1:1000), Caspase-3/Cleaved Caspase-3 (ET1608-64, HUABIO, Hangzhou, China; 1:1000), PARP/Cleaved PARP (T40050, Abmart, Shanghai, China; 1:1000), c-Myc (T55150, Abmart; 1:1000), and DDX5 (ET1705-32, HUABIO; 1:1000). For flow cytometry, Annexin V-APC (640920, BioLegend, CA, USA; 1:100) and DAPI (C1002, Beyotime) were used.


### Cell viability assay and IC50 determination
  Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. Briefly, AML cells (MOLM-13, MV4-11, THP-1, and HL-60) were seeded in 96-well plates (8,000 cells per well) and treated with a gradient concentration of TSN for 48 h. Then, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for 2 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a TECAN Spark microplate reader (TECAN, Männedorf, Switzerland).


### Cell cycle analysis
  The cell cycle was assessed using an EdU incorporation assay. MOLM-13 and MV4-11 cells treated with TSN or DMSO for 48 h were incubated with 10 µM EdU for 2 h. The cells were then washed, fixed, permeabilized, and stained according to the instructions of the BeyoClick™ EdU-647 kit (C0082S, Beyotime). After staining, the cells were counterstained with DAPI (5 µg/mL) and analyzed using a BD FACSCanto™ flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA).


### Colony formation assay
  MOLM-13 and MV4-11 cells were seeded in methylcellulose medium containing TSN or DMSO at a density of 500 cells/well. The cells were cultured for 7–10 days, after which the colonies were photographed and counted under an inverted microscope.


### Apoptosis detection
  Apoptosis was analyzed using Annexin V-APC and DAPI dual staining. MOLM-13 and MV4-11 cells were treated with TSN (20, 40, and 80 nM) or DMSO for 48 h. Bone marrow cells from control and AML model mice were treated with TSN (120 nM) or DMSO for 48 h. The cells were collected, washed with pre-cooled PBS, resuspended in 100 µL of 1× Annexin binding buffer (V13246, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) containing 2 µL Annexin V-APC and DAPI (0.5 µg/mL), and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 15 min. Apoptosis rates were immediately analyzed using a BD FACSCanto™ flow cytometer (BD Biosciences).


### Nuclear morphology observation
  Apoptosis-associated nuclear fragmentation was evaluated using DAPI staining. MOLM-13 and MV4-11 cells treated with TSN or DMSO for 48 h were collected and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. Cell suspensions were cytospun onto poly lysine-coated slides, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100, and stained with DAPI working solution (10 µg/mL). After washing with PBS, the slides were mounted, and nuclear morphology was observed using fluorescence microscopy.


### Nascent protein synthesis detection
  Nascent protein synthesis was detected using an L-homopropargylglycine (L-HPG) incorporation assay. MOLM-13 and MV4-11 cells treated with TSN or DMSO for 48 h were methionine-starved for 45 min to deplete intracellular methionine. The cells were then incubated with 50 µM L-HPG for 45 min. The cells were fixed and permeabilized, and the incorporated HPG was labeled using the BeyoClick™ HPG-647 Protein Synthesis Detection Kit (P1213S, Beyotime). Fluorescence intensity was measured using a BD FACSCanto™ Flow Cytometer (BD Bioscience).


### RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)
  Total RNA was extracted from TSN-treated cells using RNAiso Plus reagent (#9108, Takara, Kusatsu, Japan), and RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (#RR047Q, Takara). qPCR was performed using the TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ kit (RR420Q, Takara). GAPDH was used as the internal control. The primer sequences are presented in Table 1.

### Western blot analysis
  Cells were lysed, and protein concentrations were determined using a Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) Protein Assay Kit (P0012, Beyotime). Equal amounts of protein were separated by electrophoresis on 4–20% FastPAGE precast gels (TSINGKE, Beijing, China) and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). The membrane was blocked with non-fat milk and incubated overnight at 4 °C with a specific primary antibody, followed by incubation with the corresponding secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. Band intensities were quantified using the ImageJ software (version 1.54). GAPDH, β-tubulin, or β-actin was used as the loading control.


### Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)
  The cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) was employed to evaluate the effect of TSN on the thermal stability of DDX5. MOLM-13 cells were treated with 500 nM TSN or DMSO for 1 h. Cells were harvested, resuspended in ice-cold lysis buffer (1× PBS, 1 mM PMSF, and 1× phosphatase inhibitor cocktail), and aliquoted into seven equal parts. Each aliquot was heated at gradient temperatures (37, 46, 49, 52, 55, 58, and 61 °C) for 3 min using a thermal cycler (T100™, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), followed by three freeze-thaw cycles in liquid nitrogen. Soluble proteins were collected by centrifugation and analyzed for DDX5 expression using western blot.


### Drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS) assay
  The drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS) assay was constructed following the protocol of Huang et al. [23]. Cell lysates from MOLM-13 cells were prepared using M-PER lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors (#78503, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The lysates were then incubated with 500 nM TSN or DMSO at room temperature for 1 h. Subsequently, the lysates were digested with gradient concentrations of pronase (0, 1:400, 1:800, 1:1600, and 1:3200; #10165913103, Roche CustomBiotech, Basel, Switzerland) at room temperature for 30 min. The reaction was terminated by adding loading buffer and heating at 70 °C for 10 min. DDX5 protein levels were determined using western blot.


### Drug affinity pull-down assay
  TSN was covalently coupled to epoxy-activated Sepharose 6B beads (#71-7087-00 AG; GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Control beads were prepared without TSN conjugation. The unreacted active sites on the beads were blocked with ethanolamine. Cell lysates from MOLM-13 cells were incubated with TSN-conjugated or control beads overnight at 4 °C. The beads were then thoroughly washed, and the bound proteins were eluted by boiling in loading buffer. The eluates were subjected to western blot to detect DDX5 protein levels.


### RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis
  MOLM-13 cells were treated with 20 nM TSN or DMSO for 48 h (n = 3 biological replicates). Total RNA was extracted, and libraries were constructed and sequenced using the NovaSeq™ X Plus platform. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 v1.26.0 (FDR < 0.05, |log₂FC| > 0.584). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted using the MSigDB Hallmark 2023 gene set (FDR < 0.25), and the analysis was performed on the OmicShare platform v6.2 (https://www.omicshare.com/tools/).


### G-Quadruplex (G4) unfolding assay
  The FAM-labeled c-Myc promoter G4 probes (MycG4, sequences in Table 2) were designed as described by Wu et al. [24], and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) and annealed in Tris-acetate buffer with 100 mM KCl. The unfolding reaction was performed by incubating 20 nM annealed MycG4 probe with recombinant DDX5 protein and/or TSN in unfolding buffer (50 mM Tris-acetate, pH 7.4, 2.5 mM MgCl₂, 0.5 mM DTT, 50mM KCl) at 30 °C. Changes in Fluorescence Resonance Energy Transfer (FRET) signals were monitored in real-time using a TECAN Spark microplate reader (TECAN) (excitation/emission: 490/522 nm).

### Molecular docking
  The crystal structure of DDX5 (PDB ID: 3FE2) was obtained from the PDB database and pre-processed using the Schrödinger Protein Preparation Wizard module (including residue completion, hydrogen bond optimization, and energy minimization). The 3D structure of the TSN conformation was generated using the LigPrep module. Potential binding sites were predicted using the SiteMap module, and molecular docking was performed using the Glide XP mode. The binding free energy was calculated using MM-GBSA, and the binding site interactions were visualized using PyMOL 2.5.


### Animal experiments
  All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Chongqing Medical University (No. IACUC-CQMU-2023-12096). An MLL-AF9-induced AML mouse model was established as previously described [25]. Briefly, bone marrow cells from C57BL/6 mice after 5-fluorouracil (5-Fu) injection were infected with MLL-AF9-GFP retrovirus and transplanted into semi-lethally irradiated recipient mice via tail vein injections. Peripheral blood chimerism was monitored weekly after the transplantation. When chimerism reached 50–80%, the mice were euthanized, and GFP⁺ leukemia cells were isolated from the bone marrow for secondary transplantation. For therapeutic experiments, when the percentage of GFP⁺ cells reached approximately 5%, mice were randomly treated with TSN (1 mg/kg) or vehicle control via intraperitoneal injection every two days. Body weight and survival were monitored daily.


### Overexpression plasmid transfection
  Lentivirus was produced using HEK293T cells. When the cells reached 70–80% confluency, they were co-transfected with the packaging plasmids psPAX2 and pMD2.G, together with either the target overexpression plasmid pLV-DDX5 (Corues Biotechnology, Nanjing, China) or the corresponding control plasmid pLV-NC, using Lipofectamine™ 8000 Transfection Reagent (C0533, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Six hours post-transfection, the medium was replaced with fresh DMEM containing 10% FBS. The virus-containing supernatant was collected 48 and 72 h after transfection. The collected lentiviral liquid was used to infect MOLM-13 and MV4-11 cells with 10 µg/mL polybrene (TR-1003; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA).


### Statistical analysis
  Data are presented as the mean ± SD. Statistical comparisons between the two groups were performed using an unpaired Student’s t-test. Comparisons among three or more groups were performed using one-way ANOVA. Survival curves were plotted using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Statistical significance was defined as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001. “ns” indicates no significant difference. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0).


## Results

### Toosendanin significantly inhibits AML cell proliferation and induces apoptosis
  To evaluate the anti-leukemic potential of TSN, a triterpenoid compound reported efficacy in solid tumors [7, 13], we initially assessed its effects on cell viability of various AML cell lines. CCK-8 assays demonstrated that TSN significantly inhibited the proliferation of MOLM-13, MV4-11, THP-1 and HL-60 cells in a dose-dependent manner. The half-maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC₅₀) values were 32.41 nM, 20.13 nM, 35.42 nM and 41.67 nM, respectively (Fig. 1A). Given their heightened sensitivity to TSN treatment, MOLM-13 and MV4-11 cells were selected for the subsequent mechanistic investigations.

  Previous studies have indicated that triterpenoids often achieve anti-tumor effects by disrupting the cell cycle engine [26]. We performed EdU/DAPI dual-staining assay to monitor cell cycle kinetics. The results revealed that TSN treatment led to a significant, dose-dependent reduction in S phase proportion, accompanied by accumulations in the G0/G1 and G2/M phases (Fig. 1B-C), indicating cell cycle arrest. Furthermore, colony formation assays confirmed that TSN treatment substantially reduced both number and size of colonies formed by MOLM-13 and MV4-11 cells, indicating that TSN impairs the long-term self-renewal and proliferative capacity of AML cells (Fig. 1D). At the molecular level, the expression of key cell cycle regulators Cyclin-Dependent Kinase 1 (CDK1) and CDK2 was downregulated (Fig. 1E-F), which are essential drivers of the G1/S and G2/M transitions [27, 28]. To explore whether cell cycle arrest is linked to apoptosis, we analyzed TSN’s effect on apoptosis. Annexin V/DAPI staining results revealed that treatment with 20 nM TSN for 48 h significantly increased the apoptotic cell population in both MOLM-13 and MV4-11 lines in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2A-B). This was further supported by morphological assessment, where TSN-treated cells exhibited typical apoptotic features, including chromatin condensation and nuclear fragmentation (Fig. 2C-D). Apoptosis induction by natural compounds often involves the activation of the intrinsic or extrinsic proteolytic cascades [29]. We confirmed that TSN treatment triggered robust activation (cleavage) of Caspase-3 and its downstream proteolytic target, poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) (Fig. 2E-F), key events in apoptotic pathway [30, 31]. Collectively, these data indicate that TSN effectively suppresses AML progression by inducing cell cycle arrest and apoptosis.

### Identification of DDX5 as a direct target of TSN
  To elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying the potent anti-AML activity of TSN, we employed a multi-step strategy to identify its direct cellular targets. We initially re-analyzed published proteomic data from a TSN affinity pull-down assay in macrophages [8], identifying 10 candidate proteins that were significantly enriched following TSN treatment (Fig. 3A). To further confirm the potential clinically relevant in AML, we performed an integrated database analysis. The GEPIA and TCGA databases revealed that DDX5 (p68) was significantly overexpressed in AML patients’ bone marrow (Fig. 3B-C), and its high expression was strongly associated with poor overall survival (Fig. 3D, Fig. S1), consistent with prior reports implicating DDX5 in hematopoietic malignancies [21, 22]. This overexpression was further validated in our independent cohort of primary AML patient samples (Fig. 3E). As DDX5 is an RNA helicase and a recognized oncogene in diverse cancers [22, 32, 33], we selected DDX5 for experimental validation.

  To confirm the direct binding, we employed an independent affinity pull-down assay using TSN-conjugated beads that specifically enriched endogenous DDX5 from MOLM-13 cell lysates (Fig. 3F-G). The CETSA [34] further indicated that TSN treatment significantly increased the thermal stability of DDX5, evidenced by an elevated melting temperature (Tm) (Fig. 3H-I). Corroborating these findings, DARTS assays [23] showed that TSN binding protected DDX5 from pronase-mediated degradation (Fig. 3J-K). Finally, molecular docking simulations indicated that TSN occupies the ATP-binding pocket of DDX5 (PDB: 3FE2) with high affinity (MM-GBSA = -38.68 kcal/mol) (Fig. 3L). A 2D ligand-protein interaction diagram further revealed the detailed binding mode, showing that TSN forms a hydrogen bond network with DDX5: one hydrogen bond with GLN210, one with GLN184, and two with SER198, along with several hydrophobic contacts (e.g., with CYS200, TYR202, ALA205, PRO206, PRO209, and VAL218) (Fig. 3M). Collectively, these results demonstrated that DDX5 is a direct cellular target of TSN in AML cells.


### TSN binding promotes DDX5 degradation through proteasomal and lysosomal pathways
  Following confirmation of direct target engagement, we investigated the functional consequences of TSN binding on DDX5 protein, as ligand interaction frequently modulates target protein stability [35, 36]. Western blot analysis revealed a marked reduction in DDX5 protein levels in MOLM-13 and MV4-11 cells following TSN treatment (Fig. 4A-B). To determine whether the decrease resulted from enhanced degradation, we measured the half-life of DDX5 using the CHX chase assay, a conventional method for assessing protein turnover dynamics [37]. TSN treatment significantly accelerated the turnover of DDX5 compared to the control (Fig. 4C-D), confirming that TSN promotes DDX5 protein degradation. To further delineate the degradation pathways involved, we employed the proteasome inhibitor MG132 and the lysosome inhibitor CQ, which selectively block the ubiquitin-proteasome system and autophagic-lysosomal degradation, respectively [38, 39]. We found that both inhibitors partially rescued TSN-induced downregulation of the DDX5 protein (Fig. 4E-F). To further investigate whether the two degradation pathways operate independently or exhibit compensatory crosstalk, we performed combined treatment with MG132 and CQ in the presence of TSN. While either MG132 or CQ alone partially restored DDX5 protein levels, simultaneous inhibition of both pathways did not exhibit a clear additive effect on DDX5 recovery beyond single inhibitors (Fig. S3A-D). This finding indicates that the two pathways do not function independently. Pharmacological blockade of one pathway likely triggers compensatory activation of the other, consistent with established crosstalk between the ubiquitin-proteasome system and the autophagy-lysosome pathway [40].

### TSN disrupts the c-Myc-driven oncogenic network via targeted DDX5
  To systematically elucidate the downstream mechanisms underlying the anti-AML effects of TSN, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of MOLM-13 cells. GSEA revealed the significant activation of apoptosis-related gene signatures (Fig. 5A, Fig. S2A), which is consistent with phenotypic observations. Gene sets associated with protein translation—specifically ribosome biogenesis, tRNA metabolic processes, mitochondrial translation, and mRNA modification—were markedly suppressed (Fig. 5B). Concurrently, KEGG pathway analysis of differentially downregulated genes confirmed enrichment in “Ribosome” and “Aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis” pathways (Fig. S2B). To independently validate the transcriptional repression, we performed qPCR on 16 key genes involved in ribosome biogenesis and protein translation. All 16 genes were significantly downregulated in both MOLM-13 and MV4-11 cells upon TSN treatment (Fig. 5C), corroborating the RNA-seq findings. Crucially, to confirm that this broad transcriptional downregulation translated into functional deficit, we measured global protein synthesis rates using a HPG incorporation assays. TSN treatment caused a profound inhibition of protein synthesis in AML cells (Fig. 5D). These data demonstrated that TSN potently suppresses protein synthesis in AML. As c-Myc is a master transcriptional regulator of these processes [41], we investigated its involvement. At the transcriptional level, TSN treatment led to significant downregulation of the Myc Targets V1 and V2 gene sets (Fig. 6A). Sankey diagram analysis further revealed that a substantial fraction of the downregulated ribosome biogenesis genes were Myc targets (Fig. 6B), directly linking transcriptional repression to c-Myc activity. Consistently, TSN significantly reduced c-Myc mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 6C and D).

  To establish the essential role of DDX5 in this pathway, we performed genetic rescue experiments. Ectopic overexpression of DDX5 significantly reversed TSN-induced phenotypes by attenuating apoptosis, restoring protein synthesis, and rescuing the downregulation of c-Myc protein in MOLM-13 and MV4-11 cells (Fig. 6E–J), demonstrating that DDX5 is essential for TSN-mediated inhibition of the c-Myc oncogenic network.

  To determine the molecular mechanism, we tested the hypothesis that TSN disrupts reported function of DDX5 in promoting c-Myc transcription by resolving the G-quadruplex (G4) structures in its promoter [24, 42]. We hypothesized that TSN might impair this function. An in vitro G4 unwinding assay showed that recombinant DDX5 protein effectively unwound the c-Myc G4 structure, and this activity was significantly inhibited by TSN (Fig. 6K-M). To further dissect the contribution of DDX5 degradation versus direct helicase inhibition to c-Myc suppression and AML cell death, we performed rescue experiments using single or combined treatments with MG132 and CQ. While inhibition of the proteasomal and/or lysosomal pathways substantially mitigated TSN-induced DDX5 degradation (Fig. S3A-D), the restoration of DDX5 protein levels resulted in a modest rescue of downstream c-Myc expression (Fig. S3A-D). Consistently, TSN-induced apoptosis was also only partially attenuated under these rescue conditions (Fig. S3E-F). These results indicate that merely restoring the physical presence of DDX5 is insufficient to fully reactivate its downstream function, suggesting that DDX5 degradation and direct helicase inhibition act simultaneously and cooperatively to achieve TSN’s full anti-leukemic potency.


### TSN suppresses AML progression and extends survival in vivo
  To evaluate the physiological relevance and therapeutic potential of the TSN-DDX5-c-Myc axis, we utilized an aggressive MLL-AF9-driven AML mouse model, a well-established system that recapitulates the pathogenesis of high-risk human AML [43]. Following disease confirmation (defined as ≥ 5% GFP+ cells in peripheral blood), mice were treated with vehicle or TSN. TSN was administered via intraperitoneal injection every other day at a dose of 1 mg/kg, a concentration previously established to be within the pharmacological safety window for murine models [11, 44, 45]. TSN treatment significantly prolonged median survival compared to the vehicle control group (Fig. 7A-B). Crucially, TSN-treated mice maintained stable body weights and exhibited no overt signs of behavioral or physiological toxicity throughout the study (Fig. 7C). These data indicate that at 1 mg/kg, TSN exerts potent anti-leukemic efficacy in vivo with a favorable safety profile.

  To validate that this survival benefit was driven by the specific targeting of leukemia cells, we performed ex vivo analysis on primary AML cells isolated from the leukemic mice. Consistent with our in vitro findings, TSN treatment induced significantly higher rates of apoptosis in primary blasts compared to controls (Fig. 7D). Furthermore, western blot analysis of bone marrow harvested from treated mice confirmed that TSN effectively reduced the protein abundance of both DDX5 and c-Myc in the physiological environment (Fig. 7E). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that TSN suppresses AML progression via a cell-intrinsic mechanism targeting the DDX5/c-Myc axis.

  In summary, TSN directly binds to DDX5, occupying its ATP-binding pocket and inhibiting its helicase activity, while simultaneously promoting its degradation via both the proteasomal and lysosomal pathways. This dual action leads to the loss of DDX5 function, which in turn reduces c-Myc transcriptional activity. The consequent suppression of ribosome biogenesis and global protein synthesis ultimately triggers cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in AML cells (Fig. 8).

## Discussion
  This study elucidates a novel molecular mechanism through which TSN, a natural compound derived from traditional Chinese medicine, exerts potent anti-AML activity. We identified RNA helicase DDX5 as a direct cellular target of TSN and demonstrated that TSN binding triggers DDX5 degradation via dual proteasomal and lysosomal pathways. Subsequent DDX5 downregulation impairs c-Myc-driven transcription, ultimately suppressing ribosome biogenesis, inhibiting global protein synthesis, and inducing apoptosis in AML. These findings reveal a previously unrecognized mechanism of action for TSN and confirm DDX5 as a viable therapeutic target in AML.

  RNA helicases have emerged as critical regulators of gene expression and RNA metabolism in tumorigenesis, yet their specific roles and druggability in hematological malignancies remain under exploration [46, 47]. Although TSN has demonstrated anticancer properties against various solid tumors, its specific molecular targets in hematological malignancies were previously unknown. Our integrated approach, which combined affinity pull-down, CETSA, and DARTS, unequivocally established DDX5 as a direct TSN target in AML cells. This discovery is significant as it reveals that TSN functions as a natural molecular degrader of DDX5. This mechanism is particularly intriguing given the growing interest in targeted protein degradation (TPD) strategies to tackle proteins that are difficult to inhibit via traditional occupancy-driven pharmacology [48]. Our findings expand TSN’s known mechanisms beyond previously reported effects, such as translation inhibition through eEF2 binding [9–11]. This multi-target capacity exemplifies the complex pharmacology of natural products while pinpointing a specific and potent anti-AML axis.

  In addition to target identification, we established a mechanistic connection between DDX5 degradation and disruption of c-Myc oncogenic signaling. DDX5 has been characterized as a transcriptional co-activator that engages in a positive feedback loop with c-Myc to drive tumorigenesis [24, 42]. Our data are consistent with this paradigm, demonstrating that TSN-mediated DDX5 depletion leads to profound suppression of c-Myc and downstream signaling. Directly targeting c-Myc has historically proven challenging due to its “undruggable” nature—lacking defined ligand-binding pockets. Therefore, targeting upstream regulators like DDX5 represents a promising alternative strategy to destabilize the c-Myc network [49]. Given the established role of c-Myc as a master regulator of ribosome biogenesis [41, 50], the observed inhibition of protein synthesis provides a direct functional consequence of DDX5/c-Myc axis disruption. Leukemic cells exhibit an exceptionally high demand for protein synthesis to sustain rapid proliferation, rendering them hypersensitive to disruptions in ribosome biogenesis compared to normal hematopoietic counterparts [51, 52]. This underscores the therapeutic potential of targeting DDX5 to dismantle c-Myc-driven oncogenic pathways. Although FL118 has been reported to directly bind DDX5 as a molecular glue degrader [53], its binding mechanism is yet to be revealed. In the present study, we have elucidated the mode of direct binding between TSN and DDX5. The computational docking and MM-GBSA analyses demonstrated that TSN directly occupies the active ATP-binding pocket of DDX5, forming a stable hydrogen bond network with key catalytic residues (GLN210, GLN184, and SER198). This orthosteric binding mode reveals the mechanism by which TSN inhibits DDX5’s G4 unwinding activity.

  Thus, TSN exerts a dual action on DDX5: direct occupation of the ATP pocket impairs its catalytic function, while simultaneously promoting DDX5 degradation via both proteasomal and lysosomal pathways. Mechanistically, the failure of dual pathway inhibition to produce a profound additive rescue of DDX5 is consistent with the established compensatory crosstalk between the ubiquitin-proteasome system and the autophagy-lysosome pathway, wherein impairment of one route shifts the clearance burden to the other to maintain protein homeostasis [40]. More importantly, even when DDX5 protein levels were substantially restored by blocking degradation, downstream c-Myc expression and apoptosis were only modestly rescued. This functional uncoupling—protein abundance without full activity—indicates that direct helicase inhibition and degradation both contribute importantly in mediating TSN’s anti-leukemic effect. Together, these findings delineate a cooperative mechanism: acute occupancy of the ATP pocket provides immediate functional blockade, while parallel degradation ensures sustained target ablation, leading to robust suppression of the DDX5/c-Myc axis. However, the current experimental design using degradation inhibitors cannot quantitatively determine the relative contribution of each mechanism, as TSN exerts both effects simultaneously. To rigorously dissect their respective contributions, future studies employing a helicase-dead DDX5 mutant will be required. This limitation should be addressed in future mechanistic investigations.

  The potent pro-apoptotic effect of TSN in AML is largely attributed to the collapse of the DDX5/c-Myc axis. As a central regulator of cell proliferation and metabolism, c-Myc downregulation disrupts multiple pro-survival signaling pathways while activating intrinsic apoptosis [54–57]. Our rescue experiments, in which DDX5 overexpression reversed the TSN-induced apoptosis, firmly established this causal relationship. Therefore, apoptosis induction likely represents an integrated consequence of disrupting this core oncogenic circuitry, providing a mechanistic basis for TSN’s efficacy against AML.

  The therapeutic potential of TSN is further supported by our in vivo data. In an aggressive MLL-AF9-driven AML model, TSN treatment significantly prolonged survival without observable toxicity, highlighting its favorable in vivo therapeutic window. Furthermore, emerging evidence of TSN’s capacity to reprogram tumor-associated macrophages [8] suggests potential immunomodulatory functions. Targeting the leukemia microenvironment is increasingly recognized as essential for durable remission, and agents with dual cytotoxic and immunomodulatory effects are highly desirable [58]. This warrants future research on its effects on the AML microenvironment, which may yield synergistic anti-leukemic effects.

  Our finding that TSN targets DDX5 via helicase inhibition and degradation mechanisms supports the emerging concept that DEAD-box RNA helicases are actionable therapeutic targets across various cancers. For example, the RNA helicase DDX3X was recently identified as a vulnerability in KRAS-driven lung cancer, where a DDX3X-targeting degrader successfully suppressed tumor progression [59]. Furthermore, as acquired resistance often limits the long-term efficacy of direct targeted therapies (such as KRAS inhibitors), genome-wide CRISPR screens have underscored the critical need to identify targetable dependencies to enhance treatment responses [60]. While these studies focused on exploiting therapeutic vulnerabilities in KRAS-mutant tumors, our work demonstrates that DDX5 represents a fundamental vulnerability in AML, driving c-Myc hyperactivation. Given that dysregulated c-Myc/ribosome biogenesis is common in many cancers, DDX5 may be an attractive target in other malignancies with elevated c-Myc activity (e.g., lymphoma [61], breast cancer [62], hepatocellular carcinoma [63]). The dual-mechanism strategy of TSN could thus have broader translational relevance beyond AML.

  Our study has certain limitations. The specific E3 ubiquitin ligase(s) responsible for TSN-promoted DDX5 ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation remain to be identified. Furthermore, TSN is hepatotoxic [64], which presents challenges to its clinical development. However, these limitations also inform future directions, including structural optimization to generate safer delivery strategies and exploration of advanced delivery systems, such as PROTACs or nanoparticle-based systems [13, 65], to enhance specificity and minimize off-target toxicity.


## Conclusions
  In summary, our study systematically unveils a coherent signaling pathway wherein TSN suppresses AML by directly targeting DDX5 for degradation, thereby inhibiting the c-Myc oncogenic network. These insights significantly advance the understanding of TSN’s mechanism of action and validate the DDX5/c-Myc axis as a viable therapeutic target, providing a solid scientific foundation for the development of novel treatment strategies against AML.
