# Single-cell N4-acetylcytidine regulatory landscape identifies ARIH1 as a ferroptosis-suppressive oncogenic driver in lung adenocarcinoma


## Background
  N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C) is an emerging post-transcriptional RNA modification implicated in cancer biology, yet its cellular distribution, prognostic value, and functional relevance in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) remain insufficiently characterized. This study aimed to systematically define the ac4C-related regulatory landscape in LUAD and identify functionally and clinically relevant targets.


## Methods
  A single-cell RNA-sequencing dataset comprising 87,634 cells was reanalyzed to quantify ac4C-related gene (acRG) activity across major cellular compartments using AUCell scoring. Prognostic acRGs were identified through differential expression analysis and univariate Cox regression, followed by integrative machine-learning modeling using LASSO and random survival forest algorithms. The biological function of ARIH1 was examined through cell proliferation assays, ferroptosis-related biochemical measurements, and mechanistic analyses including cycloheximide chase, co-immunoprecipitation, and ubiquitination assays.


## Results
  Epithelial cells displayed the highest acRG activity within the LUAD tumor ecosystem. A prognostic acRG-based signature was established, identifying ARIH1 as a high-risk factor associated with unfavorable survival outcomes. ARIH1 knockdown significantly inhibited LUAD cell growth and tumorigenic potential. Mechanistically, ARIH1 interacted with NCOA4 and promoted its K48-linked ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, thereby suppressing ferritinophagy and ferroptosis. ARIH1 loss resulted in iron accumulation, lipid peroxidation, glutathione depletion, and ferroptotic cell death. Additionally, ARIH1 depletion markedly enhanced cisplatin-induced ferroptosis.


## Conclusions
  These findings map the single-cell ac4C regulatory architecture of LUAD and identify ARIH1 as a ferroptosis-suppressive E3 ubiquitin ligase, highlighting a potential therapeutic target to enhance ferroptosis-based and chemotherapy-based interventions.

## Introduction
  Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1–3]. Among its various subtypes, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for nearly 85% of cases, which include adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and large-cell carcinoma [4, 5]. Within these subgroups, lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most frequently diagnosed type and exhibits significant molecular heterogeneity. This heterogeneity contributes to substantial challenges in disease management and prognosis [6, 7].

  The current clinical management of LUAD primarily relies on surgical resection, targeted therapy, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and immune checkpoint blockade [8]. Although these strategies have improved survival rates for certain patient subsets, their therapeutic efficacy is often limited by factors such as tumor invasion, distant metastasis, acquired resistance, and intratumoral heterogeneity [9, 10]. As a result, many patients eventually experience disease progression [11]. Consequently, it is essential to identify and validate novel regulatory molecules that drive LUAD progression to expand the therapeutic landscape and enhance patient outcomes.

  High-throughput bioinformatics approaches, including bulk and single-cell transcriptome profiling, machine learning, and multi-omics integration, have significantly accelerated the discovery of tumor-related biomarkers and functional targets [12, 13]. These techniques facilitate the systematic screening of genes related to tumorigenesis, prognosis, and treatment response [14]. However, many computationally derived candidate targets lack sufficient mechanistic validation and translational evidence, which limits their clinical applicability and therapeutic development. Addressing this gap requires a combination of computational discovery and in-depth experimental verification [15, 16].

  Ferroptosis, a form of regulated cell death driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, has emerged as a critical vulnerability in multiple malignancies, including LUAD [17]. Unlike apoptosis or necroptosis, ferroptosis is tightly regulated by intracellular iron homeostasis, glutathione-dependent redox defenses, and lipid metabolic pathways, making it particularly relevant to the metabolic plasticity of tumor cells [18–21]. Increasing evidence indicates that ferroptosis not only influences tumor progression but also affects therapeutic responses, particularly to chemotherapy and targeted therapy [22–24]. However, despite its therapeutic potential, the upstream regulatory network governing ferroptosis—especially the role of the ubiquitin-proteasome axis in controlling key modulators such as NCOA4, SLC7A11, and GPX4—remains poorly defined [25, 26]. This lack of understanding of how ubiquitination-dependent mechanisms regulate ferroptotic sensitivity has become a significant obstacle in harnessing ferroptosis for clinical benefit. These gaps in knowledge emphasize the need to identify novel regulatory molecules of ferroptosis and to clarify their functional roles in LUAD.

  In this study, we constructed a single-cell transcriptomic atlas of LUAD and integrated it with ac4C-related gene profiling to dissect transcriptional heterogeneity within the tumor ecosystem. Differential analysis and machine-learning screening resulted in a prognostic ac4C-related molecular signature, highlighting ARIH1 as a potential high-risk regulatory gene. Functional experiments demonstrated that depletion of ARIH1 suppressed LUAD proliferation and sensitized tumor cells to ferroptotic stress. Mechanistically, we focused on NCOA4, which has been widely reported as a key cargo receptor that facilitates ferritinophagy-mediated ferroptosis by delivering ferritin to lysosomes for degradation [27–30]. Our results show that ARIH1 binds to NCOA4, enhances its K48-linked ubiquitination, and promotes its proteasomal degradation. By lowering NCOA4 protein levels, ARIH1 effectively restricts the ferritinophagy-ferroptosis axis. Collectively, these findings identify ARIH1 as a ferroptosis-suppressive factor and reveal its potential as a therapeutic target in LUAD.


## Methods

### Data collection
  The transcriptomic data and corresponding clinical information of lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) patients were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) as the training cohort. Another independent dataset, GSE68456 and GSE31210 were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) and served as the external validation cohort.


### Single-cell RNA-seq analysis
  Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data were processed and analyzed using the Seurat package (version 4.2.2) in R. Cells were excluded if they had fewer than 200 or more than 10,000 unique gene counts, or if the total unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts were below 1,000. Furthermore, to remove low-viability cells and potential doublets, we excluded cells where the percentage of mitochondrial gene counts exceeded 10% or the percentage of ribosomal gene counts was greater than 10%. Genes that were not detected in at least 3 cells were also filtered out. gene expression counts in each cell were normalized using the “LogNormalize” method, which normalizes the total expression for each cell to 10,000 transcripts and then log-transforms the result. We then identified the top 2,000 highly variable genes (HVGs) across the dataset using the FindVariableFeatures function for downstream dimensionality reduction. we employed the Harmony R package to integrate the data, enabling the merging of datasets without the influence of technical variance. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the scaled data of the HVGs.


### Construction of acRG prognostic model in LUAD
  We leveraged ensemble of 10 machine learning algorithms, resulting in 88 unique combinations, to construct a robust prognostic model based on autophagy-related genes (acRGs) (Supplement S1). The model development involved first screening for prognostic acRGs via univariate Cox regression in the TCGA-LUAD cohort, and then building and evaluating the predictive models using leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) in the TCGA-BRCA cohort. The algorithm set included Random Survival Forest (RSF), Elastic Net (Enet), Lasso, Ridge, Stepwise Cox, CoxBoost, plsRcox, SuperPC, GBM, and Survival-SVM. The acRGBS development process involved the following steps which were included in “Mime1” R package: (a) prognostic acRGs were identified using univariate Cox regression analysis in the TCGA-LUAD cohort; (b) 88 algorithm combinations were applied to these prognostic acRGs to construct predictive models using leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) within the TCGA-LUAD cohort; (c) all models were externally validated in three independent GEO datasets (GSE68456 and GSE31210); and (d) Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) was calculated for each model across all validation cohorts, and the model exhibiting the highest average C-index was selected as the optimal model.


### Cell culture and treatment
  The human non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cell line A549 and the human embryonic kidney cell line HEK-293T were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). TheA549 cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Danvers, MA, USA), while HEK-293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Lonsera, Shanghai, China) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. All cell lines were routinely cultured at 37 °C in an atmosphere containing 5% CO2.The apoptosis inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK (Z-VAD, S7023), the ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1, S7243), and the iron chelator deferoxamine (DFO, S5742) were obtained from Selleck Chemicals (USA). Additionally, the necrosis inhibitor Necrostatin-1 (Nec-1), cisplatin (P4394), cycloheximide (CHX, 01810), and the proteasome inhibitor MG132 (M7449) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA).


### Plasmids and transfection
  Human ARIH1 complementary DNA (cDNA) was cloned into the pcDNA3.0-Myc vector. Human NCOA4 cDNA was cloned into the pECMV-Flag vector. Ubiquitin plasmids (HA-Ub-WT, HA-Ub-K48, and HA-Ub-K63) were obtained from Miaoling plasmid co.Ltd. The shRNA sequences against ARIH1 (shARIH1-1: GCCAGATGAATACAAGGTCAT and shARIH1-2: GCTACCTTGAACGAGATATTT) were cloned into the pLKO.1-puro vector. Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for transfection according to the manufacturer’s protocol.


### Western blotting
  Western blotting was performed as previously described [31]. Primary antibodies used in this study were purchased from Abclonal, including HA-tag antibody (AE105), DDDDK-Tag antibody (AE092), Myc antibody (AE070), NCOA4 antibody (A25307), ARIH1 antibody (A17123), β-Actin antibody (AC038), TFR antibody (A25900), TF antibody (A21622), and FPN1 antibody (A14885).


### CCK-8 assay
  The CCK-8 assay was performed using a CCK-8 kit (C0038, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) to measure cell viability, following the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, A549 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 400 cells per well. CCK-8 solution was added to the cell medium and incubated for 1.5 h. The optical density (OD) value was then measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific).


### Colony formation assay
  Cells were seeded in a 6-well plate at a density of 500 cells per well and cultured for 2 weeks. After this period, the colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature, then stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min. The plates were air-dried, and the colonies were counted.


### EdU incorporation assay
  An EdU incorporation assay was conducted using the Cell Light EdU DNA Imaging Kit (C10310-3, RiboBio, Guangzhou, China). Cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well. After 24 h, 50 µM EdU was added to the cells, and they were incubated for an additional 2 h. Following this incubation, the cells were fixed and stained according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon ECLIPSE Ti, Shanghai, China).


### MDA detection
  MDA levels were measured using a detection kit (Solarbio Life Science, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were lysed in cold MDA lysis buffer, then centrifuged at 12,000×g for 15 min to remove precipitate. The supernatant was incubated with TBA solution at 100 °C for 1 h, cooled, and centrifuged again at 10,000×g for 10 min. MDA concentration was measured at 532 nm.


### Ferrous Iron detection
  Iron levels were assessed using the Cell Ferrous Iron Colorimetric Assay Kit (Elabscience, Wuhan, China) and FerroOrange staining (Cell Signaling Technology, 36104) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were lysed in a buffer on ice for 10 min, then centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was mixed with 80 µl of the chromogenic solution and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. Absorbance was measured at 593 nm with a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For FerroOrange staining, cells were treated with 1 µM FerroOrange at 37 °C for 30 min, and images were analyzed using a fluorescence microscope.


### Lipid ROS determination
  Reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were measured using BODIPY 581/591 C11 Lipid Probes (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A549 cells were washed with PBS, incubated with 10 µM BODIPY 581/591 C11 at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark, then washed with ice-cold PBS and observed under a fluorescence microscope.


### LDH releasing assay
  A commercial kit (C0017, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was used to analyze cell death by measuring lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release. Cells were centrifuged, and the supernatant was incubated with the LDH reaction mixture and a substrate for 25 min at room temperature. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm, and the ratio of dead cells was calculated per the kit instructions.


### Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and mass spectrometry
  Cells were lysed in Co-IP lysis buffer (20 mM Tris, 0.5% Triton X-100, 10% glycerol, 150 mM NaCl, 20 mM MgCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM PMSF, pH 7.5) at 4 °C for 30 min. The lysate was collected by centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. It was then incubated with anti-Flag M2 or anti-Myc magnetic agarose for 2 h at room temperature, or overnight with specific antibodies and protein G plus-agarose at 4 °C. After removing the supernatant, the beads were washed three times, and the protein complexes were resuspended in loading buffer for Western blotting or mass spectrometry.


### Statistical analysis
  Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 10.3.0. Data are shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD). We used the Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA for group comparisons, with p < 0.05 indicating statistical significance.


## Results

### Single cell atlas in LUAD
  A single-cell transcriptomic dataset, notable for its high quality and comprehensive representation of immune cells, was employed to reanalyze the tumor microenvironment. To ensure data integrity, meticulous quality control was performed, resulting in a final cohort of 87,634 high-quality cells that were retained for all downstream annotation and analytical procedures (Fig. 1A), these cells were classified into 7 cell-type, which were marked by classical markers (Fig. 1B). These subgroups include B cells (MS4A1, CD79A), Endothelials (VWF, PECAM1), Epithelials (EPCAM, KRT19), Fibroblasts (COL1A1, COL1A2), Myeloid (LYZ, C1QA), Plasma (JCHAIN, IGKC) and T cells (CD3D, CD3E) (Fig. 1C). Expression of classical markers were showed as UMAP. The expression of classical markers was visualized on the UMAP projection (Fig. 1D). Despite the presence of all eight cell types in tumor and normal tissues, we observed marked disparities in their proportional distribution (Fig. 1E).

### The landscape of acRG activity in LUAD single-cell transcriptome
  We leveraged a previously established set of 2,135 ac4C-related genes (acRGs) to evaluate acRG activity by “AUCell” R pachage across different cell types in LUAD (Fig. 2A). The Epithelials showed highest activity, while immune cells showed low activity (Fig. 2B). Next, we classified the cell subpopulations into acRG-high and acRG-low based on the acRG score, using the mean value as the cutoff (Fig. 2C). The results of the cell ratio plot indicated that majority of epithelial cells were acRG-High activity (Fig. 2D). To provide a holistic view of the crosstalk among two groups, we evaluated the cell communication intensity of the acRG-low and acRG-high groups through cellchat, and we found that the interaction strength between epithelial cells and other cells was higher (Fig. 2E). Meanwhile, we performed inferCNV analysis to identify malignant cells and evaluated the acRG score using the AUcell method (Fig. 2F). The results showed that the acRG score was significantly higher in malignant cells than in normal samples (Fig. 2G and H). The strength of signals both incoming and outcoming was markedly enhanced in the acRG-high group in the same way (Fig. 2I-J). To further characterize acRG-high and acRG-low cells, we performed differential gene expression analysis on acRG-high and acRG-low, identifying 1111 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (Fig. 2K). GO and KEGG functional analysis revealed that DEGs were involved Ubiquitin mediated proteolysis, RNA splicing signaling pathways (Fig. 2L).

### Construction of the acRG signature in LUAD
  A set of 1111 genes was subjected to univariate Cox regression analysis, yielding 15 genes with significant association with overall survival (OS). These genes were used to build the acRG signature via an ensemble of 10 machine learning algorithms. The predictive performance of the signature was subsequently verified in the internal TCGA dataset and 2 independent external cohorts (Fig. 3A). Through the integration of LASSO, we identified an optimal 15 prognostic signature (Fig. 3B). Next, we screened the genes with importance greater than the mean value through the RSF algorithm, and finally selected 5 genes: STAP1, PERP, ARIH1, S100A13 and INSIG2 (Fig. 3C). For each sample, a risk score was calculated using the prognostic model. Samples were then dichotomized into low- and high-risk groups based on the median risk score. Among the three cohorts, the prognosis of the High-risk sample was worse than that of the low-risk sample (Fig. 3D). The results of survival analysis indicated that the low expression of STAP1 and PERP was associated with a poor prognosis, while the high expression of ARIH1, S100A3 and INSIG2 was associated with a poor prognosis (Fig. 3E).

(B) Cross-validation curve for the LASSO regression model used to identify the optimal penalty parameter (λ) for feature selection. ### Clinical relevance of the acRG in LUAD
  To further validate the clinical relevance of the hub acRG in LUAD, we performed a multi-dimensional analysis. Spatial transcriptome profiling revealed that S100A13, INSIG2, and ARIH1 were spatially co-localized with tumor cell regions (Fig. 4A). Analysis of the GSE72094 dataset showed that patients over 65 years of age with EGFR-mutant tumors exhibited higher acRG risk scores, suggesting a poorer prognosis in this subgroup (Fig. 4B). Immunohistochemistry data from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) further demonstrated that ARIH1 protein expression was elevated in tumor tissues compared to normal lung tissues (Fig. 4C). To assess whether the acRG signature provides independent prognostic value beyond established clinical factors, we performed univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses in the TCGA-LUAD cohort. After adjusting for age, tumor stage, and smoking status, the acRG signature remained significantly associated with overall survival (Fig. 4D–E). Tumor stage also emerged as an independent prognostic factor, whereas other covariates did not reach statistical significance.

### ARIH1 knockdown markedly impairs proliferative capacity and tumorigenicity in LUAD
  To investigate the biological role of ARIH1 in the progression of LUAD, we generated stable A549 cell lines with ARIH1 knockdown using specific shRNAs. Quantitative RT-PCR and western blot analyses confirmed a significant reduction in ARIH1 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 5A-B). Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assays showed that the knockdown of ARIH1 significantly decreased cell proliferation (Fig. 5C). In agreement with these findings, colony formation assays revealed a substantial reduction in both the number and size of colonies, indicating a decreased long-term proliferative capacity (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, the EdU incorporation assay demonstrated a notable decline in DNA synthesis activity following ARIH1 knockdown (Fig. 5E), providing additional support for its inhibitory effect on LUAD cell growth.

### Knockdown of ARIH1 promotes ferroptosis in LUAD cells
  To clarify the mechanism by which ARIH1 affects the proliferation of A549 cells, we treated A549 shARIH1 cells with various cell death inhibitors and assessed cell viability using the CCK-8 assay. The results showed that treatment with the ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) significantly restored cell viability (Fig. 6A). Since ferroptosis is known to cause an increase in LDH release, we further measured LDH levels and found that the elevated LDH release observed after ARIH1 knockdown could be rescued by ferroptosis inhibitors Fer-1 or deferoxamine (DFO) (Fig. 6B). These findings suggest that ARIH1 may induce ferroptosis in A549 cells. Furthermore, Western blot analysis was performed to examine changes in ferroptosis-related protein expression. The results showed that knockdown ARIH1 decreased the expression of SLC7A11 and GPX4, while increasing the expression of ACSL4, further indicating that ARIH1 regulates the ferroptotic process in cells (Fig. 6C).

(A) Cell viability was measured by CCK-8 assay in A549 cells after treatment with different cell death inhibitors, including the necroptosis inhibitor Nec-1, the apoptosis inhibitor Z-VAD, and the ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1). The reduction in cell viability caused by ARIH1 knockdown was significantly rescued only by Fer-1, indicating ferroptosis involvement. (B) LDH release assay showing increased LDH levels in ARIH1-silenced cells, which were restored by treatment with ferroptosis inhibitors Fer-1 or deferoxamine (DFO).   The involvement of ferroptosis was further confirmed by measuring biochemical markers of lipid peroxidation and iron accumulation. Levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) were significantly elevated following ARIH1 knockdown (Fig. 6D). Intracellular ferrous iron levels were also markedly increased (Fig. 6E), while cellular glutathione (GSH) content was noticeably reduced (Fig. 6F), indicating a compromised antioxidant defense system.

  Finally, mitochondrial ultrastructure was examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to provide morphological evidence of ferroptosis (Fig. 6G). In shNC cells, mitochondria appeared normal with well-defined cristae. However, ARIH1-depleted cells exhibited typical ferroptotic hallmarks, including shrunken mitochondrial volume, increased bilayer membrane density, and the reduction or disappearance of mitochondrial cristae (Fig. 6G). Collectively, these findings from biochemical markers and ultrastructural morphology demonstrate that the knockdown of ARIH1 promotes ferroptosis in LUAD cells.


### ARIH1 binds to NCOA4 and inhibits NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy
  To clarify the molecular mechanism by which ARIH1 regulates ferroptosis, we examined the expression of proteins related to iron metabolism following the knockdown of ARIH1. Western blot analysis indicated that silencing ARIH1 significantly increased the protein level of NCOA4, while no notable changes were observed in TFR, TF, or FPN1 (Fig. 7A). To determine whether ARIH1 affects the stability of NCOA4 protein, we conducted a cycloheximide (CHX) chase assay. The results showed that the degradation rate of NCOA4 was notably reduced in A549 shARIH1 cells, suggesting that ARIH1 promotes the degradation of NCOA4 (Fig. 7B). Furthermore, to elucidate the degradation pathway of NCOA4, we found that treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 inhibited its degradation, indicating that the knockdown of ARIH1 leads to NCOA4 degradation through the proteasomal pathway (Fig. 7C).

  Fig. 7ARIH1 binds to NCOA4 and inhibits NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy. (A) Western blot analysis showing that knockdown of ARIH1 increased the protein level of NCOA4, but had minimal effects on TFR, TF, and FPN1 in LUAD cells. (B) Cycloheximide (CHX) chase assay indicating that ARIH1 knockdown prolonged the half-life of NCOA4 protein compared with the control group. (C) Western blot analysis showing that proteasome inhibitor MG132 rescued the reduction of NCOA4 degradation in control cells but not in ARIH1-depleted cells, suggesting a proteasome-dependent mechanism. (D–E) Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays performed in A549 (D) and HEK293T (E) cells demonstrating the physical interaction between ARIH1 and NCOA4. (F) Ubiquitination assay in HEK293T cells showing that overexpression of ARIH1 increased the ubiquitination level of NCOA4. (G) Ubiquitination assays using HA-tagged ubiquitin mutants (K48R and K63R) confirming that ARIH1 predominantly mediates K48-linked ubiquitination of NCOA4. Data are representative of three independent experiments. β-actin served as a loading control

  Given that ARIH1 is an E3 ubiquitin ligase, we hypothesized that it might directly mediate the ubiquitination of NCOA4, thereby enhancing its degradation. Co-immunoprecipitation assays, both endogenous and exogenous, revealed an interaction between ARIH1 and NCOA4 (Fig. 7D-E). To assess whether ARIH1 mediates the ubiquitination of NCOA4, we co-transfected HA-ubiquitin, Flag-NCOA4, and Myc-ARIH1 plasmids in HEK-293T cells. The ubiquitination assays demonstrated that overexpression of ARIH1 significantly increased the ubiquitination of NCOA4 (Fig. 7F). Additional experiments with ubiquitin mutants (K48R and K63R) indicated that ARIH1 predominantly induces K48-linked ubiquitination of NCOA4 (Fig. 7G), a modification known to target proteins for proteasomal degradation.

  In summary, these findings illustrate that ARIH1 interacts with NCOA4 and facilitates its proteasomal degradation through K48-linked ubiquitination, thereby regulating iron metabolism and contributing to the control of ferroptosis in LUAD cells.


### Knockdown of ARIH1 enhances cisplatin-induced ferroptosis
  Cisplatin is commonly used as a standard chemotherapeutic agent for lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD). However, its effectiveness is often hindered by variable sensitivity and drug resistance. To investigate whether ARIH1 influences cisplatin sensitivity, we assessed cell viability after cisplatin treatment. The knockdown of ARIH1 significantly reduced cell survival compared to the control group, indicating an enhanced cytotoxic response to cisplatin (Fig. 8A).

  LDH release was increased in A549 shARIH1 cells under normal conditions and was further elevated following cisplatin treatment (Fig. 8B). Western blot analysis showed that cisplatin treatment decreased the expression levels of SLC7A11 and GPX4, while increasing the expression of ACSL4 (Fig. 8C). Consistently, the level of the lipid peroxidation marker MDA was significantly higher in ARIH1-deficient cells after cisplatin treatment (Fig. 8D). Moreover, intracellular ferrous iron levels were elevated (Fig. 8E), while the intracellular glutathione (GSH) content was markedly decreased (Fig. 8F).

  To verify death-pathway specificity, TEM was performed on shARIH1 cells treated with cisplatin and various inhibitors. Typical ferroptotic mitochondrial features, such as shrinkage and increased membrane density, were observed in control, Nec-1, and Z-VAD groups. Notably, only Fer-1 effectively restored normal mitochondrial morphology (Fig. 8G). These results confirm that ARIH1 knockdown sensitizes LUAD cells to cisplatin specifically through ferroptosis.


## Discussion
  This study provides a detailed examination of ac4C-related regulatory dynamics in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and identifies ARIH1 as a previously unrecognized suppressor of ferroptosis with significant prognostic and therapeutic implications. By utilizing large-scale single-cell profiling, multi-algorithm modeling, and experimental validation, the researchers developed a robust acRG-based stratification system, revealing ARIH1 as a key oncogenic candidate closely linked to poor clinical outcomes. Functional assays demonstrated that the genetic depletion of ARIH1 severely impaired tumor growth, positioning ARIH1 as a critical factor in LUAD cell survival.Mechanistically, ARIH1 directly interacts with NCOA4, promoting its K48-linked ubiquitination, which accelerates proteasomal degradation. This process restricts ferritinophagy and limits susceptibility to ferroptosis. The findings establish ARIH1 at the intersection of ubiquitination mechanisms and ferroptosis regulation, enhancing our understanding of metabolic death pathways in LUAD.

  To establish the novelty and potential clinical utility of ARIH1 as a biomarker, it is important to contextualize our findings within the rapidly evolving landscape of cancer diagnostics and prognosis. Recent years have seen the development of numerous gene signatures and predictive models aimed at improving patient stratification and outcome prediction. For example, a recent study by Liu et al. proposed a dysregulation of programed cell death signature that showed promising prognostic value in the immune microenvironment of HNSC [32]. Similarly, a disulfidptosis prognostic model incorporating immunotherapy and ICI treatment prediction was developed, demonstrating robust performance in predicting survival outcomes [33]. Compared to these existing signatures, ARIH1 identified in our study offers several distinct advantages. First, ARIH1 exhibits a strong and consistent association with colorectal cancer across multiple independent cohorts, suggesting its robustness as a biomarker [34]. Second, its expression levels are readily measurable and show high specificity, which may facilitate clinical translation. Moreover, recent evidence has highlighted ARIH1 activates STING-mediated T-cell activation and sensitizes tumors to immune checkpoint blockade [35], further supporting its biological relevance. Rather than replacing existing models, ARIH1 may serve as a complementary marker that enhances the predictive power of current signatures, particularly when integrated with immune- or TME-based classifiers. Future studies exploring such integrative approaches could provide a more comprehensive framework for precision oncology.

  In the context of immunotherapy, it is essential to consider the complex interactions within the tumor microenvironment (TME), as these significantly modulate immune responses and ultimately determine therapeutic efficacy. The TME comprises a heterogeneous population of immune cells, stromal components, cytokines, and tumor-derived factors that can either promote or suppress anti-tumor immunity. For instance, recent studies have highlighted the dual role of cytokines such as IL-6 and TGF-β in shaping immune cell function and contributing to immune evasion [36]. Additionally, the composition and spatial organization of immune cells within the TME have been shown to correlate with response to immune checkpoint inhibitors [33]. A deeper understanding of these multifaceted interactions is crucial for developing more effective combination strategies and overcoming resistance to immunotherapy. Future studies integrating TME profiling with immune-based therapies may provide valuable insights into optimizing treatment outcomes in cancer patients.

  Ferroptosis has recently gained attention as a potentially exploitable vulnerability in various malignancies, particularly in those that rely on iron metabolism and redox balance. LUAD often exhibits heightened iron utilization, abnormal ROS handling, and reliance on GPX4-dependent antioxidants, making ferroptosis an attractive therapeutic strategy. This study shows that ARIH1 knockdown leads to NCOA4 accumulation, ferritin degradation, intracellular Fe2+ overload, lipid peroxidation, and ultimately, lethal ferroptotic collapse. The increased sensitivity to cisplatin in the absence of ARIH1 suggests that targeting both ARIH1 and ferroptosis-inducing chemotherapy could overcome intrinsic or acquired drug resistance, a significant hurdle in contemporary clinical management. In this context, inhibiting ARIH1 may lower the threshold for ferroptotic execution, enhancing therapeutic efficacy without depending solely on cytotoxic agents.

  A major strength of this research lies in the combination of single-cell transcriptomics, ac4C-related network analysis, machine learning for biomarker extraction, and mechanistic validation. While many computational studies report statistical correlations, they often do not progress to biochemical mechanistic proof; however, in this study, ARIH1 was advanced from bioinformatic prioritization to molecular dissection and phenotypic confirmation. This approach offers a scalable model for converting multi-omics discoveries into potential therapeutic targets. Additionally, identifying ARIH1 as an E3 ligase that modulates NCOA4 ubiquitination implies broader implications for ferroptosis regulation in other cancers characterized by dysregulated iron metabolism.

  However, several aspects need further refinement in future investigations. First, although in vitro experiments showed clear growth inhibition upon ARIH1 depletion, further exploration across additional LUAD genetic backgrounds and metastasis-competent models is necessary to ensure generalizability. Second, pharmacological inhibition of ARIH1 is currently not available; developing small-molecule degraders or ligase-substrate disruptors would greatly improve clinical translatability. Lastly, since ferroptosis is influenced by immune and stromal compartments, investigating ARIH1-regulated interactions within the tumor microenvironment-particularly concerning macrophage iron recycling, T-cell exhaustion, and myeloid-derived suppressor cell metabolism-may reveal further therapeutic synergies.

  In summary, this study identifies ARIH1 as a crucial suppressor of ferritinophagy-driven ferroptosis, establishing its prognostic and functional significance in LUAD. The integration of genomic risk prediction and mechanistic validation positions ARIH1 as a promising therapeutic target and suggests that interventions aimed at inducing ferroptosis could be a viable strategy to overcome treatment resistance. The continued development of ARIH1-targeted inhibition modalities, possibly in combination with platinum-based chemotherapy or ferroptosis inducers, may provide new clinical benefits for LUAD patients and reshape future management strategies.
