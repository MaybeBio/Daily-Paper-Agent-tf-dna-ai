# Anti-glioma mechanism of Angelica sinensis and stigmasterol: p53-associated ferroptosis identified via multi-method analysis


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52662-w.


## Introduction
  Gliomas are characterized as a class of primary central nervous system tumors originating from glial cells, constitute 40.0–50.0% of all intracranial tumors and represent the most common primary malignant brain tumors. They are characterized by rapid progression, high malignancy, surgical challenges for complete resection, and a high recurrence rate1. Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) is the most common subtype of glioma, accounting for approximately 45% of all gliomas. The current clinical standard of care is a comprehensive strategy consisting of surgical resection followed by concurrent chemoradiotherapy with temozolomide (the Stupp protocol). Nevertheless, the prognosis of GBM patients remains extremely poor, with a median survival of only about 15 months and a 5‑year relative survival rate of approximately 5%2,3. Temozolomide, bevacizumab, and other agents are commonly used in the clinical treatment of glioma. However, these drugs often exhibit significant toxic side effects, and tumor cells readily develop resistance due to genetic variations4–6. Treatment resistance and tumor recurrence are mainly driven by three key factors. First, glioma exhibits high genetic heterogeneity. Second, glioma stem cells (GSCs) persist. Third, the tumor microenvironment is highly immunosuppressive and hypoxic7,8. Therefore, it is urgent to break through the survival bottleneck in glioma treatment. Developing novel strategies that can efficiently target tumor cells, overcome drug resistance, and improve patients’ quality of life represents a critical clinical need.

  Complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) plays a vital role in cancer treatment. With its holistic philosophy and capacity to concurrently modulate multiple signaling pathways9, Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) is a pillar of CAM. Chinese herbal medicines (CHM) are an important component of CAM. Studies have shown that CHM can enhance the efficacy of radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy and immunotherapy, while alleviating the associated side effects. CHM exerts anti-tumor effects by inhibiting cancer progression and modulating the host immune system10. The traditional Chinese herb Angelica sinensis (Oliv.) Diels (AS) boasts a documented history of medicinal use spanning more than 2000 years. It exhibits diverse pharmacological activities, such as antioxidant, neuroprotective, and immunomodulatory effects11. The anti-cancer potential of AS is achieved by modulating key cellular pathways, promoting reactive oxygen species generation, and triggering apoptosis12. Previous studies have confirmed that the chloroform extract of AS (AS-C) exhibits significant anti-GBM activity in vitro and in vivo. It induces cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, inhibits tumor growth, prolongs survival, and shows no obvious cytotoxicity13. Regarding the nervous system, stigmasterol (S), an active component of AS, demonstrates protective effects on neuronal cells. Experimental studies suggest its therapeutic potential for ischemic stroke and related neurological disorders14. S helps maintain intracellular reactive oxygen species homeostasis in neural cells, protects against oxidative stress-induced cell death, and mitigates mitochondrial dysfunction and DNA damage15.

  Ferroptosis is an iron-driven, regulated form of cell death, distinguished primarily by disrupted iron homeostasis and the toxic buildup of lipid peroxides. It was first identified and reported by Dixon et al.16. It differs morphologically and mechanistically from other programmed cell death pathways such as apoptosis and autophagy. Characteristic ultrastructural alterations comprise diminished or lost mitochondrial cristae, disruption of the outer mitochondrial membrane, and increased membrane density17. Accordingly, this research seeks to examine the inhibitory mechanism of AS and its constituent, S, against glioma, which may involve ferroptosis-related pathways.

  Network pharmacology is an interdisciplinary field. It combines computer science and biological network analysis to systematically investigate the complex interactions among compounds, targets, and diseases. In the context of TCM, network pharmacology acts as a scientific method to clarify the compatibility relationships and intrinsic mechanisms of action of Chinese herbal preparations18. It identifies therapeutic mechanisms of drugs for treating diseases at the level of biological targets and pathways. The study of effective mechanisms underlying the multi-target and integrated therapeutic effects of TCM aligns well with the application of network pharmacology19. This research adopted a multi-stage computational and experimental strategy. The study commenced with network pharmacology implementation20,21. In this study, we integrated network pharmacology, protein‑protein interaction (PPI) network construction, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses, and molecular docking to systematically explore the potential targets and signaling mechanisms of AS and its active component S against glioma. The network pharmacology analyses of AS and S were not conducted independently. Instead, a progressive screening strategy of “from herbal formula to single component” was adopted. First, network pharmacology analysis based on the overall components of AS was performed to identify core pathways and potential targets associated with anti‑glioma effects. Then, we focused on its main active constituent S to further verify its binding potential and regulatory effects on core targets, thereby providing a clear research direction for subsequent in vitro experiments.


## Materials and methods

### Network pharmacology section

#### Identification of active constituents in AS and forecast of potential targets
  The bioactive components of AS were retrieved from the TCMSP (https://tcmspw.com/tcmsp.php). To identify compounds with favorable pharmacokinetic profiles, we applied screening thresholds of oral bioavailability (OB) ≥ 30% and drug-likeness (DL) ≥ 0.18. The potential protein targets of the screened constituents were forecasted with the assistance of the TCMSP, ETCM (http://www.tcmip.cn/ETCM/), HERB (http://herb.ac.cn/), and SwissTargetPrediction (http://swisstargetprediction.ch/). We compiled the predicted targets and eliminated redundant entries. Standardization of all target gene nomenclature was performed using the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/).


#### Collection of glioma-associated targets
  Glioma-associated genes were sourced from multiple authoritative biomedical databases, including GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/) and OMIM (https://www.omim.org/), using “glioma” as the search keyword. After removing duplicate entries, a consolidated set of targets associated with glioma was curated.


#### Identifying shared targets and constructing an interaction network
  The Venn diagram was generated employing the Venny 2.1 web-based platform (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/), which helped identify the overlapping genes between AS’s predicted targets and glioma-associated targets. The overlapping targets were defined as potential therapeutic candidates, based on which a drug-compound-target-disease network was constructed and graphically represented using Cytoscape (v3.10.2).


#### PPI Network construction and screening of key targets
  To build a PPI network, the common gene set was submitted to the STRING database (https://string-db.org/). The resulting network was imported into and visualized with Cytoscape 3.10.2. Core targets were then screened for using the CytoNCA plugin by applying three topological algorithms (Betweenness unDir, Closeness unDir, and Degree unDir), with consistency across all algorithms above the set threshold being the selection criterion.


#### GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis
  To decipher the biological significance of the shared targets, GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were conducted. All analytical procedures were implemented on the bioinformatics platform (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/). The results of GO enrichment were categorized into three aspects: biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF). We also performed KEGG pathway analysis to identify crucial signaling pathways, using a statistical significance threshold of P < 0.05. The results were then graphically represented using R packages “ggplot2” and “enrichplot”.


#### Molecular docking simulation
  Molecular docking was carried out to assess the binding interactions between the bioactive constituents and the core protein targets. The three-dimensional structures of key protein targets (TNF: 4QQ2, PTGS2: 5F19, GSK3B: 4AFJ, HMGCR: 8DJM, PPARG: 3E00) were sourced from the RCSB PDB (https://www.rcsb.org/), while the structures of β-sitosterol and S were obtained from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Molecular docking simulations were conducted with AutoDockTools 1.5.7 following structural preparation. The resulting docking poses were visualized and analyzed in PyMOL 4.6, and binding interactions were evaluated to determine complex stability.


### In vitro experiments section

#### Cell source and culture
  Human glioma U251-MG cells were purchased from Haixing Biotechnology (Catalog No. TCH-C366-UD). U251-MG cells were maintained in DMEM medium (Procell, China) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Corning, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin solution (Beyotime, China). Human astrocyte SVG P12 cells were obtained from Yimo Biotechnology (Catalog No. IM-H489). SVG P12 cells were maintained in a specialized medium (Saien Biotechnology, China). All cell lines were maintained in a humidified incubator set at 37 °C and supplied with 5% CO2 for culture.


#### Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay
  To evaluate the effect of the drug on the viability of U251-MG glioma cells, this study used a CCK-8 assay kit (Biosharp, China) for detection. We seeded the cells into 96-well plates (1 × 104 per well). The plates were then incubated overnight to facilitate cell attachment. Following this, the cells were exposed for 24 h to concentration gradients of AS (Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd., B26043; 0, 5, 10, 15, 20 mg/mL), β-sitosterol (MCE, HY-N0171A0; 10, 20, 40, 80, 160, 320 μg/mL), and S (MCE, HY-N0131; 0, 30, 60, 90, 120, 150, 180 μg/mL). AS (Yuanye Bio-Technology, B26043-1g) was dissolved in PBS, S (MCE, HY-N0131) in acetone, and Pifithrin-α (PFT-α) (MCE, HY-15484) and Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) (MCE, HY-100579) in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). Corresponding solvent control groups were included for all treatments, with the final solvent concentration not exceeding 0.1%. After the treatment period, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was introduced into each cell-containing well. The plates were subsequently maintained at 37 °C for 1 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was subsequently measured using a Bio-Rad microplate reader. Relative cell viability was calculated by normalization to the control group, and the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) was determined through nonlinear regression analysis using GraphPad Prism 9.5. Detailed information on AS, β-sitosterol, and S can be found in the supplementary materials.


#### Cytotoxicity assay
  The cytotoxic effects of the test compounds on normal SVG P12 human astrocytes were assessed via CCK-8 assay. We seeded the cells into 96-well plates (1 × 104 per well). Following adhesion, the cells were exposed for 24 h to a concentration gradient of AS (0–21 mg/mL) or S (0–180 μg/mL). Corresponding solvent control groups were included to account for potential solvent effects, with the final concentration of each solvent not exceeding 0.1%. After the treatment phase, we supplemented each well with 10 μL of CCK-8 solution and placed the plates in an incubator at 37 °C for 1 h. The absorbance was then quantified at 450 nm with a Bio-Rad microplate reader. Cell viability was normalized as a percentage of the control, and the IC50 values were derived through nonlinear regression in GraphPad Prism 9.5.


#### Colony formation assay
  Following plating in 6-well plates(700 per well), U251-MG cells were treated with either AS (0, 5, 10, 15 mg/mL) or S (0, 40, 80, 120 μg/mL). Separate solvent control groups were included to account for potential solvent toxicity. The cells were maintained in culture for 10–15 days, with medium changes performed every 4 days. Following colony formation, the cells were fixed and stained, then imaged, with subsequent quantification of colony numbers performed using ImageJ software.


#### Wound healing assay
  U251-MG cells were cultured in 6-well plates until they reached 90% confluency. Linear scratches were created using a sterile 200 μL pipette tip. We replaced the medium with serum-free medium supplemented with AS (0, 5, 10, 15 mg/mL) or S (0, 40, 80, 120 μg/mL) following PBS washes. Separate solvent control groups were established to assess potential solvent toxicity. Images of the wound areas were captured at 0, 24, 36, and 48 h using an inverted fluorescence microscope. The migration rate was quantified using ImageJ software.


#### Transwell migration and invasion assays
  Transwell chambers (8 μm pore size, Corning) were utilized to assess the migration and invasion abilities of the cells. Prior to the invasion assay, the polycarbonate membranes were coated with Matrigel matrix (Corning) as an initial step. We plated U251-MG cells in the upper Transwell chambers at 2 × 104 cells/well (200 μL, 2% FBS) and added 600 μL medium (20% FBS) as a chemoattractant to the lower chambers. The cells were then treated with AS (0–15 mg/mL) or S (0–120 μg/mL) for 24 h. Corresponding solvent control groups were included to ensure final solvent concentrations did not exceed 0.1%, thereby excluding potential solvent toxicity effects. To quantify cell migration, the migrated cells on the underside of the membrane were fixed and stained for visualization, and cells in five random fields were manually counted.


#### BODIPY-C11 assay
  U251-MG cells were cultured in 6-well plates at 37 °C and treated for 24 h with different concentrations of S (0, 120 μg/mL), PFT-α (20 μM), S + PFT-α, or S + Fer-1 (5 μM). After digestion, the cells were transferred to microtubes and centrifuged. A 100 mM stock solution of BODIPY-C11 (Cell Signaling Technology, USA) was diluted to a working concentration of 2 μM. Each tube was incubated with 1 mL of C11 dilution buffer. After incubation, the cells were washed three times with PBS and then analyzed by flow cytometry.


#### Mitochondrial membrane potential (JC-1) assay
  The effect of S on the mitochondrial membrane potential of U251-MG cells was assessed via flow cytometry using the JC-1 fluorescent probe (Solarbio, China). Briefly, U251-MG cells in the logarithmic growth phase were plated in 6-well plates and exposed to different treatments—including S (0, 120 μg/mL), PFT-α (20 μM), S + PFT-α, and S + Fer-1 (5 μM)—for 24 h. After treatment, the cells were resuspended at a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL and mixed with an equal volume of JC-1 working solution followed by incubation. Subsequently, the cells were washed twice with ice-cold 1 × JC-1 staining buffer, resuspended in 500 μL of the same buffer, and subjected to flow cytometric analysis within 30 min.


#### Reactive oxygen species (ROS) detection
  Intracellular ROS levels were quantified by flow cytometric analysis with the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe (Solarbio, China). For this purpose, U251-MG cells seeded in 6-well plates were subjected to 24-h treatments with S (0 and 120 μg/mL), PFT-α (20 μM), S + PFT-α, or S + Fer-1 (5 μM). Subsequently, the cells were resuspended to a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL and washed twice with DMEM. Then, 1 mL of DMEM working solution containing the DCFH-DA probe (working dilution 1:5000) was added to each EP tube, followed by incubation with gentle mixing at 5-min intervals. Following incubation, the cells were subjected to centrifugation, washed with DMEM, resuspended in 500 μL of DMEM, and finally analyzed by flow cytometry within a 30-min timeframe.


#### Malondialdehyde (MDA) assay
  The effect of S treatment on MDA content in U251-MG cells was determined using the thiobarbituric acid method (Solarbio, China). After treatment with different concentrations of S (0, 120 μg/mL), PFT-α, S + PFT-α (20 μM), or S + Fer-1 (5 μM) for 24 h, the cells were collected. Cells (5 × 105) were lysed in 1 mL of extraction buffer with ice-bath sonication. After centrifugation, 100 μL of supernatant was mixed with 300 μL MDA working solution (prepared from Reagents I and II) and 100 μL Reagent III, using equal-volume distilled water as blank. The mixture was reacted at 100 °C for 60 min, cooled on ice, and centrifuged at room temperature for 10 min. Then, 200μL supernatant was transferred to a 96-well plate for absorbance measurement at 532 nm and 600 nm using a microplate reader. MDA content = 53.763 × ΔA ÷ N (whereΔA = ΔA532 − ΔA600; N = total number of cells, in units of 104).


#### Reduced glutathione (GSH) assay
  Intracellular GSH levels were measured using a colorimetric assay (Solarbio, China). U251-MG cells treated with different concentrations of S (0, 120 μg/mL), PFT-α (20 μM), S + PFT-α, or S + Fer-1 (5 μM) for 24 h were collected. Cells (5 × 106) were lysed in 1 mL of Reagent I and disrupted via sonication in an ice bath. The supernatant was collected after homogenate centrifugation for analysis. A 10 mg/mL GSH standard was sequentially diluted to concentrations ranging from 25 to 300 μg/mL. Twenty microliters of sample, standard, or blank were added to a 96-well plate, followed by 140 μL of Reagent II and 40 μL of Reagent III. After incubation at room temperature, the absorbance was measured at 412 nm. A standard curve was generated by plotting standard concentrations against corresponding ΔA values, and a linear equation was established to calculate the GSH concentration in the samples. GSH content = x ÷ N (where N represents the total number of cells, in units of 106).


#### Ferrous ion (Fe2+) assay
  The effect of S on intracellular Fe2+ levels in U251-MG cells was determined using a colorimetric assay (Solarbio, China). After treatment with different concentrations of S (0, 120 μg/mL), PFT-α (20 μM), S + PFT-α, or S + Fer-1 (5 μM) for 24 h, approximately 2 × 106 cells were collected. The cells were lysed with 1 mL of extraction buffer and subjected to ultrasonic disruption in an ice bath. The supernatant was collected after centrifugation of the homogenate for subsequent analysis. A 40 mmol/L standard solution was serially diluted to concentrations ranging from 0.78 to 100 μmol/L. Subsequently, 200 μL of sample, standard, or distilled water (blank) was mixed with 100 μL of Reagent I and incubated. Following incubation, the absorbance of the samples was measured at 595 nm using a 96-well plate. A standard curve was constructed by plotting the standard concentrations (x-axis) against ΔA_standard (A_standard—A_blank, y-axis), and a linear regression equation was derived. The Fe2+ concentration (x, μg/mL) of each sample was determined by substituting its ΔA value into the regression equation. Fe2+ content = 0.001x ÷ N (where N = total number of cells, in units of 107).


#### Western blot (WB) analysis
  Target protein expression was analyzed by Western blotting. Total proteins were extracted and quantified using the BCA method (Biosharp, China), followed by SDS-PAGE separation and transfer onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). After blocking (Epizyme, China), the membranes were sequentially incubated with primary antibodies (overnight at 4 °C) and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (room temperature). After ECL chemiluminescence development, band intensities were assessed using ImageJ software, and relative expression levels were computed as the ratio of target protein to internal reference. The primary rabbit polyclonal antibodies used in this study included: MMP2 (Proteintech, 81515-5-RR, 1:10000), MMP9 (Proteintech, 82854-8-RR, 1:10000), p53 (Proteintech, 10442-1-AP, 1:10000), phospho-p53 (Proteintech, 28961-1-AP, 1:4000), GPX4 (Abcam, AB125066, 1:10000), SLC7A11 (Proteintech, 26864-1-AP, 1:4000), ACSL4 (Proteintech, 22401-1-AP, 1:10000), Tubulin (Huaan Biotechnology, #ER130905, 1:10000), and GAPDH (Proteintech, 10494-1-AP, 1:10000). The secondary antibody used was HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Proteintech, SA00001-2, 1:20000).


#### Immunofluorescence (IF)
  U251-MG cells were treated with different concentrations of S (0, 120 μg/mL), PFT-α (20 μM), S + PFT-α, or S + Fer-1 (5 μM). Cells were fixed and permeabilized, blocked with 5% BSA, and sequentially incubated with primary antibodies (overnight at 4 °C) and fluorescent secondary antibodies (1 h at room temperature in the dark). After DAPI (Beyotime, China) mounting, images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope, and the mean fluorescence intensity was quantified with ImageJ software. The primary rabbit polyclonal antibodies used in this study were GPX4 (Abcam, AB125066, 1:100) and ACSL4 (Proteintech, 22401-1-AP, 1:100), while the secondary antibody was an iFluor™594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG polyclonal antibody (Huaan Bio, #HA1122, 1:300).


## Statistical analysis
  All experimental results were statistically analyzed and visualized using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.5). For comparing two groups, an unpaired Student’s t-test was utilized, while one-way ANOVA was employed for comparisons among multiple groups. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All experiments were performed in three biological replicates, and each experiment included at least three technical replicates. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (± SD).


## Results

### Network pharmacology section of AS against glioma

#### Prediction of active components, targets, and mechanisms of AS against glioma
  Based on the TCMSP database, β-sitosterol and S were identified as the active components of AS. Data indicate that these components are associated with cancers such as breast cancer and bladder cancer, as well as neurological diseases. A total of 199 potential targets of AS were selected from TCMSP, HERB, ETCM, and UniProt databases. In the meantime, 3923 glioma-associated target genes were obtained from GeneCards and OMIM databases. Venn diagram analysis revealed 102 overlapping targets between AS and glioma (Fig. 1A), which are considered potential mediators of its anti-glioma activity. A drug-compound-target-disease network was built to map these interactions (Fig. 1B). The network comprises AS (dark green), its bioactive compounds (orange), the 102 overlapping targets (blue/light green/purple), and glioma (yellow), illustrating AS’s multi-component, multi-target nature. #### Building a PPI network and identifying core hub genes of AS against glioma
  To clarify the interplay between the 102 shared AS-glioma targets, we built a PPI network via the STRING database and mapped it in Cytoscape 3.10.2. As depicted in Fig. 1C, the PPI network revealed closely interconnected relationships between the targets, indicating that AS might exert its anti-glioma effects via complex biological pathways. To further pinpoint key genes in the network, topological analysis was conducted with the CytoNCA plugin. Three algorithms (Betweenness, Closeness, Degree) were applied, yielding a consensus set of 28 core targets identified by all methods. The top five prioritized genes were TNF, PTGS2, GSK3B, HMGCR, and PPARG (Fig. 1D). It is known that these genes take part in processes including apoptosis, metabolic regulation, inflammatory response, and tumor progression, which points to their potential key roles in AS’s anti-glioma effects.


#### GO and KEGG enrichment analysis of AS against glioma
  To investigate the biological functions of the common targets between AS and glioma, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were conducted. As illustrated in Fig. 1E, GO analysis indicated that the target genes were significantly enriched in biological processes (BP), including RNA polymerase II‑mediated pri‑miRNA transcription, myocyte proliferation, and response to external stimuli such as hormones and pathogens, implying their involvement in transcriptional regulation, cellular processes, and immune‑metabolic activities. With respect to cellular components (CC), the targets were predominantly distributed in membrane rafts, cyclin‑dependent kinase holoenzyme complexes, and related structures, suggesting potential roles in signal transduction and cell cycle regulation. In terms of molecular functions (MF), the targets were notably associated with DNA‑binding transcription factor binding, nuclear receptor activity, protease binding, and other molecular events, highlighting their regulatory functions in gene expression and protein stability. KEGG pathway analysis (Fig. 1F) revealed that the targets were significantly enriched in the AGE‑RAGE signaling pathway in diabetic complications, TNF signaling pathway, MAPK signaling pathway, and PD‑L1 expression and PD‑1 checkpoint pathway in cancer. These pathways are widely implicated in stress response, immune regulation, tumorigenesis, and neural system–related processes. Collectively, these findings suggest that AS may execute its anti‑glioma effects via synergistic modulation of multiple signaling pathways, thus providing a rational basis for further screening of key active components and in‑depth mechanistic investigations.


#### Docking of bioactive compounds with core targets of AS against glioma
  To verify the binding potential of key targets and active components obtained from network pharmacology and PPI analysis, molecular docking was performed to evaluate the interactions between two active compounds of AS (β‑sitosterol and S) and core target proteins (TNF, PTGS2, GSK3B, HMGCR, PPARG). The crystal structures of the top five targets ranked by Degree value were selected as receptors, including 4QQ2 (TNF), 5F19 (PTGS2), 4AFJ (GSK3B), 8DJM (HMGCR), and 3E00 (PPARG). As shown in Fig. 1G, all docking binding energies ranged from − 4.05 to − 6.17 kcal/mol, with most values below − 5 kcal/mol, indicating favorable binding capacities between the active components and target proteins. Further visualization analysis demonstrated that β‑sitosterol and S could form stable hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions with core targets such as TNF and PPARG. Upon binding to TNF (4QQ2), β‑sitosterol formed a hydrogen bond with PRO‑7 (3.3 Å) and established stable interactions with surrounding hydrophobic residues, while S exhibited tight packing within the hydrophobic pocket of TNF. Upon binding to PPARG (3E00), both compounds formed key hydrogen bonds with the DA‑3019 region (2.5 Å) and established a stable hydrophobic interaction network (Fig. 1H). These results validate the reliability of the binding between core targets and active components, suggesting that the active ingredients of AS may exert anti‑glioma effects by targeting these proteins.


### Screening of active compounds by CCK-8 assay
  Combined with the results of network pharmacology screening, β‑sitosterol and S, which exhibited high binding potential to glioma targets in AS, were selected for in vitro activity validation. CCK‑8 assay results demonstrated that both compounds inhibited the proliferation of U251‑MG cells in a concentration‑dependent manner. The IC50 value of S (118.4 μg/mL) was significantly lower than that of β‑sitosterol (226.2 μg/mL), indicating that S possessed stronger anti‑glioma proliferative activity. #### GO and KEGG enrichment analysis of S against glioma
  To explore the biological functions of the shared targets between S and glioma, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were conducted. GO analysis (Fig. 3C) showed significant enrichment in the BP category for processes like intracellular receptor signaling, transcription initiation from RNA polymerase II promoter, and cellular calcium ion homeostasis. This implicates the targets in regulating gene expression, maintaining ion balance, and modulating immune stress responses. In the CC category, the targets were primarily localized in structures including NMDA-selective glutamate receptor complex, membrane raft, and plasma membrane raft, indicating their potential association with neural cell communication and membrane function organization. Within the MF category, the targets showed a significant association with nuclear receptor activity and ligand-activated transcription factor activity, which reflects their involvement in transcriptional regulation and signal transduction. KEGG pathway analysis (Fig. 3D) demonstrated that the targets were significantly enriched in pathways such as the cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) signaling pathway, p53 signaling pathway, and PD-L1 expression and PD-1 checkpoint pathway in cancer. These pathways are widely involved in cellular metabolism, immune regulation, and tumorigenesis, suggesting that S may exert its anti-glioma effects by modulating these diverse biological mechanisms.


### In vitro experiments section of AS and S against glioma

#### Functional validation of the anti-glioma effects of AS and S in U251-MG cells
  In this study, network pharmacology was employed as a tool for hypothesis generation and target screening. Through target enrichment analysis of AS, core regulatory pathways associated with glioma proliferation, migration, and invasion (such as the p53 signaling pathway) were identified, and S was preliminarily determined as the active component. After verifying that AS exhibits significant anti-glioma activity, in vitro experiments further confirmed that S can suppress the malignant phenotypes of U251-MG cells. CCK-8 assay results demonstrated that after 24 h of drug treatment, cell survival rates decreased significantly with increasing drug concentrations. The IC50 values were 12.38 mg/mL for AS and 118.4 μg/mL for S (Fig. 4A-a,E-a), signifying that both have significant anti-proliferative activity. The colony formation assay further confirmed that as drug concentrations increased (AS: 5–15 mg/mL; S: 40–120 μg/mL), the colony-forming capacity of the cells was markedly inhibited (Fig. 4A-b,E-b). Wound healing assay results demonstrated that 48-h drug treatment effectively delayed cell migration, with the highest concentration groups (15 mg/mL AS and 120 μg/mL S) showing the most significant inhibitory effects (Fig. 4B,F).   In addition, to evaluate the effect of S on ferroptosis in U251-MG cells, we also detected a number of biochemical indicators related to ferroptosis. After treatment with 120 μg/mL S, the malondialdehyde (MDA) content in cells was significantly increased (Fig. 6I), the glutathione (GSH) level was significantly decreased (Fig. 6J), and the intracellular Fe2+ concentration was sharply elevated (Fig. 6K). These results consistently indicate that S can effectively induce ferroptosis. To further explore its potential mechanism, we performed intervention experiments using the p53 inhibitor PFT-α and the ferroptosis-specific inhibitor Fer-1, respectively. The results showed that Fer-1 could significantly reverse the S-induced increase in MDA, depletion of GSH, and accumulation of Fe2+; in contrast, PFT-α could only partially alleviate the changes in the aforementioned biochemical indicators and failed to achieve the complete reversal effect of Fer-1, suggesting that the p53 pathway is involved in the ferroptosis process induced by S but is not the only regulatory pathway. Under S intervention, the expression of key ferroptosis molecules such as ACSL4, GPX4, and SLC7A11 undergoes significant changes, indicating that the p53 pathway may be involved, and there are other non-p53-dependent regulatory pathways that jointly mediate the ferroptosis process.


## Discussion
  This study systematically investigated the potential mechanisms underlying the anti-glioma effects of AS and S using network pharmacology and molecular docking approaches. The core targets identified—TNF, PTGS2, GSK3B, HMGCR, and PPARG—are involved in inflammation, metabolism, proliferation, and apoptosis. KEGG enrichment analysis suggested that S may exert its effects through the p53 signaling pathway. Combined with the molecular docking results, both β‑sitosterol and S could form stable hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions with the core anti‑glioma targets of AS (such as TNF and PPARG), with low binding energies. These findings suggest that the active components of AS possess favorable binding potential with key glioma targets, further supporting the rationale underlying the anti‑glioma effect of AS as a whole, and providing important theoretical basis and experimental direction for subsequent focusing on the core active component (S) and in‑depth exploration of its specific anti‑glioma mechanisms. Some targets predicted by network pharmacology (such as TNF‑α and PPARs) were not experimentally validated in this study, as the present research focused on the core regulatory axis of “p53‑ferroptosis”. The above‑unvalidated targets reflect the holistic regulatory characteristics of AS featuring “multiple components, multiple targets, and multiple pathways”, which can provide a reference for future expanded studies.

  Experimental validation demonstrated that S significantly inhibited the viability, proliferation, migration, and invasion of U251-MG cells. S treatment induced ferroptosis, characterized by enhanced lipid peroxidation, decreased mitochondrial membrane potential, ROS accumulation, increased MDA levels, reduced GSH levels, and Fe2⁺ accumulation. Western blot results showed that S significantly upregulated p-p53 (ser15) protein expression (P < 0.05) and regulated ferroptosis-related proteins: downregulation of GPX4 and SLC7A11, and upregulation of ACSL4 (P < 0.05). Mechanistic studies revealed that the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 reversed S-induced ferroptosis phenotypes. While the p53 inhibitor PFT-α alone showed no effect, it significantly attenuated the pro-ferroptotic effects induced by S. Western blot analysis indicated that both PFT-α and Fer-1 partially restored p-p53 (ser15) expression; furthermore, PFT-α disrupted the regulatory effects of S on ferroptosis-related proteins. Fluorescence microscopy further supported these findings. In conclusion, S induces ferroptosis and suppresses proliferation in U251-MG cells by activating the p53 pathway and regulating key ferroptosis molecules, providing a rationale for its development as a therapeutic agent for glioma. As another important active component in AS, β‑sitosterol exhibited weaker anti‑proliferative activity against glioma in the present study. However, its favorable binding potential with core targets suggests that it may participate in synergistic regulation via multiple signaling pathways, and its specific anti‑glioma mechanism warrants further in‑depth investigation.

  S exhibits multi-mechanistic antitumor activity in various cancers. In GBM, it not only suppresses cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and vasculogenic mimicry but also induces apoptosis. Further investigation in this study uncovers that S activates the p53 pathway, a mechanism that ultimately triggers ferroptosis, thereby emphasizing the compound’s promise as a treatment for glioma25–27. In gastric cancer, S inhibits the Akt/mTOR pathway to simultaneously induce apoptosis and protective autophagy, with in vivo experiments confirming significant tumor growth suppression28–30. In breast cancer, S downregulates Bcl-2 and BCL-XL (P < 0.05), activates apoptotic pathways, and inhibits in vivo tumor proliferation in a dose-dependent manner31–34. In hepatocellular carcinoma, S upregulates Bax and p53, downregulates Bcl-2, activates caspase-8/-9, and induces apoptosis via the mitochondrial pathway35–38. Additionally, S demonstrates chemopreventive potential by counteracting 7,12-dimethylbenz[a]anthracene (DMBA)-induced genotoxicity, with its antioxidant and antigenotoxic properties contributing to its anticancer effects39. In cholangiocarcinoma, S inhibits angiogenesis and inflammation by targeting the TNF-α-VEGFR-2 axis, thereby suppressing tumor growth40. In summary, S modulates multiple pathways—including apoptosis, autophagy, ferroptosis, metabolism, oxidative stress, and angiogenesis—demonstrating broad prospects as a therapeutic agent for diverse cancer types.

  Gu’s team first reported that p53 can enhance cellular sensitivity to ferroptosis by transcriptionally repressing the expression of SLC7A11, thereby inhibiting cystine uptake and reducing GSH levels41. Previous studies have demonstrated that SLC7A11 is a downstream target of p53, which can suppress transcription by binding to response elements in its promoter region, thereby increasing the sensitivity of cancer cells to ferroptosis inducers such as erastin. Notably, the acetylation-deficient mutant p53<sup>3KR</sup> (K117/161/162R), although lacking the ability to induce cell cycle arrest, senescence and apoptosis, still effectively inhibits SLC7A11 and triggers ferroptosis, particularly under ROS stress41. Further studies have shown that acetylation of the DNA-binding domain of p53 is critical for its regulation of SLC7A1142. SLC7A11 is frequently overexpressed in various human cancers, including colorectal, liver and renal cancers41,43,44. These findings provided early evidence that ferroptosis exerts tumor-suppressive functions. The occurrence of ferroptosis is closely associated with dysregulated metabolism of iron, lipids, amino acids and ROS. As an important regulator of cellular metabolism, p53 participates in the modulation of ferroptosis by coordinating the above metabolic processes44.

  Ferroptosis is precisely regulated at multiple levels, involving key processes such as the antioxidant system, transcription factors, and metabolic reprogramming45. As an important tumor suppressor protein, p53 is widely involved in cell fate determination. In addition to regulating apoptosis, autophagy, and the cell cycle, p53 also participates in ferroptosis at both transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels. Detection of specific phosphorylation sites of p53 (e.g., Ser15) can more accurately reflect its activation status46. Since phosphorylation is a critical hallmark of p53 activation, phosphorylated p53 levels better represent the actual activation degree of this pathway than total p5347,48. Glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) serves as a core regulator of ferroptosis. Depending on GPX4 dependence, p53 mediates ferroptosis through two distinct regulatory patterns 41,45,49: The classical pathway mainly functions by modulating iron, lipid, amino acid metabolism, and ROS homeostasis50–53. The key cascade involves transcriptional repression of SLC7A11 by p53, reduced cystine uptake, decreased GSH levels, inhibited GPX4 activity, and upregulated ACSL4, thereby promoting ferroptosis. The nonclassical pathway is GPX4-independent and induces ferroptosis via lipid peroxidation mediated by lipoxygenases, iPLA2β, or other mediators54–56. Consistent with our results, S elevated p53 phosphorylation, downregulated SLC7A11 and GPX4, and upregulated ACSL4, which aligns with the classical p53-mediated ferroptosis pathway, suggesting that p53-related signaling contributes to S-induced ferroptosis. However, pretreatment with the p53 inhibitor PFT-α only partially reversed the regulatory effects of S on GPX4 and SLC7A11 expression, without significantly alleviating lipid peroxidation or ROS elevation. These results indicate that p53 signaling plays a partial, but not exclusive, role in this process. Notably, this study did not perform p53 knockdown or overexpression to verify the causal relationship between p53 activation and the expression changes of ACSL4, GPX4, and SLC7A11. Conclusions were drawn based only on inhibitor experiments; the precise causal mechanism remains to be further clarified in future studies.

  Targeted regulation of ferroptosis holds great research potential in cancer therapy. p53 participates in ferroptosis regulation through diverse mechanisms, and the related pathways may not only enhance the sensitivity of tumor cells to radiotherapy and pharmacotherapy but also possess potential value in predicting therapeutic efficacy and prognosis. The combined application of ferroptosis inducers with targeted drugs, chemotherapeutic agents, and immune checkpoint inhibitors is expected to further improve the efficacy of antitumor therapy49. Prognostic models for head and neck squamous cell carcinoma constructed based on ferroptosis-related genes can help guide precise and individualized treatment, and their mechanisms may be closely associated with the immune microenvironment and TP53 mutation status57. Although targeted regulation of ferroptosis represents a promising anticancer strategy, the underlying molecular mechanisms still require further in-depth investigation. Future translational medicine research will help clarify the regulatory role of p53 in ferroptosis and provide novel insights and experimental evidence for cancer therapy.


## Limitations
  This study has several limitations. First, although the network pharmacology analysis provided a relatively comprehensive prediction of the mechanisms, it is inherently a computational simulation approach and may have missed potential targets of AS and S. Second, systematic evaluation of the blood–brain barrier penetration capacity and overall pharmacokinetic profile of S is necessary to inform clinical dosing regimen design and translational applications. Finally, given the complexity of glioma treatment, future studies should include standard therapies such as temozolomide as positive controls to more rigorously validate the antitumor mechanisms and efficacy of S. Notably, the U251‑MG glioma cells used in this study are p53-mutant, and the defective p53 function may affect the interpretation of the related signaling pathway results and the rescue effect of PFT‑α. Therefore, the conclusions regarding p53‑mediated ferroptosis regulation should be interpreted with caution. Meanwhile, all in vitro experiments in this study were based on a single cell line, and the generalizability of the conclusions to other glioma cell subtypes or primary glioma cells remains to be verified. In addition, the safety of S in normal neural cells was only evaluated in SVG P12 cells; its safety in more central nervous system cell types still needs further exploration. In the future, relevant experiments will be further validated in p53 wild‑type glioma cell lines (such as T98G and U87‑MG), primary clinical samples, and more normal neural cell models to enhance the reliability and generalizability of the conclusions. The present study only confirms that p53 signaling is involved in ferroptosis of glioma cells induced by S, but has not directly verified it as an essential regulatory molecule through genetic manipulation. Further studies using p53 knockout and overexpression experiments will be conducted to clarify its causal role.


## Conclusion
  In this study, network pharmacology and experimental validation were combined to reveal that the antiglioma effect of S is closely associated with the induction of ferroptosis. Its mechanism is related to the activation of the p53 signaling pathway, which further regulates key molecules including GPX4 and ACSL4, thereby effectively triggering ferroptosis and inhibiting proliferation in U251-MG cells. These findings provide an experimental basis for S as a potential therapeutic strategy against glioma, while its detailed molecular mechanism and clinical translational value still warrant further in-depth investigation.
