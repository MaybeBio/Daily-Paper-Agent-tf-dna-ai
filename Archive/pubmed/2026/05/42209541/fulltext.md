# Virtual immunohistochemistry by conditional generative adversarial networks


## Abstract
  Histopathological diagnosis is crucial for assisting doctors and veterinarians with timely treatment strategy selection and optimal patient management. After the hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining process, immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining is often required, resulting in a longer turnaround time but providing more disease-specific information. In this study, we propose a Virtual IHC Generative Adversarial Network (VihcGAN) model to transform the H&E stained images of formalin-fixed sections from canine lymph nodes into CD3 and PAX5 IHC stained images, providing a feasible procedure to obtain virtually IHC stained images paired with H&E images. We further propose a novel metric stain Intersection over Union (IoU) to evaluate the accuracy of IHC stained images by incorporating the knowledge of IHC staining, such as the type, number, and position of stained cells. Virtual IHC staining via VihcGAN is computationally fast, with 1 second per image, and thus can bypass time-consuming and costly IHC procedures. Moreover, VihcGAN generates images with a high resolution of 2048××2048, which is much higher than related studies. Experimental results on image quality, metrics, expert grading, and visualization assessment demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach. Source code for VihcGAN and Stain IoU metric are available at https://github.com/coffeeNtv/VihcGAN.


## Introduction
  Immunohistochemistry is a technique that detects the presence of specific antigens in the formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections by the antigen-antibody interactions. During IHC, tissue specimens are generally formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded, sectioned into slices, and stained. Finally, pathologists interpret the IHC results for surgeons to make further diagnoses1. IHC can be applied to confirm the diagnosis of neoplasia such as lung carcinosarcoma, gastric neuroendocrine carcinoma, and murine melanoma, to confirm the subtyping of neoplasia like lymphomas2. IHC is considered the “gold standard”3 for the doctor to make a final diagnosis. Take the general diagnostic case workflow of veterinary anatomic pathologists as an example. The pathologists will make a diagnosis based on collective consideration of clinical history, signalment, and histologic changes on the H&E slides. If lymphoma is highly suspected or diagnosed, IHCs are needed to confirm the diagnosis and/or further categorized the lymphoma into appropriate diagnostic categories. Lymphoma comprises at least 30 separate disease entities, each defined by a unique combination of morphology, immunophenotype, and genetic alterations that result in distinct clinical features, behavior, prognosis, and treatment options. Immunophenotyping via IHCs for the cell lineage is one of the criteria for classifying lymphoma4. Specifically, CD3 and PAX5 are biomarkers of T cells and B cells in dogs, respectively. They are commonly used for the classification of lymphoma. In this study, we used CD3 IHC to identify T-cells and PAX5 IHC to identify B-cells within canine lymph nodes using FFPE tissue sections. While IHC staining delivers an accurate diagnosis and allows the identification of the type of lymphocyte within the section, it takes longer to complete as the tissue needs to be formalin-fixed before IHC staining. Subsequently, the time-consuming IHC procedure of antigen-antibody binding takes several hours to be performed. Therefore, compared to H&E staining, IHC staining has a longer turnaround time. Moreover, IHC also faces many other limitations, such as the cost of equipment to perform IHC, information loss, poor reproducibility that may result from the multi-step procedures.

  IHCs are performed in the laboratory where specific antibodies, special tissue preparation, good tissue quality and adequate tissue quantity, specific protocol, skills, and machinery are required, which may not always be readily available in resource limited areas. At the same time, traditional histopathological examinations typically rely on FFPE blocks stained with H&E5, a process that can be labor-intensive and time-consuming for downstream analysis. With the rapid advancement of digital pathology 6–8 and the growing accessibility of whole slide imaging (WSI), an increasing number of applications powered by artificial intelligence and machine learning have emerged in this field9,10. These applications span a wide range of tasks, including survival prediction11,12, spatial transcriptomics analysis13,14, and more specifically, virtual staining 15–17. Virtual IHC staining will have advantages over traditional IHCs in terms of limited tissue submission, quicker processing and turnaround time, saving resources in terms of chemicals and labor, and improved consistency and reliable results. This study aims to develop a virtual staining approach to evaluate the applicability of virtual IHC staining with high-resolution images. Our virtual staining model can transform the H&E stained images of an FFPE section lymph node sample into their corresponding IHC stained images, which are equivalent to traditional IHC stained images, in a few seconds. This approach could bypass the lengthy histological staining process and provide rapid IHC information. Ultimately, the technique could be implemented for its accuracy and applicability in intraoperative and postoperative pathology consultations and to achieve a more rapid and detailed lymphoma diagnosis.

  Existing studies on virtual staining are mainly based on Generative adversarial networks (GANs)18 as it has been widely investigated in image generation19,20. For instance, Lahiani et al.21 utilized CycleGAN model to generate 512××512 resolution FAP-CK IHC images from Ki67-CD8 IHC images. Liu et al.22 proposed a cycle-consistent GAN model to transform H&E images to Ki-67-stained images with unpaired 288××288 tiles. Jackson et al.16 applied a convolutional neural network to generate virtual IHC from H&E stained images to SOX10 IHC images Rivenson et al.23 proposed a GAN model to transform the wide-field autofluorescence images into various histologic stained images. Xiang et al.24 proposed a multidomain stain normalization method based on cycleGAN using H&E images with 256××256 resolution. The existing literature proved the effectiveness of deep learning methods in virtual IHC staining. However, these methods mainly focus on low-resolution images such as 256××256 or 512××512, which may not effectively process large images as histological images often have higher resolutions, such as 2048××2048. Moreover, due to the scarcity of paired medical image and the labor-intensive annotation process, few studies has focused on collecting paired datasets. As a result, many studies rely on unpaired images and adopt unsupervised learning approaches. In addition, most existing works evaluate medical image quality using metrics originally designed for natural images, such as SSIM25, PSNR26, and FSIM27, which may be less effective or appropriate for assessing medical image quality.

  Hence, based on these observations and the inspiration from previous work15, in this study, we propose VihcGAN to investigate the potential of virtual staining. Our major contributions are as follow:We propose VihcGAN to transform H&E stained images to their corresponding CD3 and PAX5 stained images, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study of virtual staining with a high resolution of 2048××2048. Our models can generate virtually stained images in a few seconds and significantly shortens the turnaround time for IHC procedures.We provide feasible procedures to obtain paired pathological images without the intervention of pathologists labelling.We propose a novel metric, stain IoU, that incorporates the knowledge from the pathology domain to better evaluate the quality of the generated IHC-stained images.We conduct extensive experiments, expert grading and apply visual assessments to indicate the feasibility of our proposed VihcGAN.


## Method
  This section first introduces our dataset and then presents details about our proposed VihcGAN.


### Dataset

#### Data acquisition
  In this study, the H&E, CD3, and PAX5 tissue samples were lymph nodes collected from canine formalin-fixed tissues sent to the City University of Hong Kong, Veterinary Diagnostic Laboratory Medical Centre for evaluation.

  Lymph node samples were received and fixed in 10% formalin. Once completely fixed, the lymph nodes were typically cut longitudinally. Tissue samples were processed overnight by standard histopathology methods, embedded in paraffin wax before cutting at 4 μμm thickness, and staining with H&E. Subsequently, the lymph node sections were cut again as adjacent sections to minimize anatomical differences before repeated H&E staining and then anti-CD3 and anti-PAX5 IHC staining was performed.

  For immunohistochemical staining, selected paraffin sections and positive and negative control were cut at 4 μμm and attached to charged slides (Leica BOND Plus Slides, Leica Microsystems Limited, Hong Kong). Note that IHC sections were always cut directly adjacent to the H&E section. The antibodies used were polyclonal mouse antibodies against CD3 (1:200, Dako, Tokyo, Japan for 10 minutes); and polyclonal rabbit antibodies against PAX5 (1:100; BD Biosciences, Hong Kong for 10 minutes). #### Sample size
  This study collected two stained tissue image pair datasets with a resolution of 2048××2048. For the first dataset, we used 1109 H&E and CD3 stained image pairs for training and 75 pairs for testing the CD3 staining model. We used 1034 H&E and PAX5 stained image pairs for training and 100 for testing the PAX5 staining model for the second dataset.


### Network architecture
  VihcGAN aims to generate realistic IHC-stained images from H&E stained images. This study has applied a conditional GAN20,29 model containing two subnetworks, a generator, and a discriminator, denoted as G and D, respectively. Figure 2 shows the architecture of our virtual staining model. The generator learns to produce realistic IHC stained images under the condition of the input H&E stained images, and the discriminator focuses on distinguishing between the antibody and synthesized IHC stained images.Fig. 2Structure of proposed VihcGAN model. (a) The generator’s first reflection pads the input image and uses a series of downsampling blocks followed by several Resnet30 blocks. Then upsample blocks are adopted, followed by a convolution function and a tanh function to generate the virtual stained images. There are skip connections among each level of the downsampled and upsampled layers; the downsampled features are copied and cropped and then concatenated into their corresponding level of upsampled features like U-Net31. The multi-scale discriminator applies a convolution function and a LeakyReLU function followed by several downsample blocks, and a convolution function is used to predict the 1-dimensional output. Between different scales, an average pooling operation is used to reduce the scale of the input. All the predictions from different scales of discriminator are combined to predict the final result. (b) The structure of downsample block, upsample block, resnet block in G, and the downsample block in D. Specifically, an instance normalization32 layer is used in these blocks after the (transposed) convolution operation to save the details of each instance in the training process.

## Experiments

### Datasets
  In this study, we evaluate our method on two in-house datasets and one public dataset to assess both performance and generalizability. The in-house datasets include CD3 and PAX5 IHC staining for canine lymphoma, while the public dataset17 features Ki67 IHC staining for human breast cancer. Specifically, the CD3 dataset consists of 1,109 paired H&E and CD3-stained images for training and 75 pairs for testing. The PAX5 dataset includes 1,034 H&E and PAX5-stained image pairs for training and 100 pairs for testing. For the Ki67 dataset, we use 4,361 paired H&E and Ki67-stained images for training and 1,000 pairs for testing. It is worth noting that the CD3 and PAX5 images are at a resolution of 2048××2048 pixels, whereas the Ki67 images have a resolution of 1024××1024 pixels.


### Experiment setting
  Two models are trained to transform the H&E stained images to CD3 stained and PAX5 stained images, respectively. Specifically, the generator uses 5 downsample blocks, 9 ResNet blocks, and 4 upsample blocks in the training process. The number of scales in the discriminator is 2, and 3 downsample blocks are used in the discriminator. αα in feature match loss and λλ in perceptual loss are 0.5 and 10, respectively. The learning rate is 0.0002. All models in our study are trained in the same setting for 300 epochs, the learning rate decay is applied after 100 epochs. The CD3 staining model is trained by an 8-core Intel(R) Xeon(R) W-2245 CPU; its batch size is 4. The PAX5 staining model is trained by four 14-core Intel(R) Xeon(R) E5-2690 v4 CPUs, and its batch size is 8. For the Ki67 staining model, training was performed on an NVIDIA RTX A800 GPU with a batch size of 8.Fig. 3Comparison between antibody CD3 stained images and virtually CD3 stained images given H&E stained images.Fig. 4Comparison between antibody PAX5 stained images and virtually PAX5 stained images given H&E stained images.Fig. 5Comparison between antibody Ki67 stained images and virtually Ki67 stained images given H&E stained images.


### Evaluation metrics
  In our study, we adopt peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR)26, similarity (SSIM)25, feature similarity (FSIM), FSIMc27 and visual saliency-based index (VSI)37,38 to evaluate our produced images from the point of view of signal quality, image quality, and human visual system. However, none of the above metrics consider the knowledge of pathology, like the types, number, and positions of stained cells suspected of lymphoma. Inspiring by a commonly used metric, Intersection over Union (IoU), in computer vision39, we further incorporated the knowledge from IHC staining and designed a novel metric, Stain IoU, to evaluate the accuracy of the cells’ positions and the types in our virtually stained images, such as positive or negative. Unlike predicting the coordinates of objects bounded by rectangle boxes in image analysis of classic computer vision models, our model generates images with both positively and negatively stained regions with irregular shapes. Therefore, instead of using the coordinates of boxes to compute the IoU, we take the masks of positively stained areas (suspected of lymphoma) from both output images and label images and then calculate the intersection over the union of these two areas to get our Stain IoU. This novel metric allows us to evaluate our images from a pathological aspect. It can be flexibly adjusted according to the specific type of IHC staining, like Ki-67 IHC staining in Liu et al.’s work22. Figure 6 and Algorithm 1 demonstrate the paradigm to compute the stain IoU. More details are in the supplementary materials.

  Algorithm 1Computation of Stain IoU. Fig. 6Computation paradigm of our stain IoU. (a) Take the RGB masks of the antibody IHC and virtual stained image pairs. (b) Get the intersection and union of the RGB masks and convert them into binary masks, and finally compute the stain IoU by the binary masks. In this case, stain IoU is 84.12%.

### Discussion
  Our virtual staining method can provide essential information for pathologists, clinicians, and surgeons to make clinical decisions when given only the H&E stained images and dramatically shorten the time of the traditional IHC procedures. However, limitations still remain. In Fig. 10, we present samples with relatively low performance of CD3 staining and PAX5 staining model. Figure 10 shows that one major problem is the anatomical differences between H&E and IHC stained images. Unlike the previous data collection strategy22 that acquires unstained and stained images from the same section, in this study, adjacent tissue sections are imaged to minimize anatomical differences. Nevertheless, image registration is still imperfect since the registration involves manual annotation, and the sampled tissues may vary considerably in the margins of the tissue. This may add noise to the model and result in wrong predictions. Moreover, our models have a relatively low number of training images, adding more training images will benefit our models to attain higher accuracy.Fig. 10PAX5 and CD3 staining cases with poor performance.
