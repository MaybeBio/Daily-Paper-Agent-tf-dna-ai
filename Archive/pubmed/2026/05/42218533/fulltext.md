# Dynamics of tumor ecosystems and microbiome in response to neoadjuvant ABFOLFOX treatment in patients with unresectable colorectal cancer with liver metastasis


## Background
  This study aims to explore the effects of neoadjuvant atezolizumab, bevacizumab, leucovorin, 5-fluorouracil, and oxaliplatin (ABFOLFOX) in patients with unresectable colorectal liver metastases (CRLM), focusing on the molecular dynamics of tumor ecosystems (TE) of CRLM and their impact on treatment outcomes.


## Methods
  The study comprises two cohorts with CRLM tissue samples analyzed with RNA sequencing and immunohistochemical staining: cross-sectional cohort A (n = 60, CRLM treated with or without neoadjuvant chemotherapy) and prospectively registered cohort B (n = 20 with serial sampling and treated with ABFOLFOX). Shotgun metagenomic sequencing was performed for stool samples from cohort B.


## Results
  Durable disease control (PFS ≥ 24 months) was observed in 35% (7/20) of patients receiving ABFOLFOX. Analysis revealed a progressive increase in the immunogenic microenvironment within CRLM tissues upon the addition of therapeutic agents, specifically bevacizumab, and the most significant TE changes in CRLM were observed in those treated with ABFOLFOX in cohort B. The monocyte lineage was significantly associated with benefit from ABFOLFOX. Good responders exhibited improved immune response and notable activation of the SP140 transcription factor regulon. Moreover, microbiome analysis revealed that high abundance of Prevotella was positively correlated with good response and enhanced immune environment within the tumor. Causal mediation analysis suggested that the gut microbiome partially links the ABFOLFOX treatment response to the tumor microenvironment.


## Conclusions
  ABFOLFOX enhances the TE immune profile of CRLM, which is further augmented by the gut-liver axis characterized by Prevotella abundance, and can induce durable disease control in a subgroup of patients.

## Background
  Recently, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) have shown great efficacy profiles and become a standard of care for microsatellite-unstable colorectal cancer (CRC) [1, 2] but have exhibited limited benefit for microsatellite-stable (MSS) tumors, which comprise more than 95% of metastatic CRC [3, 4].

  The liver is the most common metastatic site of CRC, especially in MSS tumors compared to MSI tumors [5]. Colorectal liver metastases (CRLM) are the target of multidisciplinary treatment with curative intent, and the extent of CRLM is often critical for patients’ survival. Compared to other metastatic locations, the liver has a unique tumor ecosystem (TE); it is constantly exposed to various antigens, including microbial products from intestinal bacteria, so immune tolerance mechanisms by dendritic cells, Kupffer cells, and myeloid-derived suppressor cells are also prominent [6]. Therefore, liver metastases have a generally poorer response to ICI than metastases in other organs in various types of cancer including CRC [7, 8]. While most clinical trials of ICI failed to show a clinical benefit broadly in MSS CRC, patients without CRLM experience greater benefit from ICI than those with CRLM [9]. This indicates that understanding the TE of CRLM is important to overcome ICI resistance in MSS CRC.

  Blockade of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) with PD-1/PD-L1 has been established as an effective combination strategy in kidney and liver cancer [10], and an anti-VEGF antibody, bevacizumab, is also an essential therapeutic agent in CRC. The rationale of this combined therapy is that bevacizumab enhances the priming and activation of CD8 + T lymphocytes by promoting dendritic cell maturation, increases tumor infiltration of CD8 + T lymphocytes through normalization of tumor vasculature, and establishes an immune-permissive tumor ecosystem (TE), which is expected to synergize with the ICI [11].

  To obtain a comprehensive picture of the TE and molecular characteristics of CRLM in response to ICI and anti-VEGF, we aimed to explore the immune cell infiltrates and gene expression profile of CRLM treated with neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) with atezolizumab, bevacizumab, leucovorin, 5-fluorouracil, and oxaliplatin (ABFOLFOX).


## Methods

### Patients and study treatment
  This study comprises two main cohorts including cohort A and cohort B. Detailed patient selection criteria, dosage, and tissue sampling are described in the Additional file 1: supplementary methods. In cohort A, the study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Asan Medical Center (approval No. 2019-0023) and surgical samples of patients who participated in our biomarker discovery program (approval No. 2011 − 0511) were used. In cohort B, the prospectively registered study protocol for a single-arm pilot clinical trial was also approved by the IRB (approval No. 2018 − 1489) and prospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03698461). The Cohort B patients were enrolled in a single-center, tertiary academic hospital (Asan Medical Center, Republic of Korea). The first patient was enrolled on May 8th, 2019, and the last patient on March 9th, 2021. This study was completed on October 24, 2023. The primary endpoint of the cohort B clinical trial was serial changes in CD8 + cell densities in CRLM of patients treated with ABFOLFOX, and the secondary endpoints were serial changes in immune profile and vascular markers, clinical efficacy (objective response rate by Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors 1.1, R0 resection rate, and progression-free survival) and safety (assessed by Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events Version 4.0), and changes in microbiome in patients treated with ABFOLFOX. The sample size of the trial was not based on a specific statistical hypothesis, and a total of 20 patients were supposed to be enrolled in this exploratory study. Mixed-effect model repeated measure analysis was used to analyze serial changes of CD8 + cell densities to account for multiple time points and missing data. Clinical efficacy and safety outcomes were evaluated for patients who received at least one dose of study treatment. All study procedures were conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice.


### RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and data processing
  RNA sequencing was performed on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor tissues using the Illumina platform. RNA extraction, library preparation, and sequencing followed standard protocols. Sequencing data were processed and analyzed using RNA-seq pipelines [12]. Depending on the specific input requirements of each analytical tool, either RSEM upper quartile normalized expression data or raw count data were used for downstream analyses. Details are provided in the Additional file 1: supplementary methods.


### Pathway analysis from RNA-seq data
  Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the edgeR R package v4.8.2 based on RNA-seq count data. Pathway analysis with the Reactome/KEGG database was conducted using clusterProfiler R package v3.12.0. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) [13] and gene set variation analysis [14] were also performed to identify enriched molecular pathways using the hallmark gene set collection database in the Molecular Signatures Database (version 7.2) [15]. Antigen processing and presentation signature scores were calculated using the singscore R package (v1.19.1) based on KEGG gene lists derived from molecular signature database (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/). To account for multiple testing, p-values were adjusted using the False Discovery Rate (FDR) method and a threshold of q-value < 0.05 was applied to identify significant DEGs or pathways.


### Profiling of tumor-infiltrating immune cells
  Infiltrating immune cells were imputed from the RNA-seq using the CIBERSORT [16] absolute mode with LM22 (22 immune cell types) gene signatures from the normalized gene expression data. Microenvironment Cell Populations-counter (MCP-counter) [17] was also used to quantify the abundance of eight immune and two nonimmune stromal populations in tissue samples. The total immune score was calculated by summing the absolute quantities of immune cells estimated by CIBERSORT. The status of immune cells was classified as immuno-desert, immuno-active, and immuno-exhausted using the Nearest Template Prediction [18] module in the GenePattern web application (https://cloud.genepattern.org/).


### Immunohistochemistry (IHC)
  FFPE tumor tissues were used for IHC analyses of CD3 (1:100, Rabbit polyclonal, catalog no. A0452, Dako, Glostrup, Denmark), CD8 (1:400, Mouse monoclonal, clone C8/144B, catalog no. NCL-L-CD8-4B11, Novo, CA, USA), CD68 (KP-1) (1:2000, Mouse monoclonal, clone KP1, catalog no. M0814, Dako, Glostrup, Denmark), and CD31 (1:500, Mouse monoclonal, clone JC70, catalog no. 131 M-95, Cell Marque, CA, USA). IHC was performed using the OptiView DAB IHC Detection Kit on a BenchMark XT automatic immunostaining device (Ventana Medical Systems, Tucson, AZ, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.


### Immunoscore® analysis
  For surgical specimens, the density of CD3 + and CD8 + T cells in the center (CT) of CRLM and its invasive margins (IM) were measured and determined for the Immunoscore® (Veracyte SAS, Marseille, France) of liver metastases. The Immunoscore® ranges from 0 (I0) for low densities of CD3 + and CD8 + T cells in the CT and IM to 4 (I4) for high densities of CD3 + and CD8 + T cells. For patients with multiple metastases, the least infiltrated deposit was selected by the pathologist (JHK) and measured. For biopsied specimens, only the density of CD3 + and CD8 + T cells in the tumor core was measured, and the Immunoscore® was not determined.


### PD-L1 IHC and interpretation
  PD-L1 staining was performed using the PD-L1 IHC 22C3 pharmDx Assay Kit following the manufacturer’s instructions. The combined positive score (CPS) was used to evaluate PD-L1 expression. Details are provided in the Additional file 1: supplementary methods.


### Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)
  Driver genes in co-expression according to treatment response were identified using the WGCNA package (v1.72-5). Details are provided in the Additional file 1: supplementary methods.


### Transcriptional regulatory network analysis
  RTN (Reconstruction of Transcriptional Regulatory Networks) (v2.26.0) was used to analyze transcriptional regulatory networks. In this analysis, transcriptional network inference was performed using ARACNE, followed by transcriptional network analysis involving differential expression analysis and GSEA. Regulons targeting genes with a count of ≥ 45 were filtered, and those showing significant differences between good responders and poor responders were selected using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Pathway analysis using clusterProfiler was performed for regulon target genes.


### Microbiome sequencing and data analysis
  Fecal samples from cohort B were collected at three time points (T0, T1, T2) and processed for metagenomic sequencing using standard protocols. Taxonomic profiling was performed using Kraken2 (v2.1.2) for read classification, followed by Bracken (v2.6) to obtain estimated read counts at the species level through Bayesian re-estimation. The estimated counts were then normalized to relative abundances or Centered Log-Ratio (CLR) transformation using compositions R package v2.0.9 for downstream statistical analyses. To identify differentially abundant taxa, t-tests were performed on CLR transformed data. Multiple testing was accounted for by applying FDR correction. Functional profiling of shotgun metagenomic data was performed using HUMAnN3 (v3.9). Details are provided in the Additional file 1: supplementary methods.


### Causal mediation analysis
  To assess the gut microbial landscape, the relative abundance of Prevotella and Bacteroides was determined, and the Prevotella/Bacteroides (P/B) ratio was calculated. To account for the compositional nature of microbiome data, the P/B ratio was log-transformed. To test the hypothesis that the gut microbial landscape influences the ABFOLFOX treatment response through the modulation of tumor immune microenvironment, a causal mediation analysis was performed using the mediation (v4.5.1) R package. The analysis consisted of two regression models: (1) a mediator model (linear regression) predicting the cytotoxic lymphocytes score from the log-transformed P/B ratio, and (2) an outcome model (logistic regression) predicting the prognosis group from both the P/B ratio and the cytotoxic lymphocytes score, adjusted for potential confounders including prior lines of therapy. The Average Causal Mediation Effect (ACME), Average Direct Effect (ADE), and Total Effect were estimated using non-parametric bootstrapping with 1000 simulations. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.


### Statistical analysis
  This study was a pilot study that is not based on any specific statistical hypothesis. ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare immune cell densities. Gradual changes in immune cell densities over time in cohort B were analyzed with a linear mixed model. Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to analyze continuous variables. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test or Student’s t-test was used to evaluate the significance of differences in continuous variables between groups. Fisher’s exact test or the chi-square test was used to evaluate the significance of differences between categorical variables. Cox proportional hazards regression or logistic regression analyses were also performed. All statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.3.3 and SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).


## Results

### Clinical characteristics and outcomes
  The characteristics of patients in cohort A are shown in Fig. 1a and Additional file 2: Table S1. All patients had MSS tumors, and all specimens were obtained from surgically resected liver metastases. The follow-up duration for cohort A was 29 months (range 12–96 months). The characteristics, including the number of metastatic deposits and mutation status, were generally balanced among the three groups (G1 group, no NAC; G2 group, NAC; G3 group, BEV-NAC). However, the G2 group tended to have more unfavorable clinical features (higher number of metastatic deposits and higher percentage of R1 or R2 resection than R0 resection), and the G2 and G3 groups exhibited poorer clinical outcomes in terms of recurrence and overall survival (OS) compared with the G1 group (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). The study protocol for cohort A is provided in Additional file 3.

### Comparison of immune cell profiles in liver metastases following various treatments
  We investigated differences in the immune cell profiles associated with different neoadjuvant treatments in cohort A and T2 samples in cohort B. The immune cell profiles were obtained through the deconvolution of RNA-seq data using MCP-counter. The MCP-counter scores were validated using IHC and CD3 and CD8 densities measured using the Immunoscore® platform in cohort B (Additional file 1: Fig. S5). There is an overall tendency for various immune cells to increase along with the addition of drugs before surgery (group 1, no drugs; group 2, NAC; group 3, BEV + NAC) (Fig. 2a). The least-squares mean value of CD8 + cell count, the primary endpoint of the clinical trial for cohort B, was 70.6/mm2 (95% CI 46.0- 95.2) in T0 (N = 19), 105.0/mm2 (95% CI 43.4-166.6) in T1 (N = 18), and 333.4/mm2 (95% CI 68.0–598.9) in T2 (N = 4) ( P value for T0 vs. T1: 0.2315; for T0 vs. T2: 0.0549). The progressive increase in total immune scores observed with the addition of drugs remained an independent factor, even after adjusting for clinical and molecular characteristics associated with patient severity and aggressiveness (Additional file 1: Fig. S6). However, the Immunoscore® distribution among the three groups did not differ statistically (Additional file 1: Fig. S5). The total immune cell score, which reflects the amount of overall immune cells, increased with the addition of drugs, especially in the T2 group where atezolizumab and bevacizumab were used (Fig. 2b and Additional file 1: Fig. S7a and S7b). Among the cells comprising the TE, the expansion of immune cells was particularly notable, with a significant increase in various immune cells, including T cells, B cells, and monocytic cell lineages (Fig. 2c and Additional file 1: Fig. S7c). There was also a significant correlation at the single gene level between the mRNA expression of representative T-cell genes such as CD3 and CD8 from RNA-seq, protein expression from IHC, and CD3 + and CD8 + T-cell densities from Immunoscore® (Fig. 2d and Additional file 1: Fig. S7d). PD-L1 protein expression (CPS score) was also significantly correlated with CD274 mRNA expression (Fig. 2e). This finding suggests that the quality of RNA-seq expression values at the single gene level is reliable. Then, when classified by categories of genes regulating immunity, we observed that genes associated with antigen presentation signals, including human leukocyte antigen, immune stimulation, and immune inhibition, are variably simultaneously activated with the addition of medications (Fig. 2f). This finding indicates that various immune signaling pathways are activated along with the increase in quantitative infiltration of immune cells in the T2 group. In this context, we also observed an increase in immune exhaustion signatures in the T2 group (Fig. 2g). Specifically, the increase in PDCD1 (PD-1) expression was pronounced (Fig. 2h). Meanwhile, in addition to interleukin and interferon (IFN) pathways, the cell cycle and p53 pathways were activated with the addition of medications (Fig. 2i). When drawing the entire network of genes constituting the related pathways, the cell cycle and p53 pathways are connected to inflammatory pathways (Fig. 2j). Ubiquitin genes such as UBB and UBV are involved in numerous interactions (Fig. 2j). This is suggested to be a response to various drug treatments, regulating altered protein expression [19].

### Transformation into an immunogenic TE with ABFOLFOX
  We also observed an increase in various inflammatory cells in the T2 group than in the T0 or T1 group in cohort B, which comprises serial follow-up samples from the same patients (Fig. 3a). In comparing cohorts A and B, we found that the T2 group has the most immunogenic gene expression profile (Fig. 3b). There was a significant difference in immune cells and PDCD1 expression in patients treated with bevacizumab without atezolizumab (G3 group) compared with untreated patients (G1 group). By contrast, patients treated with atezolizumab alone (T1 group) did not show significant microenvironmental immune changes compared with baseline (T0) (Fig. 3c). This finding indicates that bevacizumab itself might contribute to creating an immunogenic environment. In this study, not only bevacizumab plus chemotherapy (G3 group) but also combined treatment with bevacizumab, atezolizumab, and chemotherapy (T2 group) created an immunogenic environment and increased PDCD1 expression (Fig. 3c). In summary, the combination of bevacizumab and atezolizumab (T2 group) among various anticancer treatments resulted in the creation of an immunogenic environment compared with atezolizumab alone. On the other hand, the expression of CD274 (PD-L1) showed an increasing trend, but it was not statistically significant (Fig. 3c). In PD-L1 immunostaining using a 22C3 clone, there was an overall increase in the positive rate to 91% (10/11 patients) in the T2 group, including two patients with conversion to PD-L1 positive (CPS ≥ 1), which indicated a potential enhancement in the effectiveness of immunotherapy when combined with bevacizumab (Fig. 3d). Immunostaining for CD31, which is primarily expressed in vascular endothelial cells, was used to measure the tumor vasculature area. No significant differences were observed among the treatment groups (Fig. 3e). From the perspective of cancer signaling using 50 hallmark gene sets, combined therapy (T2 group) caused significant changes in signaling, notably highlighting alterations in immune-related pathways such as IL2/STAT5 and TNF-α and inflammatory response signaling pathways (Fig. 3f and Additional file 1: Fig. S8). This finding suggests that signaling changes associated with an increased immune response in the TE may be linked to combination therapy.

### Immune profile changes with ABFOLFOX according to the response
  We investigated immune profile changes based on the response to ABFOLFOX in cohort B (Fig. 4a and Additional file 1: Fig. S9a). In tissues obtained after treatment (T1 and T2), there was a pronounced expansion of immune cells among good responders. Although the expansion of these immune cells becomes more pronounced after treatment, this trend can also be observed before treatment (T0 sample). Good responders tended to be more immunogenic even at the T0 state, and CD3 + and CD8 + cell densities measured using the Immunoscore® platform from the tumor core (T0) were significantly different according to the response (Fig. 4b and Additional file 1: Fig. S9a). This finding suggests that it may be possible to predict the response to ABFOLFOX based on the immune profile of CRLM. Among inflammatory cell types, monocytic cells were identified as the most significant cells in relation to good response in all groups (T0–T2; Fig. 4c). Analysis using PFS as time-to-event data in relation to immune profile also showed that monocytic cells were the cell type that influenced survival (Fig. 4d). When we analyzed T0 samples for DEG analysis between good and poor responders, we found that various immune-related pathways appeared to be enriched in good responders, whereas the fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) pathway showed the most significant up-regulation in poor responders (Fig. 4e, Additional file 2: Table S8, and Additional file 1: Fig. S9b). This may suggest that an immune response present at the T0 state (before treatment) was correlated with the subsequent good clinical response to ABFOLFOX. The genes that were consistently associated with better PFS in all serial samples (T0, T1, and T2) were related to immune-related pathways, including PD-1 signaling (Fig. 4f and Additional file 1: Fig. S10a). By contrast, among the genes associated with poor PFS, HOXA-AS3 was the only common gene in all serial samples (T0, T1, and T2) (Fig. 4f, right and Additional file 1: Fig. S10b).

### Identification of driver genes associated with combined therapy response through WGCNA
  Instead of focusing on individual genes, we conducted WGCNA, which focuses on co-expressed genes (gene modules), to provide more comprehensive biological processes related to combined treatment response. WGCNA revealed 49 distinct gene modules (Additional file 1: Fig. S11a-b). Among the gene modules identified, Module 5 (ME5) emerged as the most relevant for poor responders, in contrast to Module 1 (ME1), which aligned more closely with good response. The boxplot of module eigengenes showed the association of ME5 with poor response and ME1 with good response (Additional file 1: Fig. S11c). We further investigated the key driver genes within ME5 by analyzing their correlation with the poor response phenotype and their intramodular connectivity among member genes of ME5 (Additional file 1: Fig. S11d). The potential key driver genes identified from this analysis were mostly genes coding for small nucleolar RNA (snoRNA) (Additional file 1: Fig. S11e). Using the same approach, the key driver genes within ME1, which were associated with a good response, were involved in immune-related pathways, including PD-1 signaling (Additional file 1: Fig. S11f).


### Identification of the transcriptional regulatory network associated with combined treatment response
  Using transcriptional regulatory network analysis, we constructed transcriptional regulatory networks to determine the transcription factor (TF)-driven gene regulation network responsible for the outcome and identified gene regulation networks controlled by TFs (regulon) associated with response to ABFOLFOX (Fig. 5a). The TFs of the most significant regulons related to a good response were SP140 and SPI1 TFs. The expression of these genes remained consistently high in tumor tissues in good responders from baseline through the period following the administration of medications (Fig. 5b). The SP140 and SPI1 genes were among the common genes associated with good PFS (Fig. 4f). SP140, the most significant gene, can predict response to ABFOLFOX with high performance across tumor tissue groups in all drug treatment stages (Fig. 5c). The expression of SP140 mRNA was significantly associated with monocytic cells (Fig. 5d), which were identified as the most significant immune cell type related to good response with ABFOLFOX. On the other hand, GTF2IRD2B was the most significant TF associated with poor response (Fig. 5e). When we investigated the pathways of target genes of TFs that were significantly associated with response, we found that inflammatory pathways were mainly associated with good response and that cell cycle pathways were mainly associated with poor response (Fig. 5f). SP140 target genes were also associated with inflammatory pathways, which aligns well with the signaling pathways observed in good response (Fig. 5g). Moreover, the pathways of target genes of the GTF2IRD2B TF were related to the cell cycle (Fig. 5g, right), representing the altered cell cycle pathway in poor responders. Among the target genes of GTF2IRD2B, the inclusion of many snoRNA genes aligns well with the results where snoRNA genes were identified as key driver genes in WGCNA (Additional file 1: Fig S11f).

### Association of the microbiome with good response with ABFOLFOX
  We collected fecal samples at T0, T1, and T2 in cohort B and investigated microbial changes in the patient’s gut using whole-genome shotgun sequencing (Fig. 1b and Additional file 1: Fig. S12a). Twenty fecal samples were collected at each T0 and T1. However, only 19 samples were collected at T2 because of one early withdrawal previously mentioned. When we examined the most highly abundant bacterial genera in fecal samples, we found differential microbial communities between good and poor responders (Fig. 6a). Six participants used antibiotics, antacids, or steroids during the stool collection period, however, no significant differences in microbiome abundance were observed according to the use of these medications (Additional file 1: Fig. S13). We calculated the alpha diversity to compare the within sample microbiome diversity based across prognostic groups. Overall, there was no association between various alpha diversity indices and treatment responses (Fig. 6b). This indicates that global microbial richness is not correlated with therapeutic outcomes, suggesting that the clinical response may instead be driven by specific functional taxa rather than overall community diversity. Notably, Prevotella relative abundances were greater in good responders, while Bacteroides and Lachnospira were greater in poor responders (Fig. 6c and Additional file 2: Table S9). When we examined how bacterial relative abundances changed on treatment, we found no significant changes in the microbiome composition over time (Fig. 6d and Additional file 1: Fig. S12b). This finding indicates that the microbiome composition is not considerably influenced by the medications, including atezolizumab, and remains consistent with the baseline microbiome composition. While the abundance of Prevotella remained stable regardless of drug addition or treatment duration (Fig. 6e), immune-related pathways, such as antigen processing and presentation signaling, exhibited a progressive increase following the repeated administration of therapeutic regimens (Fig. 6f). Notably, this immune activation was more pronounced in the Prevotella-abundant group (Fig. 6g). Furthermore, enhanced antigen processing and presentation signaling was positively associated with increased infiltration of cytotoxic lymphocytes into the CRLM tissues (Fig. 6h). To further characterize the metabolic potential of the microbiome, we performed functional profiling using HUMAnN3 (HMP Unified Metabolic Analysis Network 3). Among the identified features, the L-methionine biosynthesis IV pathway (PWY-7977) emerged as a significantly enriched metabolic route (Fig. 6i). Recent evidence from mouse models suggests that dietary methionine supplementation can stimulate anti-tumor immunity and inhibit tumor progression [20]. Given our observation that Prevotella-abundant CRLM tissues exhibited increased antigen processing and presentation signatures along with elevated cytotoxic lymphocyte infiltration, we hypothesize that Prevotella may facilitate the transport of methionine or its sulfur-containing precursors to the liver via the portal vein. This process could potentially modulate the intrahepatic metabolic landscape and promote inflammatory responses [21]. Consistent with this hypothesis, we found that several inflammatory pathways and xenobiotic metabolism pathways were significantly upregulated in the CRLM tissues of the Prevotella-abundant group (Fig. 6j). Collectively, these findings suggest a potential association where the gut microbiota is linked to the intrahepatic immune landscape via the gut-liver axis, which may correlate with the therapeutic response to ABFOLFOX.

### Tumor immune microenvironment mediates the association between gut microbiome and ABFOLFOX response
  Given that specific microbiomes are associated with treatment outcomes, these findings suggest that the tumor-host immune interface via the gut-liver axis may influence the response to immunotherapy in patients with CRLM. Therefore, we investigated whether the observed association between the gut microbiome and clinical outcomes was mediated by the tumor’s immune interface. Using the P/B ratio as a proxy for the gut microbial landscape, we found a significant total effect on the ABFOLFOX response (Estimate = 0.1751, P = 0.0012), indicating that a higher P/B ratio is associated with a favorable therapeutic response. The causal mediation analysis revealed a significant indirect effect (ACME) through the cytotoxic lymphocyte score (Estimate = 0.0355, P = 0.0332) in CRLM tissue. Specifically, the cytotoxic lymphocyte accounted for 20.3% of the total effect of the P/B ratio on the ABFOLFOX response (Proportion Mediated = 0.203, P = 0.0336). The direct effect (ADE) of the P/B ratio remained significant (Estimate = 0.1395, P = 0.0104), suggesting that while cytotoxic lymphocyte is a critical mechanistic link, other microbial-driven pathways may also contribute to the overall treatment efficacy. These findings indicate that the gut-liver axis may be linked to microenvironmental changes in liver metastases that correlate with the response to ABFOLFOX therapy (Fig. 6k).


## Discussion
  In this study, we demonstrated that ABFOLFOX with or without hepatic surgery resulted in durable disease control for more than 24 months in 35% (7/20) of patients and that good response (PFS ≥ 24 months) was associated with increased inflammatory signaling pathways possibly targeted by the atezolizumab and bevacizumab combination. In cohort A, we demonstrated that cytotoxic chemotherapy with bevacizumab can form an immunogenic TE compared with no treatment or cytotoxic chemotherapy alone. On the other hand, atezolizumab alone did not lead to significant changes in the immune profile in cohort B. However, the bevacizumab and atezolizumab combination played a crucial role in significantly enhancing the tumor immunogenic environment of CRLM. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies, showing that VEGF pathways play a major role in immune resistance in MSS CRC [22, 23].

  In terms of predicting the response to ABFOLFOX, good responders tended to show higher immune cell scores at baseline and higher CD3 + and CD8 + T-cell densities measured using the Immunoscore® platform. This finding is in line with the findings from the AtezoTRIBE study, which showed that the densities and proximities of PD-L1 + and CD8 + cells in the tumor core (Immunoscore IC) were associated with clinical benefit from atezolizumab, bevacizumab, and FOLFOXIRI in MSS CRC [24].

  We also found that good responders exhibited a boosted immune response upon drug administration in serial biopsied or surgical tumor samples. This finding suggests that immune monitoring, including T-cell receptor repertoire analysis or mass cytometry, could be helpful for predicting the response of ICI-based combination treatment for CRLM [25]. Unfortunately, we did not analyze peripheral blood-based biomarkers. The constant favorable prognostic implication of monocyte lineage from RNA-seq in all time points (T0–T2) implied that it could be also used as a predictive marker in flow cytometry analysis [26]. This finding is consistent with that of a previous study using spatial transcriptomics to analyze the TE of CRLM, which showed that CRLM samples achieving partial response after chemotherapy were enriched with monocyte phenotype because of the blocked reprogramming process from monocytes to immunosuppressive M2-like macrophages [27].

  From a regulatory network perspective, tumor tissues associated with good response were characterized by regulatory networks induced by the SP140 TF, whereas tumor tissues associated with poor response had regulatory networks induced by the GTF2IRD2B TF, which were characterized by the overexpression of snoRNA genes. A recent study found that tumors with high SP140 expression are associated with favorable TE formation for immunotherapy [28]. This study also indicated that SP140 activation in macrophages increases the production of anti-tumor cytokines, including IFN-γ, and chemokines, leading to an anti-tumor effect. We also showed that SP140 could be used as a biomarker for predicting the response to ABFOLFOX.

  Although there is limited research on the relationship between snoRNA and cancer immunotherapy response, snoRNA dysregulation in cancer tissues is well documented and known to be independently involved in cancer progression [29]. Moreover, the regulation of snoRNA classes is primarily controlled by the cancer-promoting mTOR signaling pathway, suggesting their potential importance in immune cell function and anti-tumor immune response [29]. A previous study indicated that atezolizumab and bevacizumab combination therapy improves PFS in renal cell carcinoma with enriched snoRNA [30]. However, this contradicts our finding of increased snoRNA in poor responders to ABFOLFOX. This finding suggests that the role of snoRNA may vary depending on the organ where the tumor originated because snoRNA may function in a tissue-specific manner and could be associated with the tissue’s unique functions [31]. In addition, our study differs in that the study drugs included not only bevacizumab and atezolizumab but also cytotoxic agents (oxaliplatin and 5-fluorouracil) for CRC. According to Cai et al., higher levels of tumor immune infiltration-related snoRNA signature in CRC were associated with poor response to immunotherapy and lower levels of immune cell infiltration within the tumor, which aligns well with our findings [32].

  There seems to be no unified conclusion regarding the microbiome composition and its association with response to ICIs [33]. In our study, Prevotella was found to be associated with a good response to ABFOLFOX. The abundance of Prevotella was also found to be related to a favorable response to ICI treatment in gastrointestinal cancer, non-small cell lung cancer, and melanoma [34].

  We also showed that the expression of genes in tumor tissues associated with the abundance of Prevotella was involved in inflammatory pathways. These results are consistent with a recent clinical trial of fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) in patients with anti-PD-1-refractory solid cancers (NCT04264975). In that study, Prevotella merdae isolated from responders was shown to promote T-cell activity and suppress tumor growth, whereas Bacteroides species were reported to potentially inhibit anti-tumor immunity [35].

  Our study has several limitations. The sample sizes in each group of cohorts A and B were too small to conduct statistical tests to obtain robust conclusions. In the present study, multivariate analysis showed that the increase in the total immune score following drug administration was an independent factor influencing immune composition, even after adjusting for major clinical variables. However, an inherent limitation of this study is the heterogeneity of Cohort A, where baseline clinical characteristics, such as tumor severity and aggressiveness, may not be perfectly matched. Consequently, we cannot exclude the possibility that unmeasured confounding variables omitted from the statistical model may have complexly influenced the immune microenvironment, necessitating a cautious interpretation of these findings. In cohort B, several tumor samples were lost because of insufficient amounts of tumor or stromal cells or the inability to perform liver biopsy. Good and poor responses were arbitrarily defined based on PFS, which might have been largely affected by clinical characteristics and the antitumor activity of ABFOLFOX.

  In evaluating the survival benefit of ABFOLFOX, the higher rate of liver resection in the good responders may have contributed to improved outcomes, although this association did not reach statistical significance. Furthermore, potential imbalances in baseline molecular characteristics and clinical backgrounds between the two groups might have exerted confounding effects on prognosis, imposing limitations on isolating the specific therapeutic efficacy of the treatment. However, it is noteworthy that the potent cytoreductive effect of ABFOLFOX led to a high conversion rate to surgery, successfully rendering patients resectable. This capacity to enable definitive surgical intervention should be considered a critical component of its overall therapeutic impact. In addition, most good responders had multiple metastases not amenable to surgery at the time of diagnosis, and some underwent R1 or R2 resection. The median PFS in such populations is reported to range from 10 to 18 months, even though they have undergone hepatic surgery [36, 37]. Therefore, maintaining disease free status for over 24 months in this group is notable and may be associated with the enhanced immune response observed following ABFOLFOX treatment.

  In summary, our study demonstrated that neoadjuvant treatment of ABFOLFOX can induce durable disease control for over 24 months in about one-third of patients with CRLM. The patients who showed a good response with ABFOLFOX typically had the following characteristics: (1) they already exhibit immunogenic tendencies in the tumor before treatment, (2) their immune cell reaction is boosted as treatment progresses, (3) their tumors are characterized by the activation of the SP140 TF regulon, and (4) their fecal samples are rich in Prevotella. On the other hand, patients who showed a poor response to ABFOLFOX generally had the following characteristics: (1) less immunogenic tumors that do not activate an immune response during treatment; (2) tumors characterized by the activation of the GTF2IRD2B TF regulon, with high snoRNA expression; and (3) fecal samples that are relatively rich in Bacteroides.


## Conclusions
  The combination therapy of atezolizumab, bevacizumab, and FOLFOX can maximize the therapeutic effectiveness of immunotherapy in CRLM, especially in tumors exhibiting distinctive molecular profiles in the tumor and specific microbiome environment.
