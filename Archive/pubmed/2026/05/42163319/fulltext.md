# Development and validation of an MRI-based deep learning system for triple-class ER expression classification in breast cancer: a large-scale multicenter study


## Background
  Estrogen receptor (ER) expression is a key prognostic and predictive marker in breast cancer. The 2020 ASCO/CAP guidelines classify tumors with ≥ 1% ER-positive nuclei as ER-positive, yet ER-low positive (1%–10%) breast cancer remains biologically distinct with an unclear response to endocrine therapy (ET). Given the limitations of invasive ER assessment, we developed and validated an MRI-based deep learning system (BERC) for non-invasive classification of ER negative, ER-low positive, and ER-high positive breast cancer using multicenter data.


## Methods
  This diagnostic study retrospectively analyzed pretreatment DCE-MRI data from 3500 breast cancer patients across six institutions (February 2016–August 2023). Patients were categorized into ER negative, ER-low positive, and ER-high positive groups based on pathology. Tumor segmentation on DCE T1-weighted images was performed using an automated deep learning algorithm. A DCE-MRI-based model was developed and evaluated for classification performance using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), along with sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) with 95% confidence intervals. Model interpretability was evaluated through t-SNE and UMAP for feature visualization, along with SHAP analysis for interpreting key predictive features.


## Results
  The training dataset included 1862 patients (median age 49 years, interquartile range [IQR] 21–88), while 1638 patients from four external centers formed the test dataset (median ages ranging from 47 to 51 years across centers). The model achieved Micro-/Macro-average AUCs of 0.918/0.882 in training and 0.923/0.900 in internal validation. In external testing, Micro-average AUCs ranged from 0.828 to 0.923 and Macro-average AUCs from 0.825 to 0.905 across four independent centers. Visualization techniques revealed clear, biologically plausible clustering patterns across the ER expression categories.


## Conclusions
  BERC shows potential for non-invasive, preoperative ER status prediction in breast cancer, with implications for personalized therapy.


## Background
  Estrogen receptor (ER) expression is prevalent in most invasive breast cancers, playing a pivotal role as a predictive and prognostic marker [1]. The 2020 American Society of Clinical Oncology/College of American Pathology (ASCO/CAP) guidelines define ER-positive breast cancer as specimens with 1% or greater tumor nuclei positivity for ER expression via validated immunohistochemistry (IHC) [2]. This threshold aids in identifying patients who are eligible for endocrine therapy (ET), while those with ER-negative tumors are more likely to benefit from chemotherapy and typically have a less favorable prognosis [3]. However, approximately 3% to 9% of breast cancer patients, with ER expression between 1% and 10%, designated as ER-low positive breast cancer according to guidelines, have a controversial response to ET [2, 4–6]. Despite the global burden of breast cancer, cumulative evidence strongly supports ER-low positive breast cancer as a distinct entity that warrants significant attention [7].

  Recent research indicates that cases of ER-low positive breast cancer may exhibit distinct biological characteristics compared to those with ER-high (≥ 10%) and ER-negative (< 1%) status. Poon et al observed that ER-low positive cases, compared to ER-negative cases, displayed higher progesterone receptor (PR) expression but lower histological grade, reduced expression of CK5/6 and CK14, increased prevalence of lymphovascular invasion (LVI), and a younger age [8]. Another large population-based cohort analysis suggested that ER-low positive cases without PR expression resembled aggressive ER-negative breast cancer, while those with PR expression displayed less aggressive traits [9]. Studies evaluating ET sensitivity between ER-low positive and ER-negative tumors reported no significant differences in terms of recurrence-free survival, disease-free survival (DFS), overall survival, and time to recurrence [5, 6]. However, Shi et al found that ER-low positive breast cancers generally displayed superior prognostic outcomes following ET compared to ER-negative tumors [10].

  Currently, ER status analysis relies on obtaining partial tumor tissue through biopsy or surgery for IHC. Nevertheless, this approach is invasive and may inadequately represent tumor characteristics due to breast cancer’s high heterogeneity. Moreover, subjective and sampling bias can affect the pathological assessment of ER expression levels. A non-invasive method capable of assessing the whole tumor could provide complementary information for longitudinal monitoring of ER status during treatment or for evaluating intratumoral heterogeneity. It’s conceivable that distinct molecular subtypes might exhibit diverse spatial relationships and voxel intensity information in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), a non-invasive method with high soft tissue resolution. Previous studies have utilized MRI-based radiomics or deep learning techniques to predict breast cancer molecular subtypes [11–15]. However, no studies to date have employed MRI-based deep learning to assess breast cancer subgroups based on ER expression levels. Here, we aimed to develop and validate the potential of an MRI-based deep learning system (BERC: mri-based Breast Cancer ER Classification) in distinguishing ER-negative, -low positive, and -high positive breast cancer using multicenter data.


## Methods

### Study participants
  This study adheres to the updated TRIPOD (Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis) Statement to ensure comprehensive and transparent reporting of the development and validation of our model [16]. The data that support the findings of this study were retrospectively collected between February 2016 and August 2023 from six Chinese academic medical centers (Center 1–6: Peking University Shenzhen Hospital, Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital, The First Hospital of China Medical University, Henan Cancer Hospital, Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital and Yunnan Cancer Hospital), which were approved by the institutional ethics review boards of all participating centers; the requirement for informed consent was waived. Data from Peking University Shenzhen Hospital and Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital were randomly divided into training and internal validation sets at an 8:2 ratio, while data from the other four centers were used as independent external test sets. Female patients with breast cancer confirmed by pathological assessment of biopsy or resection specimen, who underwent pre-treatment breast MRI were included in the study. The exclusion criteria were: (1) lack of integrity of clinical data; (2) incomplete pathologic information on ER status from IHC staining; (3) poor MR image quality due to any kinds of artifacts; (4) patients with prosthesis implantation; (5) non-mass lesions without delineated boundaries or lesion diameter < 5 mm, and (6) any treatment including surgery, chemotherapy or radiotherapy prior to MRI scan.


### Clinical data and pathological evaluation
  The clinical and pathological data (age, histology type, histological grade, ER, PR, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 [HER2], Ki-67 index and molecular subtype) were gathered by reviewing the patients’ medical records and pathological reports of specimens. The evaluation of ER and PR statuses followed ASCO/CAP guidelines [2]. The ER cut-off values were set at 1% and 10%, respectively (< 1% for negative, ≥ 1% and < 10% for low positive, ≥ 10% for high positive). The PR cut-off value was set at 1% (< 1% for negative, ≥ 1% for positive). For HER2 status evaluation, tumors with equivocal scores of 2+ were further tested using fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH). The HER2 results were then categorized as follows: HER2-negative (IHC score of 0, 1+ or 2+ and negative findings at FISH), HER2-positive (IHC score of 3+ or 2+ and positive findings at FISH) [17]. The Ki-67 cut-off value was established at 30% (< 30% for low proliferation, ≥ 30% for high proliferation) [18].


### MRI acquisition
  All breast MRI examinations were conducted using 1.5-T or 3.0-T systems from different vendors of 6 centers, equipped with phased-array breast coils. Detailed MRI acquisition parameters for each institution are provided in Additional file 1: Table S1. The contrast medium Gd-DTPA 0.2 mmol/kg was intravenously administered after the pre-contrast enhancement images were acquired at a rate of 2 ml/s, followed by a 20 ml saline flush at the same rate. The phase with peak tumor enhancement during pre-treatment dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI was utilized.


### Image preprocessing and lesion segmentation
  The phase with the most tumor enhancement on the DCE-MRI was selected for analysis. Image pre-processing included N4 bias field correction, resampling with isotropic voxels of 1 mm using B-spline interpolation, and histogram standardization of intensity values on MRI [19, 20].

  For patients with multiple breast lesions, only the largest lesion was selected for segmentation and subsequent analysis. This approach was adopted to avoid clustering effects, to maintain consistency with clinical practice where the dominant lesion typically guides treatment decisions, and to ensure alignment with previous studies.

  Breast lesions were initially segmented from the DCE-MRI phase with peak enhancement using an in-house developed deep learning model, the UNETR (UNEt TRansformers) architecture. As the MRI volumes exceeded the network’s input size (48 × 48 × 48 voxels), a sliding window strategy was used to process the volumes into smaller, overlapping patches. For each 48 × 48 × 48 patch, the network divided it into non-overlapping 3D sub-patches, which were then embedded and processed through a Transformer encoder to capture global contextual information. The Transformer’s output was reshaped and passed to a decoder that upsamples the features and incorporates skip connections from the input sub-patches, ultimately producing a segmentation mask for the patch. The segmentation results from all patches were combined to generate the full segmentation map for the entire volume.

  Subsequently, the pre-segmented lesion delineations were reviewed by two experienced radiologists with more than 5 years of experience in breast MRI using ITK-SNAP software (http://www.itksnap.org, version 3.8.0). Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion to ensure consistency. The radiologist-reviewed and corrected segmentations served as the final ROIs for subsequent feature extraction and model development.


### Models development
  We utilized a foundational model, FM-BCMRI, as the feature extractor. The FM-BCMRI model was previously pretrained in our earlier work, using a diverse dataset encompassing various breast cancer types and stages. The model was trained with the Moco-V3 contrastive learning algorithm [21], which is a self-supervised learning approach that learns transferable feature representations through contrastive learning on unlabeled DCE-MRI data. We employed a Vision Transformer (ViT-Base) architecture with a patch size of 8 × 8 × 8 as the backbone network. During pre-training stages of FM-BCMRI, each input sample was derived from three sequential DCE-MRI phases: pre-contrast, peak-phase, and delayed-phase, using single-channel images, which were treated as separate input channels. A 3D region of 48 × 48 × 48 voxels was randomly cropped from the MRI volume, resulting in a final input tensor of 48 × 48 × 48 × 3. Two augmented views were then generated through stochastic transformations for contrastive learning. The model was trained for 1000 epochs using the AdamW optimizer with a base learning rate of 1.5e-4 and weight decay of 0.05. Synchronized batch normalization was employed across 8 NVIDIA L40S GPUs with a total batch size of 1024. The learning rate followed a cosine decay schedule with a 40-epoch warmup initialization. In the present study, however, only the DCE-MRI phase showing the strongest tumor enhancement was used as the input to the pretrained FM-BCMRI feature extractor for downstream BERC classification. For more details, please refer to the project’s repository on GitHub (https://github.com/zhenweishi/FM-BCMRI), where the source code and pretrained model weights are publicly available without restriction.

  The FM-BCMRI model was employed to extract deep learning feature sets from lesion bounding boxes, with each feature set comprising a vector of length 768. To prevent overfitting, principal component analysis (PCA) was applied for feature dimension reduction, retaining 16 key features. Additionally, an XGBoost prediction model was developed using these deep learning features. To quantify the impact of MRI imaging features on model prediction, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis was conducted [22]. Finally, BERC was developed to predict ER status by distinguishing ER-negative, -low positive and -high positive. ### Model evaluation and statistical analysis
  To assess the performance of BERC for the discrimination of ER status, we used area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). For the multi-class classification task, both macro-average AUC and micro-average AUC were calculated. Macro-average AUC computes the average AUC across all classes with equal weight assigned to each class, whereas micro-average AUC aggregates the predictions from all classes and evaluates overall performance based on all individual predictions. Quantitative statistics were presented as median (IQR). Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test and Wilcoxon’s test. In addition to discrimination performance, model calibration was assessed using calibration curves and Brier scores to evaluate the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes. Unordered categorical variables were assessed using Pearson’s Chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test. For deep learning features visualization, gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM), analyses of t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE), Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP), confusion matrices and SHAP were conducted. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. All computations and statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.9; Python Software Foundation), and R software (version 3.6.0; https://www.r-project.org). Key Python libraries included PyTorch (version 1.12.0) for deep learning model development, XGBoost (version 1.6.0) for classification, scikit-learn (version 1.0.2) for PCA and performance metrics, and SHAP (version 0.41.0) for model interpretability.

## Discussion
  In this study, a deep learning system (BERC) based on DCE-MRI for the automated segmentation and classification of breast cancer ER status was proposed. BERC was developed using a dataset of 3500 breast cancer patients’ DCE-MRI scans, divided into training and external validation sets to evaluate its generalizability in distinguishing ER status. The results suggested that our system achieved outstanding performance in distinguishing ER-negative, ER-low positive, and ER-high positive breast cancer, with Micro-average and Macro-average AUCs ranging from 0.827 to 0.921 and 0.818 to 0.896, respectively, in the external test sets. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first effort to develop and validate a clinically viable, noninvasive deep learning system for three-class ER status classification in breast cancer using multicenter MRI data.

  In recent years, a substantial body of research has been dedicated to the identification of imaging features correlated with the molecular subtypes of breast cancer [23–27]. Meanwhile, endeavors have been undertaken to develop radiomics models utilizing various imaging techniques for the prediction of breast cancer molecular subtypes [28–33]. Among which, few investigations have focus on the links between imaging features and ER expressions. Li et al tried to find the associations between background parenchymal enhancement (BPE) and LVI and ER status in invasive breast cancer, and their results indicated that the high BPE group showed higher frequencies of LVI (83% vs 13%, P < 0.0001) and positive ER (79% vs 42%, P < 0.0001) compared with the low BPE group [23]. Horvat et al employed diverse apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) metrics to predict IHC receptor status, proliferation rate, and molecular subtype in invasive breast cancer [25]. Their findings revealed that ER-positive tumors exhibited significantly lower ADC values (P < 0.05 for both maximum whole tumor ADC and mean whole tumor ADC in two readers) compared to ER-negative tumors. In a single center study conducted by Zhong et al [32], multiregional (intra- and peri-tumoral) radiomic signatures based on DCE-MRI extracted from 443 patients achieved AUC values of 0.851 and 0.833 for predicting the ER status in the training and validation sets, respectively. Similar to our study, the research by Ramtohul et al [12], Peng et al [13] and Zheng et al [13] also focused on the application of MRI-based radiomics in the three-class prediction of HER2-positive breast cancer, achieving satisfactory predictive performance. However, the aforementioned investigations were conducted at single or two centers with relatively small sample sizes, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, most previous studies exclusively utilized MRI-based radiomics models without incorporating deep learning techniques, potentially introducing biases in feature selection. Furthermore, the model performance fell short of attaining the requisite level, rendering them unsuitable for direct clinical implementation.

  In this study, we utilized a large dataset from six hospitals across different regions of China to train and validate the model both internally and externally. The substantial variations in baseline clinicopathological data across centers allowed for a comprehensive assessment of the model’s performance in diverse clinical settings. Moreover, the extensive data volume and wide geographical distribution improved the model’s ability to generalize by capturing diverse medical cases and patient populations, thereby better reflecting real-world clinical scenarios. Importantly, as the BERC model was developed using only MRI-derived imaging features without incorporating any clinical or pathological variables, these baseline variations do not constitute confounding factors but rather provide a rigorous test of the model’s generalizability. The consistent performance across these heterogeneous cohorts (AUCs 0.825–0.923) confirms its robustness. An additional consideration for multi-center MRI studies is scanner heterogeneity. In this study, beyond standardized image preprocessing, the consistent performance of BERC across external test sets acquired with different vendors and field strengths (1.5 T and 3.0 T, Additional file 1: Table S1) provides empirical evidence of its robustness to scanner-related variability, supporting its generalizability to diverse clinical settings. In addition, this study applied deep learning throughout the entire process, from image segmentation to model development, significantly reducing the time required for lesion annotation and minimizing biases associated with feature extraction and selection. Moreover, we employed various visualization techniques to analyze the interpretability of the model. Some studies suggest that during treatment, the use of different therapeutic agents may alter the molecular expression profile of breast tumors, leading to discrepancies in IHC findings between pre-treatment biopsy pathology and post-surgical pathology [34–36]. This underscores the advantage of our model, as its non-invasive nature allows for dynamic monitoring of tumor characteristics, such as tracking ER status changes during neoadjuvant or palliative therapy, without the need for repeated invasive procedures. Furthermore, by analyzing the entire tumor volume, BERC has the potential to provide insights into intratumoral heterogeneity in ER expression, offering a more comprehensive view that complements the information obtained from a single biopsy site. By providing a reliable three-tier classification of ER status (ER-negative, ER-low positive, and ER-high positive), the model can effectively support treatment decision-making. Additionally, BERC could reduce dependence on time-consuming and costly IHC. Ensuring the interpretability of its outputs through clear visualizations will further facilitate adoption by radiologists and oncologists, ultimately enhancing patient care and treatment outcomes.

  This study has limitations. First, the retrospective design of the study introduces the possibility of bias in patient selection. Second, although our model was trained and tested on a large dataset, all data were derived from Chinese centers, without collaboration from international multicenter studies. This may introduce selection bias, as racial differences can influence breast cancer histological subtypes, glandular composition, and tumor heterogeneity. Future studies should aim for multicenter validation to enhance the model’s generalizability across diverse populations. Third, ER status was determined using immunohistochemistry at each participating institution according to routine clinical practice and ASCO/CAP guidelines; however, potential inter-laboratory variability in ER assessment may have influenced the ground-truth labels, which underscores the clinical value of developing noninvasive imaging-based tools that may provide complementary information for ER status assessment. Fourth, although calibration analysis showed acceptable overall performance, the limited sample size of ER-low cases in certain external test sets affected the stability of calibration curves for this subgroup, highlighting the need for larger prospective studies to further validate model calibration. Fifth, despite utilizing various visualization techniques to interpret the model, the “black box” nature of deep learning still limits its biological interpretability, which needs further enhancement. In future experiments, exploring genomics methods such as transcriptomics and single-cell sequencing should be considered to improve the biological interpretability of the model.


## Conclusions
  In conclusion, our study proposed BERC, a DCE-MRI-based deep learning system for noninvasively distinguishing ER expression status in breast cancer patients. The ability to identify ER-low positive tumors before treatment could facilitate personalized therapeutic strategies, ultimately improving treatment precision and patient outcomes.
