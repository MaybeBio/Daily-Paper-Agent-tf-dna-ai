# 5-Hydroxytryptamine 6 Receptor Antagonism Ameliorates Depression-Like Behaviors in Temporal Lobe Epilepsy Rats with Potential Involvement of the MEF2C/NF-κB Pathway

## Introduction
  Epilepsy is a chronic neurological disorder characterized by recurrent seizures due to abnormal neuronal discharges in the brain (Pong et al. 2023). Temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE), the most prevalent form of refractory epilepsy, exhibits highly synchronized aberrant neuronal activity and is frequently accompanied by depression, manifested as persistent low mood, anhedonia and insomnia, severely impairing patients’ life quality (Zhang et al. 2025a; Zhong et al. 2022; Wen et al. 2024). The pathogenesis of epilepsy-associated depression is multifactorial, involving neurotransmitter imbalance (especially 5-HT), neuroinflammation, and neural network dysfunction (Huang et al. 2024; Kwon et al. 2022). Notably, epilepsy may induce emotional disturbances through structural and functional damage to neural circuits or repeated seizure activity while depression may lower the seizure threshold, thereby establishing a vicious cycle that exacerbates both conditions (Wen et al. 2024; Bølling-Ladegaard et al. 2023). Despite its clinical significance, epilepsy-related depression remains underrecognized, and effective therapeutic strategies are still lacking.

  The 5-HT6 receptor (5-HT6R) is a G protein-coupled receptor mainly expressed in the hippocampus, cortex, and striatum, brain regions critically involved in cognition, mood regulation, and seizure susceptibility (Chagraoui et al. 2025). Polymorphisms in the 5-HT system have close association with TLE, and elevated 5-HT6R expression has been observed in chronic epilepsy models (Liu et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2015). Preclinical studies show that 5-HT6R blockade can modulate neuronal excitability and reduce seizure frequency (Sun et al. 2021). The underlying mechanisms of its anticonvulsant effects may include the inhibition of excitatory glutamatergic transmission, enhancement of GABAergic signaling, and regulation of intracellular signaling pathways associated with synaptic plasticity (Sun et al. 2021; Chagraoui et al. 2025).

  5-HT6R antagonists have been reported to modulate memory and emotional behaviors, exerting both anti-anxiety and antidepressant-like effects in preclinical models (Anderson et al. 2023; Knowles et al. 2022). Mechanistically, 5-HT6R signaling modulates intracellular cascades such as cAMP/PKA-CREB and MAPK pathways (Sun et al. 2021; Gao et al. 2022; Deng et al. 2024), which are closely associated with the regulation of MEF2C (Lu et al. 2024). MEF2C, a member of the MADS/MEF2 transcription factor family, plays a crucial role in epileptogenesis and is involved in the regulation of inflammatory factors and various signaling pathways (Zheng et al. 2021). Neuroinflammation, particularly through the activation of NF-κB, contributes to abnormal neural connectivity and hyperexcitable networks, thereby facilitating both seizure development and mood disturbances (He et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2023; Allemeier et al. 2025). Elevated NF-κB p65 expression and reduced IκBα level have been observed in the hippocampus of epileptic rats (Zhou et al. 2022). Notably, MEF2C has been shown to exert anti-inflammatory effects partly via NF-κB inactivation (Xu et al. 2015; Yu et al. 2020). These findings suggest a potential mechanistic link by which 5-HT6R antagonism may influence seizures and comorbid depression through possible interactions with the MEF2C/NF-κB signaling axis.

  SB271046 is a selective, high-affinity antagonist of the 5-HT6R, exhibiting favorable pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties in rodents. It possesses good oral bioavailability and efficiently crosses the blood–brain barrier (Ahmed et al. 2005). Pharmacodynamically, SB271046 exhibits nanomolar potency in inhibiting 5-HT6R-mediated cAMP production, and shows high receptor specificity (Chagraoui et al. 2025). These characteristics make SB271046 a valuable tool compound for investigating the role of 5-HT6R in seizure modulation and associated behavioral changes.

  Collectively, this study aimed to investigate whether SB271046, a selective 5-HT6R antagonist, can ameliorate depression-like behaviors in TLE rats and to explore whether these effects are associated with the MEF2C/NF-κB pathway, thereby providing valuable experimental evidence to support the development of novel therapeutic strategies in epilepsy with comorbid depression.


## Methods

### Laboratory Animals
  This study was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital, and all procedures were conducted in accordance with ethical guidelines to minimize animal suffering. Thirty specific pathogen-free (SPF) grade male Sprague Dawley rats (weighing 200–220 g, aged 6–8 weeks) were purchased from Beijing Huafukang Biotechnology Co., Ltd., [China, license No. SCXK (Beijing) 2020-0004]. All rats were housed individually with one rat per cage under controlled environmental conditions (temperature: 23–25 °C; humidity: 50–60%; 12-h light/dark cycle), with ad libitum access to standard chow and water. All rats were allowed to acclimate to the housing conditions for one week prior to experimental procedures.

### Modeling
  Thirty rats were randomly assigned using a computer-generated randomization list into two groups: Normal control (NC) group (n = 10) and Model group (n = 20). Chronic TLE rat models were established as previously described (Zhu et al. 2020). First, rats were first intraperitoneally injected with lithium chloride (3 mmol/kg) to enhance the sensitivity to pilocarpine. After 20 h, atropine sulfate (1 mg/kg, i.p.) was administered to antagonize the peripheral cholinergic effects. Thirty minutes later, pilocarpine hydrochloride (30 mg/kg, i.p.) was injected to induce seizures, typically manifested as involuntary facial twitching and partial tonic–clonic seizures. Two independent raters, who were blinded to the group assignments, observed the rats for seizure activity, including salivation, nodding, forelimb twitching, generalized tonic–clonic seizures, and loss of postural control. If no seizure symptoms occurred within 30 min, an additional dose of pilocarpine (10 mg/kg) was administered once, if necessary. The induction of TLE was considered successful when seizures reached at least Racine class IV (seen Table 1) and lasted for at least 1 h (Racine 1972). To terminate seizures, diazepam (10 mg/kg; i.p.; Sigma-Aldrich, cat. no. D0899) was administered after 1 h of seizure activity; if seizures persisted, a second dose was given 30 min later (Qian et al. 2024). Rats that survived for at least two weeks post-seizure termination were considered to have successfully developed chronic TLE. In this study, one rat died during the modeling process, and another failed to reach Racine class IV seizures, both of which were excluded. ### Drug Administration
  The rats were anesthetized with intraperitoneal injection of a ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) mixture prepared in 0.9% saline (Kelemen et al. 2025), followed by stereotactic surgery. The purpose of this surgery was to implant a cannula into the lateral ventricle to allow precise intracerebroventricular (i.c.v.) administration of SB271046, ensuring accurate targeting and consistent dosing. The i.c.v. route was chosen to bypass the blood–brain barrier, thereby enabling SB271046 to achieve effective concentrations within the brain while minimizing systemic exposure and peripheral side effects (Fahoum and Eyal 2023). As a pretreatment, SB271046 was initially administered 24 h before pilocarpine-induced modeling at a concentration of 10 μg/μL, and was delivered manually at a rate of 1 μL/min for 10 min. The injection coordinates for the lateral ventricle were: 0.8 mm behind the bregma, 1.5 mm lateral to the midline, and 4 mm in depth (Rogers and Hagan 2001; Liu et al. 2019). After the establishment of the chronic TLE model (two weeks post-seizure induction), a second i.c.v. injection of SB271046 (10 μg/μL, 1 μL/min for 10 min) was administered via the implanted cannula. Rats in the NC group and Model group received equal volumes of normal saline at the same time points and infusion rates. Following each administration, all rats were housed in a clean environment to prevent postoperative infection.


### Observation of Seizure Behaviors of Rats
  The seizure behaviors of rats were observed and scored according to the Racine scale 2 h after drug administration (Racine 1972). To minimize observer bias, all seizure scoring and behavioral assessments were performed by two independent experimenters blinded to the treatment groups. Behavioral monitoring continued for 4 h to capture both the onset and progression of seizures, as recommended in pilocarpine-induced epilepsy models (Turski et al. 1983). The duration of seizures within this observation period was recorded.


### Open Field Test
  Four weeks after pilocarpine-induced seizures, all rats were subjected to a battery of tests. The open field test was used to evaluate spontaneous locomotor activity, exploratory behavior, and anxiety levels in a novel environment (Li et al. 2025). The open field apparatus consisted of an open field box with a black interior, measuring 100 × 100 cm at the base and 30–40 cm in height. The base was evenly divided into 25 squares (20 × 20 cm each), with the 16 squares along the perimeter designated as peripheral zones and the remaining 9 squares in the center designated as central grids. A digital camera was positioned 2 m above the apparatus to capture the entire field of view. To minimize stress and external disturbances, test personnel and equipment were located in an adjacent room, and ambient noise was kept to a minimum. The box was cleaned with low-concentration alcohol solution before each trial to eliminate residual olfactory cues. Each rat was gently placed in the central grid, and the times of entering the central grid and the total distance travelled within 10 min were observed and recorded. A reduced frequency of center grid crossings was interpreted as an indicator of increased anxiety (Prut and Belzung 2003). All behavioral testing and data analysis were performed by observers blinded to group assignments.

### Forced Swimming Test
  Each rat was individually placed in a transparent cylindrical container filled with water (water temperature maintained at 23–25 °C), with a depth sufficient to prevent the rats from touching the bottom, and allowed to swim for 5 min. The duration of immobility was recorded during this period. Immobility was defined as the rat ceasing active movements and floating in an upright position with only minimal movements necessary to keep its head above water. An increased duration of immobility is considered a hallmark of depression-like behavior in rodents (Cryan et al. 2005). All immobility measurements were conducted by experimenters blinded to the treatment groups to ensure objective assessment.


### Elevated Plus-Maze Test
  The elevated plus-maze was a white, " + "-shaped apparatus consisting of two open arms, two closed arms and a central platform, all elevated 50 cm above the ground. To ensure consistent lighting and eliminate shadows, the maze was positioned directly beneath a lamp. At the start of each trial, the rat was placed in the central area facing one of the open arms, with its back to the experimenter, and allowed to explore the maze freely for 5 min. After each trial, the rat was returned to its home cage and any feces present on the maze were removed. The entire apparatus was wiped clean with alcohol before the next animal to eliminate olfactory cues. The number of entries into the open arm and the time spent in the open arm were recorded. Both the percentage of entries into the open arms and the time spent in them are validated indicators for anxiety (Rodgers and Dalvi 1997). All behavioral assessments were conducted by observers blinded to the treatment allocation.


### Morris Water Maze Test
  The Morris water maze test was used to evaluate spatial learning and memory skills (Foreman and Gillet 1997). The test consisted of two components: the place navigation test and the spatial probe test, which evaluate cognitive function and spatial memory, respectively. (1) Place navigation test: The apparatus consisted of a circular pool (diameter: 160 cm; depth: 50 cm) filled with water maintained at 22–24 °C and rendered opaque using non-toxic ink. The pool was divided into four quadrants, with a hidden escape platform submerged 1 cm below the water surface in one designated quadrant. During each trial, the rats were placed into the pool from a randomly selected quadrant, with their backs facing the pool wall. Escape latency was defined as the time (in seconds) required for the rats to locate and climb onto the hidden platform. If the rats failed to find the platform within 90 s, a maximum latency of 90 s was recorded, and the rat was gently guided to the platform and allowed to remain there for 10 s to reinforce spatial learning. Each rat received two trials per day (morning and afternoon) for 5 consecutive days. (2) Spatial probe test: On the day following the final training session, the hidden platform was removed to evaluate memory retention. Each rat was released from the same starting point used during training, and its swimming trajectory was recorded using a video tracking system. The number of times the rat crossed the previous platform within 90 s, and the residence time in the target quadrant were recorded as indices of spatial memory performance. All Morris water maze assessments and data analyses were performed by investigators blinded to the group assignments to ensure objectivity.


### Sample Collection
  After completion of the final behavioral test (within 30 min), the rats in each group were deeply anesthetized using isoflurane (Kyllo et al. 2023). Once fully anesthetized, the rats were fixed in a supine position on the operating table, and a thoracotomy was performed to expose the heart. A needle was inserted into the left ventricle, and 150 mL of pre-cooled PBS was perfused within 2 min to flush out blood from the circulatory system. Following perfusion, the brain was quickly harvested. The hippocampus was immediately separated from a portion of the brain tissue and stored in a refrigerator at −20 °C. Another portion was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution, and the remaining brain tissue was quickly placed in liquid nitrogen and stored in a refrigerator at −80 °C.


### Detection of TNF, IL-1β, IL-6, 5-HT and KYN Levels in the Hippocampus by ELISA
  The stored hippocampus tissue was minced on ice and homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer using an ultrasonic grinder. The homogenate was then centrifuged at 15,000 r/min for 5 min at 4 °C (Hashemi et al. 2025). The resulting supernatant was collected and the levels of TNF, IL-1β, IL-6, 5-HT and KYN were measured using ELISA-related assay kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions.


### TUNEL Staining
  The brain tissue fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution was placed into an embedding box, rinsed in clean water overnight and then dehydrated with 70, 80 and 95% and absolute ethanol. Following dehydration, the tissue was cleared in xylene solution and infiltrated with paraffin for three times (30 min each time). After paraffin embedding, the tissue blocks were left at room temperature for 24 h before being sectioned. Paraffin-embedded tissues were serially cut into 4 μm-thick coronal sections using a microtome. The sections were floated on warm water and mounted to glass slides, dried in an incubator at 60 ℃, and then the sections were washed with PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 for 5 min at room temperature. After drying, 50 μL of reaction mixture was added to each section, and slides were incubated for 1 h at 37 ℃ in a humidified chamber (Zhang et al. 2025b). TUNEL-positive neuronal cell death was observed in five random fields under a fluorescence microscope.


### Detection of p-NF-κB p65, NF-κB p65, MEF2C, Iba1 and CD68 Protein Expressions in Hippocampal Tissues by Western Blotting
  The hippocampal tissue (100 mg), previously stored in the refrigerator at −80 ℃, was homogenized in 100 μL of pre-prepared lysis buffer using a tissue homogenizer. The homogenate was centrifuged at 15,000 r/min for 15 min at 4 ℃ (He et al. 2021), and the resting supernatant was collected in EP tubes. Total protein concentration was determined using BCA assay kits. Protein samples were denatured by boiling in hot water for 10 min, then were stored in a refrigerator at −20 ℃. For Western blot analysis, the protein samples were mixed with loading buffer and denatured by boiling. Proteins were separated by 5% SDS-PAGE, and subsequently transferred onto a PVDF membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature, followed by washing with TBST solution for 1 h. The membrane was then incubated with primary antibodies against NF-κB p65, p-NF-κB p65, MEF2C, Iba1, CD68 or GAPDH (1:500) overnight at 4 ℃ in the dark. Subsequently, the membrane was incubated with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:1000) for another 2 h at room temperature. Signal detection was performed using ECL solution, and protein bands were visualized by exposure. The grayscale intensity of each protein band was quantified by Image Lab software. The original uncropped western blot images are provided in Supplementary Figure S1.

  NF-κB p65, p-NF-κB p65, MEF2C, Iba1, CD68 and GAPDH were detected on the same PVDF membrane using sequential stripping and re-probing to ensure consistent loading and normalization across targets.

  To ensure antibody specificity, the primary antibodies used in this study were validated based on manufacturer-provided datasheets, available published literature employing the same antibodies (Hu et al. 2020; Ruan et al. 2022), and the observation of single immunoreactive bands at the expected molecular weights in rat hippocampal tissue. However, these antibodies were not validated using knockout models, which should be considered a methodological limitation.


### Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) Staining
  Hippocampal tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 4–5 μm slices. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through graded ethanol, and stained with hematoxylin followed by eosin. After dehydration and mounting, histopathological changes were observed under an optical microscope.


### Immunofluorescence Staining
  Hippocampal tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 4–5 μm slices. After deparaffinization and rehydration, sections were subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer, followed by permeabilization with 0.3% Triton X-100. Non-specific binding was blocked using 5% BSA. Sections were then incubated with primary antibodies against Iba1 or CD68 at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with appropriate fluorescent secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope.


### Differential Gene Expression Analysis and KEGG Enrichment Analysis
  The Microarray data GSE88992, comprising 8 epilepsy samples and 9 normal samples, was obtained from the GEO website (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gds) (Kalozoumi et al. 2018). The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the limma R package (version 4.2.2), with thresholds set at |logFC|> 1 and adjusted P value < 0.05. Then, KEGG enrichment analysis was conducted using the clusterProfiler package (version 4.7.1.003).


### Molecular Docking
  Molecular docking was conducted to evaluate the binding interactions between 5-HT6R antagonist SB271046 and the target proteins NF-κB p65 (PDB ID: 1MY5) and MEF2C (PDB ID: 5F28) using Autodock 4, a widely used in silico protein–ligand docking software (http://autodock.scripps.edu/) (Morris et al. 2008). The molecular structure of SB271046 was retrieved from PubChem Compound (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) (Wang et al. 2017). The protein structures were downloaded from the protein data bank RCSB (http://www.rcsb.org/pdb/home/home.do) and were saved as the PDB format. For docking analysis, all water molecules were removed from the protein structure and hydrogen atoms were added. Both ligand and receptors files were converted into PDBQT format. The docking grid box was set to dimensions of 50 Å × 50 Å × 50 Å and centered on the active domain of each protein to allow sufficient space for molecular movement and interaction. The docking results were evaluated based on the number of hydrogen bonds and the binding energy (kcal/mol), which reflect the affinity and stability of the protein–ligand complexes.


### Theoretical Mechanism Prediction Analysis

#### Downstream Signaling Pathway Exploration of HTR6
  To explore the potential molecular mechanisms by which the 5-HT6R antagonist SB271046 might influence the MEF2C/NF-κB signaling axis, we first investigated the known downstream signaling of HTR6, a Gs protein-coupled receptor, through database mining and literature review. The GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org) and the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (https://ctdbase.org) were queried using the keywords "HTR6" and "SB271046" to identify relevant signaling components and molecular interactions. Based on the retrieved data, HTR6 is known to activate the ADCY-cAMP-PKA-CREB1 signaling cascade. These pathway elements were recorded and incorporated into a hypothesized regulatory network in which HTR6 inhibition by SB271046 may be associated with CREB1-related changes in MEF2C expression and potential downstream modulation of NF-κB activity.


#### Cis-Regulatory Motif Analysis of the MEF2C Promoter
  To evaluate the potential transcriptional regulation of MEF2C by CREB1, we performed a cis-regulatory motif analysis of the MEF2C promoter region using the JASPAR 2022 database (https://jaspar.genereg.net). The reference sequence of the human MEF2C gene (RefSeq: NM_182919.3) was retrieved, and a 2-kb upstream promoter region (chromosome 5: 88,674,218–88,676,217, hg38 assembly) was extracted via the UCSC Genome Browser. Motif scanning was conducted using the CREB1 position frequency matrix (MA0018.1) in JASPAR, with a relative profile score threshold set at 80%. Both the forward and reverse strands were analyzed. Only high-confidence motif matches that fully conformed to the canonical CREB1-binding motif "TGACGTCA" were retained for further interpretation. The genomic positions, strand orientation, and corresponding binding site sequences were recorded and analyzed in the context of potential CREB1-mediated transcriptional activation of MEF2C.


#### Construction of the Predicted HTR6-MEF2C-NF-κB Signaling Pathway
  Based on the integrated results from molecular interaction data and cis-regulatory motif analysis, a putative signaling pathway was constructed to illustrate a possible framework by which HTR6 inhibition might be associated with changes in MEF2C expression and potential downstream influences on the NF-κB signaling cascade. This predicted pathway is intended only as a conceptual and hypothesis-generating model, providing a preliminary perspective on how 5-HT6R antagonism could be linked to anti-inflammatory and antidepressant effects without implying a definitive causal mechanism.


### Statistical Analysis
  GraphPad Prism 8.0 software was used for statistical analysis. Measurement data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s). The comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

### 5-HT6R Antagonist SB271046 Relieved Anxiety in TLE Rats
  The elevated plus-maze test was used to investigate the anxiety-like behavior. For the number of entries into the open arm, one-way ANOVA showed a significant effect of group, F(2, 25) = 244.5, P < 0.001. Compared with the NC group, rats in the Model group displayed markedly fewer open-arm entries, whereas SB271046 treatment significantly restored exploratory behavior (Fig. 4A). ## Discussion
  The incidence of depression is significantly higher in epilepsy patients compared with the general population, and the risk is further increased in specific groups such as patients with TLE (Qin et al. 2022; Aljafen et al. 2024). Comorbid epilepsy patients often exhibit a low response rate to current treatment strategies (Alkhaldi 2025). Therefore, it is crucial to investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying epilepsy-associated emotional and cognitive disturbances and identify reliable and effective therapeutic targets. Our results confirmed that 5-HT6R antagonist SB271046 could reduce epilepsy-associated depression- and anxiety-like behaviors, neuroinflammation and TUNEL-positive neuronal cell death in TLE rats, and these effects may be associated with alterations in the MEF2C/NF-κB pathway. These findings provide preliminary evidence supporting the therapeutic potential of 5-HT6R modulation in alleviating emotional and cognitive disturbances secondary to epilepsy-related brain dysfunction, while the contribution of multiple brain regions involved in affective regulation should also be considered.

  Currently, the mechanisms underlying TLE-related reaction are extensively probed through the establishment of TLE models using lithium chloride-pilocarpine regimen (Lévesque et al. 2016). Although this model is primarily validated for epilepsy, previous evidence indicates that chronic epileptic activity can also lead to persistent affective and cognitive disturbances, including anxiety- and depression-like behaviors (Krishnan 2020). Similarly, we established rat models of TLE via the injection of lithium chloride and pilocarpine. Following successful model induction, the rats were treated with 5-HT6R antagonist SB271046. Previous research has shown that 5-HT2C antagonist agomelatine can regulate circadian rhythms and improve depressive symptoms in epilepsy patients (Savino et al. 2023). Köhler-Forsberg et al. have demonstrated that 5-HT4 receptors exhibited reduced binding in the brain of patients with depression, including those with epilepsy comorbidity and are closely related to memory dysfunction (Köhler-Forsberg et al. 2023). More importantly, in epilepsy-related depression, abnormalities in the 5-HT signaling pathway may lead to dysregulation of monoamine neurotransmitter levels. Modulating 5-HT6R can improve depressive symptoms by restoring the balance within these pathways (Oh et al. 2023). Serotonin receptors, including 5-HT6R, are distributed across multiple brain regions such as the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex, which are critically involved in mood regulation, emotional processing, and cognitive function (Nirogi et al. 2023).

  In this paper, behavioral assessments were conducted four weeks after pilocarpine-induced status epilepticus, a time point commonly considered to reflect the chronic phase of TLE (Nizinska et al. 2021). At this stage, rats exhibited reduced exploratory activity in the open field and elevated plus maze tests, decreased sucrose preference, and increased immobility time in the forced swimming test. Additionally, cognitive impairments were evident in the Morris water maze test, characterized by extended escape latency and reduced spatial learning and memory performance. These findings suggested that chronic TLE is accompanied by widespread emotional and cognitive dysfunction, potentially mediated by dysregulated neurotransmission and increased neuro-excitability. Interestingly, locomotor activity showed distinct patterns across behavioral tests. In the open field tests, TLE rats exhibited increased total distance traveled, possibly reflecting altered exploratory behavior associated with chronic epileptic activity. In contrast, locomotor activity in the elevated plus maze tests did not differ among groups, suggesting that the observed anxiety-like behaviors were not solely attributable to generalized locomotor alterations. This discrepancy may reflect the different behavioral dimensions assessed by the two paradigms, with the open field test evaluating spontaneous exploration and the elevated plus maze being more sensitive to anxiety-related avoidance behavior. It is noteworthy that affective and cognitive behaviors are governed by an integrated neural network involving not only the hippocampus but also the prefrontal cortex and other limbic-related regions. Given the widespread distribution of serotonin receptors, including 5-HT6R, across these interconnected brain regions, the behavioral improvements observed following SB271046 administration may reflect broad neurostabilizing and neuromodulatory effects. Against this background, treatment with the 5-HT6R antagonist SB271046 significantly alleviated these behavioral deficits, as evidenced by increased exploratory behavior, enhanced sucrose preference, and improved cognitive performance. Taken together, these findings suggest that SB271046 improved emotional and cognitive outcomes secondary to the normalization of epilepsy-induced brain dysfunction, consistent with previous reports indicating that 5-HT receptor antagonists could mitigate cognitive impairments and depression-like behaviors (Lahogue et al. 2023; Tang et al. 2023).

  Previous studies have demonstrated that epilepsy seizures can activate microglia cells, increase the release of pro-inflammatory factors, including TNF, IL-1β and IL-6 and induce neuroinflammation. These processes exacerbate the vicious cycle of epilepsy and further promote depression-like behaviors (Kuang et al. 2024; Wen et al. 2024). Importantly, although pro-inflammatory cytokines are classically associated with acute or subacute inflammatory responses, accumulating evidence indicates that a single episode of status epilepticus can trigger a persistent state of chronic neuroinflammation, characterized by sustained microglial activation and prolonged inflammatory signaling lasting for weeks after the initial seizure insult (Wyatt-Johnson et al. 2017). In patients with TLE and hippocampal sclerosis, neuronal loss and degeneration are closely associated with an increased risk of depression (Wang et al. 2024). Moreover, neuroinflammation can contribute to neuronal loss and thus aggravate depressive symptoms (Chen et al. 2024). Therefore, suppressing neuroinflammation and neuron injury can simultaneously improve both epilepsy and depression symptoms. It has been shown that 5-HT6R inhibition can induce the release of anti-inflammatory cytokines, and thus alleviate neuroinflammation (Wang et al. 2025). Hashemi-Firouzi and co-workers have reported that 5-HT6R antagonist SB258585 can suppress injury in the hippocampus (Hashemi-Firouzi et al. 2018).

  Similarly, our data revealed that in epileptic rats, the increased levels of TNF, IL-1β and IL-6 in the hippocampus, along with increased neuronal TUNEL-positive neuronal cell death, and that treatment with SB271046 markedly attenuated these changes. Given the chronic time point of assessment, these alterations likely reflect epilepsy-associated chronic neuroinflammation, although contributions from residual acute seizure-related injury cannot be completely ruled out. Furthermore, the imbalance between 5-HT and KYN facilitates depression and neuronal degeneration (Shu et al. 2025). In this study, we also found that the increase in 5-HT and the reduction in KYN were reversed by the intervention of SB271046. Taken together, these findings suggested that SB271046 may help reduce depression-like behaviors in TLE partly by attenuating neuroinflammation and reducing TUNEL-positive neuronal cell death in brain tissues. Collectively, these findings suggest that SB271046 may alleviate epilepsy-associated emotional and cognitive disturbances by attenuating neuroinflammation and preserving neuronal integrity.

  According to network pharmacological analysis and molecular docking studies, we found that the genes up-regulated in TLE were significantly enriched in the NF-κB signaling pathway and 5-HT6R antagonist SB271046 showed predicted interactions with NF-κB p65. NF-κB is a key regulator of the inflammatory pathway in the comorbidity of epilepsy and depression. In epilepsy models, the activation of NF-κB promotes the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, exacerbating neuroinflammation in the hippocampus and ultimately worsening depressive symptoms (Khan et al. 2024; Wen et al. 2024; Wu et al. 2023). Additionally, MEF2C may contribute to the comorbidity mechanism of epilepsy and depression by modulating the expression of neurotrophic factors such as BDNF (Sun et al. 2024). Furthermore, MEF2C may exert neuroprotective function by inhibiting the pro-inflammatory effect of NF-κB (Sun et al. 2024). In this study, increased NF-κB p65 expression and reduced MEF2C expression in the hippocampus of TLE rats were reversed following SB271046 administration, supporting an association between 5-HT6R antagonism and neurostabilizing molecular changes linked to inflammation and neuronal survival.


## Limitations
  Several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. First, only a single dose of SB271046 was administered, and dose-dependent effects or potential toxicity were not evaluated. Second, the absence of a standard treatment comparator limits the contextual interpretation of efficacy. Third, while molecular docking suggested involvement of the MEF2C/NF-κB pathway, direct mechanistic validation was not performed. Fourth, TUNEL staining does not distinguish specific modes of regulated cell death. In addition, while the pilocarpine-induced TLE model is commonly used to study epilepsy-associated affective disturbances, it is not a primary model of depression; thus, the observed depression-like behaviors should be interpreted in the context of epilepsy-related mood alterations. Moreover, given the widespread distribution of 5-HT receptors, off-target neuromodulatory effects beyond the hippocampus cannot be excluded. Furthermore, although the primary antibodies used for western blot analysis were selected based on manufacturer-provided datasheets and prior literature, they were not validated using knockout models, which may raise concerns regarding antibody specificity. Finally, no a priori sample size calculation was conducted.


## Conclusion
  In conclusion, the 5-HT6R antagonist SB271046 was associated with improvements in epilepsy-associated emotional and cognitive disturbances in TLE rats, accompanied by reduced neuroinflammation, decreased neuronal cell death, and modulation of MEF2C/NF-κB signaling. These findings suggest that SB271046 may confer behavioral benefits secondary to its neurostabilizing and anti-inflammatory actions in the epileptic brain. Although the mechanistic links remain preliminary and require further experimental validation, the present study supports the potential of SB271046 as a candidate for managing neuropsychiatric comorbidities associated with epilepsy.
