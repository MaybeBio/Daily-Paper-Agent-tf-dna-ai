# Mononuclear Mg(ii) and Ca(ii) metallocarboxylates of N-phthaloyl-γ-aminobutyric acid: synthesis, characterization, ds-DNA binding, preliminary anticancer evaluation against MDA-MB-231 cells, and computational studies


## Abstract
  This study reports the synthesis of neurotransmitter-inspired, biolabile mononuclear Mg(ii)/(NY1) and Ca(ii)/(NY2) metallocarboxylates of N-phthaloyl-γ-aminobutyric acid ligand (NPGABA), motivated by the growing relevance of metallocarboxylates in addressing essential metal-ion deficiencies. Extensive spectral (i.e. IR, UV-vis, 1H-NMR, 13C-NMR, AAS, and ICP-AES) and analytical (SEM, micro-analysis and thermal gravimetric analysis) techniques were used to characterize and confirm the formation of the ligand and its metal carboxylate complexes. The complexes were confirmed to have the formulas [Mg(NPGABA)2(H2O)2]4 (NY1) and [Mg(NPGABA)2(H2O)4] (NY2), where NPGABA is the abbreviation of the ligand in its deprotonated form. The DNA-binding interactions of the NPGABA ligand (NY0) and its alkaline earth metal carboxylate complexes with magnesium (Mg/NY1) and calcium (Ca/NY2) were investigated through a combination of biophysical, computational, and biological assays. UV-visible absorption studies revealed a significant hyperchromic effect without a bathochromic/hypsochromic shift, indicative of a non-intercalative external binding mode (i.e., minor groove binding or electrostatic interaction with the anionic sugar phosphate backbone of DNA). Binding constants (Kb) were calculated by UV-vis spectroscopy at a temperature of 310.15 K and pH of 7.4 as 2.18 × 105 M−1 (NY0), 4.05 × 105 M−1 (NY1), and 4.21 × 105 M−1 (NY2), with Gibbs free energies (ΔG°) of −31.68, −33.28, and −33.38 kJ mol−1, respectively, confirming spontaneous interactions. Similarly, fluorescence emission spectroscopic studies demonstrated eminent quenching without any shift, providing complementary evidence for the presence of external non-intercalative interactions. Accordingly, the binding constants (Kb) calculated by this technique under the same physiological conditions were 1.2 × 104 M−1 (NY0), 4.41 × 104 M−1 (NY1), and 8.61 × 104 M−1 (NY2), with binding site numbers of 0.86 (NY0), 0.95 (NY1), and 1.01 (NY2) and Gibbs free energies (ΔG°) of −24.23, −27.56, and −29.27 kJ mol−1, respectively, confirming the spontaneity of the interaction process. Molecular docking simulations against the canonical B-DNA (PDB: 1BNA) and the aromatase cytochrome P450 enzyme (PDB: 3EQM) also supported the occurrence of non-intercalative external binding interactions, with NY2 demonstrating the most favorable binding pose and energy. This non-intercalative external binding interaction was further endorsed by minute perturbation in the DNA solution viscosity and analogical consistency with classical groove binders like netropsin and distamycin A (Kb ≈ 105–107 M−1). The observed DNA binding affinity trend (NY2 > NY1 > NY0) has a direct correlation with the cytotoxic potency of the investigated compounds against triple negative breast cancer cells (MDA-MB-231). This consolidated investigation proved NY2 as a non-intercalative DNA-binding small molecule with preliminary anticancer activity, offering a strategic route for developing sustainable metallocarboxylate-based therapeutics.


## Introduction
  γ-Aminobutyric acid (GABA) is a naturally occurring, non-proteinogenic amino acid that primarily functions as an inhibitory neurotransmitter in the central nervous system, playing a crucial regulatory role in maintaining neuronal balance, sleep modulation, and the alleviation of anxiety and depressive disorders.1 Although GABA naturally occurs in various dietary sources,2 including cereals, legumes, vegetables, fruits, and tea, its natural abundance is typically low, which has motivated the development of strategic enrichment techniques such as germination, abiotic stress, thermal processing, and microbial fermentation.3–6 Consequently, GABA-enriched foods have attracted considerable attention owing to their reported neuroprotective, antihypertensive, antidiabetic, and anti-inflammatory effects, positioning GABA as a key bioactive component in functional foods and nutraceuticals. However, despite all these advancements, its low stability, bioavailability, and molecular targeting capability still limit its broader material and therapeutic applications.2 In parallel, N-protected amino acid-based metal carboxylates have been materialized as an important class of compounds at the interface of bioinorganic and medicinal chemistry.7,8 Beyond their conventional role in modern organic and peptide-based drug synthesis, these complexes present diverse biological activities,9–11 including antimicrobial, anticancer, and agrochemical effects, largely governed by their coordination and structural tunability. Among various protecting groups, the N-phthaloyl protecting group remains ingeniously transformative, with its ability to convert a simple zwitterionic amino acid into a sophisticated multidentate organic carboxylic acid ligand by suppressing the basic amine site and enabling coordination to metal ions through the carboxylate oxygen, similar to an ordinary carboxylic acid, and the additional two oxygen atoms of the carbonyl groups of the phthalimide ring.12 This tunable coordinative mode transforms simple amino acids into structurally defined tridentate ligands capable of forming stable metal chelates with enhanced capability to introduce special biological activities and relevance for the further functionalization of bioactive food components. Consequently, these phthaloyl derivatives of amino acids have vast significance in synthetic organic chemistry because they can form various functionalized biologically active derivatives, which in turn transform into different heterocyclic compounds, amides and metal complexes. This distinctive tridentate binding capability induces stable, structurally defined coordination crucial to the biological and chemical properties of the resulting metallocarboxylate complexes. In essence, the metal complexes demonstrated their inherently enhanced and multi-directional bioactivities.13 For example, triorganotin(iv) complexes acted as dual-action agents for crop protection, demonstrating potent insecticidal activity against the grain pest (Sitophilus granaries) (Bean Weevil)14 and robust fungicidal activity against pathogens like Aspergillus niger. This positioned them as sustainable lead compounds for protecting stored grains, a critical area of global food security.

  Thus, the privileged N-phthaloyl scaffold is considered an important precursor in medicinal chemistry for the synthesis of pharmacologically active compounds, including analgesics,15 antibacterial agents,16 and antitumor drugs. Similarly, metal complexes derived from N-protected amino acids have emerged as an important class of compounds at the interface of bioinorganic and medicinal chemistry. Coordination to a metal center often enhances the activity of these complexes by modulating bioavailability, altering membrane permeability, and providing novel mechanisms of action that can overcome drug resistance.17–20 Furthermore, these tunable and well-characterized metallocarboxylates serve as excellent synthetic models for introducing novel chemical entities and understanding fundamental metal–macromolecule interactions, providing insights into more complex biological processes.21–23 The literature12 exclusively presents that N-protected amino acid metal complexes are functionally sophisticated and crucial molecules due to their strong and diverse biological applications, their unique chemical behavior as ligands, and their role as fundamental tools and precursors in medicinal and agricultural chemistry. Their value arises from the synergistic combination of a biologically active organic scaffold with the unique inherent electronic and structural features conferred by the metal coordination through the oxygen atom, rendering these metal carboxylate complex systems a compelling subject for continued research in food, agrochemical and pharmaceutical developments.

  These metal carboxylate complexes of bioactive metabolites, food components and other natural products exhibit enhanced bioavailability and physiological compatibility, making them ideal candidates for targeted nutritional interventions as bridged herbal and mineral supplementation.24 They offer improved solubility, reduced gastrointestinal irritation, and optimized cellular uptake, as compared to conventional mineral salts. Herbiotic integration combines the natural benefits of plant-based compounds with the precision of metal–organic systems, thereby supporting the growing demand for clean-label, functional supplements. This synthetic approach not only reinforces the therapeutic potential of trace metals but also opens a new window for material-to-medicinal chemistry via the convergence of natural product chemistry and biomedical innovation. This herbiotic synergy bridges the gap between herbal wisdom and biochemical efficacy, leading to new frontiers in personalized supplementation via the fusion of metal–organic chemistry and nutraceuticals, where science meets nature to empower holistic health. Thus, harnessing bioactive natural products in metallocarboxylates might be a transformative approach in the development of next-generation herbiotics and it may redefine the standards for safe, effective, sustainable and bioactive supplementation.

  In the same context, the strategically designed and developed N-phthaloyl GABA ligand combines the intrinsic bioactivity of the GABA food ingredient with the inherent coordination versatility of the phthaloyl scaffold.25 Its further complexation with biologically essential alkaline-earth metal ions, such as Mg(ii) and Ca(ii), is particularly attractive for the synthesis of metallocarboxylates, as these metals play fundamental roles in cellular signaling, enzyme regulation, and nucleic-acid stabilization while exhibiting low toxicity and high physiological compatibility.26–29 Coordination to Mg(ii) and Ca(ii) is expected to tune the electronic environment, improve molecular stability, and enhance biomolecular interactions, thereby extending the functional potential of GABA-based systems.

  Prompted by the fact that metallocarboxylates not only represent a promising frontier in the formulation of herbiotic supplements aimed at addressing deficiencies in essential metal ions, such as magnesium, calcium, zinc, and iron, but also offer an important anchorage for engineering of novel molecular entities by incorporating bioactive natural food constituents, the present study reported the synthesis of mononuclear magnesium(ii) (NY1) and calcium(ii) (NY2) metallocarboxylates using N-phthaloyl-γ-aminobutyric acid (NPGABA) as the coordinating bioactive ligand. The resulting complexes were structurally characterized and subsequently evaluated for a range of in silico and in vitro biological activities, including non-covalent DNA binding, enzyme inhibition and anticancer efficacy.

  To elucidate the molecular basis for these interactions, molecular docking studies of the optimized structure were performed using the canonical B-DNA (PDB ID: 1BNA) and the human aromatase cytochrome P450 enzyme (PDB ID: 3EQM) as representative nucleic acid and protein targets, respectively. The 1BNA model offered a well-established structure for evaluating the binding propensity of the compounds to DNA, groove recognition and non-covalent stabilization of DNA, which facilitated the critical understanding of potential genotoxic or therapeutic interactions with DNA.30 Conversely, aromatase, a key enzyme in estrogen biosynthesis, represents an important pharmacological target in hormone-dependent cancers, making 3EQM a relevant model for evaluating enzyme–ligand affinity and inhibitory potential.31 Collectively, DNA binding and aromatase interaction profiles emphasize the potential of these systems not only as functional bioinorganic models but also as possible candidates for nutraceutical, therapeutic, and herbiotic applications, thereby bridging natural product chemistry with modern medicinal, structural and food chemistries.


## Materials and methods

### Materials
  Analytical-grade preliminary reagents, including phthalic anhydride, γ-aminobutyric acid, ethyl acetate, methanol, ethanol, and glacial acetic acid, were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Magnesium acetate [Mg(CH3COO)2·H2O] and calcium acetate [Ca(CH3COO)2·H2O] were obtained from BDH, and chloroform was obtained from Fluka. Chicken blood was collected during slaughter via a ventral neck incision. The chicken blood used solely for the extraction of ds-DNA was obtained from Danish Chicken Shop, Sheershah Suri Road, Sadaat Market, Jhangi Syedan, Islamabad, Pakistan, during routine slaughter at a facility located within the territorial jurisdiction of the Capital Development Authority (CDA), Islamabad. The sample was collected in accordance with the applicable institutional protocols and regulatory requirements of the National University of Sciences and Technology (NUST) governing the use of biological materials for in vitro research. The blood was used exclusively for in vitro ds-DNA extraction. The triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cell line MDA-MB-231 (ATCC® HTB-26™) and the normal human breast epithelial cell line MCF-10A (ATCC® CRL-10317™) used in this study were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), USA.

  No human participants or live animals were involved in the experimental procedures. All work was carried out in accordance with the relevant institutional protocols and applicable regulatory requirements, with approval from the NUST Ethics/Health and Physical Safety Approval Committee, where applicable. Informed consent was not required because the study did not involve human participants.


### Preparation of N-phthaloyl-γ-aminobutyric acid ligand
  Equimolar quantities of phthalic anhydride (1 g, 6 mmol) and γ-aminobutyric acid (1 g, 6 mmol) were well ground and then introduced into the pre-heated Pyrex test tube at 135 °C. The reaction mixture was fused for 15 min at 135 °C in an oil bath. The molten reaction product was then cooled to room temperature to solidify the product. Excess phthalic anhydride sublimed onto the walls of the test tube and was then scraped off.32,33 The colorless solid product (85% yield) was collected and labelled as NY0. Fig. 1a represents the synthesis scheme of the ligand, and its mechanism is given in SI Fig. S1.


### Synthesis of metallocarboxylates
  An equimolar solution of N-phthaloyl-γ-aminobutyric acid (5.0 mmol/40 mL) (NPGABA) in ethanol and sodium hydroxide (5.0 mmol/45 mL) in water were intermixed dropwise under vigorous stirring at room temperature for 2 h. Then, 2.5 mmol of metal acetates (magnesium acetate [Mg(CH3COO)2]) were added under continuous stirring and heating at 80 °C for 1 hour. The reaction mixture was continuously monitored by thin-layer chromatography using a methanol/chloroform (2 : 8) solvent system and after consumption of the N-phthaloyl γ-aminobutyric acid reactant. The hot solution of the reaction mixture was filtered and purified by recrystallization. After air drying under vacuum, ∼1.5 g (73% yield) of the colorless solid product was collected and labelled as NY1. The calcium-based metallocarboxylate of NPGABA was separately prepared using calcium acetate [Ca(CH3COO)2] and the same method. Its final dried product (∼1.4 g, 71% yield) was obtained and labelled as NY2. The scheme in Fig. 1b represents the synthesis of metallocarboxylate complexes.


### Characterization
  The presence of functional groups in the ligand (NY0) and its metal carboxylates (NY1 & NY2) was investigated with the help of a Fourier Transform Infrared Spectrometer (FT-IR ATR, ALPHA 20488). The CHNS Analyzer (PerkinElmer, 2400 Series, Soung Ryu & Gwen Tenney) was used for microanalysis, and melting points were determined with an electrothermal apparatus (Gallen Kamp, Cat No. MPD350, Sanyo, UK). The Mg and Ca contents (in wt%) in the complexes were estimated by inductively coupled plasma-atomic emission spectrometry (ICP-AES) and atomic absorption spectrometry (AAS) (PerkinElmer Precisely A Analyst 200). In addition, a UV-visible (UV-1800 Shimadzu) spectrophotometer and a fluorescence spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer, USA) were used to determine the optical properties. The crystallinities of ligand NY0 and its Mg & Ca metallocarboxylate complexes (NY1 & NY2, respectively) were evaluated by X-ray diffraction analysis (XRD, Dron-8, Cu-Kα, λ = 1.5406 Å). The thermal stability of the prepared carboxylate complexes was evaluated by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), and a heating rate of 10 °C min−1 was used.


### DNA binding studies
  The double-strand DNA (ds-DNA) was isolated from the chicken blood and then dissolved in double-distilled autoclaved water to prepare the stock solution of 4.6 × 10−5 M. The purity of DNA was checked by measuring the A260/A280 ratio, and the concentration of DNA was determined spectrophotometrically by measuring at A260, using a molar extinction coefficient of ε260 = 6600 M−1 cm−1.


#### Photophysical titration method
  All the photophysical titrations were performed at 310 K (body temperature) in a 0.1 M phosphate buffer (blood pH: 7.4) under physiological conditions. The concentration of each test compound was kept constant at 8 µM throughout the absorption as well as fluorescence emission studies, while the concentration of the chicken blood ds-DNA gradually increased from 0 to 14 µM in the sample cell under standard addition. The spectra were measured after each addition of DNA, with the mixture incubated for 5–7 minutes at the desired temperature before the spectrum was taken to allow the complex to reach equilibrium. To investigate the interaction of the compounds with ds-DNA, the changes in the absorbance/emission spectra (physical variations in spectral intensities (hypochromism or hyperchromism) and possible shifts in λmax (hypsochromic or bathochromic)) were monitored. Subsequently, the data were used to compute several kinetic parameters, such as binding constants (Kb) and Gibbs free energy, to gain insight into the binding modes (intercalation, groove binding or electrostatic interactions).

#### Molecular docking studies
  Molecular docking simulations of the synthesized compounds (NY0, NY1, and NY2) were performed against double-stranded DNA (PDB: 1BNA) and aromatase cytochrome P450 (PDB: 3EQM), a protein target associated with breast cancer, to elucidate the binding interactions and affinity. All simulations were conducted using Gaussian 09 for ligand optimization and the Molecular Operating Environment (MOE, 2024) for docking analysis. The DNA and protein structures were retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank. Crystallographic water molecules were retained, and structures were protonated and energy-minimized using the AMBER force field, followed by refinement with the PM3 semi-empirical method. The geometry of the test compounds (NY0–NY2) was optimized to their minimum energy states using Gaussian 09 at the B3LYP/6-31G(d) level. A systematic conformational search was performed with an RMS gradient threshold of 0.01 kcal mol−1. The MOE Site Finder tool was used to identify potential binding sites. Five independent docking runs were performed for each compound, and the pose with the lowest binding free energy was selected for detailed interaction analysis. Potential binding sites were identified using the Site Finder tool in MOE. For each compound, five independent docking runs were executed, and the pose with the most negative binding free energy was selected for detailed interaction analysis.

### MTT assay
  The cytotoxic potentials of the synthesized ligand NY0 and its corresponding complexes, NY1 and NY2, were evaluated against the triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) MDA-MB-231 cell line using the MTT assay. The normal human breast epithelial cell line MCF-10A was employed to assess biocompatibility and determine the selectivity of the compounds toward cancer cells. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2) until reaching exponential growth. Cells were seeded in an autoclaved 96-well cell culture plate and exposed to ligand NY0 and its magnesium (NY1) and calcium (NY2) complexes in varying concentrations of 25, 50, 100, 150, 200, 250, and 300 µg mL−1. Untreated cells were considered as the control (C). Cytotoxicity and biocompatibility were assessed by analyzing the viable cells using tetrazolium salt MTT (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) as a colorimetric agent. After the cell treatment of MDA-MB-231 and MCF-10A cells for 24 h, the MTT solution was added to each well, followed by incubation again for a further 4 h. Then, the supernatant was carefully separated without disturbing the formazan crystals and 100 µL of DMSO was added to each well to dissolve the crystals. #### UV-vis analysis
  The UV-vis absorption spectra of the fabricated free ligand (NY0) and the corresponding metal complexes, namely, NY1 and NY2, are presented in Fig. 2(a–d). The free ligand NY0 shows an absorption band at 220 nm, attributed to the π → π* electronic transitions within the aromatic system of the indoline structure. Changes in the spectral profile were noticed on complexation with transition metal ions. The absorbance intensity of both the metal carboxylate complexes exhibited a clear change at a constant λmax of 220 nm, without any shift in the absorption maxima,34,35 indicating a strong coordination between the metal ions and the ligand. The binding site (receptor) was synthetically decoupled from the chromophore via a spacer, and the molecular orbitals involved in the light absorption did not directly participate in the bond formation with the metal ion. Hence, the electronic environment of the chromophore did not sense any electronic perturbation, and the simple increase in the absorption intensity occurred due to the increase in the net concentration of the ligand chromophore. In the instant case, the binding site (e.g., a carboxylic acid group) of NY0 is electronically isolated from the chromophore (e.g., an imidazole ring). Concomitantly, the molecular orbitals involved in the electronic transition (e.g., π → π* in NY0) are not significantly perturbed by the binding to the metal ion via the electronically isolated carboxylate (COO−) binding site. Therefore, the metal ions may bind to the ligand without altering the energy levels (ground and excited states) of the chromophore (the light-absorbing part of the ligand). Such interactions may be primarily electrostatic or mediated by atoms that are not directly part of the π-system, and spectral changes confirm the successful formation of metal–ligand complexes.

#### FTIR spectroscopic analysis
  The infrared spectra of complexes 1–3 exhibit distinct variations, particularly within the 3500–3000 cm−1 and 1600–1350 cm−1 regions, which are diagnostic of coordination modes and the presence of solvent or aqua ligands. In the high-frequency region, the spectra of metal complexes NY1 and NY2 display broad bands at 3535 and 3511 cm−1, attributed to the ν(H2O) coordinated water stretching vibration.36–41 The presence of broad absorptions attributable to O–H stretching vibrations suggested that the complexes crystallize in a hydrous form, a conclusion that was further supported by elemental and thermogravimetric analyses. In contrast, the IR spectrum of the ligand is dominated by a broad, intense band in the 3050–2920 cm−1 region, characteristic of the ν(O–H) vibrations of the free carboxylic acid (–COOH) group. The disappearance of this band in the spectra of metal complexes NY1 and NY2 confirmed the formation of metal carboxylate complexes. The coordination geometry and binding mode of the carboxylate group were probed by analyzing the asymmetric [νas(COO)] and symmetric [νs(COO)] stretching vibrations. The magnitude of the separation, Δν [νas(COO)–νs(COO)], serves as an empirical indicator of carboxylate coordination. For metal complexes NY1 and NY2, the large Δν values of 266 cm−1 (1647 vs. 1381 cm−1) and 225 cm−1 (1635 vs. 1410 cm−1) were consistent with the monodentate carboxylate binding mode,36,42,43 supporting the proposed octahedral geometry around the Mg(ii) and Ca(ii) ions, as elaborated in SI, Table S1.


#### 1H/13C (D2O solvent) NMR results and diagnostics
  The 1H and 13C magnetic resonance spectra of ligand NY0 and its metal carboxylates were also recorded in D2O (SI, Fig. 2s–7s). In the 1H NMR spectra, the carboxylic acid H was not observed due to its exchange with the D2O solvent.44 The conversion from the carboxylic acid (–COOH) into the metal carboxylate (–COO−M+) results in a localized reduction in the electron-withdrawing inductive (–I) effect at the chain head. This was the primary driver of all observed NMR changes. The 1H NMR results are presented in SI, Table S2, and the experimentally observed diagnostic NMR shifts (in D2O), including the significant upfield shift (2.36 ppm to 2.14 ppm) of Hα (α-CH2) as the primary signature of metal binding to the carboxylate and the smaller upfield shift (1.90 ppm to 1.83 ppm) of Hβ (β-CH2), confirmed the through-bond propagation of the electronic effect. Additionally, a minor upfield shift (3.65 ppm to 3.56 ppm) of Hγ (ω-CH2) was also reflected, and this balancing shift was not due to direct metal interaction but to relief from long-range electrostatic repulsion or minor conformational changes. Moreover, a small downfield shift (7.60 ppm to 7.71 ppm) of aromatic protons indicated a weak, through-bond inductive pull and/or an electric field effect from the bound metal cation, demonstrating that the complexation effects extend across the entire molecule.

  The 1H NMR spectra provide clear evidence of site-specific metal coordination in the Mg(ii)/Ca(ii) N-phthaloyl–GABA complexes. The most pronounced upfield shift observed for the Hα protons (Δδ ≈ −0.22 ppm) identified the carboxylate moiety as the primary coordination site. A systematic decrease in chemical shift perturbation (|Δδ|: Hα > Hβ > Hγ) confirmed the distance-dependent inductive transmission of electron density upon metal binding. There was no direct coordination to the phthalimide carbonyls (which would cause large downfield shifts for Hγ and the aromatics). The effect was transmitted inductively through the σ-bond framework, decaying with distance. Notably, Mg2+ induced slightly larger shifts than Ca2+, consistent with its higher charge density and stronger polarizing ability. In contrast, only minor changes in the aromatic proton region were detected, ruling out direct metal coordination to the phthalimide carbonyls. Conclusively, the pronounced upfield shift of the Hα triplet (the most reliable indicator), as well as small shifts of Hβ (upfield), Hγ (slight upfield), and the aromatics (slight downfield), observed via1H NMR in D2O collectively provided compelling spectroscopic evidence for selective carboxylate-driven metal binding for complex formation and its long-range electrostatic nature.

  In the 13C NMR spectra, the 13C chemical shift of C1 (δ 177.74 ppm) of the free ligand NY0) showed a pronounced downfield shift upon complexation with Mg and Ca to δ 181.79 ppm (NY1) and 180.01 ppm (NY2) respectively, thereby confirming metal coordination via the carboxylate oxygen atoms. This significant deshielding (Δδ = +4.05 ppm for Mg; +2.27 ppm for Ca) signals the electron withdrawal associated with the metal–oxygen bond formation, while the phthalimide carbonyl signal reflects no change (∼170.8 ppm). Hence, any involvement of the phthalimide ring carbonyl oxygen was ruled out, and the metallocarboxylate complexation was concluded to be mediated purely by the aliphatic carboxylate oxygen. The inverse 1H–13C correlation (Cα downfield by +2.94 ppm with simultaneous upfield shifts of Hα by −0.22 ppm) supported the inductive polarization along the α-methylene framework. Moreover, the larger CO shift observed for Mg2+ compared to Ca2+ demonstrated stronger polarization and tighter binding, attributable to Mg2+'s higher charge density. Collectively, the 13C NMR data provided definitive, site-specific evidence for selective carboxylate coordination in the Mg(ii)/Ca(ii) N-phthaloyl–GABA complexes, with no detectable interaction with the phthalimide moiety (SI, Table S3). The key findings of the 13C NMR analysis included a shift in the carboxyl CO signal, which serves to quantitatively measure the metal–oxygen bond strength for the direct observation of the binding site. Simultaneous large downfield shifts of the carboxyl CO (177 → 180–182 ppm (+4.05 ppm Mg, +2.27 ppm Ca)) and the phthalimide CO invariance ((∼170.8 ppm) Δδ ∼0/no shift) in the 13C NMR spectra provided irrefutable evidence that the metallocarboxylate formation occurred purely via a carboxylate coordination binding mode, excluding alternative coordination binding modes. In other words, the 13C NMR spectra of the investigated compounds provided definitive, site-specific proof that metal binding occurred exclusively at the carboxylate group, with no coordination to the phthalimide moiety. The α-carbon (Cα) signal shifted downfield (+2.94 ppm), while the corresponding α-protons shifted upfield (Δδ = −0.22 ppm), consistent with classical α-methylene behavior in metallocarboxylates, exhibiting an inverse 1H–13C characteristic correlation. Additionally, the larger CO shift difference between Mg2+ and Ca2+ (1.78 ppm), compared to Hα (0.04 ppm), demonstrates that 13C NMR is more sensitive towards the metal center.

  Thus, collectively, the 1H NMR analysis had traced the cascade of changes, whereas 13C NMR's chemical signatures represented the molecular fingerprint of metal bindings. Overall, 13C NMR provided a complete structural dossier to identify the binding site, confirm the binding mode, discriminate metal ions, and map the net electronic consequences throughout the molecule.

#### Thermal analysis
  The thermal stability of N-phthaloyl-γ-aminobutyric acid (NPGABA, NY0) and its alkaline earth metal carboxylate complexes (NY1: Mg; NY2: Ca) was assessed by TGA/DTG (Fig. 5a–c)/Table 2. Ligand NY0 exhibited a two-stage decomposition pattern. An initial mass loss of 3.88–7.57% below 150 °C was attributed to the removal of physically adsorbed moisture and residual solvents. The major decomposition occurred between 90 °C and 255 °C (∼38.05% weight loss; DTG peak at ∼250 °C), corresponding to intramolecular dehydration and oxidative decarboxylation of the carboxylate group. Further degradation between 256 °C and 330 °C led to extensive breakdown of the ligand framework (total weight loss: ∼60.19%; DTG peak: ∼280 °C), consistent with its zwitterionic structure and strong intramolecular hydrogen bonding.

  Complexation with Mg(ii) (NY1) further stabilized the thermal profile against higher temperatures. The initial mass loss of ∼11.65% below 130 °C was attributed to the elimination of four coordinated/lattice water molecules.45–47 The main decomposition appeared between 150 °C and 550 °C (∼56.75% loss) and was attributed to the fragmentation of the NPGABA ligand (2-methylisoindoline-1,3-dione), leaving magnesium propionate as the final residue. The shift of the degradation onset to higher temperatures indicated enhanced thermal stability due to metal coordination. The Ca(ii) complex (NY2) exhibited the highest thermal stability. A mass loss of 12.31% up to 290 °C corresponded to a loss of moisture and four coordinated water molecules.45–47 Subsequent decomposition occurred in two stages: 290–410 °C (33.11% loss) and 410–740 °C (23.4% loss), reflecting the stepwise degradation of ligand fragments and the formation of calcium propionate residue. Overall, the TGA results demonstrate that NY0 has the lowest thermal stability, while complexation with Mg and Ca significantly improves thermal resistance, with NY2 showing slightly greater stability than NY1, consistent with its stronger chelation and higher residual mass. Overall, the complexation with Mg2+ and Ca2+ stabilizes the ligand, with NY2 being slightly more thermally robust than NY1.


#### Powdered X-ray diffraction analysis
  To gain insights into the crystallinity and the structural order of the free ligand, as well as its metal carboxylate complexes, powder X-ray diffraction analysis was carried out.48 The diffraction pattern of the NY0 free ligand exhibited sharp and strong Bragg peaks, indicative of a highly crystalline material with a well-defined long-range ordered structure. In stark contrast, the diffraction pattern of the metal carboxylate complexes NY1 and NY2 showed a significant decrease in both peak intensity and sharpness (Fig. 5(d)). This high degree of crystallinity decrease was directly attributed to the metal–ligand coordination.49 The metal ions chelated with the free ligand made the molecular packing of the latter less efficient and homogeneous, resulting in a less ordered metal–organic framework with irregular morphology and an increased average grain size (SI, Table S4), as corroborated by SEM analysis. The observed XRD trend, from sharp, intense peaks for the ligand to broadened, attenuated peaks for the complex, served as strong evidence for the successful formation of the metal carboxylate complex, where the original crystalline lattice of the ligand was precluded upon integration into the coordination sphere.

  The XRD findings were consistent with the electronic absorption spectroscopic data. Notably, complexation did not cause a bathochromic or hypsochromic shift in the ligand-centered π → π* absorption band. Instead, a hyperchromic effect, i.e., an increase in the absorption intensity at a fixed λmax (220 nm), was observed. This could be attributed to the synthetic design where the chromophoric indoline unit of the ligand was electronically decoupled from the metal binding site. The molecular orbitals involved in the light absorption were not directly perturbed by the coordination to the metal ion. The observed increase in absorbance was, therefore, ascribed to an effective increase in the concentration of the chromophoric ligand units within the coordinated complex, rather than to a change in the electronic environment of the chromophore itself. Together, the XRD and UV-vis data provide complementary evidence for successful complex formation. XRD confirms a disruption in long-range crystallinity, while UV-vis confirms the integrity and additive contribution of the ligand chromophores within the new coordination architecture.

#### DNA binding by fluorescence emission spectroscopic studies
  The intrinsic fluorescence emission spectra of the free and ds-DNA-bound compounds (NY0, NY1 and NY2) were investigated to study the binding behavior of the compounds with increasing concentration of ds-DNA under the standard addition method. All the investigated compounds exhibited sensitivity to the addition of ds-DNA because all of them are intrinsically photoluminescent. The intrinsic fluorescence intensities of ds-DNA were gradually quenched by successive titrations with increasing amounts of ds-DNA, but the band shape in the fluorescence spectrum did not change with the concentration of ds-DNA. This fluorescence quenching and retention of the spectral band shape/emission maxima indicated a ground-state complex and the lack of rearrangement in the emissive state.

  The fluorescence emission spectra of NY0, NY1 and NY2 upon the gradual addition of ds-DNA (0–14 µM) at 310 K temperature/blood pH 7.4 are presented in Fig. 7a–c, respectively. The photophysical changes for all compounds resulting from the formation of corresponding compound–DNA complexes via non-covalent interactions demonstrated a progressive decrease in the emission intensity without any significant shift in the peak emission maximum. The strong quenching effect suggests a well-defined interaction between the tested compounds and ds-DNA. The quenching patterns of all the compounds further indicated a comparable interaction of the compounds with ds-DNA via a non-intercalative binding mode.56

  Although the observed fluorescence attenuation indicated the existence of non-intercalative interactions between the compounds and ds-DNA, the absence of a pronounced shift in the emission maximum indicated that the electronic environment of the principal pharmaceutical fluorophore is not substantially altered upon association with DNA, ruling out the formation of a DNA-fluorophore adduct. However, the retention of the maximum emission is not sufficient to determine a specific binding mode or to assign static or dynamic quenching. ##### Quenching mechanism and DNA-binding analysis
  Moreover, the fluorescence quenching mechanisms of NY0, NY1, and NY2 upon interaction with ds-DNA were quantitatively investigated using the Stern–Volmer (S–V) relationship:Hence, Ksv = Kqτ0. Here, F0 and F represent the fluorescence intensities of the test compound in the absence and at varying concentrations of DNA macromolecules, respectively; Kq is the biomolecular quenching rate constant; [Q] stands for the concentration of the quencher (ds-DNA Macromolecules); Ksv is the Stern–Volmer quenching constant, and τ0 is the half-life of the fluorophore in the absence of DNA macromolecules (10−9–10−7). For the present calculations, a fluorescence lifetime of 1 × 10−8 s was assumed, which falls within the commonly reported range for organic fluorophores.

  The plots of F0/F versus [Q] were approximately linear for NY0, NY1, and NY2 (ref. 57–59) (Fig. 7d). The corresponding Stern–Volmer constants, Ksv (M−1), were (5.973 × 104), (8.325 × 104), and (6.914 × 104) for NY0, NY1, and NY2, respectively (Table 4). Accordingly, the corresponding apparent bimolecular quenching rate constants, Kq (M−1 s−1), were calculated as (5.973 × 1012), (8.325 × 1012), and (6.914 × 1012) for NY0, NY1, and NY2, respectively (Table 4). The calculated (Kq) values are substantially higher than the reported diffusion-controlled bimolecular quenching limit generally expected in aqueous systems (approximately 2.0 × 1010 M−1 s−1). Therefore, the observed fluorescence attenuation cannot reasonably be attributed exclusively to diffusion-controlled dynamic quenching. Instead, the unusually high apparent (Kq) values support a substantial contribution from static quenching, involving the association of the compounds with ds-DNA and the formation of a non-fluorescent ground-state complex. This highlighted that the Kq values calculated using an assumed fluorescence lifetime are apparent; definitive discrimination between static and dynamic contributions would preferably require experimentally determined fluorescence lifetimes and/or temperature-dependent Stern–Volmer measurements.

  The Stern–Volmer constants followed the order NY1 > NY2 > NY0, indicating that NY1 exhibited the greatest fluorescence quenching response toward ds-DNA, followed by NY2 and NY0. The enhanced quenching observed for the metal complexes may arise from changes in molecular rigidity, electronic distribution, charge characteristics, and DNA-binding properties, induced by the coordination of Mg(ii) and Ca(ii). The present observations are consistent with the intrinsic fluorescence titration results, where progressive addition of ds-DNA caused a marked decrease in fluorescence intensity without significant displacement of the emission maxima.

  Importantly, fluorescence quenching alone cannot be used as definitive evidence for the assignment of a particular non-intercalative external binding mode (electrostatic interaction or groove binding interaction). A decrease in fluorescence intensity can result from several processes, including static complex formation, dynamic collisional quenching, groove binding, or other association-induced photophysical effects. Therefore, the present fluorescence results demonstrate the interaction of NY0–NY2 with ds-DNA and provide evidence for a substantial quenching process, leading to the involvement of non-intercalative binding in general without any assignment of a specific non-intercalative binding mode.

  The preservation of the emission-band shape and the absence of a significant bathochromic or hypsochromic shift nevertheless suggest that DNA association does not produce a major change in the emissive chromophore's electronic environment. The magnitude of the quenching followed the order NY1 > NY2 > NY0, as reflected by both Ksv and Kq. Taken together with the Stern–Volmer analysis, these findings indicate appreciable association of NY0, NY1, and NY2 with ds-DNA, with NY1 exhibiting the greatest apparent quenching efficiency among the investigated compounds, whereas NY0 showed the weakest quenching under the same experimental conditions. The enhanced quenching observed for the metal complexes relative to the free ligand may be associated with changes in their electronic properties, molecular rigidity, charge distribution, and/or DNA-binding affinity following metal coordination.

#### Hydrodynamic approach: viscosity as a probe for DNA binding modes
  Viscosity measurements, being susceptible to minute structural changes in length and shape of molecules, serve as a sensitive hydrodynamic probe for interpreting the binding modes of small molecules with ds-DNA. The viscosity method exploits the relationship between changes in the conformation/shape and effective contour length of ds-DNA and its specific viscosity upon interaction with the tested compounds. Thus, the contour length and shape of ds-DNA are altered upon the introduction of small interacting molecules or ligands, and the extent of such mechanical alterations is reflected in the characteristic viscosity-change pattern, which is used to predict the interaction mode between the interacting species. Moreover, ds-DNA macromolecules suspended in aqueous solutions exist as extended polyanions due to electrostatic repulsion between phosphate groups. Ligand binding modifies this structure, concomitantly changing the solution's viscosity. The direction and magnitude of such viscosity changes are diagnostic of the interaction mode.12,50,64,65

  (1) Classical intercalation inserts a planar ligand between base pairs, lengthening and stiffening the DNA helix, resulting in a significant increase in viscosity.

  (2) Electrostatic/groove binding (non-intercalative) is the binding along the major or minor groove, often involving charge neutralization, compacting the DNA structure. This leads to little to no change or a decrease in viscosity.

  (3) Partial/non-classical intercalation can cause bending or kinking of the helix, typically producing less pronounced or negative viscosity changes.

  Relative specific viscosity (η/η0)1/3 was plotted against the binding ratio (r = [compound]/[DNA]), where η and η0 are the viscosities of DNA in the presence and absence of the compound, respectively. Viscosity was derived from flow-time measurements using the following relation:η = (t − t0)/t0,where ‘t’ and ‘t0’ are the flow times of the DNA solutions (without and with compounds) and buffer alone.

  For compounds NY0, NY1 and NY2, a minor, steady increase in DNA viscosity was observed with increasing compound concentration (Fig. 6d) under the standard addition method. This pattern of minor and steady increase in DNA viscosity is typical for groove binding interactions. The phenomenon of a modest increase in viscosity itself suggests that the investigated small molecule has induced regularity or slight structural changes without significant helix elongation, possibly due to steric hindrance preventing deep intercalation, which concomitantly results in a significant increase in viscosity. The order of the binding affinity, deduced from the extent of viscosity change, is as follows:NY0 < NY1 < NY2.

  The viscometric profile was characterized by a modest, sub-intercalative increase in the DNA solution viscosity, concomitantly providing compelling hydrodynamic evidence for a groove-localized binding mechanism and decisively discriminating it from classical intercalation. This finding was mechanistically and energetically consistent with spectroscopic observations of marked hyperchromism in the absence of significant bathochromic or hypsochromic shifts.50 The hyperchromism indicated an external association and a loss of base-pair oscillator strength due to compound proximity and the perturbation of nucleobase stacking, while the absence of a spectral shift contended against the stabilized, coplanar π-orbital insertion of deep intercalative binding. The convergence of hydrodynamic and photophysical data establishes a unified model for a non-classical intercalative-groove binding mechanism as the dominant non-covalent interactive mode. Here, the planar ligand systems engaged the DNA via shallow, asymmetric insertion at the groove bordering base pairs, inducing only minor local conformational adjustments as they nested within the groove architecture. This might result in partial, groove-directed helix elongation that is sufficient to modestly increase the contour length and disrupt base stacking, accounting for the observed viscosity and hyperchromism while avoiding the significant torsional and bending penalties of full intercalation. This intermediary modality preserved the overall B-form helicity while leveraging groove accessibility for affinity, resulting in a high-affinity, low-distortion complex that facilitated specific interactions with the groove floor and walls. However, the sophisticated complementary structural investigation techniques, including circular dichroism spectroscopy, fluorescence displacement assays, NMR spectroscopy, and other advanced biophysical approaches, will be required to conclusively characterize the DNA-binding mode.

The predicted binding modes of NY0, NY1, and NY2 toward both DNA and the aromatase enzyme are illustrated in 2D ligand-interaction diagrams (Fig. 9 and 10) and elaborated in molecular docking results and non-covalent intermolecular interaction profiles (SI, Tables S5 to S8). Each diagram represents the network of non-covalent contacts, stabilizing the newly formed compound-macromolecule adduct complexes (compound–DNA/compound-3EQM). NY2 binds in the minor groove of DNA, forming hydrogen bonds with different nucleosides (deoxyguanosine, deoxycytidine, etc.), and it interacts with the active site of aromatase (3EQM) via multiple hydrogen and ionic bonds with ILE 133 and ARG115. The enhanced binding of NY2 can be attributed to its metal-coordinated architecture, which promotes a semi-planar conformation complementary to the DNA minor groove and the active site of aromatase. The presence of carbonyl groups facilitated additional polar and ionic interactions, improving selectivity and binding strength. The metal-coordinated structure of NY2 enhanced its groove binding capability and protein-target engagement, correlating well with its superior in vitro anticancer activity. These in silico results align with experimental DNA binding and anticancer activity data, suggesting that NY2 may act through dual targeting of DNA and cancer-associated proteins.68,69 The overall docking simulations also predict distinct interaction profiles, with NY2 exhibiting a relatively better predicted binding affinity. These findings also provide a structural rationale for the observed bioactivity and support further development of NY2 as a possible dual-targeting anticancer agent.


### Discussion of structure–activity relationship, interaction mechanisms, and binding correlations with DNA and aromatase

#### Spectroscopic signatures and mechanistic interpretations

##### DNA interaction mode
  Hyperchromism and hypochromism in the UV-vis and fluorescence spectroscopic titrations with DNA, without a noticeable bathochromism or hypsochromism in the absorption/emission maximum, indicate that external binding occurs, predominantly without intercalation. This sort of behaviour is characteristic of interaction via the DNA minor groove or electrostatic association with the DNA phosphate backbone or a combination of these interactions, but not classical intercalation, as further confirmed by the ds-DNA melting experiments.


#### Energetic and affinity trends in DNA binding
  The intrinsic binding constants (Kb) followed the order of NY2 (4.21 × 105 M−1) > NY1 (4.05 × 105 M−1) > NY0 (2.18 × 105 M−1), indicating that coordination to Ca2+/Mg2+ enhances the DNA affinity by approximately twofold relative to the free ligand. The magnitude of Kb (∼105 M−1) remained substantially lower than that of classical intercalators (e.g., ethidium bromide, ∼107 M−1) but was consistent with the established range for AT-rich minor groove binders, supporting a groove binding mechanism. The negative Gibbs free energy values (ΔG° ≈ −30 to −35 kJ mol−1) further confirmed that the binding was spontaneous and energetically favorable, consistent with moderate-to-strong groove binders, such as berenil and DAPI. Collectively, these thermodynamic and affinity trends corroborate the presence of non-intercalative external binding (a groove binding mode in which metal coordination enhances DNA recognition and the presence of electrostatic interaction further enhances the binding constants).


#### Structural and biophysical correlations
  Viscosity measurements revealed that all compounds induced only a modest, sub-intercalative increase in DNA solution viscosity, supporting the presence of a groove binding mechanism as well as electrostatic interactions in which no significant elongation or conformational unwinding of the helix takes place, like that in classical intercalators. Molecular docking with the Dickerson dodecamer (PDB: 1BNA) demonstrated that the NY2 metal carboxylate complex compactly fitted within the AT-rich minor groove region, stabilized by van der Waals interactions with groove walls, hydrogen-bond contacts to groove-floor electronegative atoms (e.g., N3 of adenine and O2 of thymine), and electrostatic interactions with the phosphate backbone. This theoretically predicted binding geometry is analogous to that of the accepted minor groove binders, such as netropsin. Moreover, molecular docking studies against human aromatase (PDB: 3EQM) further predicted a favorable binding of NY2 within the active site of the enzyme, potentially supporting a dual-target mechanism, which is relevant to estrogen-dependent breast cancer. The theoretically calculated binding energies for 1BNA (NY2: −6.75 kcal mol−1; NY1: −6.24 kcal mol−1; NY0: −5.61 kcal mol−1) align with the experimental Kb trend, designating NY2 as the most potent interacting small molecule from among the test compounds across DNA macromolecular targets. Collectively, the molecular docking simulations also support the presence of groove binding via hydrogen bonds, as well as electrostatic interactions as predominant mixed non-intercalative binding modes.


#### Structure–activity relationship (SAR) and cytotoxic potency
  A trending structure–activity relationship was established, wherein the enhanced DNA groove binding affinity of metal-incorporated compounds directly correlated with increased cytotoxic potency against MDA-MB-231 cells. The binding constants determined via UV spectroscopy followed the trend, NY2 (4.21 × 105 M per bp) > NY1 (4.05 × 105 M per bp) > NY0 (2.18 × 105 M per bp), which aligns with the docking energies (kcal mol−1): NY2 (−6.75) < NY1 (−6.24) < NY0 (−5.61). Correspondingly, the cytotoxic activity was increased in the order of NY2 > NY1 > NY0, confirming that the phenomenon of metal coordination had resulted in an enhanced biological activity. The free ligand exhibited the lowest activity, pointing out the inherent pharmacophoric role of the metal atoms. NY1 demonstrated moderate cytotoxicity, consistent with its intermediate DNA affinity and docking score, whereas NY2 had the highest DNA binding affinity and the most favorable docking score, hence the greatest potential for cytotoxicity. These correlations indicate the importance of the designed strategy and scientifically suggest NY2 as a possible anticancer prodrug candidate operating via a DNA groove binding mechanism, as well as via electrostatic or coordination interactions, potentially improved by its aromatase inhibition role. Furthermore, it is explicitly acknowledged that molecular docking provides supportive computational evidence but should not be regarded as definitive proof of the DNA-binding mechanism.


### DFT study
  The intervention of a prolonged alkyl chain arm of the NPGABA ligand (NY0) hindered the growth of suitable single crystals for powdered X-ray diffraction (XRD), thus preempting practical structural elucidation.70 Accordingly, the density functional theory (DFT)-based theoretical approach was relied upon to obtain optimized geometries of NY0 (ligand), NY1 (Mg(ii) complexes) and NY2 (Ca(ii) complexes) at the RB3LYP/6-311+G(d,p) level using Gaussian 09 randomly, without any symmetry-based restrictions.71 The obtained optimized structure of NY1 predicts a distorted octahedral coordination pattern around the Mg(ii) metal center, with a Mg–O bond length of ∼1.83 Å and significant deviations from ideal octahedral angles, consistent with chelation through the carboxylate oxygen atoms. However, longer and more flexible Ca–O bonds (1.99–2.41 Å) were found in the Ca(ii) ion in NY2, showing more octahedral distortion as a consequence of the larger ionic radius and coordination flexibility of Ca(ii). Selected bond lengths, bond angles, and dihedral angles for all optimized structures are compiled in SI (Tables S9–11). These computational models confirm the monodentate carboxylate binding mode of the protected ligand and provide structural insights into the metal-dependent geometric variations that may influence their respective biological activities.


### In vitro cytotoxicity analysis
  The in vitro cytotoxicities of the synthesized ligand NY0 and its magnesium NY1 and calcium NY2 complexes were evaluated by MTT assay against MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells. Fig. 11a represents the cell viability of MDA-MB-231 cancer cells treated with NY0, NY1 and NY2 across a dose-dependent range of 25, 50, 100, 150, 200, 250 and 300 µg mL−1. The cell viability result indicated dose-dependent behavior. At a relatively high dosage of 300 µg mL−1, the NY2 complex achieved a cell viability of 17.24% ± 2.65%, while NY1 and NY0 complexes achieved viabilities of 44.71.93% ± 2.54% and 66.15% ± 2.83%, respectively. The NY2 complex showed the higher reduction in the viability of MDA-MB-231 cells in comparison to NY0 and NY1. The greater cytotoxic effect of NY2 toward MDA-MB-231 cells may be attributed to its enhanced cellular interactions, improved intracellular availability, stronger biomolecular interactions and higher biological activity following complexation. Fig. 11b represents the cytocompatibility of the synthesized complexes NY0, NY1 and NY2 against normal MCF-10A cell lines with varying concentrations. The cell viability results indicated that NY0, NY1 and NY2 achieved reductions in cell viability to 78% ± 2.75%, 59.72% ± 3.25% and 41.36% ± 3.11%, respectively, at the highest dosage of 300 µg mL−1. The assessment of the developed complexes against non-tumorigenic MCF-10A cells revealed comparatively higher cell viabilities for all compounds at equivalent concentrations, although appreciable cytotoxicity was also observed at the highest dose, particularly for NY1 and NY2. The current results provided preliminary evidence for the biological activity of the synthesized complexes, especially NY2.

  Table 7, presenting the dose–response analysis results, indicates that NY2 exhibited the lowest IC50 value against MDA-MB-231 cells and the highest selectivity index among the investigated compounds. However, the selectivity indices of all three compounds remained below 2, which indicated only modest preferential cytotoxicity toward cancer cells.

  In vitro cytotoxicity findings suggested that, while the metal complexation enhanced cytotoxic potency, further structural and targeted-delivery optimizations are needed. These strategies will be necessary to improve cancer selectivity and minimize toxicity toward normal cells.


## Conclusion
  This comprehensive study has successfully demonstrated the strategic functionalization of the bioactive food component, N-phthaloyl-γ-aminobutyric acid (NPGABA), through coordination with biologically relevant alkaline earth metals, Mg(ii) and Ca(ii), to construct novel herbiotic metallodrug candidates, NY1 and NY2. The synthesized mononuclear alkaline earth metallocarboxylates (NY1 and NY2) derived from N-phthaloyl-γ-aminobutyric acid were successfully characterized. Convergent experimental (UV-vis, fluorescence emission, DNA melting transitions, and viscosity measurements) and computational analyses established a coherent mechanism of action. The combined use of viscometric assays, DNA melting (thermal denaturation) spectroscopy, UV-vis absorption and fluorescence emission titration studies, and molecular docking simulations conclusively establishes a non-intercalative external binding mechanism as the DNA interaction mode for all the investigated metal carboxylate complexes and the bioactive ligand. This non-intercalative behavior is characteristic of interactions occurring via the DNA minor groove or electrostatic association with the DNA phosphate backbone or a combination of these interactions, but not classical intercalation. Critically, metal coordination significantly enhanced the biomolecular affinity, with the Ca(ii) complex, NY2, exhibiting relatively better DNA binding constants (Kb by UV-vis/4.21 × 105 and fluorescence spectrosopy/8.61 × 104), the most favorable docking scores in kcal mol−1 (for 1BNA/6.75 and for 3EQM/−9.03), and the highest cytotoxic potency against the aggressive MDA-MB-231 triple-negative breast cancer cell line. This phenomenon established a direct, positive correlation between the non-intercalative external DNA binding affinity and the anticancer efficacy. Furthermore, NY2 demonstrated dual-targeting potential through additional predicted binding to the aromatase enzyme. Its dominant, external non-intercalative binding mechanism had further bypassed the mutagenic risks directly associated with intercalators and positioned it as a candidate lead compound. In summary, this work validates a rational design strategy: transforming a dietary bioactive component into a multidimensional “pill” via metal-linkage to create novel herbiotics. This represents a new scaffold-hopping strategy that exploits the untapped potential in the chemical space surrounding existing bioactive food components to introduce new chemical entities derived from natural products. This approach aims to yield real, sustainable therapeutic and business returns, embodying the currency of Sir James Black, winner of the 1988 Nobel Prize in Physiology and Medicine, and inspired by his renowned saying: “The most fruitful basis for the discovery of a new drug is to start with an old drug”.

  We present a foundational blueprint for developing next-generation, non-intercalative DNA targeting metallotherapeutic agents engineered from functionalized food ingredients, offering a new avenue in chemotherapeutic design and engineering. This comprehensive study successfully demonstrates the strategic functionalization of the bioactive food component, γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), through coordination with biologically relevant alkaline earth metals, Mg(ii) and Ca(ii), to engineer novel herbiotic metallodrug candidates, NY1 and NY2. Overall, this work established N-phthaloyl-γ-aminobutyric acid alkaline-earth metallocarboxylates as structurally novel bioinorganic compounds with measurable DNA interactions and biological activity.


## Limitations and future perspective
  The present study conducted an initial investigation into the synthesis, structural characterization, DNA interaction and in vitro cytotoxicity of N-phthaloyl-γ-aminobutyric acid-derived Mg(ii) and Ca(ii) metallocarboxylates. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Although spectroscopic analyses (both UV-vis and Fluorescence Emission), viscosity measurements and molecular docking collectively suggested the external, non-intercalative minor groove mode of DNA interaction, the precise molecular binding mechanism requires further validation using complementary biophysical techniques. Similarly, the molecular docking results should be interpreted as predictive computational evidence that provides support, rather than confirmation, and further experimental investigation shall be mandatory for confirmation. The biological evaluation was limited to in vitro cytotoxicity against a single breast cancer cell line (MDA-MB-231) and one normal breast epithelial cell line (MCF-10A). Although the test compounds demonstrated concentration-dependent cytotoxicity, the observed activity toward normal cells indicated that cancer selectivity under the investigated experimental conditions remained modest. Therefore, the present results should be regarded as preliminary evidence for the biological activity and not as proof of therapeutic efficacy.

  Further extensive mechanical studies should be conducted to understand the pathways responsible for cytotoxicity, such as apoptosis, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, DNA damage, and cell-cycle regulation. The uptake of these complexes into cells, engagement of the biomolecules targeted, and quantitative analysis of a wider range of cancer and non-cancer cell lines will further elucidate the biological activity of these complexes. Furthermore, the translational potential of these alkaline earth metallocarboxylates as bioactive coordination compounds will require structural optimisation of the ligand core to achieve cancer selectivity, alongside systematic in vivo efficacy, biosafety, and pharmacokinetic characterisations.
