# Identification of Mitochondrial Dynamics-Related Genes as Diagnostic Biomarkers for Osteoarthritis Using Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis and Machine Learning


## Background
  Osteoarthritis (OA) is a chronic, painful joint disease whose early diagnosis is critical for effective treatment. Since mitochondrial dynamics have been implicated in the onset and progression of OA, this study aimed to explore the diagnostic value of mitochondrial dynamics-related genes (MDRGs) in OA.


## Methods
  Weighted gene co-expression network analysis and 3 machine learning algorithms, including least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression, support vector machine-recursive feature elimination, and Boruta, were used to screen signature genes. The intersection of genes identified by all 3 algorithms was taken as the candidate set. Expression levels of these genes were compared between OA and normal tissues, and receiver operating characteristic curves were used to validate their diagnostic performance, yielding final hub genes. An in vitro OA model was established, and the expression of key genes was validated using quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) and Western blot analysis. Immune infiltration in OA was assessed, and hub genes’ functional analogs, microRNAs (miRNAs), transcription factors (TFs), and potential drugs were predicted.


## Results
  Two hub genes, CDH11 and MSX2, were identified. Both genes were up-regulated in OA and demonstrated promising diagnostic efficacy. qRT-PCR and Western blot validation revealed that mRNA and protein levels of CDH11 and MSX2 were significantly higher in OA cell models than in normal cells. Based on transcriptomic data, the single-sample gene set enrichment analysis algorithm inferred that significantly higher levels of B cells, macrophages, mast cells, neutrophils, T helper cells, and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes were found in OA samples than in controls. Twenty functional analogs of the 2 hub genes were predicted, mainly enriched in skeletal system development and bone development functional pathways. Additionally, 34 miRNAs (e.g., hsa-miR-568, hsa-miR-548d-5p, hsa-miR-144) and 17 TFs (e.g., CEBPA, NFATC1, SP1, EGR1, FOXO1) were predicted based on CDH11 and MSX2. Entinostat, estradiol, cobaltous chloride, and valproic acid were identified as potential OA therapeutics.


## Conclusions
  CDH11 and MSX2 may serve as candidate diagnostic biomarkers for OA, identified from a predefined MDRG set, but requiring further mechanistic and clinical validation.


## METHODS
  No human or animal subjects were involved in this study, and ethical approval is not applicable. All data in this study were obtained from publicly available, de-identified public databases. This study is a retrospective bioinformatics exploratory study based on public transcriptome datasets. According to the criteria of the Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine, the level of evidence of this study is level 4.


### Data Acquisition
  We retrieved 4 OA-relevant datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), including 3 sets of synovial tissue: GSE55235 (10 normal and 10 OA), GSE55457 (10 normal and 10 OA), and GSE12021 (9 normal and 10 OA), along with 1 cartilage dataset, GSE114007 (18 normal and 20 OA). We then obtained 1,123 MDRGs from GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/), including gene sets related to mitochondrial fusion and mitochondrial fission (relevance score > 10)14) (Supplementary Table 1).


### Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis
  The 3 synovial datasets (GSE55235, GSE55457, GSE12021) were integrated with the “sva” R package15) using the Com-Bat algorithm to remove batch effects and create a training cohort. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed with the “WGCNA” R package16) to identify modules correlated with OA status. The specific steps are as follows: (1) Genes in the top 50% of variance after merging training datasets were selected as input data for WGCNA, and sample grouping information (OA group/normal control group) was organized for subsequent module correlation analysis. This threshold represents a moderately lenient criterion, which can effectively control technical noise while maximally retaining weak biological signals associated with OA, avoiding the loss of key disease-related genes such as low-abundance regulatory factors, cell communication molecules, and metabolism-related enzymes caused by overly stringent screening (e.g., top 25%). (2) To ensure the scale-free property of the network (R2 ≥ 0.8), the soft threshold β = 15 (corresponding to R2 = 0.9) was determined through iterative screening; based on this threshold, the adjacency matrix between genes was calculated and converted into a topological overlap matrix (TOM) to reduce noise, and the dissimilarity between genes (1-TOM) was also computed for subsequent gene clustering. To further evaluate the parameter robustness of the model, we additionally tested analytical performance under different thresholds, including β = 12 (R2 = 0.87) and β = 18 (R2 = 0.92). (3) Genes were clustered using the dynamic tree cut algorithm, with the minimum number of genes per module set to 30 and the module merging threshold set to 0.25 (modules with dissimilarity < 0.25 were merged), finally yielding multiple gene co-expression modules. (4) The module eigengene (ME) value of each module was calculated, and its Pearson correlation coefficient with sample grouping was analyzed; the correlation heatmap was plotted using the “pheatmap” R package17) to visualize correlation coefficients and p-values, and key modules significantly associated with OA were finally identified.

  To explore the biological functions of the identified module genes, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using the “clusterProfiler” R package (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).18) Module genes were mapped to the GO database, and enrichment analysis was conducted for biological processes (BP). An adjusted p-value (the false discovery rate [FDR]) < 0.05 was set as the threshold for significant enrichment.


### Hub Gene Selection
  OA-related module genes identified by WGCNA were intersected with the MDRGs to yield OA-related MDRGs (OA-MDRGs). The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) method adopted 10-fold cross-validation to select the optimal λ value, which was used to identify important features from large-scale data and screen gene models; the “glmnet” R package19) was utilized to implement this method in the process. Support vector machine-recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) was implemented via the “e1071” R package,20) with 10-fold cross-validation employed to evaluate feature importance, and it has been widely used as an effective feature selection algorithm for identifying key diagnostic genes. The Boruta algorithm in the “Boruta” R package21) completed 500 iterative validations through random forest to screen features significantly associated with disease phenotypes from the initial features and eliminate irrelevant ones, with a significance threshold of p < 0.01. Genes simultaneously identified by the 3 ML algorithms were taken as the intersection set. Their diagnostic performance was evaluated by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis using the “pROC” R package.22) Box plots for the expression of these genes were generated across the normal and OA groups. The final hub genes were retained for downstream analyses.


### Construction of an OA Diagnostic Model Based on Hub Genes
  The “rms” R package23) was used to integrate the 2 hub genes into a logistic regression model predicting OA occurrence, and a nomogram was plotted. The regression equation for the model is: logit(P) = β0 + β1 × Gene1 + β2 × Gene2 + … + βn × Genen, where logit (P) is the log-odds of OA, β0 is the intercept, and β1–βn are the regression coefficients of the respective genes. Additionally, model accuracy was quantified by the ROC curve in the OA diagnosis. Calibration curves were used to assess the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes. Decision-curve analysis (DCA) estimated the net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities to judge the model’s practicality in clinical applications and its diagnostic value.


### Immune Cell Infiltration Analysis
  We next examined the alterations in infiltrating immune cells in OA. Single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was implemented with the “GSVA” R package24) to comprehensively quantify the abundance of infiltrating immune cells and immune function scores in each sample. Spearman correlation coefficients between hub genes and immune cells were calculated and visualized with the “ggplot2” R package. The 28 immune signature gene sets used in this study were all publicly available and experimentally verified, derived from the LM22 signature matrix of CIBERSORT,25) which defines 22 immune cell subsets. They also included 6 well-characterized immune function/pathway signatures from MSigDB (C7: immunologic signatures) and ImmPort. In addition, the FDR method was applied to adjust p-values for multiple testing, and statistical inferences and graphical visualization were performed based on the adjusted results to strictly control false positives.


### Construction of the TF-miRNA-Hub Gene Network
  NetworkAnalyst (https://www.networkanalyst.ca), integrating multiple databases, can be used to predict potential TFs and miRNAs. In this research, TF-miRNA, TF-gene and miRNA-gene interaction data were retrieved from the embedded RegNetwork database.


### GeneMANIA Analysis
  The GeneMANIA web platform (http://genemania.org) was used to build a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network to explore interactions between the hub genes and their functions. GeneMANIA generates functional predictions for genes and identifies genes with similar biological roles to facilitate the analysis and interpretation of the PPI network.26)


### Protein-Chemical Interactions of the Hub Genes
  Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD; https://ctdbase.org/) provided the data for the analysis of the interactions between proteins and chemicals.27) An interaction network of hub gene-encoded proteins and compounds was subsequently constructed through NetworkAnalyst.


### Cell Culture and Treatment
  Human SW1353 chondrocytes were purchased from the Chinese Academy of Sciences Type Culture Collection (TCHu128) and maintained under standard conditions (37℃, 5% CO2) in L-15 complete medium (11415064, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (A5669701, Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (15140122, Gibco). Medium was changed every 2 days. When SW1353 cells reached 50% confluence, they were stimulated with interleukin (IL)-1β (10 ng/mL) (HY-P7028, MCE) for 24 hours to establish an in vitro OA model for subsequent detection.


### Quantitative Reverse Transcription-Polymerase Chain Reaction (qRT-PCR)
  According to the manufacturer’s instructions, total RNA was extracted from cells treated with different methods using the MiniBEST Universal RNA Extraction Kit (Takara). Subsequently, the Prime-Script RT Reagent Kit (Takara) was used for reverse transcription to synthesize complementary DNA (cDNA), and amplification was performed on an ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher) using the Talent qPCR Detection Kit (SYBR Green) (TIANFEN). Each sample was run in triplicate, β-actin was used as the internal reference gene, data were analyzed by the 2-ΔΔCt method, and results were expressed as fold change. All primer sequences for target genes are shown in Table 1.


### Western Blot
  After collection, cells treated with different methods were lysed using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime), and protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit (Beyotime). Protein samples were then separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. The separated proteins were transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (Thermo Fisher Scientific), which was blocked with 5% non-fat milk at room temperature for 45 minutes. Subsequently, the membrane was incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 ℃. The next day, after washing with phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 (PBST), the membrane was incubated with goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G (IgG) at room temperature for 1 hour. Finally, the membrane was washed again with PBST, and protein bands were visualized using a chemiluminescence kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and the iBright Imaging System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The information of the primary antibodies used is as follows: MMP13 (ab51072, Abcam); Collagen II (ab307674, Abcam); β-actin (ab179467, Abcam); CDH11 (ab151302, Abcam); MSX2 (ab227720, Abcam); goat anti-rabbit IgG (ab6721, Abcam).


### Statistical Analysis
  Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software version 8.0 (GraphPad Software). Differences between the 2 groups were analyzed by Student t-test, with a p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.


## RESULTS

### Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis
  An expression matrix of the top 50% most variable genes in the training cohort was subjected to WGCNA. A soft threshold β = 15 was chosen to ensure the scale-free topology of the network (Fig. 1A). Under these conditions, the scale-free topology fitting index R2 of the network reached 0.9 for the first time, while maintaining a high average connectivity, effectively avoiding an overly sparse or dense network and ensuring the rationality of the co-expression network construction. Hierarchical clustering, utilizing average linkage and dynamic tree cut, was employed to partition genes with similar expression profiles into 10 distinct gene modules (Fig. 1B). Module-trait correlation analysis revealed that MEwhite (cor = 0.55), MEdarkgrey (cor = 0.64) and MEsalmon (cor = 0.65) showed the strongest positive association with OA (Fig. 1C), jointly containing 2,200 genes (Supplementary Table 2). GO enrichment analysis was performed on genes of these 3 modules. The results showed that the functional enrichment of each module was highly consistent, collectively pointing to core pathways closely associated with the pathology of OA, including BP such as immune and inflammatory regulation (lymphocyte proliferation/differentiation, adaptive immune response), chemotaxis and cell migration (chemokine signaling, immune cell infiltration), as well as muscle/skeletal system development and function (muscle tissue development, muscle contraction) (Supplementary Fig. 1).

  In addition, to assess the robustness of WGCNA network construction, 2 distinct soft-threshold values (β= 12, R2 = 0.87; β = 18, R2 = 0.92) were used to repeat the analysis. At β = 12, a total of 26 co-expression modules were identified, among which the darkturquoise module exhibited the strongest positive correlation with OA (cor = 0.68). This module contained 353 genes, including the hub genes CDH11 and MSX2 (Supplementary Fig. 2A-C, Supplementary Table 3). At β = 18, 14 modules were detected, with the magenta module showing a similarly strong positive correlation with OA (cor = 0.533) and also containing CDH11 and MSX2 (Supplementary Fig. 2D-F, Supplementary Table 3). Under all 3 β values, CDH11 and MSX2 were consistently assigned to the same module with a significant positive correlation to OA, confirming the stability of network topology and the reliability of hub gene identification.


### Hub Gene Identification
  Intersection of the WGCNA-derived module genes with the MDRGs yielded 124 OA-MDRGs (Supplementary Table 4). Three ML algorithms were applied: LASSO selected 13 genes (Fig. 2A). SVM-RFE selected 41 genes (Fig. 2B). Boruta selected 50 genes (Fig. 2C). The consensus across all 3 approaches produced 11 signature genes: ALK, MN1, CDKN1A, GATM, BCL2L11, MSX2, GDF15, CDH11, SLC25A40, ACO2 and AFF1 (Fig. 2D).

  Based on the 11 candidate genes, further screening was conducted according to their expression stability across datasets and diagnostic efficacy. We found that in both the training set and validation set, CDH11 and MSX2 were significantly up-regulated in OA samples compared with normal controls (p < 0.001) (Fig. 3A and B). We performed ROC analysis for each candidate gene individually (Fig. 3C-F, Supplementary Fig. 3). In the training set, 6 genes exhibited area under the curve (AUC) values exceeding 0.70: GATM (AUC, 0.871; 95% CI, 0.782–0.961), ACO2 (AUC, 0.869; 95% CI, 0.781–0.957), MSX2 (AUC, 0.860; 95% CI, 0.769–0.950), MN1 (AUC, 0.854; 95% CI, 0.757–0.951), CDH11 (AUC, 0.795; 95% CI, 0.674–0.917), and SLC25A40 (AUC, 0.751; 95% CI, 0.625–0.876). The remaining 5 genes (GDF15, BCL2L11, ALK, AFF1, CDKN1A) had AUC values below 0.3, indicating negligible diagnostic performance in the training set (Fig. 3C and D, Supplementary Fig. 3A). However, only CDH11 (AUC, 0.822; 95% CI, 0.692–0.952) and MSX2 (AUC, 0.811; 95% CI, 0.673–0.949) maintained high diagnostic performance in the independent validation set. Other candidate genes with favorable performance in the training set showed marked performance decline in the validation set: ACO2 decreased from 0.869 to 0.503, GATM from 0.871 to 0.444, and MN1 from 0.854 to 0.186, suggesting overfitting to the training dataset (Fig. 3E and F, Supplementary Fig. 3B). Therefore, CDH11 and MSX2 were the only candidate genes with robust and reproducible diagnostic efficacy across both datasets. To validate the bioinformatics findings, SW1353 cells were treated with 10 ng/mL IL-1β for 24 hours to establish an in vitro OA model. qRT-PCR showed that CDH11 and MSX2 mRNA levels were significantly up-regulated in IL-1β-induced cells compared with controls (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 3G). Western blot was then used to examine the OA markers Collagen II and MMP13. The results revealed that IL-1β reduced the protein level of Collagen II—the main component of the chondrocyte extracellular matrix—and increased the expression of the matrix metalloproteinase MMP13 as well as the hub genes CDH11 and MSX2 (Fig. 3H). These 2 genes exhibited relatively higher diagnostic efficacy for OA and were selected as hub genes for all subsequent analyses.


### Development and Assessment of the Diagnostic Model
  A diagnostic model incorporating CDH11 and MSX2 was developed, and a nomogram was generated (Fig. 4A). ROC analysis yielded an AUC of 0.87, indicating high diagnostic accuracy for OA (Fig. 4B). Calibration curves showed excellent agreement between predicted probabilities and actual observed probabilities (Fig. 4C). DCA further confirmed the model’s superior clinical net benefit (Fig. 4D). Subsequently, in the validation set, a nomogram model was constructed based on CDH11 and MSX2 (Fig. 4E). DCA results also demonstrated that this model provided better clinical net benefit (Fig. 4F).


### Immune Microenvironment Landscape
  Immune cell infiltration is a pivotal driver of OA initiation and progression. We therefore compared the immune cell infiltration of OA and control samples. Results showed significant infiltration of B cells, macrophages, mast cells, neutrophils, T helper cells and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes in OA tissues, whereas activated dendritic cells and regulatory T cells (Tregs) had higher infiltration levels in normal samples (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5A). Functionally, checkpoint, human leukocyte antigen, parainflammation, T cell co-inhibition, T cell co-stimulation and type I interferon (IFN) response scores were all elevated in the OA group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5B). We further examined the associations of the expression of CDH11 and MSX2 with immune infiltration levels. The immune infiltration results inferred by the ssGSEA algorithm showed that CDH11 and MSX2 were positively correlated with immature dendritic cells and Tregs. CDH11 showed negative correlations with T helper cells, macrophages, and neutrophils, whereas MSX2 was negatively associated with B cells, mast cells, and T helper 2 (Th2) cells (Figs. 5C and D). In addition, a correlation heatmap between CDH11 and MDRGs revealed that CDH11 was significantly positively correlated with the mitochondrial fission gene OPA3, and significantly negatively correlated with mitochondrial function regulatory genes PINK1, PPARGC1A, and SLC25A46 (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5E).


### Construction of the Hub Gene PPI Network
  GeneMANIA identified 20 functional analogs of the 2 hub genes. A strong direct interaction was detected between MSX2 and PITX2, suggesting a pivotal role in signaling pathways (Fig. 6). These genes were significantly enriched in functional pathways such as viral latency, skeletal system development, telomere cap complex, extrinsic component of membrane, and bone development (Fig. 6).


### TF-miRNA-Hub Gene Network
  To dig deeper into the underlying mechanism of OA, we constructed a TF-miRNA-hub gene network. Based on interaction data obtained from the RegNetwork database, 17 potential upstream TFs were identified. Among the resulting TFs, CEBPA was found to co-regulate both CDH11 and MSX2, whereas NFATC1, NFATC2, NFATC3, NFATC4 and CTNNB1 regulated CDH11, and 11 additional TFs (SP1, EGR1, FOXO1, GTF2F1, etc.) governed MSX2 (Fig. 7). Moreover, 34 miRNAs targeting CDH11 and MSX2 were predicted. Except for hsa-miR-568 that interacted with both genes, MSX2 also had interactions with 3 additional miRNAs (hsa-miR-548a-5p, hsa-miR-548c-5p, and hsa-miR-548d-5p), whereas CDH11 interacted with 30 other miRNAs, including hsa-miR-144, hsa-miR-33a, and hsa-miR-576-5p (Fig. 7). Collectively, these predicted regulatory interactions among TFs, miRNAs, and the hub genes may be relevant to OAassociated regulatory networks, but require further experimental validation.


### Prediction of Potential Therapeutic Compounds
  To identify potential therapeutics for OA, we predicted hub gene-related compounds. Results yielded 52 potential therapeutic compounds, such as entinostat, tretinoin, estradiol, arsenite, cobaltous chloride and valproic acid (Fig. 8).


## DISCUSSION
  In this study, we combined WGCNA with 3 ML algorithms to systematically screen hub genes CDH11 and MSX2 associated with mitochondrial dynamics in OA based on public GEO datasets. Their mRNA expression levels were verified to be significantly up-regulated in an in vitro SW1353 OA cell model induced by IL-1β. Both CDH11 and MSX2 were significantly correlated with immune cell infiltration in OA joints. We further predicted potential miRNAs, TFs regulating CDH11 and MSX2, as well as candidate therapeutic drugs, providing hypotheses for future diagnostic and mechanistic studies in OA.

  Among the 2 hub genes identified in this study, CDH11 is a classic member of the cadherin family. Previous studies have confirmed that CDH11 participates in OA cartilage injury and disease progression by regulating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway and being modulated by non-coding RNAs such as circRNAs.2829) However, these studies were limited to its traditional functions in cell adhesion and tissue remodeling, without involving the association between mitochondrial dynamics and immune regulation. Different from previous studies, this study identified CDH11 as a candidate hub gene derived from a predefined MDRG set and observed a correlation with OPA3, although its mechanistic relevance remains to be established. Meanwhile, this study found that CDH11 was closely associated with immune cell infiltration, showing a positive correlation with immature dendritic cells and Tregs, and a negative correlation with helper T cells, macrophages, and neutrophils.

  Mitochondrial dynamic dysfunction is a key factor in the initiation and progression of OA.30) However, current studies on MDRGs in OA remain controversial and incomplete, showing obvious limitations compared with previous OA-related gene research. Most previous studies on OA-related genes focused on functional verification of single genes or screening genes directly related to cartilage injury and inflammatory response, without taking mitochondrial dynamics as a core screening criterion. Moreover, they were mostly restricted to single datasets and single analytical methods, resulting in a lack of specificity and robustness of the identified genes.313233) In contrast, taking mitochondrial dynamics as the core entry point, this study combined WGCNA with 3 ML algorithms for crossvalidation, and simultaneously integrated multiple GEO datasets for combined analysis and validation. The 2 identified hub genes, CDH11 and MSX2, showed diagnostic potential, and their correlations with a predefined MDRG set and immune infiltration were observed, remedying the shortcoming of “overemphasis on screening but lack of mechanistic linkage” in previous studies. Furthermore, most previous studies on MDRGs in OA focused on mitophagy-related genes such as PINK1 and Parkin, without exploring their correlation with the immune microenvironment.34) This study systematically screened MDRGs and simultaneously analyzed their associations with immune cell infiltration, representing a significant difference from the single perspective of mitochondrial energy metabolism in previous reports. Collectively, through multi-dataset integration, multi-algorithm validation, and multi-dimensional correlation analysis, this study more comprehensively and precisely identified clinically valuable MDRGs, providing a novel perspective for therapeutic strategies of OA.

  miRNAs, TFs, and potential therapeutic drugs of CDH11 and MSX2 were also predicted in this study. miRNAs orchestrate a wide range of BP through direct interaction with target mRNAs.35) In OA, miRNAs not only hold promise as early diagnostic markers but also constitute potential therapeutic agents, and they govern key steps in OA initiation and progression.36) miR-127-5p has repeatedly been shown to potently restrain OA advancement. Building on this, we screened 34 miRNAs predicted to target CDH11 and MSX2, highlighting hsa-miR-568, hsa-miR-548d-5p and hsa-miR-144. Notably, miR-144-3p has already been demonstrated to down-regulate IL-1β expression by direct mRNA targeting, thereby dampening inflammation and slowing OA progression.37) We also identified 17 upstream regulators: CEBPA, NFATC1, SP1, EGR1 and FOXO1. FOXO1, mainly expressed in bone and cartilage, orchestrates skeletal development and preserves chondrocyte homeostasis.38) FOXO1-deficient mice develop symptoms of early-stage OA, whereas FOXO1 shields chondrocytes from oxidative stress and drives expression of autophagy genes and PRG4.39) EGR1 accelerates cartilage hypertrophy and degeneration by regulating the expression of KLF5 and the β-catenin signaling pathway. SP1 is a C2H2 zinc-finger TF that facilitates apoptosis and suppresses proliferation by binding to miR-145.40) Collectively, the identified miRNAs and TFs govern the regulation of the BP in OA pathogenesis and represent promising potential therapeutic targets for OA.

  Leveraging data from the CTD, we identified a panel of candidate drugs linked to CDH11 and MSX2, including entinostat, estradiol, cobaltous chloride and valproic acid. Among these candidates, declining levels of estradiol have been implicated in OA onset and progression. Physiological doses of estradiol treatment can exert protective effects by alleviating OA symptoms. Mechanistically, estradiol can modulate the FTO/IGF2BP1/m6A-NLRC5 axis to promote the progression of OA in temporomandibular joints. Valproic acid, a histone-deacetylase inhibitor, suppresses cytokine-induced metalloproteinase expression in human articular chondrocytes, thereby protecting against cartilage degradation.41) Valproic acid also mitigates chondrocyte injury by modulating the miR-302d-3p/ITGB4 axis and activating PI3K-AKT signaling.42) Nevertheless, the efficacy and safety of these compounds must be validated in further research. The association between these drugs and CDH11 and MSX2 found in this study provides theoretical support for the precise application of these drugs and new ideas for the screening of OA treatment drugs.

  Despite the findings mentioned above, this study still has several limitations that need to be improved in future research: First, the biomarker screening in this study was based on synovial tissue expression profiles, while independent validation was performed on cartilage tissue, which presents tissue heterogeneity in cellular composition, microenvironment, and pathological phenotype. Although cross-tissue validation suggests the conservation of biomarkers to a certain extent, different tissue sources may still affect the stability and extrapolation of the model. In addition, the sample size was relatively small, and detailed clinical information such as unified OA severity (Kellgren-Lawrence grade), disease course, age, sex stratification, and comorbidities was lacking, which prevented stratified analysis and may compromise the accuracy and generalizability of the conclusions. Meanwhile, in vitro validation was only performed using the IL-1β-induced SW1353 cell model, without involving other OA-related cells (e.g., synovial cells, primary chondrocytes), resulting in insufficient comprehensiveness of the validation results. Second, this study only revealed the correlations of CDH11 and MSX2 with mitochondrial dynamics and immune cell infiltration, without in-depth verification of their specific molecular mechanisms, such as the precise pathways by which CDH11 regulates mitochondrial dynamics, the molecular targets underlying the interaction with immune cells, as well as the exact function and regulatory mechanism of MSX2 in OA. Furthermore, immune infiltration analysis was only based on computational inference, and correlation does not necessarily indicate causation. Third, the predicted miRNAs, TFs, and potential therapeutic drugs in this study were only derived from bioinformatic simulation analysis without in vitro and in vivo experimental validation. Their actual therapeutic efficacy, target specificity, dosage, and safety remain unclear, thus not directly applicable in clinical practice. Fourth, this study only employed bioinformatic analysis and in vitro cell model validation, without animal experiments or clinical sample verification; therefore, the reliability and clinical translational value of the conclusions still need further confirmation. In addition, the predefined MDRG set was derived from GeneCards annotations, which may include indirect or low-specificity associations and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

  In view of the above limitations, future research can be carried out in the following directions. First, the sample size can be expanded by including multi-center and largescale OA clinical samples, collecting comprehensive clinical information, and conducting stratified analyses to further verify the diagnostic efficacy and clinical relevance of CDH11 and MSX2. Meanwhile, a variety of OA cell models, such as primary cultures of synovial cells and chondrocytes, as well as OA animal models, including the destabilization of the medial meniscus mouse model, can be adopted to improve the system of in vitro and in vivo validation. Second, the underlying molecular mechanisms can be further explored. Cell function experiments, such as CDH11 and MSX2 silencing or overexpression and molecular interaction assays, can be applied to clarify the specific pathways through which CDH11 regulates mitochondrial dynamics and the molecular targets involved in the interaction between CDH11, MSX2, and immune cells, thereby revealing the core mechanisms by which these 2 genes contribute to OA progression. In addition, the specific function and regulatory network of MSX2 in OA can be systematically investigated. Third, the potential therapeutic targets and drugs can be verified. The effectiveness and safety of the predicted miRNAs, TFs and candidate therapeutic agents can be validated through in vitro and in vivo experiments, so as to screen out targets and drugs with high clinical application value and provide support for the precise treatment of OA. Fourth, the research perspective can be broadened. The integration of multiomics analyses, including transcriptomics, proteomics and metabolomics, can be used to further explore the interactive mechanism between mitochondrial dynamics and immune regulation in OA, identify additional biomarkers and therapeutic targets with important clinical value, and promote the development of precise and individualized diagnosis and treatment for OA.

  In this study, combining bioinformatic analysis and ML methods, we screened and validated 2 candidate diagnostic biomarkers (CDH11 and MSX2) identified from a predefined MDRG set in OA based on GEO datasets. Both genes were significantly highly expressed in OA and showed diagnostic potential in the analyzed datasets. Meanwhile, this study demonstrated the correlation of 2 hub genes with immune cell infiltration and predicted their potential regulatory miRNAs, TFs, and therapeutic drugs. These findings provide candidate biomarkers and hypotheses for future mechanistic and clinical studies in OA.
