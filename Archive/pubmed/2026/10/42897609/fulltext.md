# FUBP1 lactylation suppresses trophoblast function in fetal growth restriction


## Background
  Fetal growth restriction (FGR) is a common complication of pregnancy; however, its exact molecular mechanisms remain unclear. Excessive lactate accumulation in the placenta is often observed during FGR. High concentrations of lactate can regulate the proliferation of pathological cells through lactylation of key proteins. Therefore, we aimed to screen key lactylation modification-related genes (LMRGs) and provide new directions for the diagnosis and treatment of FGR.


## Methods
  The Differentially expressed LMRGs (DE-LMRGs) in FGR were evaluated using the Gene Expression Omnibus database. Functional enrichment analysis was performed, and a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was created to explore the characteristics of placental gene networks associated with FGR. In addition, secondary cluster analysis was performed using a machine learning algorithm to screen critical genes. We evaluated the degree of immune cell infiltration of key genes using single-sample gene set enrichment analysis. The expression and function of key genes in FGR were verified experimentally. Furthermore, FUBP1 lactylation was assessed using mass spectrometry and immunoprecipitation. We overexpressed wild-type and mutant FUBP1 in HTR-8/SVneo cells to investigate how its lactylation regulates trophoblast function. Finally, patient-derived organoids (PDO) were cultured to confirm the underlying mechanism of FUBP1.


## Results
  In total, 35 DE-LMRGs were identified, and their PPI network was constructed. Two distinct FGR clusters were obtained using unsupervised consensus clustering. Moreover, the DE-LMRGs between the two patterns were mainly enriched in immune cell regulation-related molecular functions. We identified 5 overlapping key genes (BCLAF1, FUBP1, S100A6, CSRP1, CSRP2) by integrating the LASSO, random forest (RF), and SVM-RFE algorithms and screening for genes with a large fold change (using log2-FC). FUBP1 and BCLAF1 expression was lower in FGR placental tissues than in normal controls. Functional assays showed that both genes typically promote HTR-8/SVneo cell proliferation and invasion. However, lactylation of FUBP1 exerted the opposite effect by inhibiting proliferation and invasion and promoting apoptosis, indicating that FUBP1 lactylation regulates trophoblast function. Furthermore, PDO derived from the trophoblast tissues of patients with FGR revealed that FUBP1 lactylation suppressed PDO formation.


## Conclusions
  FUBP1 lactylation is identified as a novel mechanism suppressing trophoblast function in FGR. This finding expands the field of FGR research by highlighting the role of lactylation-modified proteins and provides crucial insights into the pathological mechanisms of FGR.

## Introduction
  Fetal growth restriction (FGR), a common complication of pregnancy, is defined as the failure of a fetus to achieve its genetically determined growth potential [1]. FGR is a major cause of fetal, perinatal, and neonatal morbidity and mortality. In addition, it can cause various adverse pregnancy outcomes [2]. Fetuses with growth restriction tend to develop cognitive delays in childhood and chronic diseases in late adulthood [3, 4]. FGR is related to maternal, fetal, placental, and genetic factors, among which placental factors are the most important [5]. However, the aetiology and pathogenesis of FGR remain unclear. Furthermore, selection of an appropriate delivery time and provision of symptomatic treatment remain the only known intervention measures [6].

  Lactate is an important metabolite of the cellular glycolytic pathway. In addition, it is an important signalling and immunomodulatory molecule in humans [7]. Lactate modifies the lysine residues in histones via lactylation [8]. Lactylation plays an important epigenetic regulatory role in many cellular processes and directly regulates gene expression [9]. Lactylation has been demonstrated to regulate crucial processes in various diseases, such as tumor cell proliferation, metastasis, and immune microenvironment [10, 11]. Moreover, emerging research indicates that lactylation also regulates trophoblast function in pregnancy-related diseases through multiple mechanisms, including: inducing pyroptosis [12], elevating mitochondrial ROS production, sensitizing cells to apoptosis [13], and directly impairing proliferation, migration, and invasion of trophoblasts [14]. However, research of lactylation on FGR remains limited. The placenta in patients with FGR is in a hypoxic, metabolic disorder state, with abnormal glucose consumption and excessive lactate accumulation [15–17], and lactate can lead to a global elevation of protein lactylation in trophoblast cells [14]. Therefore, we hypothesised that histone lactylation in the placenta is aberrant during FGR. Thus, the potential function of histone lactylation in the pathogenesis of FGR warrants further study.

  The development of FGR is associated with impaired cellular immunity, and placental dysfunction may be caused by altered immune cell balance [18]. Therefore, it is of clinical significance to evaluate potential diagnostic markers of FGR from the perspective of immune cell infiltration. This study aimed to identify key lactylation modification-related genes (LMRGs) and investigate their protein functions in FGR. Our findings provide new insights into the pathological mechanisms underlying FGR.


## Methods

### Data collection
  FGR gene expression data were collected from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database(https://www.ncbi.nl-m.nih.gov/geo/). The GSE12216, GSE24129, and GSE147776 datasets were downloaded. The microarray data of GSE12216 and GSE24129 contained eight FGR samples and eight control samples, whereas GSE147776 data contained 13 FGR samples and eight control samples. All samples from the three datasets were obtained from human placental tissues.


### Data preprocessing and screening of differentially expressed genes
  The expression data for the three datasets were combined and renormalised using the R package “preprocessCore”. Batch variability between platforms was eliminated using the “limma” and “SVA” packages and evaluated through principal component analysis (PCA). Furthermore, the R packages “FactoMineR” and “factoextra” were used to produce PCA maps. We used the R package “limma” to screen differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between FGR and control groups based on the combined datasets, with thresholds of P < 0.05 (no adjusted). This transcriptome screening step as an exploratory preliminary analysis. The results were visualised using volcano plots and heat maps.


### Protein–protein interaction network construction, gene ontology analysis and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis
  Protein–protein interactions (PPI) were analysed using the STRING online tool (http://string-db.org/). Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed using the R package “cluster-Profiler” to further explore the biological pathways associated with the identified DEGs. The screening criteria for significant terms were adjusted to P < 0.05 and q < 0.2. Combined with |log2FC| values, the enrichment analyses were performed by calculating the Z-scores using the R package “GOplot”.


### Identification of differentially expressed lactylation modification‑related genes
  We obtained 327 genes associated with lactylation modification from Cheng et al. and Wan et al. [19, 20]. Differentially expressed lactylation modification-related genes (DE-LMRGs) were identified by intersecting DEGs and lactylation modification genes; the overlapping regions were used for subsequent analysis.


### Cluster analysis
  We categorised the FGR cohort using the “ConsensusClusterPlus” package to determine whether the expression of genes related to lactylation modification was associated with FGR. The intragroup correlation was strong, and the intergroup correlation was modest for k = 2. DE-LMRGs between the two types are shown in a box plot. Moreover, we connected the DE-LMRGs and FGR subtypes using heatmaps. PCA maps and volcano plots were then used to show the distribution of the FGR samples and DEGs between the two subtypes.


### Machine-learning algorithm-based screening of key DE-LMRGs in FGR
  The significant LMRGs were screened using three machine-learning algorithms: least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), random forest (RF) and support vector machine-recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE). LASSO regression, also known as L1 regularisation, is a linear regression algorithm particularly suitable for parameter reduction and selection. Therefore, it is used to estimate linear models with sparse parameters [21]. We identified genes significantly associated with FGR and normal samples using LASSO through the “glmnet” package in R. RF is an excellent representative algorithm of ensemble learning. It trains, classifies and predicts target samples based on multiple decision trees. Furthermore, it can be applied to both regression and classification scenarios [22]. RF identified the top 10 genes in order of importance. SVM-RFE, a supervised machine learning protocol widely used for classification and regression, was used to find the best variables by deleting SVM-generated eigenvectors via the “kernlab” package in R.

  We combined the overlapping genes between the LASSO, RF, and SVM-RFE algorithms for further analysis. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Correlation analysis of five genes was then performed using the “circlize” package in R. Next, the ability of each gene to predict disease occurrence was assessed through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves using the “pROC” package in R.


### Evaluation of the degree of immune cell infiltration
  Immune cell subgroup analysis was performed to better recognise the immune cell characteristics in normal and FGR placentas. The differences in immune cell subsets in the samples were compared. The single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) algorithm, an extension of the GSEA approach, was used to compare the differential composition of 23 immune cells between the two groups [23]. Correlation between five key genes and immune cell infiltration were visualised using boxplots drawn with the R package “ggplot2”; P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.


### Collection of placental tissue samples
  Placental tissue samples were collected from pregnant women who delivered at term between March and August 2024 at the Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University. Informed consent was obtained from all patients. Placental tissue obtained during caesarean section were immediately stored at − 80 °C for subsequent RNA and protein extraction. Additionally, part of the tissue was placed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded into paraffin specimens for subsequent experiments.


### Cell culture and transfection
  Human trophoblast-like cell lines (HTR-8/SVneo, ATCC, CRL-3271) were cultivated in Roswell Park Memorial Institute medium (RPMI 1640; Corning Inc., Corning, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; PAN-Biotech GmbH, Germany) at 37 °C and 5% CO2. HTR-8/SVneo cells were cultured in six-well plates, and the plasmids were transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The information of plasmids used in this study are provided in the Supplementary Material 5.


### Immunohistochemistry
  Paraffin specimens were sectioned at 4 μm and stained using an immunohistochemical kit (E-IR-R217; Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sections were observed and photographed under a microscope (Nikon, Japan). The following antibodies were used: BCLAF1 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and FUBP1 (Abcam).


### RNA extraction and reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction
  Total RNA was isolated using the RNA Isolator Total RNA Extraction Reagent (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China) and reverse-transcribed to cDNA using HiScript Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd.). Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was performed using a real-time PCR system (7500; Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA) with ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd); a 20-μL reaction system was prepared. The gene expression value relative to the control gene β-actin was evaluated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. The primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Material 1.


### Western blot analysis
  Total protein was extracted from placental tissues and HTR-8/SVneo cells using RIPA lysis buffer containing the protease inhibitor PMSF. Protein concentration was detected using the BCA method. Protein samples were separated through 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to a PVDF membrane (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). Next, the membrane was blocked in 5% non-fat milk and TBST solution for 2 h and incubated with different primary antibodies for 14 h at 4 °C. The membrane was then washed thrice with TBST for 10 min and incubated with the HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, the membrane was washed with TBST and visualised using enhanced chemiluminescence. The following antibodies were used: BCLAF1 (Abcam; 1:1000), FUBP1 (Abcam; 1:1000), Pan-Kla (PTM, China; 1:1000) and β-actin (Proteintech, China; 1:5000), HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG(H + L) (Proteintech, China; 1:5000).


### CCK-8 assay
  Cells were inoculated in 96-well plates (2 × 103 cells/well) and incubated for 0, 24, 48, 72 and 96 h at 37 °C. Next, 10 μL of CCK-8 was dropped into each well and the samples were incubated at 37 °C for another 2 h. The absorbance of each well was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader to evaluate cell viability.


### Transwell assay
  HTR-8/SVneo cells grown to the logarithmic phase were resuspended in serum-free medium to 1 × 105 cells/mL. Next, 200 μL cell suspension was inoculated into a transwell chamber pre-coated with Matrigel matrix (Corning Inc.). Further, 500 μL of medium supplemented with 20% FBS was added to the lower culture plate. The samples were then incubated for 24 h before the upper chamber was removed and stained. Five randomly selected fields were selected for microscopic observations and imaging.


### EdU
  HTR-8/SVneo cells were seeded into six-well plates containing 2 mL RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS. An EdU-555 cell proliferation kit (Epizyme, China; CX003) was used to treat cells and measure cell proliferation. Five randomly selected fields were observed and imaged using a fluorescence microscope.


### Annexin V-FITC/PI double staining assay
  HTR-8/SVneo cells were stained using an Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit (KeyGen Biotech Co. ltd., Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The apoptosis rate was determined using flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter Life Sciences).


### Immunoprecipitation
  The cell lysates were incubated overnight with Flag (Proteintech, China) antibodies at 4 °C; IgG (Proteintech, China) was used as a control. Both antibodies were added 0.5–4.0 ug for 1.0–3.0 mg of total protein lysate, and were diluted to 1:10,000 in Westen Blot assay. 30 μL Protein A/G beads (Sellseck, The United States) were prewashed and incubated with the product for another 2 h. Beads containing bound proteins were washed thrice and boiled with 1 × loading buffer for western blotting.


### LC‒MS/MS analysis and database search
  LC‒MS/MS analysis was performed by Jingjie PTM BioLabs (Hangzhou, China). HTR-8/SVneo cells were collected for protein extraction and trypsin digestion, dissolved in NETN buffer, and incubated overnight with prewashed antibody beads at 4 °C with gentle shaking. The bound peptides were eluted from the beads and vacuum dried for subsequent LC–MS/MS analysis. The tryptic peptides were dissolved in solvent A, directly loaded onto a home-made reversed-phase analytical column (25-cm length, 100 μm i.d.). The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (0.1% formic acid, 2% acetonitrile in water) and solvent B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile). Peptides were separated with following gradient: 0–42 min, 6%-22%B; 42–54 min, 22%-30%B; 54–57 min, 30%-80%B; 57–60 min, 80%B, and all at a constant flow rate of 450 nl/min on a NanoElute UHPLC system (Bruker Daltonics). The peptides were subjected to capillary source followed by the timsTOF Pro2 mass spectrometry. The electrospray voltage applied was 1.5 kV. The Collision Energy were set 20.00 eV, 36.00 eV and 65.00 eV. Precursors and fragments were analyzed at the TOF detector, with a MS/MS scan range from 100 to 1700. The timsTOF Pro was operated in parallel accumulation serial fragmentation (PASEF) mode. Precursors with charge states 0–5 were selected for fragmentation, and 10PASEF-MS/MS scans were acquired per cycle. The dynamic exclusion was set to 24 s. The resulting MS/MS data were processed using the MaxQuant search engine (v.1.6.15.0). 4D label free platform adds a fourth dimension, ion mobility (mobility) separation, on the basis of 3D separation. 4D label free platform relies on the timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer, this enables the low-abundance protein signals that are masked in 3D conditions to be distinguished and identified, greatly enhancing the detection sensitivity. Tandem mass spectra were searched against the human SwissProt database (20,422 entries) and concatenated with a reverse-decoy database. The FDR was adjusted to < 1%. The biological processes of the proteins were annotated using GO analysis.


### Molecular docking simulations
  To systematically evaluate the binding affinity and interaction mode between the candidate drug and its target protein FUBP1, molecular docking simulations were performed in this study. The three-dimensional structure of the ligand L-lactic acid (CAS: 79-33-4) was obtained in SDF format from the PubChem database, while the protein structure was prepared based on experimentally determined data. Both the protein and ligand molecules were preprocessed using AutoDock 4.0 software, which included adding polar hydrogens, calculating Gasteiger charges, and converting files to PDBQT format. The docking grid was defined as a cubic box measuring 40 Å × 40 Å × 40 Å centered at coordinates (34.061, 21.287, –21.285), with a grid spacing of 0.05 nm. Molecular docking simulations were conducted using AutoDock 4.0, and the conformation with the lowest binding energy was selected as the optimal binding mode. The interaction interface of the complex was further analyzed in detail using PLIP and LigPlot + software, and the three-dimensional structure along with key interactions of the protein–ligand complex was visualized with PyMOL 2.5 software.


### Covalent docking simulations
  To investigate the potential covalent binding mode between Lys394 of FUBP1 and L-lactic acid, covalent docking was performed using the Glide covalent docking module in Schrödinger 2019–1. Prior to docking, the FUBP1 structure was prepared by adding hydrogen atoms, assigning bond orders, optimizing protonation states, and performing local energy minimization. The receptor grid was generated around the ε-amino group of Lys394, with a grid box size of 20 Å × 20 Å × 20 Å. During docking, L-lactic acid was treated as a flexible ligand, and residues within 4 Å of Lys394 were allowed to undergo local flexibility. Docking poses were generated and scored using the Glide covalent docking protocol. The optimal binding pose was selected based on docking score, covalent bond geometry, and stabilizing non-covalent interactions around the binding site.


### Patient-derived organoid culture model
  A patient-derived organoid (PDO) model was established using trophoblast tissues derived from patients with FGR. Briefly, freshly removed placental tissues were cut into 1–3 mm3 pieces, washed several times with antibiotic-containing PBS, and digested with 200 U/mL collagenase (bioGenous, Suzhou, China) and 100 U/mL hyaluronidase (bioGenous, Suzhou, China) for 30 min at 37 °C. The digested tissues were filtered through a 100-μm cell filter, centrifuged, and the supernatant discarded. Next, the cells were resuspended in Matrigel. Matrigel cell resuspension solution (50 µL) was then dispensed into each well of a 48-well plate to generate gel droplets with 50,000 cells per droplet, and 500 µL of EC organic medium (AimingGEO, Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China) was added post-solidification.


### Tubule formation assay
  Add 50 μL Matrigel matrix (Corning, America) to a 24-well plate, which was then incubated for 30 min. HUVEC cells were collected and resuspended after transfection for 24 h. HUVEC cells were then added to the pre-coagulated matrix glue for further culturing in an incubator. After 12 h, the culture plate was photographed under an inverted microscope, and ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) was used to calculate the length of the tubule.


### Statistical analysis
  All data are expressed as the mean ± SD and were analysed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests were used to confirm the normal distribution of data. Student's T test was used to compare the differences between two groups. At least three replicates were performed for each experiment. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

  We performed GO and KEGG enrichment analyses using the R software to evaluate the potential biological functions of the identified DEGs. ### Evaluation of the correlation between five key genes and construction of the networks between these genes with miRNAs and transcription factors
  Correlation analysis was conducted between the five key genes and all genes, and the top 50 positively correlated genes are shown in a heat map (Supplementary Materiral 4). Single-gene reactome-based GSEA was performed based on the results of the correlation analysis, and the top 20 results of the five key genes are shown (Fig. 4G). BCLAF1 was positively correlated with gene expression, RNA metabolism, RNA polymerase II transcription, and the cell cycle. Contrastingly, it was negatively correlated with cell surface interactions at the vascular wall. FUBP1 expression was positively correlated with gene expression (transcription) and negatively correlated with eukaryotic translation elongation and assembly of the pre-replicative complex. These findings suggest that these five key genes may participate in multiple pathophysiological processes in FGR through the regulation of molecular transcription and immune cells. These key genes may act as transcription factors and vital signalling molecules or interact with intracellular non-coding RNAs. To predict the upstream molecules of the five key genes and elucidate the potential mechanism of action for each key gene, we predicted their upstream miRNAs and transcription factors using the RegNetwork database and built a network using Cytoscape software (Supplementary Material 3).

### The identification of FUBP1 lactylation sites at K394 in HTR-8/SVneo cells

## Discussion
  FGR is a public health concern and one of the main challenges faced by obstetricians [24]. The pathogenesis of FGR remains unclear so it is necessary to explore the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying the occurrence of FGR. FGR can have various causes, among which placental dysfunction is central to its pathophysiology [25]. Accumulating evidence indicates that Post-translational modifications(PTMs) play a pivotal role in regulating placental function and are significantly associated with FGR [26, 27]. For example, overexpression of GALNT2 promoted O-GalNAcylation of β1-integrin and inhibited the invasion of HTR-8/SVneo EVT cells [27]. Ubiquitination modifies the expression and degradation of key proteins in placental cells, thereby influencing normal placental function [28]. Elevated SUMOylation levels can impair placentation and organogenesis. SENP2 deficiency leads to hyper-SUMOylation, while inactivation of SUMO2 or SUMO3 can mitigate the placental defects and abnormal embryonic development caused by SENP2 deficiency [29]. As a newly recognized PTMs, lactylation regulates tumour cell proliferation, metastasis, angiogenesis, and the immune microenvironment in various diseases [10, 11]. While most lactylation research has focused on cancer, only limited studies have addressed its role in pregnancy-related disorders. Therefore, this study aims to explore the contribution of histone lactylation in FGR.

  Lactate is not only a metabolite, it also regulates metabolic pathways, immune responses, and intercellular communication in the tumour microenvironment [7]. Lactate-derived histone posttranslational modifications (PTM) were first reported by Zhao et al. [8]. Lactate can increase the stemness of CD8 + T cells and augment antitumour immunity [30]. Furthermore, Wang et al. suggested that YY1 lactylation in microglia substantially contributes to retinal neovascularisation by upregulating FGF2 expression. In addition, HIF1-α lactylation enhances KIAA1199 transcription to promote angiogenesis and vasculogenic mimicry in prostate cancer [31]. The placenta in FGR exhibits a state of hypoxia and metabolic dysregulation, characterized by abnormal glucose consumption and excessive lactate accumulation [15, 32]. Elevated levels of lactate and histone lactylation have been observed in preeclamptic placentas [33]. Moreover, hypoxia stimulates lactate production in HTR-8/SVneo cells, which in turn induces histone lactylation [34]. These findings suggest that histone lactylation may contribute to the development of FGR by modulating angiogenesis and other signalling pathways.

  Our study suggests that the placentas of patients with FGR exhibit inflammatory and immune abnormalities. The role of the maternal immune system in pregnancy complications has been investigated, particularly the involvement of immune cell subpopulations in placental development and maternal-foetal tolerance [35]. Most DEGs in FGR are involved in inflammatory and immune diseases [36]. In addition, placental dysfunction may be caused by an altered immune cell balance [18]. Natural immune cells affect trophoblast invasion, spiral artery remodelling, and angiogenesis through immune regulation, growth factor supply, and maintenance of the uterine vascular system, thereby affecting placental development [35, 37]. Natural killer cells in the decidua highly express the transcription factor PBX1, which enhances the transcription of growth-promoting factors and promotes embryonic development. A decline in natural killer cells leads to FGR, and the adoption of such these cells can reverse the occurrence of FGR [38]. However, abnormal interactions between trophoblasts and natural killer cells in the uterus may also inhibit spiral artery remodelling [39]. Therefore, we evaluated the degree of immune cell infiltration of key genes during lactylation and explored their potential regulatory relationships with immune cells to elucidate the immune response mechanisms of FGR.

  Consistent with our findings, FGR was closely associated with placental immune disorders in previous studies. Immune cells affect placental development through immune regulation, the provision of growth factors, and the facilitation of adaptations in the uterine vasculature to support trophoblast invasion [37]. An altered immune cell balance may play a role in the development of FGR. Moreover, changes in various immune cells may be key factors in the cause or exacerbation of these complications during pregnancy [35].

  In this study, we identified five overlapping key LMRGs: BCLAF1, FUBP1, S100A6, CSRP1, and CSRP2. FUBP1 and BCLAF1 had inextricable links with the development and immune cell infiltration of the placenta. Thus, we verified their expression in FGR and normal placental tissues and evaluated their functional phenotype in HTR-8/SVneo cells.

  Far upstream element binding protein 1 (FUBP1) is highly expressed in various tumour tissues. It displays a broad spectrum of activities, such as the promotion of proliferation, the cell cycle, invasion, and metastasis of tumour cells [40]. FUBP1 enhances Hk1 and Hk2 mRNA levels, contributing to lactate production and Akt-mTOR activation. Furthermore, a positive correlation in mRNA expression has been observed between FUBP1 and both hexokinase genes in several cancer types [41]. FUBP1 can target c-MYC; FUBP1 silencing and c-MYC overexpression led to malignant biological behaviour and glycolysis in colon cancer cells [42]. FUBP1 promotes NB cell proliferation and inhibits cell apoptosis by enhancing glycolysis and ATP production. In particular, it enhanced glycolysis by targeting HIF1α to upregulate the expression of lactate dehydrogenase isoform B (LDHB) [40]. BCL2-associated transcription factor 1 (BCLAF1) is involved in apoptosis, transcription regulation, and DNA damage repair [43]. CK downregulated BCLAF1 expression, inhibited the HIF-1αmediated glycolysis pathway, and affected the proliferation of hepatoma cells in a primary hepatocellular carcinoma rat model [43]. Furthermore, BCLAF1 can promote angiogenesis by regulating HIF-1α transcription in hepatocellular carcinoma [44]. It also affects HCC progression by inhibiting c-MYC mRNA stability [45]. These studies indicated that FUBP1 and BCLAF1 are strong connected to glycolysis, lactate production, angiogenesis, proliferation, invasion, and apoptosis in cancer cells. However, their roles in trophoblast cells and FGR placenta remain unclear. BCLAF1 contributes to the regulation of cellular apoptosis and tissue development in the placenta [46]. In this study, FUBP1 and BCLAF1 expression was downregulated in FGR placental samples. In addition, their overexpression promoted HTR-8/SVneo cell proliferation, infiltration, and apoptosis.

  LC–MS/MS detection identified only one lactylated lysine residue in FUBP1 (K394) in HTR-8/SVneo cells. This suggests that lactylation at this site affects cellular function. Furthermore, mutation of FUBP1 at K394R did not change the protein level of FUBP1 compared to the wild-type, which weakened the lactylation level of this protein. In the present study, a reduction in FUBP1 lactylation significantly improved proliferation and infiltration, suggesting that this process plays a negative role in regulating trophoblast function. Similarly, lactylation of Lys72 in MOESIN can be modulated by tumour metabolite lactate, which promotes tumorigenesis and enhances TGF-β signalling in regulatory T cells [47].

  PDO are highly similar to source tissues in terms of tissue structure, cell type, and function. They are becoming critical tools in disease modelling, pharmacodynamic research, and precision medicine [48]. Many researchers have established organoid culture systems to study placental function. In addition, ALI‐based PVOs have been successfully developed from normal and diseased placentas spanning different gestation stages [49]. Wang et al. [50] confirmed the functions of transcription factors identified in hTSC and trophoblast organoid-derived STBs. In this study, FUBP1 lactylation suppressed PDO formation.

  However, the mechanism through which lactylated FUBP1 regulates trophoblast function and the proteins that interact with it and concrete mechanisms remain unclear. Lactylation has been demonstrated to regulate trophoblast cell function in pregnancy-related diseases. Kong’s study demonstrates that AMPA exposure promotes lactate production via the PPARγ/ANGPTL4 pathway, which subsequently inhibits the proliferation, migration and invasion of trophoblasts through JunB K38 lactylation, ultimately leading to spontaneous abortion [14]. Feng found that preeclamptic placentas exhibit distinct lactylation profiles compared to normal pregnancies, primarily affecting mitochondrial and TCA cycle-related energy metabolism. These changes contribute to the pathophysiology of preeclampsia by involving metabolic pathways critical for angiogenesis and endothelial function [33]. Protein lactylation can also influence trophoblast cell function through cell pyroptosis and increases mitochondrial ROS production and sensitizes trophoblasts to apoptosis [12, 13]. Nevertheless, how do FUBP1 mechanistically affect trophoblast proliferation, invasion, or vascular remodel, this is a question worth considering. To further dissect the regulatory network of FUBP1 linked to FGR, we plan to characterize its potential downstream targets and binding partners, with c-MYC and HIF-1α as representative candidate molecules, so as to deepen our understanding of the molecular basis driving FGR progression. In addition, we will further conduct confirmatory experiments with human trophoblast stem cells (hTSCs) and more investigations on trophoblast organoids will be performed in our subsequent research to enrich the evidence of this work.

  In summary, we find LMRGs dysregulated in FGR and experimentally validated that FUBP1 lactylation suppressed trophoblast function. Our study enhances the understanding of lactylation-modified protein functions in FGR and offers new insights into how post-translational modifications contribute to placental insufficiency in FGR.


## Conclusion
  In this study, we obtained five DEGs related to the modification of lactylation, and our research findings indicated LMRGs were closely related to the degree of immune cell infiltration. We experimentally validated the function of FUBP1 and BCLAF1 in vitro, demonstrated that FUBP1 lactylation at K394 suppressed HTR-8/SVneo cell proliferation, invasion, and migration. Our study expands the study field of FGR in the proteins function related to the modification of lactylation, these findings may provide insights and experimental basis to the pathological mechanism underlying in FGR from the perspective of placenta.
