# PF4 drives mechanical overload-induced temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis through TLR2-dependent activation of NF-κB


## Background
  Mechanical overload‑related cytokines contribute to temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis (TMJOA) pathogenesis. This study investigated the role of overload‑associated platelet factor 4 (PF4).


## Methods
  Combined proteomic screening of human TMJOA synovial fluid (SF) and finite element simulation identified mechanically linked cytokines. A rat unilateral anterior crossbite (UAC) model was used to induce TMJ cartilage degeneration. To dissect the mechanism, primary rat TMJ chondrocytes were treated with PF4 and analyzed by transcriptomics and functional assays. Further, specific inhibitors targeting the canonical nuclear factor‑κB (NF-κB) pathway (TPCA-1) or TLR2 (C29) were applied in vitro to pinpoint the signaling cascade. Their potential therapeutic effects were further assessed by administration in the UAC model.


## Results
  Clinical samples revealed elevated PF4 levels in the SF of TMJOA patients, correlating with simulated mechanical stress at the condyle. In a rat UAC model, mechanical overload similarly upregulated PF4 in TMJ, concomitant with cartilage degeneration, anabolic-catabolic imbalance, and cellular senescence. In vitro, PF4 stimulation of primary chondrocytes suppressed anabolic markers while upregulating catabolic factors and senescence markers. Mechanistically, PF4 uniquely activated the canonical NF-κB pathway by directly engaging surface TLR2, bypassing its classical chemokine receptor C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 3 (CXCR3). This PF4-TLR2 interaction was both necessary and sufficient for downstream NF-κB-driven gene dysregulation. Notably, this PF4-TLR2 axis upregulates B-cell CLL/lymphoma 3 (BCL3), which in turn promotes pathway activation by accelerating early phosphorylation of p65, revealing an intrinsic feedback loop that fine-tunes the inflammatory response. In vivo, inhibition of TLR2 or canonical NF-κB signaling in the UAC model significantly mitigated overload-induced cartilage dysregulation and subchondral bone damage.


## Conclusion
  We delineate a novel mechanotransduction pathway in TMJOA, where overload-induced PF4 acts as an alarmin that signals through TLR2 to activate the canonical NF-κB cascade, a process further amplified by the downstream regulator BCL3, ultimately disrupting cartilage homeostasis. This work establishes the PF4-TLR2-NF-κB axis as a crucial pathogenic driver and a promising therapeutic target for load-related TMJ degeneration.

## Introduction
  Temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis (TMJOA) is a highly prevalent and complex disorder resulting in chronic pain and functional disability of the joint [1]. Mechanical overloading is recognized as a pivotal factor in its pathogenesis and is also recognized to be associated with abnormal occlusal relationships or improper orthodontic treatment, contributing to disease onset and progression by disrupting joint homeostasis [2–7]. The resulting dysregulation of mechanosignaling represents a central driver linking mechanical overload to TMJOA progression and the phenotypic drift of chondrocytes, a process notably marked by chondrocyte loss [5, 8]. The condylar cartilage, which contains a higher proportion of fibrocartilage, is particularly susceptible to homeostatic imbalance under overload, leading to a remodeling process characterized by rapid degradation [9–11]. Despite its clinical relevance, a fundamental gap persists in our understanding of the precise molecular sequence that translates physical force into a sustained biological response within the chondrocyte. Current management strategies remain predominantly palliative, underscoring the critical need to decipher these initial mechanotransduction events for identifying novel, disease-modifying therapeutic targets.

  Chondrocytes effectively respond to mechanical stresses either ‘directly’ through sensing pericellular matrix deformation via cell–extracellular matrix (ECM) adhesions and/or cell sensory structures, or ‘indirectly’ following the mechanically induced release of sequestered cytokines and their interaction with cell receptors [8]. Currently, research on “indirect” mechanosignalling, characterized by alterations in cytokine profiles, remains notably scarce. Elucidating these “indirect” pathways appears to be more closely linked to the identification of early biomarkers and therapeutic targets for disrupted cartilage homeostasis. Targeting the cytokines which transfer these pathways is an emerging therapeutic strategy to suppress the downstream release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and catabolic enzymes that drive disease progression [12]. Therefore, understanding the alterations in ‘indirect’ mechanosignaling within TMJ cartilage following mechanical overloading is a critical and complex issue worthy of investigation.

  Cytokines such as TGFβ, BMP, and FGF, released from the pericellular matrix under mechanical load, are recognized as core mediators of “indirect” mechanosignaling in cartilage. Beyond the pericellular matrix, however, the structure and composition of the periarticular environment can directly influence TMJOA pathogenesis, especially the synovial fluid (SF). SF also serves as a reservoir for cytokines originating from plasma, synovium, and bone, in addition to its role as a natural lubricant between articular surfaces [13]. Consequently, aside from increased friction due to diminished lubrication [14], the cytokine profile within SF constitutes a critical element in osteoarthritis (OA) progression [15]. For instance, Luo et al. proposed that complement C8α may enhance membrane attack complex activity, thereby exacerbating cartilage destruction [16]. Tumor necrosis factor-α (TNFα) is established as a key driver of inflammation, cartilage homeostasis disruption, and pain in OA, and its inhibition has been shown to alleviate OA by reducing TNF-α‑induced senescence in human chondrocytes [17, 18]. Furthermore, Fu et al. demonstrated that increased joint loading elevates RANTES levels in SF, which promotes macrophage osteoclast differentiation via the CCRs‑Akt2 axis and accelerates subchondral bone loss [19]. Despite these insights, the specific mechanisms through which mechanical loading regulates OA cartilage by modulating SF components remain poorly understood, highlighting a significant gap in our knowledge of cytokine-mediated “indirect” mechanotransduction.

  Here, through an integrated translational approach combining clinical correlation, animal models, and primary cell experiments, we systematically investigate the mechanotransduction mechanisms in TMJOA. We first identified platelet factor 4 (PF4) as the predominant load-correlated cytokine in human TMJOA synovial fluid following mandibular advancement surgery, a procedure we have previously characterized as inducing mechanical overload [20]. Using a rat model, we demonstrated its pathogenic role in driving cartilage degeneration. In vitro studies revealed that PF4 signals through a Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2)–canonical NF‑κB pathway to disrupt chondrocyte homeostasis, with B-cell CLL/lymphoma 3 (BCL3) acting as a key feedback modulator within this cascade. Finally, pharmacological inhibition of this axis in vivo robustly mitigated OA progression. This work uncovers a novel mechanotransduction pathway of TMJOA pathogenesis and highlights promising therapeutic targets for load-induced joint degeneration.


## Materials and methods

### Patients and samples
  This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Hospital of Stomatology, Sun Yat-sen University. All participants were recruited from the same hospital and provided written informed consent. SF samples were collected via arthrocentesis from the intra‑articular space of two patient groups: individuals diagnosed with TMJOA (n = 12) and those with temporomandibular joint disc displacement (TMJDD) but without radiographic evidence of osteoarthritis (n = 6). Samples were obtained during mandibular advancement surgery. Diagnoses of TMJDD and TMJOA were based on the Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorders (DC/TMD), supported by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and cone‑beam computed tomography (CBCT).


### Experimental UAC-induced OA rat model
  Male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (n = 46 total, 6 weeks old, 140–160 g body weight) were supplied by the Experimental Animal Center of Sun Yat-sen University. All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Sun Yat-sen University. Unilateral anterior crossbite (UAC) treatment was performed under sodium pentobarbital anesthesia, and every effort was made to minimize animal suffering. Two animal experiments were conducted. In the first experiment (n = 22), rats were divided into two control groups (8-week group, n = 5; 12-week group, n = 6) and two experimental groups (8-week group, n = 5; 12-week group, n = 6). The experimental groups underwent UAC treatment and were observed for the corresponding number of weeks. In the second experiment (n = 24), rats were divided into one control + vehicle group (n = 6), one UAC + vehicle group (n = 6), and two UAC + inhibitor groups (n = 6 each). The experimental endpoint for all groups in the second experiment was 12 weeks after UAC treatment. Intra-articular injections began one week after UAC treatment and were administered twice weekly. All procedures were performed as previously studies [21, 22]. The specific concentrations of all inhibitors used for in vivo administration are provided in Table S8.


### Isolation and culture of primary chondrocytes
  Primary chondrocytes were isolated from the condylar cartilage of 4-week-old male Sprague-Dawley rats via enzymatic digestion. The harvested cartilage tissue was sequentially digested with 0·25% trypsin (25200114, Gibco) and 0·025% collagenase II (1148090, Sigma). After digestion, the cell suspension was centrifuged and washed. The isolated cells were then resuspended and cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. Cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂, with the medium replaced every two – three days. Cells were passaged at 80–90% confluence. Chondrocytes from passages two – four were used for all subsequent experiments. Prior to any pharmacological treatment, cells were serum-starved for 24 h. For inhibitor experiments, the corresponding inhibitor was added to the culture medium 1 h before stimulation. The detailed concentrations of inhibitors applied in the in vitro experiments are listed in Table S8.


### Statistical analysis
  All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 10·0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA), with statistical significance defined as a two‑sided p < 0·05. Data are presented as group means with 95% confidence intervals (CI), and each experiment included at least three biological replicates, with the exact replicate number (n) provided in the corresponding figure legends. After assessing normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and quantile–quantile (Q–Q) plots and evaluating variance homogeneity with Levene’s test (or Brown–Forsythe test when appropriate), statistical tests were selected based on experimental design and data characteristics: for within‑donor (paired/repeated‑measures) comparisons, normally distributed data were analyzed with paired Student’s t‑tests (two conditions) or repeated‑measures one‑way ANOVA with Geisser–Greenhouse correction followed by Tukey’s post‑hoc test (multiple conditions), while non‑normally distributed data were analyzed using the Friedman test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test; for independent‑group comparisons, normally distributed data with homogeneous variances were analyzed with independent two‑tailed Student’s t‑tests (two groups) or ordinary one‑way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post‑hoc test (multiple groups), and non‑normally distributed or heteroscedastic data were analyzed with the Mann–Whitney U test (two groups) or the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post‑hoc test (multiple groups); ordinal or non‑transformable data were analyzed using the corresponding non‑parametric tests as described. Exact statistical data for all key comparisons are provided in Table S4.


## Results

### Elevated PF4 levels in TMJOA correlate with condylar mechanical overload
  To investigate mechanically induced proteomic alterations in TMJOA, we performed high-throughput antibody microarray analysis on SF from TMJOA patients post-mandibular advancement surgery. Among the 105 factors analyzed, PF4, matrix metalloproteinases 9 (MMP9), C-C motif chemokine ligand 5 (CCL5) and Insulin-like growth factor-binding protein 3 (IGFBP3) exhibited significantly higher levels in the SF of TMJOA patients compared to those with TMJDD (Fig. 1A-B). Given the most pronounced elevation of PF4 in OA samples, we prioritized its validation via enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Results confirmed that PF4 levels were elevated in OA versus DD (Fig. 1C). To quantify the mechanical load associated with mandibular advancement, finite element analysis (FEA) was employed. And the baseline demographic characteristics of the enrolled patients are presented in Table S7. Simulations based on both standardized incremental distances and the actual surgical advancement measured in individual patients demonstrated a progressive increase in maximum principal stress within the posterior region of the condyle (Fig. 1D–F). Critically, PF4 levels exhibited positive correlations with both the actual surgical advancement distance and the simulated overall condylar load (Fig. 1G). These findings implicate PF4 as a potential mediator in overload-induced TMJOA.

### Overload‑induced PF4 activation disrupts cartilage homeostasis
  We further investigated the effects of PF4 in rat overload-related TMJOA using an unilateral anterior crossbite (UAC) model, which has been reported to induce OA-like lesions (Fig. 2A). Pilot FEA of condyles from UAC-treated rats demonstrated elevated mechanical stress on the condylar surface, corroborating that the UAC model reliably induces condylar overload (Fig. S6). SF and condyles were collected at various time points (weeks 8 and 12) for ELISA and immunohistochemistry (IHC). Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) analysis revealed a time-dependent osteoarthritic bone remodeling, characterized by a significant decrease in bone volume fraction (BV/TV) and an increase in trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) in UAC-stimulated condyles compared to controls (Fig. 2B, D). ELISA indicated that UAC upregulated PF4 levels in SF regardless of stimulation duration, while IHC revealed sustained higher PF4 expression in the condylar cartilage of UAC rats compared with controls, with prominent localization in the middle (proliferative) zone (Fig. 2C, E). IHC confirmed OA progression, showing reduced COL2 expression in the deep (hypertrophic) zone and increased MMP13 in the middle (proliferative) zone of condylar cartilage (Fig. 2G-J). Furthermore, dysregulation of anabolic and catabolic markers, as well as elevated PF4 expression, was observed in rat primary chondrocytes subjected to a mechanical overload of 2 g/cm² (Fig. S1). These data indicate that mechanical overload induces persistent PF4 activation concurrent with cartilage dyshomeostasis.

  We further investigated the effect of exogenous PF4 on the cartilage, using rat primary TMJ articular chondrocytes (rTMJACs) and rat TMJ cartilage explants. Results showed that PF4 treatment suppressed anabolism of rat primary chondrocytes and promoted catabolism (Fig. 3A, B, G and Fig. S2). Alterations in cellular morphology and reduced proliferation levels, further indicated by microscopic examination and cell proliferation assays (Fig. 3 C, F), prompted us to examine cellular senescence. Subsequent validation confirmed the upregulation of senescence-associated markers p53, p21, and p16 following PF4 stimulation (Fig. 3D, E). This senescence-inducing activity was confirmed by β‑galactosidase (β‑gal) staining, which demonstrated a marked increase in positive chondrocytes (Fig. 3F). These in vitro results establish PF4 as an inducer of chondrocyte senescence and homeostasis disruption.

### PF4 activates NF-κB canonical pathway to dysregulate cartilage anabolic-catabolic axis
  To elucidate the downstream signaling mechanisms of PF4, we performed RNA sequencing on PF4-treated and untreated primary chondrocytes. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes highlighted significant involvement of several OA-relevant pathways, most notably the canonical nuclear factor‑κB (NF-κB) signaling pathway, alongside cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction and rheumatoid arthritis pathways (Fig. 4A, Fig. S3B). A cluster of senescence-associated genes was also upregulated (Fig. 4B). As NF‑κB pathway is a key driver of inflammation in OA, we next examined whether PF4 directly regulates this cascade.Western blot analysis confirmed the activation of the canonical NF-κB pathway by PF4, as evidenced by a time-dependent phosphorylation of p65 and degradation of IκBα (Fig. 4C, D) [23]. To establish a functional link, we used pharmacological inhibitors. Co-treatment with specific inhibitors of the canonical NF-κB pathway (TPCA-1 or BMS-345541), but not an inhibitor of the non-canonical pathway (B022), effectively rescued PF4-mediated suppression of anabolic factors, induction of catabolic factors, and elevation of senescence markers (Fig. 4G-J and Fig S4). Moreover, these inhibitors attenuated PF4-induced increases in senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) positive cells and loss of proteoglycans, as shown by toluidine blue staining (Fig. 4E, F, K, L). These results indicate that PF4 disrupts chondrocyte homeostasis primarily through activation of the canonical NF-κB pathway.

### TLR2, not CXCR3, functions as the key receptor for PF4 in chondrocytes
  We next sought to identify the cell surface receptor responsible for transducing PF4 signaling. Initial experiments using selective inhibitors for C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 3 (CXCR3) (AMG487 and SCH546738), a previously reported potential receptor for PF4 [24], failed to reverse its effects on anabolic and catabolic markers (Fig. 5A, B). We therefore performed a protein-protein interaction network analysis, which nominated TLR2 as a promising candidate for mediating PF4’s effects (Fig. 5C, Fig S3A). PF4 stimulation upregulated TLR2 expression in chondrocytes (Fig. 5D). Molecular docking analysis predicted a stable binding interaction between PF4 and TLR2 (Fig. 5E, Table S5), which was experimentally validated by reciprocal co-immunoprecipitation assays using His-tagged PF4 and endogenous TLR2 (Fig. 5F). Functionally, pre-treatment with a TLR2 inhibitor completely abrogated PF4-induced phosphorylation of p65 and degradation of IκBα (Fig. 5G, H). Accordingly, TLR2 inhibition also rescued the PF4-induced increase in senescent chondrocytes (Fig. 5I, J). These data establish TLR2 as the functional receptor mediating PF4-induced NF-κB activation and cartilage dyshomeostasis.

### BCL3, an IκB protein upregulated by PF4-TLR2, precociously activates the canonical NF-κB pathway in overload-induced OA
  Further analysis of our RNA-seq data identified Bcl3, an atypical IκB protein and transcriptional regulator, as a gene significantly upregulated by PF4 in a TLR2-NF-κB-dependent manner (Fig. 6A-C, Fig. S3A). IHC also showed elevated BCL3 protein levels in the PF4-enriched middle zone of condylar cartilage from UAC rats (Fig. 6D, E). Given its known role in fine-tuning NF-κB responses, we investigated BCL3’s function in our model. Inhibition of the BCL3-p50 interaction with the small molecule inhibitor JS6 delayed the onset of PF4-induced anabolic suppression and catabolic induction (Fig. 6F, G). Mechanistically, in PF4-stimulated chondrocytes co-treated with JS6, we observed a precocious peak in p65 phosphorylation (Fig. 6H-K), while p52 levels remained unchanged (Fig. 6J, K). These findings suggest that BCL3, induced by the PF4-TLR2 axis, plays a modulatory role by accelerating the onset of canonical NF-κB activation and cartilage dyshomeostasis.

### Pharmacological inhibition of the TLR2–NF-κB axis mitigates overload-induced TMJOA progression in vivo
  Finally, to evaluate the therapeutic potential of targeting the identified pathway, we administered the canonical NF-κB inhibitor TPCA-1 or the TLR2 inhibitor C29 in the 12-week rat UAC model (Fig. 7A). Both treatments significantly improved osteoarthritic outcomes. Micro-CT analysis revealed a rescue of subchondral bone microarchitecture, with increased BV/TV and decreased Tb.Sp in treated animals compared to vehicle controls (Fig. 7B, C). Histopathological evaluation using the Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) scoring system confirmed marked attenuation of cartilage degeneration (Fig. 7D, E). While UAC-induced PF4 upregulation in SF and cartilage was not affected by either inhibitor (Fig. 7F, G and Fig S5), confirming its upstream position, the downstream pathological responses were effectively blocked. Both TPCA-1 and C29 treatment mitigated the UAC-induced downregulation of COL2, upregulation of MMP13, and elevation of the senescence marker p21 in condylar cartilage (Fig. 7H-M). These in vivo results validate the PF4–TLR2–canonical NF-κB axis as a critical driver of overload-induced TMJOA and demonstrate that its pharmacological inhibition effectively alleviates disease progression.

## Discussion
  The central question of how abnormal mechanical loading is transduced into the biochemical signals that drive TMJOA remains incompletely answered. While inflammation is recognized as a key effector, the specific molecular entities that function as the initial “translators” of force in the TMJ are poorly defined. Our study bridges this critical gap by identifying PF4 as a principal, load-correlated cytokine in TMJOA and delineating a novel PF4-TLR2-canonical NF-κB signaling axis that directly disrupts chondrocyte homeostasis. This work not only extends the known PF4’s role beyond its classical hematopoietic functions but also establishes a coherent pathogenic circuit linking mechanical overload to progressive joint degeneration, thereby highlighting the therapeutic potential of this axis.

  Mechanically induced cytokine release and subsequent receptor interaction represent the “indirect” mechanosensing pathway, a crucial mechanism by which chondrocytes sense and respond to mechanical stress, making the search for biomarkers of this process critical for advancing OA diagnosis and treatment [25]. Classical pathways involve the release of sequestered growth factors (e.g., FGFs, BMPs, TGFβ), while pan‑articular OA pathology also features dysregulated inflammatory mediators such as chemokine (C-X-C motif) ligand (CXCL) chemokines and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) [26–30]. These alterations are reflected in SF composition, whose diagnostic value in TMJOA is supported by multiple studies [31, 32], as exemplified by stage‑dependent SF protein changes and load‑induced CCL5‑mediated subchondral bone remodeling [13, 19]. Against this background, although the cellular source of PF4 in the middle condylar zone remains to be definitively established, its upregulation following TMJ overloading identifies PF4 as a novel overload‑responsive cytokine present in both rat and human TMJs. This finding provides even greater insight for the specific diagnosis and targeted therapies of overload-induced TMJOA.

  PF4, or CXCL4, is a chemokine predominantly released by platelets and also produced by other immune cells [33]. It exhibits a dual role in inflammatory diseases, potentially linked to variations in its forms, duration of action, or downstream targets. On the one hand, PF4 can limit inflammation by associating with neutrophil extracellular traps in vasculitis and by stimulating NK cell‑derived CXCL8/IL‑8 to facilitate vascular repair [34–36]; on the other hand, it accumulates at high concentrations in injured or inflamed tissues and promotes inflammatory responses [37–39]. In this study, we uncover a previously underrecognized role for PF4 in sterile inflammation, demonstrating that it drives TMJOA through NF‑κB pathway modulation. Consistent with this, PF4 has been shown to upregulate matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) via NF‑κB in periodontitis [40], and to promote inflammation via NF‑κB in intracerebral hemorrhage models [41]. Together, these findings establish PF4 as a potent regulator of NF‑κB signaling across diverse tissues, thereby modulating diverse inflammatory responses.

  PF4 has been classically studied for its angiostatic function via CXCR3B in rheumatoid arthritis [42], but more recent evidence implicates it in sterile inflammation through CXCR3-independent engagement of Toll-like receptors (TLRs). PF4 can trigger TLR9-dependent type I interferon (IFN‑I) production in plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) [43], and can form complexes with nucleic acids or heparin that initiate TLR‑driven inflammatory responses [44, 45]. Mechanical forces may also activate the TLR‑NF‑κB pathway to promote MMPs release [46]. Our findings extend this paradigm by demonstrating that mechanical overload may “unmask” or release PF4, which co‑opts the TLR2 pathway to convert a physical signal into a potent catabolic and pro‑senescent response, as evidenced by the suppression of COL2, induction of MMP13, and promotion of cellular senescence. This shifts the understanding of TMJOA pathogenesis from traditional concept of “low‑grade inflammation” toward a model in which abnormal mechanical forces trigger innate immune responses via TLR2.

  In addition, we observed that PF4–TLR2 signaling further upregulates BCL3, a known regulator of NF-κB, to facilitate cartilage dyshomeostasis. This finding suggests the activation of an intrinsic mechanism to fine-tune inflammatory output. BCL3 is known to exhibit dual roles in inflammation and immunity [47]. Previous studies have primarily described BCL3 as a negative regulator in acute sterile inflammation [48]. In contrast, our findings suggest that upregulated Bcl3 may exert a pro‑inflammatory function in overload-associated TMJOA. This regulatory pattern aligns with previous study by Franzoso et al., who demonstrated that BCL3 can neutralize the inhibitory influence on p50/NF‑κB homodimers to promote κB‑site‑dependent transcription [49]. Further studies have revealed a specific mechanism whereby BCL3 removes repressive p50 homodimers from NF‑κB binding sites, permitting the connection of NF-κB heterodimers with typical signaling (p65/p50) to promote transcription at these sites [50]. Thus, when BCL3 is inhibited, p50 homodimers sustain repression and delay p65 phosphorylation—aligning with our kinetic data.

  Alternatively, BCL3 has also been shown to directly transactivate NF‑κB‑dependent transcription through its N‑ and C‑terminal domains by binding p50 and p52 homodimers, or by recruiting co‑activators to target gene promoters [51, 52]. Specifically, Bours et al. demonstrated that BCL3 directly transactivates target genes through κB motifs by associating with DNA‑binding p50 homodimers [53]. Moreover, BCL3 can recruit co‑activators such as Tip60, TORC3, and p300 to NF‑κB binding sites [54, 55], remodeling local chromatin and increasing κB site accessibility for p65/p50 heterodimers, thereby indirectly facilitating p65 phosphorylation. While our study did not fully delineate the precise mechanism by which BCL3 modulates the canonical NF‑κB pathway in this setting, it is plausible that BCL3 accelerates—rather than directly executes—p65 phosphorylation through these convergent mechanisms. This suggests a potential role for BCL3 as a key regulator of acute NF‑κB activation in determining the rate of OA disease progression.

  Our study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Firstly, the clinical correlation was established in a relatively small patient cohort, and baseline data on disease duration were unavailable. Given the small sample size and limited statistical power, these clinical findings should be interpreted with due caution. We therefore acknowledge that larger prospective studies with comprehensive clinical characterization are warranted to validate these findings. Secondly, further research is warranted to elucidate the mechanisms that regulate PF4 elevation in osteoarthritic chondrocytes under conditions of excessive mechanical stress. Thirdly, our mechanistic insights, particularly concerning the BCL3-mediated mechanism, are primarily derived from rodent cells and a single animal model. Further validation in human samples and more complex in vivo systems would strengthen the translational relevance of these findings. Lastly, the therapeutic potential of targeting the PF4-TLR2 axis, although promising in our model, requires extensive preclinical evaluation prior to future clinical testing to fully assess its efficacy and safety.

  In summary, our study identifies PF4 as a novel overload‑responsive cytokine that drives TMJOA progression by disrupting cartilage homeostasis via the TLR2–NF‑κB/BCL3 signaling axis. Targeted modulation of this pathway—by inhibiting PF4 activity, blocking TLR2 engagement, or dampening downstream NF‑κB/BCL3 signaling — represents a promising therapeutic strategy to prevent or slow cartilage degeneration in mechanical overload‑induced TMJOA.


## Conclusions
  We have delineated a novel mechanotransduction pathway in TMJOA, in which overload-induced PF4 functions as an alarmin that activates the canonical NF-κB cascade via TLR2 signaling. This process is further amplified by the downstream regulator BCL3, ultimately leading to the disruption of cartilage homeostasis. Our findings thus establish the PF4-TLR2-NF-κB axis as a critical pathogenic driver and a promising therapeutic target for overload-related TMJ degeneration.
