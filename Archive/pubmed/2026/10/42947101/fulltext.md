# Identification and validation of candidate biomarkers associated with mitochondrial metabolism in diabetic foot


## Background
  Although the precise regulatory mechanisms underlying diabetic foot (DF) remain incompletely understood, increasing evidence suggests that mitochondrial metabolism may be involved in DF progression. The study aimed to identify and validate candidate biomarkers associated with mitochondrial metabolism in DF.


## Methods
  Transcriptome sequencing data from 12 DF and 18 control tissue samples were analyzed together with 1,234 mitochondrial metabolism-related genes (MMRGs), which were obtained from MSigDB by integrating curated gene sets directly associated with mitochondrial metabolic processes. First, overlapping genes between MMRGs and DEGs between DF and control samples were used as candidate genes. Then, candidate biomarkers were identified using PPI network construction, two machine learning algorithms, and ROC curve analysis. Next, based on the candidate biomarkers, functional enrichment, immune infiltration, regulatory network, compound prediction, as well as molecular docking analyses were undertaken. Finally, to explore the expression of candidate biomarkers in clinical samples, the reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was performed.


## Results
  GPAT3 and PTGS2 were identified as mitochondrial metabolism-associated candidate biomarkers for DF, with area under the curve values of 0.963 and 0.958, respectively. Functional enrichment analysis showed that multiple pathways were significantly enriched by both GPAT3 and PTGS2, such as ribosome and Leishmania infection. GPAT3 had the strongest positive and negative correlations with neutrophils and resting CD4 memory T cells, respectively. In contrast, PTGS2 exhibited the strongest negative connection with M1 macrophages and the strongest positive correlation with neutrophils. Subsequently, multiple transcription factors (TFs) were found to co-target GPAT3 and PTGS2, such as BRD4 and NR0B1. Moreover, compound prediction analysis identified several compounds potentially associated with these biomarkers, such as lipopolysaccharides and cisplatin. The binding free energy between PTGS2 and tetradecanoylphorbol acetate was − 8.2 kcal/mol, while the binding free energy between GPAT3 and cyclosporine was − 10.4 kcal/mol, according to molecular docking data. Finally, the expression levels of GPAT3 and PTGS2 were significantly different in clinical samples.


## Conclusion
  GPAT3 and PTGS2 were identified as candidate biomarkers associated with mitochondrial metabolism in DF, providing molecular clues for further investigation of DF pathogenesis and potential intervention strategies.

## Introduction
  Diabetic foot (DF), a debilitating complication of diabetes mellitus, is characterized by chronic ulceration, infection, and progressive tissue destruction, frequently culminating in lower extremity amputation when left unmanaged. Globally, DF affects 15 to 25% of individuals with diabetes, imposing substantial economic burdens and profoundly diminishing patients’ quality of life due to prolonged hospitalization, disability, and elevated mortality rates [1, 2]. The pathophysiology of DF arises from a complex interplay of peripheral neuropathy, vascular insufficiency, and dysregulated immune responses, while current diagnostic and therapeutic approaches often fail to target the underlying molecular drivers [3, 4]. Consequently, the identification of novel candidate biomarkers for early detection and precision therapeutics remains an urgent unmet need.

  Mitochondrial metabolism serves as a central regulator of cellular energy homeostasis, redox balance, and programmed cell death, and its dysfunction is implicated in a spectrum of metabolic disorders, including diabetes and its complications [5, 6]. Accumulating evidence indicates that mitochondrial dysregulation may exacerbate DF progression by amplifying oxidative stress, impairing tissue repair, and sustaining chronic low-grade inflammation [7, 8]. Beyond these canonical mechanisms, mitochondrial metabolic perturbations are increasingly recognized as upstream drivers of interconnected pathological cascades in DF. Impaired mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation disrupts lipid homeostasis and promotes aberrant triacylglycerol accumulation, further amplifying inflammatory signaling [9]. Concurrently, mitochondrial dysfunction compromises ribosomal biogenesis and translational fidelity, undermining the protein synthesis machinery essential for wound repair and growth factor production [10]. Furthermore, dysregulated mitochondrial metabolism sustains a pro-inflammatory immune microenvironment dominated by excessive neutrophil activation while impairing macrophage-mediated inflammation resolution [11]. Nevertheless, the precise mechanistic links between mitochondrial metabolic perturbations and DF pathogenesis remain insufficiently defined. Previous bioinformatics studies of DF have mainly focused on general differentially expressed genes or specific pathological processes, including immune infiltration, inflammatory responses, and oxidative stress [12–13]. In contrast, mitochondrial metabolism has been less frequently investigated in an integrative framework as a potential upstream factor connecting metabolic imbalance, impaired tissue repair, and immune-inflammatory remodeling in DF. Given the recognized involvement of mitochondrial dysfunction in diabetic wound healing, systematic analysis of mitochondrial metabolism-related genes may provide additional insight into DF pathogenesis and help identify mechanism-oriented biomarkers.

  Building on this framework, the present study employed an integrative bioinformatics strategy combining transcriptome sequencing with multi-dimensional computational analyses to identify and validate mitochondrial metabolism-associated candidate biomarkers in DF. Leveraging transcriptome sequencing data from 12 disease specimens and 18 control specimens, we conducted differential expression analysis, protein-protein interaction (PPI) network reconstruction, and machine learning-based feature selection to pinpoint pivotal genes. Subsequent functional enrichment, immune landscape profiling, regulatory network mapping, and computational drug prediction analyses were performed to explore the potential associations among mitochondrial metabolic dysregulation, lipid metabolic imbalance, ribosomal dysfunction, and neutrophil-dominated immune perturbation in DF. This study aimed to provide molecular clues and candidate targets for future mechanistic and translational studies of DF.


## Materials and methods

### Data collection
  This study prospectively recruited patients with diabetic foot ulcers and non-DF control participants from the Second Hospital of Shanxi Medical University between 1 June 2025 and 31 December 2025. Diabetic foot ulcer tissue samples were collected from the wound margins of DF patients during debridement procedures performed as part of standard clinical care. Control skin tissue samples were obtained from macroscopically normal skin tissues of non-DF control participants. Necrotic, infected, ulcerated, or visibly inflamed tissues were excluded from sampling. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. After RNA quality assessment, a total of 12 DF tissue samples and 18 control skin tissue samples met the sequencing criteria and were included in the transcriptome sequencing analysis. Detailed clinical and demographic characteristics of the study participants are summarized in Table 1. The raw transcriptome sequencing data generated in this study have been deposited in the Genome Sequence Archive for Human (GSA-Human) at the National Genomics Data Center (NGDC), China National Center for Bioinformation, under accession number HRA018406. The dataset is publicly accessible at https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa-human/browse/HRA018406. The mRNA sequences of GPAT3 and PTGS2 analyzed in this study were retrieved from NCBI GenBank under accession numbers NM_032717.5 (GPAT3) and NM_000963.4 (PTGS2), respectively. The corresponding protein sequences were retrieved from the UniProt Knowledgebase (UniProtKB) under accession numbers Q53EU6 (GPAT3) and P35354 (PTGS2). Furthermore, 1,234 mitochondrial metabolism-related genes (MMRGs) were obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb, MSigDB). These MMRGs were defined based on curated MSigDB gene sets related to mitochondrial metabolic processes, including energy metabolism, lipid metabolism, redox metabolism, amino acid catabolism, and mitochondrial biogenesis, with a focus on metabolic rather than general mitochondrial functional annotations (Supplementary Table 1).

### Transcriptome sequencing and data preprocessing
  Total RNA was isolated and purified using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA) according to the established protocol. The extracted RNA subsequently underwent rigorous integrity testing and quality control, which included verification via agarose gel electrophoresis. Samples meeting the specific criteria of concentrations exceeding 50 ng/µL, an OD260/280 ratio greater than 1.8, an RNA integrity number above 7.0, and a total RNA amount exceeding 1 µg were selected for further analysis. Messenger RNAs were captured using polyadenylate oligonucleotides and subsequently fragmented utilizing the Magnesium RNA Fragmentation Module (NEB, cat.e6150, USA). The fragmented mRNAs were reverse transcribed into complementary DNA. Following this, RNase H and Escherichia coli DNA polymerase I were employed to synthesize double-stranded cDNA. The sequencing library was constructed through magnetic bead purification, followed by junction ligation, poly-A tail addition, and polymerase chain reaction amplification. Finally, the library was sequenced on the Illumina platform utilizing a paired-end 150 sequencing strategy. Upon acquisition of the raw transcriptome sequencing data, quality control was executed using the fastp program (v0.22.0, https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp) to eliminate adapter sequences, duplicates, and low-quality reads. The preprocessed data were then aligned to the reference genome utilizing HISAT2 software (v2.2.0, https://ccb.jhu.edu/software/hisat2). Gene and transcript assembly, alongside quantification in fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads, were accomplished using StringTie software (v2.2.3, https://ccb.jhu.edu/software/stringtie/).


### Principal component analysis (PCA) and differential expression analysis
  PCA was conducted using the prcomp function within the stats package (v4.4.1, https://search.r-project.org/R/refmans/stats/html/stats-package.html) to examine the sample dispersion between the DF and control samples. Differential expression analysis was subsequently performed utilizing the DESeq2 package (v1.40.1) to identify differentially expressed genes between the two cohorts. The specific screening thresholds were set at |log2FC| > 2 and adj.P < 0.05. Visualizations of these differentially expressed genes, including volcano plots and heatmaps, were generated using the ggplot2 (v3.4.1) and ComplexHeatmap (v2.20.0) packages, respectively.


### Identification and functional enrichment of candidate genes
  Genes intersecting between MMRGs and the identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were designated as potential candidate genes. A Venn diagram illustrating this intersection was plotted using the ggvenn package (v0.1.10, https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=ggvenn). Following candidate identification, Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analyses were performed using the clusterProfiler package (v4.7.1.3), applying an adjusted P-value threshold of less than 0.05. These analyses were conducted to elucidate the specific biological processes, cellular components, molecular functions, and signaling pathways in which these candidate genes might be implicated during DF pathogenesis.


### PPI network construction
  To elucidate gene interactions at the protein level, the candidate genes were mapped to the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) to construct a protein-protein interaction network with a minimum required interaction confidence score of 0.4. Homo sapiens was selected as the reference organism. The resulting network was visualized utilizing Cytoscape software (v3.9.1) [14]. Subsequently, the MCC, Degree, and EPC algorithms in the CytoHubba plugin were used to rank genes in the PPI network from different topological perspectives [15]. Referring to previous hub-gene screening studies using CytoHubba to select the top 30 hub genes [16], the MCC, Degree, and EPC algorithms were applied to rank genes in the PPI network from different topological perspectives. The top 30 genes identified by each algorithm were selected for further intersection analysis, which helped retain topologically important genes while reducing the potential bias of a single algorithm.


### Machine learning and identification of candidate biomarkers
  Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis was conducted using the glmnet package (v4.1.4) based on the identified key candidate genes, with parameters set to standardize = True, alpha = 1, family = binomial, and nfolds = 10. A preliminary set of feature genes was determined at the minimum lambda value where the respective gene coefficients were not penalized to 0. Concurrently, support vector machine-recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) analysis was executed via the caret package (v6.0.93), selecting a second set of feature genes at the point of maximum model accuracy. The intersection of these two feature gene sets ultimately defined the final key genes, which was visualized using a Venn diagram generated by the ggvenn package (v0.1.10). To evaluate diagnostic performance, receiver operating characteristic curves were plotted for these key genes utilizing the pROC package (v1.18.5). Genes exhibiting an area under the curve value greater than 0.7 were established as candidate biomarkers, as this threshold indicates a favorable diagnostic capability for DF. To further evaluate the robustness of these candidate biomarkers, two independent external datasets, GSE199939 and GSE134431, were used for validation. ROC curve analysis was performed to assess the discriminative ability of candidate biomarkers between DF and control samples in the external validation datasets.


### Enrichment analysis and construction of gene-gene interaction (GGI) network
  Gene set enrichment analysis was performed across all transcriptome sequencing samples to further investigate the specific signaling pathways involving the identified candidate biomarkers. Initially, Spearman correlation coefficients between each biomarker and all other genes within the dataset were computed using the psych package (v0.92). The entire gene set was subsequently ranked in descending order based on these correlation values. The clusterProfiler package (v4.7.13) was then employed to execute the gene set enrichment analysis, applying screening thresholds of P < 0.05, absolute normalized enrichment score > 1, and false discovery rate < 0.25. The background gene set (c2.cp.kegg.v7.4.symbols.gmt) was sourced from the Molecular Signatures Database (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/). The top five significantly enriched pathways, ranked by the lowest P-values, were visualized utilizing the enrichplot package (v1.18.3). Finally, a gene-gene interaction (GGI) network was constructed via the GeneMANIA database (http://www.genemania.org/) to elucidate the functional relationships and mechanistic roles between the candidate biomarkers and their associated genes.


### Chromosomal localization and subcellular localization
  The chromosomal positions of the identified candidate biomarkers were determined utilizing the RCircos package (v1.2.2). For subcellular localization analysis, the mRNA sequences of candidate biomarkers in FASTA format were retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). These sequences were subsequently analyzed using the mRNALocater online tool (http://bio-bigdata.cn/mRNALocater/) to estimate the predicted subcellular distribution of each biomarker. The resulting localization patterns were visualized via a bar chart generated by the ggplot2 package (v3.4.1).


### Immune infiltration analysis
  The infiltration abundance of 22 immune cells was assessed in DF and control samples using the CIBERSORT algorithm. The ggplot2 package (v3.4.1) was applied to create the stacked chart, which showed the immune cell infiltration abundance. After calculating the degree of immune cell infiltration, immune cells that were present in at least half of the samples were kept. The difference in immune cell infiltration abundance between DF and control samples was next examined through the Wilcoxon test (P < 0.05). To illustrate the results, a box plot was created by the ggplot2 package (version 3.4.1). The psych package (v0.92) was then employed to perform Spearman correlation studies between differential immune cells as well as between differential immune cells and candidate biomarkers (|cor| > 0.3, P < 0.05). The corrplot package (v0.92) was used to plot the heat map to present the results.


### Regulatory network analysis
  To explore the potential regulatory mechanisms of the candidate biomarkers, regulatory network analysis was performed. First, microRNAs (miRNAs) targeting candidate biomarkers were predicted through Pictar algorithm for the identification of microrna targets (PicTar) database (https://pictar.mdc-berlin.de/) and TargetScan database (http://www.targetscan.org/), respectively. The intersecting miRNAs predicted by the 2 databases were used as key miRNAs through ggvenn package (v0.1.10). Then, based on the Cistrome Data Browser (DB) database (http://cistrome.org/db/), the transcription factors (TFs) targeting candidate biomarkers were predicted. The TF-mRNA-key miRNA regulatory network was then constructed using TFs with RP larger than 0.5, and Cytoscape package (v3.9.1) was employed to visualize the results.


### Compound prediction and molecular docking
  The Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD; http://ctdbase.org/) was used to explore chemical-gene interactions involving the identified biomarkers. The resulting compound-biomarker network was constructed and visualized using Cytoscape software (v3.9.1). Compounds with high interaction scores were selected for molecular docking analysis to preliminarily assess potential binding interactions, Specifically, the 3D chemical structures of the active components of pharmaceuticals were gathered from the PubChem database (http://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) and the Drugbank database (https://go.drugbank.com). The 3D protein structures of the candidate biomarkers were obtained from the Protein Data Bank database (https://www.rcsb.org/). Finally, molecular docking simulations were executed utilizing the CB-Dock2 web server (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2/index.php), and the resulting interaction conformations were visualized via PyMOL software (v2.5).


### Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)
  Total RNA was extracted from 8 frozen diabetic foot tissue specimens and 8 control tissue specimens using TRIzol reagent. The RNA concentration and purity were subsequently assessed utilizing a NanoPhotometer N50 instrument. Subsequently, reverse transcription was performed following the protocol of the HP All-in-one qRT Master Mix II RT203-Ver.1 Kit (Kunming Yungen Biotechnology Co., Ltd). Following cDNA synthesis, real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction analysis was conducted on a CFX96 real-time PCR detection system. The specific primer sequences utilized in this assay are detailed in Supplementary Table 2. Based on its common use as a housekeeping gene in previous DFU-related RT-qPCR studies, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as the internal reference gene for normalization [17]. Relative expression was calculated using the standard comparative Ct method (2−ΔΔCt), with the control group used as the calibrator. Statistical plotting and significance calculations were performed utilizing GraphPad Prism 10 software.


### Statistical analysis
  All statistical analyses were conducted utilizing R software. Differences between the 2 groups were compared using the Wilcoxon test (P < 0.05). In the RT-qPCR investigations, the t-test was implemented to compare the differences between the 2 groups (P < 0.05).


## Results

### The 174 candidate genes were determined
  The PCA results demonstrated a clear distinction between the DF and control samples, indicating robust dataset quality (Fig. 1A). Subsequently, 2,468 DEGs were identified between DF and control samples, including 1,638 up-regulated genes and 830 down-regulated genes in DF samples. Ranked by the |log2FC| from highest to smallest, the top 10 up-regulated and down-regulated DEGs were labeled and presented in the volcano plot and heat map, respectively (Fig. 1B-C). After that, the 2,468 DEGs were intersected with 1,234 MMRGs, and 174 candidate genes were obtained (Fig. 1D). These candidate genes therefore represented DF-related differentially expressed genes with mitochondrial metabolism-related annotations and were used for subsequent biomarker screening.


### Candidate genes were associated with multiple pathways
  The 529 Gene Ontology terms (372 biological processes, 16 cellular components, and 141 molecular functions) and 38 Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways, respectively, showed substantial enrichment in the candidate genes. Focusing on the most prominent functional categories, the top three terms with the highest gene counts in each ontology group were selected for detailed examination. These prominent terms encompassed mechanisms such as fatty acid metabolic process, lipid droplet localization, and acyltransferase activity (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table 3). KEGG analysis displayed the top 10 pathways with the greatest number of enriched genes, such as glycerolipid metabolism and arachidonic acid metabolism (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Table 4). The convergence of these metabolic pathways suggests their potential involvement in DF-related pathological changes.


### Identification of GPAT3 and PTGS2 as candidate biomarkers
  A PPI network was constructed, in which genes such as PTGS2 and DGAT1 had more interactions with other genes (Fig. 3A). Subsequently, the top 30 genes ranked by 3 algorithms were intersected, and 23 key candidate genes were obtained (Fig. 3B-C). This refined gene set was subsequently subjected to dual machine learning frameworks for precise biomarker feature selection. Through least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression analysis, six primary feature genes, namely CD36, PLIN2, GPAT3, PTGS2, CYP7A1, and HELZ2, were identified at an optimal minimal lambda value of 0.0408 (Fig. 3D-E). Parallelly, support vector machine-recursive feature elimination analysis maximized model accuracy by selecting a secondary set of five feature genes, including GPAT3, PTGS2, ACSL1, DHCR24, and CYP2E1 (Fig. 3F). The cross-validation and intersection of these two distinct algorithmic outputs isolated GPAT3 and PTGS2 as the ultimate robust key genes (Fig. 3G). To assess their clinical diagnostic efficacy across the entire transcriptome sequencing cohort, receiver operating characteristic curve analysis was performed. Both GPAT3 and PTGS2 showed favorable discriminative performance, with AUC values of 0.963 and 0.958, respectively, supporting their potential value as candidate diagnostic biomarkers for DF (Fig. 3H). To further assess the robustness of GPAT3 and PTGS2, external validation was performed using GSE199939 and GSE134431. In GSE199939, the AUC values of GPAT3 and PTGS2 were 0.709 and 0.718, respectively (Fig. 3I). In GSE134431, the corresponding AUC values were 0.729 and 0.792, respectively (Fig. 3J). These external validation results further supported the discriminative potential of GPAT3 and PTGS2 and reduced concerns regarding dataset-specific overfitting.


### Pathways and functions associated with candidate biomarkers were explored
  Through enrichment analysis, GPAT3 and PTGS2 significantly enriched 62 and 79 pathways respectively. The top 5 pathways significantly enriched by GPAT3 and PTGS2 were presented respectively, ranked by P-value from smallest to largest. The pathways significantly enriched by GPAT3 included Leishmania infection, graft-versus-host disease (GVHD), etc. (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Table 5). The pathways significantly enriched by PTGS2 included chemokine signaling pathway, killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity, etc. (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Table 6). Notably, ribosome and Leishmania infection were co-enriched by GPAT3 and PTGS2. In addition, in the constructed GGI network, the functions of genes such as PTGIS and TBXAS1 were similar to those of these candidate biomarkers. The functions they were involved in included unsaturated fatty acid biosynthetic process, eicosanoid biosynthetic process, and so on (Fig. 4C). Moreover, it was found that PTGS2 and GPAT3 were located on chromosome 1 and chromosome 4 respectively (Fig. 4D). Meanwhile, GPAT3 had the highest percentage in nucleus, while PTGS2 had the highest percentage in cytoplasm (Fig. 4E). These analyses provided additional information for interpreting the potential functional context of GPAT3 and PTGS2 in DF-related molecular changes.


### Correlations between immune cell infiltration and candidate biomarkers
  Figure 5A displayed the infiltration abundances of 22 immune cells in the DF and control samples. The 10 immune cells were retained after removing immune cells without infiltration levels in at least half of the samples. Following this, it was discovered that there was a clear difference in the infiltration abundances of 6 immune cells between the DF and control samples (P < 0.05). Specifically, the DF samples exhibited elevated infiltration levels of neutrophils and naive B cells, whereas the control samples presented higher proportions of M0, M1, and M2 macrophages, alongside resting CD4 memory T cells (Fig. 5B). M1 macrophages exhibited the largest negative connection with neutrophils (cor = -0.78, P = 6.82 × 10− 6) and the strongest positive correlation with resting CD4 memory T cells (cor = 0.64, P = 1.70 × 10 − 3), according to Spearman correlation analysis (Fig. 5C, Supplementary Table 7). Furthermore, GPAT3 had the most positive connection with neutrophils (cor = 0.75, P = 2.23 × 10 − 6) and the strongest negative correlation with resting CD4 memory T cells (cor = -0.75, P = 1.78 × 10 − 6). According to Fig. 5D, Supplementary Table 8, PTGS2 exhibited the strongest negative connection with M1 macrophages (cor = -0.82, P = 3.49 × 10 − 8) and the strongest positive correlation with neutrophils (cor = 0.84, P = 4.37 × 10 − 9). These findings suggest that GPAT3 and PTGS2 may be associated with immune microenvironment remodeling in DF, particularly neutrophil-related inflammatory changes and macrophage-related immune imbalance.


### Candidate biomarkers were regulated by multiple molecules simultaneously
  The 22 and 29 miRNAs were predicted by the PicTar database and TargetScan database, respectively. Then, after taking the intersection of 22 miRNAs and 29 miRNAs, 8 key miRNAs were obtained (Fig. 6A). After that, 93 and 92 TFs targeting GPAT3 and PTGS2, respectively, were predicted. Finally, a TF-mRNA-key miRNA regulatory network was established. It is noteworthy that multiple TFs co-targeted GPAT3 and PTGS2, such as BRD4 and NR0B1 (Fig. 6B). These results provided candidate upstream regulatory molecules for GPAT3 and PTGS2, offering a basis for further exploration of their regulatory networks in DF.


### Compound-candidate biomarker interaction prediction and molecular docking analysis
  A total of 154 and 1,601 compounds were predicted to be associated with GPAT3 and PTGS2, respectively, among which 112 compounds were shared by both biomarkers. The top 30 compounds associated with each biomarker were selected to construct the compound-biomarker interaction network. Notably, lipopolysaccharides, cisplatin, and other compounds were identified in this network as predicted chemical interaction signals rather than direct therapeutic candidates for DF (Fig. 7A). The compounds with the highest interaction scores for GPAT3 and PTGS2 were then selected for molecular docking analysis. The docking results showed that GPAT3 and cyclosporine had a binding free energy of -10.4 kcal/mol, with binding via residue ARG-408 (Fig. 7B). Tetradecanoylphorbol acetate and PTGS2 had a binding free energy of -8.2 kcal/mol, with binding via residues ARG-376 and ASN-375 (Fig. 7C). These results suggested favorable predicted binding affinity between selected compounds and biomarkers, providing preliminary clues for future experimental validation.


### Validation of GPAT3 and PTGS2 expression
  In order to further verify the expression levels of GPAT3 and PTGS2, RT-qPCR analysis was performed. The results showed that the expression levels of GPAT3 and PTGS2 in the DF samples were significantly higher than those in the control samples (P < 0.05), and these results were consistent with the bioinformatics results (Fig. 8A and B). These results were consistent with the bioinformatics findings and supported GPAT3 and PTGS2 as candidate biomarkers for subsequent functional studies.


## Discussion
  This study employed integrated bioinformatics methods, combining external datasets and RT-qPCR validation, to identify GPAT3 and PTGS2 as candidate biomarkers related to mitochondrial metabolism in DF. This classification was primarily based on overlap screening of DF-related DEGs with the predefined MMRG, combined with PPI network analysis, machine learning-based feature selection, ROC curve analysis, and external validation. Furthermore, functional analysis revealed that these genes are associated with lipid metabolism, arachidic acid biosynthesis, immune inflammation regulation, and infection-related host response pathways, which may be related to the pathological changes related to mitochondrial metabolism in diabetic foot ulcers. RT-qPCR further confirmed their differential expression in DF tissues. In summary, these findings provide multidimensional evidence for GPAT3 and PTGS2 as candidate biomarkers, warranting further mechanistic investigations rather than simply experimental validation as mitochondrial metabolic regulators.

  Glycerol-3-Phosphate Acyltransferase 3, encoded by GPAT3, functions as a rate-limiting enzyme in triacylglycerol biosynthesis and has been previously implicated in metabolic derangements such as hepatic steatosis and insulin resistance [18]. Based on our bioinformatics analysis and the known role of GPAT3 in lipid metabolism, GPAT3 may be associated with lipid metabolism imbalance and mitochondrial stress-related alterations in diabetic foot (DF) [19, 20]. One possible explanation is that alterations in GPAT3 expression may affect triglyceride metabolism and lipotoxic stress, leading to oxidative stress and inflammatory responses in poorly healing diabetic foot wounds. This hypothesis needs further validation in experimental models related to diabetic foot.

  PTGS2, the gene encoding the inducible isoform of cyclooxygenase, is a well-established mediator of inflammatory cascades in malignancies and other chronic disorders [21, 22]. Its significant upregulation of PTGS2 in DF tissues observed in our study is consistent with the persistent pro-inflammatory environment typical of non-healing diabetic wounds. Mechanistically, mitochondrial dysfunction-derived ROS have been shown to directly activate NF-κB signaling, thereby transcriptionally upregulating PTGS2 expression; in turn, PTGS2-catalyzed prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) biosynthesis feeds back to suppress mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and amplify mitochondrial membrane depolarization. Therefore, PTGS2 upregulation may be associated with inflammatory activation and mitochondrial stress in DF tissue, suggesting a possible link between prostaglandin-mediated inflammation and mitochondrial dysfunction in diabetic non-healing wounds. Similar pathological patterns of PTGS2 overexpression have been documented in other inflammatory diseases, supporting its relevance as a candidate inflammatory mediator and warranting further investigation [23, 24]. Additionally, RT-qPCR validation in this study further confirmed the potential regulatory roles of these candidate biomarkers in DF.

  Functional enrichment analysis revealed that both GPAT3 and PTGS2 were significantly enriched in ribosome-related and Leishmania infection pathways. Ribosomal dysfunction has been increasingly implicated in diabetic complications, where impaired protein synthesis exacerbates cellular stress and disrupts critical repair mechanisms in peripheral tissues. Specifically, dysregulated ribosome activity may contribute to delayed wound healing in DF by perturbing the translation of growth factors and extracellular matrix components [10]. The enrichment of the Leishmania infection pathway should be interpreted cautiously. This finding does not indicate that Leishmania infection directly participates in DF pathogenesis. Instead, this KEGG pathway may reflect shared host-response features related to pathogen recognition, inflammatory activation, macrophage-associated immune regulation, and impaired pathogen clearance. These processes are relevant to the infection-prone and immunocompromised microenvironment of diabetic foot ulcers, in which immune dysfunction and microbial infection contribute to delayed wound healing and disease progression [25–27]. Further studies are required to determine whether these pathway-level signals have specific biological relevance in DF tissues. Collectively, these findings not only validate the robustness of our biomarker selection but also highlight the regulatory mechanisms of these pathways involving mitochondrial metabolism-related genes during DF progression. Furthermore, they provide relevant insights for DF treatment strategies.

  The targeted immune profiling revealed a pathological neutrophil-dominated microenvironment in DF, characterized by elevated neutrophilic infiltration (GPAT3: r = 0.75; PTGS2: r = 0.84) concurrent with M1 macrophage depletion (PTGS2: r = -0.82). These findings align with emerging evidence that sustained neutrophil activation exacerbates DF progression through NETosis-mediated tissue damage and impaired inflammation resolution [28]. The strong biomarker-neutrophil correlations suggest GPAT3 and PTGS2 may regulate neutrophil recruitment via lipid mediator production and prostaglandin signaling, respectively, while mitochondrial dysfunction perpetuates neutrophil persistence. This neutrophil-macrophage imbalance creates a self-sustaining pro-inflammatory circuit that hinders wound healing. In this study, the relative proportions of M0, M1, and M2 macrophages were lower in DF tissues than in control tissues. This finding should be interpreted in the context of CIBERSORT-based immune cell estimation, which reflects relative infiltration patterns rather than absolute immune cell counts [29]. One possible explanation is that the increased neutrophil-related inflammatory profile in DF tissues may proportionally reduce the estimated abundance of macrophage subsets. In addition, diabetic wounds are characterized by dysregulated macrophage recruitment, impaired macrophage polarization, and delayed transition from inflammation to tissue repair. Therefore, the reduced relative proportions of M0, M1, and M2 macrophages may reflect disturbed macrophage homeostasis and an imbalance between persistent neutrophil-driven inflammation and macrophage-mediated resolution/repair responses in DF tissues. Further validation using immunohistochemistry, flow cytometry, or single-cell analysis would help clarify the absolute abundance, spatial distribution, and functional states of macrophage subsets in DF.

  Computational drug mapping identified several compounds that may be associated with GPAT3 and PTGS2, and molecular docking further suggested possible binding interactions between some compound-candidate biomarker pairs. These results should be considered hypotheses rather than direct pharmacological evidence. For example, cyclosporine was predicted to interact with GPAT3, and previous studies have reported its effects on mitochondrial stability, inflammatory signaling, and neutrophil extracellular trap formation [30]. However, its relevance to DF treatment still requires specialized pharmacological experiments, dose-response assessments, and safety evaluations in appropriate DF models.

  Compared with previous studies that mainly focused on a single pathological axis, this study integrated mitochondrial metabolism-related genes, immune microenvironment analysis, regulatory network prediction, and in silico compound screening into a unified analysis framework [9]. This integrated strategy provides a broader perspective for understanding DF-related molecular changes and highlights GPAT3 and PTGS2 as candidate molecules linking mitochondrial metabolism-related changes with immune-inflammatory remodeling [22]. These findings may help guide subsequent experimental studies and biomarker validation in larger clinical cohorts.While our study provides substantive mechanistic insights, certain limitations warrant acknowledgment. Although GPAT3 and PTGS2 were further validated on two external datasets, the sample size of the transcriptome sequencing cohort remains relatively small, necessitating a larger, multi-center cohort to further evaluate their diagnostic value. Furthermore, current findings are primarily based on bioinformatics analysis and RT-qPCR expression validation. Moreover, while GAPDH was used as an internal control gene in previous RT-qPCR studies related to DFU, its stability was not independently assessed in this cohort. Future research utilizing cell and animal models associated with diabetic foot ulcers, combined with more rigorous RT-qPCR standardization strategies, will help elucidate the biological roles of GPAT3 and PTGS2 in mitochondrial function, inflammation activation, and wound repair.


## Conclusion
  This study, using an integrative bioinformatics framework combined with RT-qPCR validation, identified GPAT3 and PTGS2 as candidate biomarkers related to mitochondrial metabolism in DF. These genes are differentially expressed in DF tissues and are associated with specific biological pathways, alterations in immune inflammation, regulatory networks, and interactions with predicted compounds. These findings provide molecular clues for understanding changes in mitochondrial metabolism in DF and offer candidate molecules for future mechanistic and translational research. Further functional experiments and larger-scale clinical cohort studies will help elucidate their biological functions and biomarker value.
