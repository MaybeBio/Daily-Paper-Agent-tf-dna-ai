# Position-Dependent
Activation and Repression by As(III)
in an Engineered BetI Transcriptional Repressor


## Abstract
  Arsenite [As­(III)]-responsive transcriptional regulation is widely employed by microorganisms for detoxification and homeostasis. Engineering As­(III)-responsive behavior into transcription factors that are intrinsically metal-insensitive remains challenging. Here, we introduced a third cysteine residue at different positions in the engineered BetIL79C/Q81C scaffold and evaluated the resulting whole-cell sfGFP reporter responses. The magnitude and direction of the As­(III)-dependent reporter change varied with the position of the introduced cysteine. Under combined As­(III) and choline inputs, selected variants exhibited operational OR-like or As­(III)-inhibited reporter-output patterns over defined concentration ranges. To explore possible structural interpretations for these reporter phenotypes, we performed molecular dynamics simulations using predefined dithiol- and trithiol-coordination models. Simulations of these models yielded distinct fluctuation patterns in the DNA-binding domain, providing testable structural hypotheses for the observed reporter phenotypes. Because neither As­(III) binding to the engineered cysteine sites nor As­(III)-dependent changes in BetI–DNA binding were directly measured, the reporter assays and simulations do not establish the underlying molecular mechanism. Nevertheless, our experiments show that cysteine placement can tune the direction of As­(III)-dependent whole-cell sfGFP output in an engineered BetI regulatory system and thereby expand the design space for multi-input transcriptional devices.


## Introduction
  Arsenic is a ubiquitous environmental toxin, and chronic exposure to arsenic poses serious risks to human health and ecosystems. To cope with arsenic stress, many microorganisms have evolved transcriptional regulatory systems that sense and respond to arsenic. A representative example is ArsR, a member of the SmtB/ArsR family of metalloregulatory transcriptional repressors, which represses the expression of the ars operon in the absence of arsenite (As­(III)). In the presence of As­(III), however, binding of As­(III) to ArsR induces a conformational change that leads to dissociation of ArsR from DNA, resulting in activation of detoxification-related gene expression. Recent evolutionary studies of ArsR/SmtB family members have revealed that individual proteins have evolved distinct metal coordination sites and that ArsR proteins possess a variety of cysteine-containing metal-binding sites located at different positions within the protein. For instance, Escherichia coli ArsR has an As­(III)-binding motif consisting of Cys32-Val-Cys34-Asp-Leu-Cys37, whereas the As­(III)- binding site in Corynebacterium glutamicum CgArsR is composed of Cys15, Cys16, and Cys55.

  This diversity of cysteine-containing metal-binding sites reflects the coordination chemistry of As­(III), which preferentially adopts trigonal coordination geometry and binds strongly to dithiol and trithiol sites in proteins with dissociation constants in the micromolar range (K d  = 1–20 μM). Because of this property, As­(III) responsiveness can be introduced into proteins that naturally lack cysteine residues by engineering appropriate cysteine-containing binding sites. Indeed, our previous study has shown that introduction of cysteine residues into a cysteine-free transcriptional repressor can confer As­(III) responsiveness by creating an artificial As­(III)-binding site. ,  In general, binding of As­(III) to proteins often perturbs their native structures and leads to loss of biological functions. Interestingly, however, arsenic trioxide has been reported to rescue the function of structurally destabilized p53 mutants by binding to a cryptic allosteric site, thereby restoring protein stability and transcriptional activity. These studies indicate that the functional consequences of As­(III) binding depend critically on the local structural environment of its coordination site.

  Rationally engineering As­(III) responsiveness into transcription factors that are intrinsically metal-insensitive remains challenging, and general design principles for such systems have not yet been fully established. Overcoming this challenge would not only expand the repertoire of As­(III)-responsive proteins but also enable the design of regulatory behaviors that are difficult to achieve in natural systems, such as tunable sensitivity, inversion of response direction, and integration of multiple chemical inputs into sophisticated transcriptional control. Here, we investigated this issue using BetI, a TetR-family transcriptional repressor that functions as a homodimer and regulates the bet operon in response to environmental signals including choline, oxygen, and osmotic stress. −    Importantly, BetI contains no cysteine residues and does not naturally function as an As­(III)-responsive transcriptional regulator, making it a useful scaffold for engineering metal-responsive behavior through site-specific cysteine introduction. In our previous study, ,  the BetIL79C/Q81C/S102C variant exhibited an As­(III)-dependent increase in whole-cell reporter output, and computational analysis suggested that L79C and Q81C could form a putative dithiol-coordination motif. Building on this observation, the present study examined whether introduction of a third cysteine at different positions in the L79C/Q81C scaffold alters the magnitude or direction of the As­(III)-dependent reporter response (Figure  a). Molecular dynamics simulations were used to compare predefined dithiol- and trithiol-coordination models and to generate possible structural hypotheses for the reporter phenotype.


## Results and Discussion

### Position-Dependent
As­(III) Responsiveness
Induced by Cysteine Substitution in BetI
  In our previous study, we showed that introduction of three cysteine residues (L79C, Q81C, and S102C) into BetI confers As­(III) responsiveness. In this mutant, L79C and Q81C, located in the reverse turn connecting helices α4 and α5, were suggested to form an engineered dithiol As­(III)-binding site that promotes dissociation of BetI from DNA through As­(III)-induced rearrangement of helices α4 and α5. ,  In the present study, we examined whether introducing an additional cysteine residue near this engineered dithiol site could alter the arsenic responsiveness of BetI by modulating its functional conformation. To test this idea, we generated a series of BetIcys79,81,X mutants, in which an additional cysteine residue was introduced at position X. The substitution residues were located on helix α4 (V72, L73, N74, R75, L76, H77, and A78), the adjacent turn region (S83 and A84), helix α5 (E85, Q86, R87, L88, Q89, A90, and I91), and at the tip of helix α7 (R143) (Figure  b).

  Figure  c shows the transcriptional responsiveness of the BetIcys79,81,X mutants in the absence or presence of 10 ppm (130 μM) As­(III), evaluated using sfGFP fluorescence driven by the betO promoter as a reporter. Comparison of leaky sfGFP expression among the BetIcys79,81,X mutants in the absence of As­(III) revealed that the degree of leakage was not uniform, even when adjacent residues within the same helix were substituted with cysteine. These results show that basal reporter output varied with the position of cysteine substitution. FoldX calculations further predicted position-dependent differences in ΔΔG values, with an approximate helical periodicity and an overall correspondence with leaky reporter expression.

  Among the mutants with relatively small predicted destabilization, BetIcys79,81,89 showed particularly strong As­(III) responsiveness. In the absence of As­(III), this mutant exhibited markedly reduced leaky expressionless than half of that observed for the previously reported BetIcys79,81,102. ,  Upon As­(III) addition, sfGFP fluorescence increased by 8.3-fold relative to the untreated condition, resulting in an exceptionally high ON/OFF ratio (Figure  c). In contrast, introduction of Q86C produced an opposite response. As­(III) addition decreased reporter output in BetIcys79,81,86, indicating a reversal in the direction of the whole-cell reporter response. This decrease is consistent with increased net transcriptional repression at the reporter level. However, the reporter assay does not directly demonstrate that the decrease results from enhanced BetI–DNA binding, and its underlying molecular basis remains unresolved.


### Position-Dependent Logic-Like Responses to
Choline and As­(III) in BetI Mutants
  To investigate how the BetI mutants that exhibited opposite As­(III)-dependent reporter responses, BetIcys79,81,89 and BetIcys79,81,86, integrate signals from As­(III) and the native ligand choline, we measured sfGFP fluorescence output at various concentrations of As­(III) and choline. As a reference, we used the cysteine-free BetII22T variant, which was previously obtained through evolutionary engineering and exhibits enhanced sensitivity and output toward choline. The reference BetII22T variant displayed high reporter expression only in the presence of choline and did not show a clear As­(III)-dependent response under the tested conditions (Figure  a).

  In contrast, BetIcys79,81,89 showed a marked As­(III)-dependent increase in fluorescence output in the absence of choline (Figure  b). When choline was added, this mutant retained choline-dependent reporter activation while also showing As­(III)-dependent activation at low-to-intermediate choline concentrations. At 100 mM choline, however, As­(III)-dependent activation was no longer clearly observed, most likely because reporter expression was already strongly induced by choline-dependent derepression. These results indicate that BetIcys79,81,89 can transmit either As­(III) or choline input into increased reporter output, consistent with an OR-like reporter-output pattern under defined concentration ranges.

  BetIcys79,81,86 exhibited a distinct response pattern (Figure  c). Choline alone induced sfGFP expression in a concentration-dependent manner, indicating that this mutant retained responsiveness to the native ligand. However, As­(III) addition reduced reporter output at low choline concentrations and also suppressed choline-induced reporter expression at 1 mM choline. At higher choline concentrations, particularly 10 and 100 mM, this inhibitory effect of As­(III) became less pronounced. At the reporter-output level, the effect of As­(III) opposed the choline-dependent increase under low-choline conditions. The present assay does not distinguish whether this pattern arises from altered BetI–DNA affinity, changes in intracellular BetI abundance or stability, or other cellular processes. Thus, BetIcys79,81,86 is best described as exhibiting an As­(III)-inhibited choline response, or an inhibitory logic-like reporter-output pattern, under low-choline conditions.

  To make these logic-like responses more transparent, we summarized the apparent As­(III) response thresholds and the operational truth-table-style reporter-output patterns in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2. The apparent response threshold was defined as the lowest tested As­(III) concentration that produced a statistically significant change in fluorescence/OD600 compared with the corresponding As­(III)-untreated condition under the same choline concentration. This analysis showed that BetIcys79,81,89 exhibited significant As­(III)-dependent activation at 2 and 10 ppm As­(III) under 0 and 1 mM choline conditions, and at 10 ppm As­(III) under the 10 mM choline condition (Supplementary Table S1). The corresponding operational truth-table-style summary supports an OR-like reporter-output pattern for BetIcys79,81,89 within this low-to-intermediate choline concentration range (Supplementary Table S2). By contrast, BetIcys79,81,86 showed significant As­(III)-dependent reduction of reporter output mainly under low-choline conditions, including 0 and 1 mM choline (Supplementary Table S1). The operational summary for BetIcys79,81,86 is therefore most consistent with an As­(III)-inhibited choline response or inhibitory logic-like reporter-output pattern under low-choline conditions (Supplementary Table S2). These logic-gate descriptions should be interpreted as operational classifications of whole-cell reporter outputs under the tested conditions, rather than as direct biochemical logic of BetI–DNA binding.

  Because fluorescence values were normalized by OD600, we further examined whether the observed changes in fluorescence/OD600 could be explained by As­(III)-dependent effects on cell growth or sfGFP fluorescence independent of BetI-mediated regulation. For the cysteine-free BetI reference variant and the key cysteine-engineered mutants, BetIcys79,81,86 and BetIcys79,81,89, OD600 values remained largely comparable across the tested As­(III) concentration range, whereas the raw fluorescence values reflected the variant-dependent As­(III) responses observed in the normalized data (Supplementary Figure S1a,b). In addition, the pAC void vector control showed constitutively high reporter expression with little ligand-dependent change, while BetII22T showed choline-dependent derepression but did not exhibit a clear As­(III)-dependent response (Supplementary Figure S1c,d). These control analyses support the interpretation that the As­(III)-dependent changes in fluorescence/OD600 observed for BetIcys79,81,86 and BetIcys79,81,89 are not primarily attributable to general effects of As­(III) on cell growth or sfGFP fluorescence.

  Under As­(III)-untreated conditions, BetIcys79,81,89 showed a choline-dependent response profile broadly similar to that of the cysteine-free BetII22T reference variant, whereas BetIcys79,81,86 retained choline responsiveness but exhibited a somewhat different response profile at low-to-intermediate choline concentrations. These results suggest that the position of the engineered cysteine residue may influence not only As­(III)-dependent regulation but also native choline-dependent allosteric regulation. Because only four choline concentrations were tested, however, the present data were not intended to determine precise apparent EC50 values for choline-induced reporter expression. A more detailed quantitative comparison of choline sensitivity will require dose–response measurements using more finely spaced choline concentrations.


### Structural
Dynamics of Predefined As­(III)
Coordination Models
  To investigate the structural effects of As­(III) binding on BetI, we conducted MD simulations of two mutants, BetIcys79,81,86 and BetIcys79,81,89, in both their As­(III)-bound and unbound forms. The As­(III)-bound models were constructed based on the distance distributions among the three cysteine S atoms observed in the As­(III)-unbound simulations. This analysis revealed that the three S atoms were frequently located close to one another (within 4 Å) in BetIcys79,81,86, suggesting the formation of a trithiol As­(III)-binding site. In contrast, in BetIcys79,81,89, the S atoms of L79C and Q81C remained in close proximity, whereas the S atom of Q89C was separated by approximately 6 Å or more (Supplementary Figure S2), consistent with a dithiol As­(III)-binding site. Based on these observations, we modeled the As­(III)-bound BetIcys79,81,86 structure with As­(III) coordinated by all three cysteines, whereas in BetIcys79,81,89 As­(III) was assumed to coordinate only with two cysteines of L79C and Q81C.

  Figure   shows the root-mean-square fluctuations (RMSF) of backbone atoms for the As­(III)-unbound and bound states of BetIcys79,81,86 and BetIcys79,81,89 (Figure  a,b). In both mutants, As­(III) binding leads to increased flexibility in the DNA-binding domain, with the increase being slightly more pronounced in the BetIcys79,81,89 mutant. Moreover, only in the BetIcys79,81,89 mutant, significant increases in fluctuations were observed at the As­(III)-binding site.

  The collective motions of the DNA-binding domain within the dimer were further analyzed using principal component analysis (PCA). PCA was performed on the backbone atoms of helices α2 and α3 (residues 32–49), independently for each of the four systems using the corresponding trajectory. Thus, the PC1 and PC2 axes were defined separately for each system and were used to characterize the dominant motions within each trajectory. Figure   shows a structural representation of the motions projected along the first two principal components, PC1 and PC2, in the As­(III)-unbound and bound states of BetIcys79,81,86 and BetIcys79,81,89 (Figure  a,b). For the As­(III)-unbound state of both mutants, PC1 captures motions characterized by changes in the distance between the two helices within the dimer, while PC2 captures motions with smaller variance in the interhelical distance.

  When As­(III) was bound to the BetIcys79,81,89 mutant, the fluctuations in interhelical distance captured along PC1 showed a significant increase, with the contribution rate of the corresponding eigenvalue increasing by a factor of 1.3 (72% compared to 54%). In contrast, for the BetIcys79,81,86 mutant, As­(III) binding altered the fluctuation pattern. In this case, PC1 (contribution rate: 55%) captured motions characterized by small fluctuations in interhelical distance, whereas larger changes in interhelical distance appeared in PC2. Meanwhile, the contribution rate of the eigenvalue corresponding to larger interhelical distance motions decreased from 36% in the As-unbound state to 25% in the As-bound state.

  To directly compare the four systems, namely the As­(III)-unbound and As­(III)-bound states of BetIcys79,81,86 and BetIcys79,81,89, we defined a common PC1 space by performing PCA only on the trajectory of As­(III)-unbound BetIcys79,81,89 using the same atom selection as in Figure  . This PC1 mode corresponds to fluctuations in the interhelical distance within the dimer, as shown in Figure  b. The trajectory of each system was then projected separately onto this common PC1 axis (Supplementary Figure S3). In BetIcys79,81,89, the As­(III)-bound state showed a broader distribution along the common PC1 axis than the As­(III)-unbound state, with standard deviations of 2.5 and 1.9 nm, respectively. In contrast, the BetIcys79,81,86 trajectories showed narrower distributions along the common PC1 axis, with standard deviations of 1.0 nm for the As­(III)-unbound state and 1.3 nm for As­(III)-bound state. These results support the interpretation that As­(III) binding enhances sampling along the interhelical fluctuation mode in BetIcys79,81,89, whereas BetIcys79,81,86 samples this mode to a lesser extent in both the As­(III)-unbound and As­(III)-bound states.

  The results of the MD simulations provide an explanation for the response inversion to As­(III) observed in BetIcys79,81,89 and BetIcys79,81,86 observed in the fluorescence experiments (Figure  ). In the TetR family, to which BetI also belongs, a pendulum-like motion of helix α4 is thought to increase the separation of the DNA-binding domains (helices α2−α3). In the BetIcys79,81,89 mutant, L79C and Q81C form the As­(III)-binding site, located in a helix–loop region at the C-terminal end of helix α4. As­(III) binding with bidentate coordination to these cysteines induces significant fluctuations at the As­(III)-binding site (Figure  b). These fluctuations propagate through helix α4, resulting in an increased separation of the DNA-binding domains (Figure  b). Such enhanced fluctuations of the DNA-binding domains in the As­(III)-bound BetIcys79,81,89 mutant are likely to disrupt its DNA-binding ability, consistent with experimental results demonstrating arsenic responsiveness (Figure  b).

  In contrast, in the BetIcys79,81,86 mutant, Q86C is located at the N-terminal end of helix α5, and the three cysteines span a helix–loop–helix structure. As­(III) binding to these residues likely stabilizes the helix–loop–helix structure, leading to reduced fluctuations at the As­(III)-binding site (Figure  a). Consequently, the motions captured along PC1 in the As-bound state no longer lead to large separations of the DNA-binding domains (Figure  a). This interpretation is consistent with the reporter results, which suggest that transcriptional repression by the As-bound BetIcys79,81,86 mutant is maintained or enhanced (Figure  c).

  We note the scope and interpretation of the present computational models. The As­(III)-bound structures used in the MD simulations were model structures based on assumed local coordination geometries. The templates from other proteins were used to define the local As­(III)-S bonded parameters, including As–S bond lengths and S–As–S angles, rather than the global conformation of BetI. The mean As–S bond length and S–As–S angles used in our models were within the ranges observed in available As­(III)-thiolate protein structures deposited in the PDB (Table S3), supporting the reasonableness of the local coordination geometries. Nevertheless, because the exact As­(III)-bound structures of the present BetI mutants have not been experimentally determined, these models should be regarded as plausible mechanistic models rather than unique structural solutions. In BetIcys79,81,89, the formation of a trithiol As­(III)-binding site cannot be completely excluded. However, the present dithiol-binding model showed enhanced fluctuations of the DNA-binding domains, supporting the interpretation that As­(III) binding destabilizes DNA binding in this mutant.


### Comparison with Previous
Studies of As­(III)-Induced
Protein Structural Changes
  Notably, the As­(III)-dependent stabilization or destabilization of protein structure observed in this study is consistent with previous observations. For example, arsenic trioxide restores the function of structurally destabilized p53 mutants by stabilizing the protein and recovering transcriptional activity. Similarly, in an oligopeptide (Ac-NCCHGTRDCA-NH2) encompassing the effector-binding fragment of AfArsR, coordination of As­(III) induces a structural transition from a random coil to a well-defined folded structure. These findings highlight the ability of As­(III) coordination to modulate protein structure through allosteric interactions. More broadly, these findings suggest that the coordination chemistry of As­(III), when embedded in an appropriate structural context, can serve as a versatile mechanism for programmable control of protein conformation and regulatory function. Viewed in this context, our whole-cell reporter results suggest that the position of an engineered putative As­(III)-binding site may influence not only the magnitude but also the direction of reporter output. However, direct As­(III) binding, associated conformational changes, and changes in BetI–DNA binding were not experimentally measured in the present study.


### Toward Engineering Applications of As­(III)-Responsive
Transcriptional Switches
  The present study provides a proof-of-concept that the direction of an As­(III)-dependent whole-cell reporter response can be altered by changing the position of an engineered cysteine within a common BetI scaffold. Under the tested conditions, BetIcys79,81,89 and BetIcys79,81,86 produced increased and decreased reporter outputs, respectively, following As­(III) exposure. Thus, the experimentally demonstrated design feature is the ability to generate complementary reporter phenotypes from closely related BetI variants, irrespective of the precise molecular mechanism underlying these responses.

  One potential application of this response inversion is the construction of paired “turn-on” and “turn-off” modules in a whole-cell sensor. Oppositely responding reporter outputs could support ratiometric detection, internal normalization, or logic-gated signal processing, thereby helping to distinguish analyte-dependent responses from nonspecific changes in cell growth or reporter performance. Implementation of such a system would require orthogonal promoter–operator pairs or other circuit-insulation strategies to minimize cross-regulation between BetI-derived modules. For future environmental arsenic sensing, further optimization of sensitivity and metal selectivity, together with calibration in real water samples and evaluation of matrix effects, will also be necessary.

  More broadly, the position-dependent inversion of reporter output illustrates a strategy for diversifying regulatory behaviors without changing the overall transcription-factor scaffold. Analogous switches could be incorporated into synthetic gene circuits in which the same chemical input is required to generate either an activating or an inhibitory output. Recent material-based platforms for growth hormone and IL-2 delivery have highlighted the importance of controlling the timing and localization of protein exposure. ,  Genetically encoded transcriptional switches may eventually provide a complementary means of controlling when and where a target protein is produced. However, the BetI variants described here were evaluated only in a bacterial reporter system and are not therapeutic modules in their current form. Application in therapeutic or nonbacterial settings would require extensive redesign and validation of host compatibility, circuit orthogonality, performance, and biosafety.


### Mechanistic Limitations
and Future Directions
  The present study does not directly establish As­(III) binding to the engineered BetI variants, the identity or stoichiometry of the coordinating residues in cells, or As­(III)-dependent changes in BetI–betO affinity. Although we attempted to purify the key BetI variants, these proteins were highly unstable during purification, and we were unable to obtain purified samples of sufficient quality and stability for reliable EMSA, other quantitative DNA-binding assays, or direct As­(III)-binding measurements. The coordination geometries used in the MD simulations were therefore predefined structural hypotheses rather than experimentally determined As­(III)-bound structures.

  The absence of these measurements limits the mechanistic scope of the study. Accordingly, the experimentally supported conclusion is restricted to the reproducible, position-dependent inversion of whole-cell sfGFP reporter output under the tested As­(III) and choline conditions. This reporter phenotype is supported by the cysteine-free BetII22T and pAC-void controls, as well as by the raw fluorescence and OD600 data. Nevertheless, the reporter assay cannot distinguish altered BetI–DNA affinity from changes in intracellular BetI abundance, folding, stability, degradation, aggregation, or other cellular processes. Direct mechanistic validation will require stabilized BetI constructs, alternative purification strategies, or suitable in-cell approaches that can directly assess As­(III) binding to the engineered cysteine sites and As­(III)-dependent changes in BetI–DNA binding affinity.


## Conclusion
  In this study, introduction of a third cysteine at different positions in the engineered L79C/Q81C BetI scaffold produced position-dependent changes in whole-cell sfGFP reporter output, including opposite response directions, upon As­(III) exposure. Under combined As­(III) and choline conditions, BetIcys79,81,89 and BetIcys79,81,86 exhibited operational OR-like and As­(III)-inhibited reporter-output patterns, respectively, over defined concentration ranges. MD simulations using predefined dithiol- and trithiol-coordination models yielded distinct fluctuation patterns and provided testable structural hypotheses for these phenotypes. Because neither As­(III) binding to the engineered cysteine sites nor As­(III)-dependent changes in BetI–DNA binding were directly measured, the present study does not establish the underlying molecular mechanism. Nevertheless, our experiments demonstrate that the position of an engineered cysteine can tune the direction of As­(III)-dependent whole-cell reporter output in a BetI-based regulatory system. This position-dependent response inversion expands the design space for engineering multi-input transcriptional devices.


## Materials and Methods

### Bacterial
Strain, Media, and Chemicals
  Escherichia coli strain MG1655 was used throughout this work, and E. coli XL10-Gold (Kan) (Stratagene, La Jolla, CA) was used for cloning. E. coli strain MG1655 was incubated in LB medium or on LB-agar plates (2.0% (w/v) LB, 1.5% (w/v) agar; Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) at 37 °C. Then, 30 μg/mL of chloramphenicol (Cm; Nacalai Tesque) was used to maintain the pAC-based vector, and 100 μg/mL of ampicillin (Amp; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) was used to maintain the pET-based vector. Stock solutions (1 M) of choline chloride (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) was prepared by dissolving appropriate amounts of the compound in LB medium and filter sterilized through a 0.2 μm cellulose acetate filter (MN Sterilizer CA, Macherey-Nagel GmbH & Co. KG, Düren, Germany), and stored at 4 °C. The As­(III) used in this study was As­(III) Standard Solution: As 1000 mg/L, composition: As2O3 and NaOH in water with HCl, pH 5.0 (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan).


### Plasmid
Construction and Cysteine Insertion
  All the plasmids used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S4. Primers were designed to introduce a TGC sequence at the site of cysteine insertion, and site-specific mutagenesis was performed on pAC-Pj23116-betII22T using KOD One PCR Master Mix and Gibson assembly. The obtained plasmids were transformed into E. coli XL10-Gold, and three randomly selected colonies were checked for the insertion by sequencing. The confirmed colonies were cultured in LB liquid medium and the plasmids were isolated.


### Analysis
of BetI Cysteine Mutants to As­(III)
with sfGFP as the Reporter
  The reporter plasmid pET23d-PT5(betO)-sfgfp and a plasmid expressing BetI variant (I22T) or BetIcys79,81,X were cotransformed into E. coli strain MG1655 by electroporation. The vector pAC (Void; BetI (−)), which does not code for BetI, was used as a control. Transformed colonies were grown on solid LB (Amp/Cm) medium, and three colonies were randomly selected and inoculated into 96-deep well plates containing 500 μL of LB (Amp/Cm) liquid medium, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 10 h. Subsequently, each culture was inoculated with 1/100 volume into 100 μL of LB (Amp/Cm) liquid medium containing varying concentrations of As­(III) (0–10 ppm), choline chloride (0–100 mM), or both, within the range of concentrations previously shown to induce a response in E. coli. The cultures were then incubated at 37 °C for 12 h. Cells were resuspended in physiological saline before measuring cell density and fluorescence intensity. Cell density (OD600) was measured on a SpectraMax (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA), and fluorescence intensity (excitation: 485 nm, emission: 510 nm) was measured using FilterMax F5 (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA, USA).


### MD Simulations
  All MD simulations conducted in this study were performed using GROMACS 2021 software (Abraham MJ, http://www.gromacs.org.) and PLUMED (the open-source, community-developed PLUMED library, version 2.7.6). All MD simulations were performed with a time step of 2 fs. We used the LINCS method for bond constraints and the particle-mesh Ewald method with a cutoff of 10 Å for Coulomb interactions. The following procedures were applied to each system: (a) Energy minimization with 250.0 kJ/mol/nm condition; (b) A 100 ps MD equilibration using the V-rescale thermostat at 300 K under constant-NVT ensemble; and (c) a 600 ps MD equilibration with isotropic Berendsen exponential relaxation pressure coupling at 300 K under 1 atm. During these steps, positional restraints were applied to the protein heavy atoms, except for those within 4–6 Å of mutated amino acid residues or As­(III)-bound residues. The resulting structures were then used as inputs for the replica exchange with solute tempering 2 (REST2) simulations. All protein and As­(III) atoms were included in the “hot” region. A total of 8 replicas were used with effective temperatures of 300, 307, 314, 320, 328, 335, 342, and 350 K.

  To construct the BetIcys79,81,86 and BetIcys79,81,89 structures, we started from a structural model of BetII22T generated using the same protocol as described in our previous study. ,  For the present study, the initial geometries of BetIcys79,81,86 and BetIcys79,81,89 were generated from a BetII22T structure obtained after thermal equilibration through ten independent 100 ns MD simulations. After replacing L79, Q81, and Q86 or Q89 with cysteine residues in AmberTools 22 (D.A. Case, 2023, https://ambermd.org/AmberMD.php), a 300 ns REST2 simulation was performed with isotropic Parrinello–Rahman barostat under constant-NPT (300 K and 1 atm) ensemble without positional restraints. Exchanges of configurations between neighboring replicas were attempted every 2 ps with an average exchange probabilities of about 15%.

  The As­(III)-bound models of BetIcys79,81,86 and BetIcys79,81,89 were constructed by first extracting the Cβ and S atom coordinates of the engineered cysteine residues from the 300 ns REST2 trajectories of each mutant. For each coordinate, root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) was then calculated relative to reference cysteine Cβ and S atoms obtained from X-ray structures of As­(III)-binding sites, using a trithiol site from a designed peptide (PDB ID: 2JGO) for BetIcys79,81,86, and a dithiol site from an As­(III) S-adenosylmethionine methyltransferase (PDB ID: 4FSD) for BetIcys79,81,89. Frames exhibiting low RMSD values were identified, and from these, representative structures were selected such that coordination of As­(III) could be introduced with minimal steric clashes with surrounding protein residues. As­(III) was then coordinated to L79C, Q81C, and Q86C in BetIcys79,81,86, and to L79C and Q81C in BetIcys79,81,89 (L79C-S–AsOH–S-Q81C). For both the As­(III)-bound systems and the corresponding As­(III)-free systems, 600 ns REST2 simulations were performed under constant-NPT conditions (300 K and 1 atm). The last 590 ns of the simulation were used for analysis. Exchanged of configurations between neighboring replicas were attempted every 2 ps with an average exchange probabilities of about 13%.

  The force field for the As­(III)-bound residues was built using antechamber and MCPB.py (Version 7.0) in AmberTools 22 using the X-ray structures of the designed peptide (PDB ID: 2JGO) and the As­(III) S-adenosylmethionine methyltransferase (PDB ID: 4FSD). The Amber ff14SB force field was used for standard residues. All parameters for the As­(III) complexes were derived from quantum mechanical calculations by density functional theory (B3LYP/6-31G*) using Gaussian 16 software. The force constants were calculated from the method proposed by Seminario, and the restrained electrostatic potential fitting scheme was used for the partial charges. Using the obtained force field parameters, 600 ns REST2 simulations were performed for these systems, and it was confirmed that backbone RMSD values with respect to the corresponding X-ray structures were within 2 Å.


### Statistical
Analysis
  For Figure  c, statistical significance between the As­(III)-untreated and 10 ppm As­(III)-treated conditions was evaluated for each cysteine-substituted BetI variant using a two-tailed Welch’s t test. For Figure  , statistical analysis of the As­(III) dose–response data was performed for the cysteine-free BetII22T reference variant, BetIcys79,81,86, and BetIcys79,81,89. Under each choline concentration, each As­(III)-treated condition was compared with the corresponding As­(III)-untreated control using a two-tailed Welch’s t test. Statistical analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel. A p value or adjusted p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
