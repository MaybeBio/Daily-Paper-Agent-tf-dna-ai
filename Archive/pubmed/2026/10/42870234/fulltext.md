# A TRIM15–CDX2 loop drives bile acid-induced gastric intestinal metaplasia


## Background
  Gastric intestinal metaplasia (GIM), a precancerous lesion and risk factor for gastric cancer, is closely associated with chronic bile acid (BA) exposure; however, its molecular mechanisms remain unclear. This study aimed to identify key regulators involved in BA-associated GIM and to elucidate their functional roles.


## Methods
  TRIM15 and GIM markers (CDX2, MUC2, KLF4, and VIL1) were assessed in human GIM and paired normal tissues by RNA-seq, RT‒qPCR, Western blotting (WB), and immunofluorescence (IF). BA profiles were analyzed using UHPLC‒MS/MS. In vitro, GES-1 cells were treated with CDCA and subjected to TRIM15 gain- and loss-of-function assays. CDX2-mediated transcriptional regulation of TRIM15 was evaluated by dual-luciferase reporter and ChIP assays. NF-κB signaling and related proteins were assessed by WB and IF. The interaction between TRIM15 and IκBα and the levels of ubiquitination were investigated by coimmunoprecipitation, LC–MS/MS and protein–protein docking analysis. Functional assays were used to assess proliferation and apoptosis. In vivo validation was performed using an AAV9-mediated TRIM15 knockdown rat model.


## Results
  TRIM15 expression was consistently upregulated in GIM and increased stepwise with disease severity, accompanied by a positive association with CDX2 expression. BA profiling revealed the selective enrichment of cholic acid (CA) and chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA), which induced GIM-associated phenotypes in vitro. Mechanistically, CDX2 directly bound to the TRIM15 promoter to increase its transcription, whereas TRIM15 promoted CDX2 expression via the NF-κB pathway, resulting in the formation of a regulatory loop. Further mechanistic analyses revealed that TRIM15 promoted K48-linked ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation of IκBα, thereby enhancing p65 phosphorylation. Notably, this effect was abolished by expression of the degradation-resistant IκBα S32A/S36A mutant. In vivo, TRIM15 knockdown attenuated gastric mucosal injury and intestinal metaplasia and reduced NF-κB signaling and CDX2 expression.


## Conclusion
  These findings suggest the involvement of a TRIM15–NF-κB–CDX2 regulatory loop in BA-associated GIM. TRIM15 may contribute to epithelial reprogramming under BA exposure and could be a potential biomarker and therapeutic target for GIM progression.

## Introduction
  Globally, particularly in China, the incidence and mortality rate of gastric cancer (GC) are high annually, imposing a substantial socioeconomic burden (Bray et al. 2024). In 1970, Correa proposed the concept of the Correa cascade for GC, in which gastric intestinal metaplasia (GIM) is recognized as a precancerous lesion (Correa et al. 1970; Correa 1988). Increasing evidence has demonstrated a strong temporal and spatial association between GIM and the initiation and progression of GC (Altayar et al. 2020; Lee et al. 2022; Huang et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2020). Notably, a large prospective multicenter cohort study revealed that individuals with GIM had a 5.36-fold increased risk of developing both early and advanced GC, confirming its critical role as a major risk factor (Lee et al. 2022). Epidemiological data further reveal substantial geographic variation in GIM prevalence, ranging from 3.4% in low-risk Western countries to 29.6% in high-risk regions of East Asia (Song et al. 2015; Du et al. 2014; Sonnenberg and Genta 2015; Jiang et al. 2017). Collectively, these findings highlight the urgent need to identify novel biomarkers of GIM and develop effective strategies to prevent its malignant transformation.

  Long-term bile acid (BA) exposure has been identified as a major environmental factor and an independent risk factor for GIM (Jiang et al. 2017; Matsuhisa et al. 2013; Hegyi et al. 2018; Sugano et al. 2023; Qu and Shi 2022). In recent years, increasing clinical evidence has strengthened the association between bile acids (BAs) and GIM. A large multicenter clinical study(n = 2,283) demonstrated that elevated BA levels were associated with an increased risk of GIM, independent of Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection (Matsuhisa et al. 2013), a well-recognized etiologic factor for GIM and a major driver of its progression to gastric cancer (Sugano et al. 2023). Similarly, a large-scale endoscopic study involving 28,745 subjects identified bile reflux, H. pylori infection, and age as independent risk factors for GIM in southeastern China (P < 0.01) (Jiang et al. 2017). Consistently, chronic BA reflux has also been implicated in another form of metaplasia, Barrett’s esophagus, in which BA-induced metaplasia was first characterized (Das et al. 2011; Kazumori et al. 2011). Kazumori et al. reported that cholic acid (CA) activates the caudal-type homeobox transcription factor 2 (CDX2) promoter via NF-κB signaling, inducing CDX2 expression and metaplasia in Barrett’s esophagus (Kazumori et al. 2006). In addition, BA-induced sequential expression of CDX1 followed by CDX2, together with their reciprocal transcriptional activation, facilitates Barrett’s epithelial development (Kazumori et al. 2009). These findings further support the role of BAs in the development of intestinal metaplasia.

  Mechanistically, BAs exert cytotoxic effects on epithelial cells by inducing acidification, DNA damage, and apoptosis (Goldman et al. 2010), with toxicity further enhanced in acidic environments (Nehra et al. 1999; Kauer and Stein 2002). In addition to these cytotoxic effects, BAs have been reported to modulate multiple signaling pathways that contribute to epithelial reprogramming in GIM (Sugano et al. 2023; Jin et al. 2022). For example, Wang et al. demonstrated that deoxycholic acid (DCA) activates the FXR/SNAI2 signaling axis, thereby suppressing the expression of the miR-1 promotor (Wang et al. 2021) and relieving its inhibition of the HDAC6/HNF4α loop, which in turn upregulates CDX2 expression in GES-1 cells (Wang et al. 2021). Similarly, Zhen et al. reported that BA treatment activated the TGR5–ERK1/2 signaling pathway and induced HNF4α expression, which subsequently promoted the GIM phenotype through direct regulation of KLF4 and CDX2 (Ni et al. 2020). Moreover, a previous study showed that DCA induces GIM through the activation of STAT3 signaling, leading to increased epithelial proliferation, apoptosis resistance, and disruption of gastric BA metabolism (Jin et al. 2022). Despite these insights, the precise molecular mechanisms of BA-induced GIM remain largely undefined.

  The development of GIM is frequently accompanied by acute and chronic inflammatory infiltration of the gastric mucosa, whereby inflammatory stimuli can affect the expression of CDX2 in gastric cells (Sugano et al. 2023). Among the regulators of inflammation, the tripartite motif (TRIM) protein family has emerged as important modulators of both inflammatory and oncogenic pathways, including the NF-κB pathway (Meroni and Diez-Roux 2005; Hatakeyama 2011, 2017; Huang et al. 2022; Cambiaghi et al. 2012). Notably, the expression of TRIM15, a member of the TRIM family, has been reported to be upregulated in GIM and gastrointestinal tumors and is closely associated with tumorigenesis (Zhang et al. 2021; Zhou et al. 2021; Cai et al. 2024; Sun et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2024). Liu et al. used an air‒liquid interface model to culture GIM stem cells isolated from patients with GIM and found that TRIM15 expression was significantly elevated in GIM, suggesting that TRIM15 may serve as a promising marker for GIM pathogenesis (Liu et al. 2024). Another study demonstrated that TRIM15 expression is a specific and independent adverse predictor in patients with GC (Zhou et al. 2021). However, despite these observations, the functional role and underlying mechanisms of TRIM15 in GIM, particularly in the context of BA exposure, remain unclear.

  Given the established role of inflammation-associated signaling networks in metaplasia (Qu and Shi 2022; Tong et al. 2024), we hypothesized that TRIM15 may promote GIM progression by regulating inflammatory pathways in response to BA exposure. In this study, integrated transcriptomic analysis revealed that TRIM15 is markedly upregulated in human GIM tissues. Furthermore, immunofluorescence and histopathological analyses of human gastric tissues confirmed that elevated TRIM15 expression is closely associated with GIM risk stratification. Collectively, these findings suggest that TRIM15 may play a critical regulatory role in GIM progression. Elucidating its molecular mechanism may provide new insights into disease pathogenesis and identify potential therapeutic targets for preventing gastric carcinogenesis.


## Materials and methods

### Study population and clinical samples
  All participants were recruited from adult gastroenterology outpatients and inpatients at Southeast University Hospital. A total of 70 adult H. pylori–negative patients with GIM and BA reflux were enrolled. H. pylori infection status was assessed by rapid urease testing and histopathological examination. Endoscopic biopsies were obtained according to established guidelines (Chinese Society of Gastroenterology, Cancer Collaboration Group of Chinese Society of Gastroenterology, Chinese Medical Association 2022), with systematic sampling of the antrum (n = 2), angle (n = 2), and body (n = 1) and an additional two samples from normal gastric mucosa.

  In accordance with the Operative Link on Gastric Intestinal Metaplasia (OLGIM) staging system (Dinis-Ribeiro et al. 2024), patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups. The high-risk group was defined as patients meeting any of the following criteria: OLGIM stage III/IV, moderate-to-severe GIM, extensive GIM, incomplete GIM subtype, or OLGIM stage II with multifocal GIM. The low-risk group included patients with OLGIM stage I or focal GIM.


### Targeted bile acid analysis by ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography‒tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC‒MS/MS)
  The UHPLC‒MS/MS targeted BA panel covered all major human primary and secondary BAs as well as their conjugated forms (Table S1). Briefly, standard BA working solutions were prepared. BAs were quantitatively analyzed by UHPLC‒MS/MS in paired lesional and adjacent normal gastric tissues from 14 randomly selected patients with GIM. Raw peak intensities obtained from UHPLC‒MS/MS were used for downstream statistical analysis. BA metabolomic data were normalized using Z score transformation prior to multivariate analysis. Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was performed to assess group separation and metabolic differences between experimental groups. The detailed procedures are provided in (Dunn et al. 2011).


### RNA sequencing (RNA‑seq)
  Human GIM tissues (mixed-type, n = 3) and paired normal control tissues (NC, n = 3) were collected as described above. RNA libraries were subsequently sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq™ X Plus platform (PE150; LC-Bio Technology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China). Raw reads were aligned to the human reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.2.1) (https://daehwankimlab.github.io/hisat2/), and gene-level read counts were generated for downstream analysis. Differential expression analysis was performed in R (v4.1.3) using the limma package with the voom transformation. Genes with |log2 fold change|> 1.5 and adjusted P < 0.05 were defined as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed using hypergeometric testing with adjusted P < 0.05. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted using GSEA software (v4.1.0) and MSigDB; gene sets with |NES|> 1, nominal P < 0.05, and FDR q < 0.25 were considered significant. Data visualization was performed using the online platform Microbioinformatics (Wei Sheng Xin) based on processed expression and enrichment analysis results. Sequencing data from this study have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) under accession number PRJNA1274625.


### Cell culture and in vitro GIM modeling
  Human GES-1, AGS, HGC27, N87, and HCT116 cells were purchased from CELLCOOK Bio (Guangzhou, China). The cells were maintained at 37 °C in an incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with 5% CO2 in medium supplemented with 10% FBS (ExCell Bio) and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin. GES-1, N87, and HCT116 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium, whereas HGC27 and AGS cells were cultured in F-12 K medium (KeyGEN Bio). All the cell lines used in this study were authenticated by STR sequence profiling and tested for mycoplasma contamination.

  Based on gastric tissue BA profiling, CA and chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA), which were significantly enriched in GIM samples, were selected as representative BAs for in vitro stimulation.

  GES-1 cells were treated with CA or CDCA over a gradient of concentrations (0–100 μmol/L) and for different durations (0–24 h; 50 μmol/L) to establish an in vitro GIM-like model (Jin et al. 2022; Li et al. 2019; Yu et al. 2019). The experimental design is shown in Fig. S1A. The expression of GIM-associated molecular markers, including CDX2, MUC2, KLF4, and VIL1, was assessed by RT‒qPCR under different concentrations and treatment durations. The condition showing the most significant upregulation of these markers was defined as the optimal induction condition. CA, CDCA, Z-VAD-FMK, IL-1β, TNF-α, JSH-23, Cycloheximide (CHX) and MG132 were purchased from MCE (NJ, USA).


### CCK-8 assay
  Pretreated cells were seeded into 96-well plates at 3,000 cells/well and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 24–72 h. Fresh medium and CCK-8 solution (KeyGEN Bio, China) were mixed at a 100:10 ratio, 100 μL was added per well, and the plate was incubated at 37 °C for 2.5 h in the dark. The absorbance of each well was then measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. The obtained data were used for subsequent analysis.


### EdU staining
  EdU-positive cells directly reflect the proliferating cell population and its DNA synthesis activity. A kFluor555-EdU Cell Proliferation Test Kit (KeyGEN Bio, China) was used for this experiment according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Briefly, 4 × 103 cells per well were seeded into 96-well plates for drug or transfection treatment. After staining, the cells were imaged using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus) for subsequent analysis.


### Flow cytometry for cell apoptosis
  CDCA-treated transfected GES-1 cells were treated with or without Z-VAD-FMK, a pancaspase inhibitor. Cells were collected either directly or 48 h after transfection, digested with EDTA-free trypsin and collected by centrifugation. Annexin V-APC/7-AAD apoptosis detection kits were purchased from KeyGEN Bio. At least 1 × 105 cells per group were counted and resuspended in binding buffer to obtain single-cell suspensions. The cells were stained with Annexin V-APC (5 μL) and 7-AAD (5 μL), mixed gently, and incubated at room temperature for 5–10 min in the dark. The samples were then immediately analyzed by flow cytometry. Flow cytometry data were acquired using instruments from Thermo Fisher Scientific (USA).


### Plasmids, lentivirus construction, and reagents
  Human-derived TRIM15-interfering plasmids, CDX2-interfering plasmids, and control constructs were generated using pRNA1.1-Neo-GFP-U6 as the vector and verified by sequencing. Human overexpression plasmids for CDX2, Flag-TRIM15(WT)/ΔRING, HA-IκBα (WT), HA-IκBα S32A/S36A, Myc-Ub WT/K63Only/K63R/K48Only/K48R and the corresponding control plasmids were constructed using pcDNA3.1(+). All sequences were constructed and verified by KeyGEN Bio. Plasmids were transfected with LIPO3000 (Invitrogen, USA). A schematic overview of the plasmid transfection and CDCA treatment protocol in GES-1 cells is shown in Fig. S3. Cells stably transduced with TRIM15-overexpressing lentivirus were selected using 3 µg/mL puromycin.


### Frozen tissue sections and histological staining
  The tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for 10–15 s immediately after excision and then fixed by the addition of precooled OCT to cover the whole tissue. The tissue was subsequently sectioned at a constant temperature (− 15 °C to − 20 °C) and imaged.

  For tissue staining, the tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, and hardened by paraffin embedding. Blocks were sectioned at 3 μm, and formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) slides were dewaxed and rehydrated. Sections were stained for nuclei and cytoplasm using hematoxylin‒eosin (H&E) or costained with alcian blue‒periodic acid–Schiff (AB‒PAS). A fluorescence microscope (IX73, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) was used in this experiment. Scanner instrument models: Pannoramic DESK, P-MIDI, P250, P1000; 3D HISTECH, Hungary. Scanning imaging software: CaseViewer, CHN.


### Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF) staining
  For IHC staining, briefly, FFPE sections were retrieved, blocked with 3% BSA (30 min, RT), incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, and then incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1 h, RT). The signals were developed with DAB and counterstained with hematoxylin. For IF, sections or permeabilized cells were incubated with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (10–15 min, protected from light). The above steps were repeated for multiple-fluorescence staining. Images were acquired on a confocal microscope (A1 HD25, Nikon, Japan).


### Dual‑luciferase reporter assay
  GES-1 cells stably expressing TRIM15 (LV-TRIM15) or empty vector (LV-Vector) were treated with CDCA. The pNF-κB-luc reporter plasmid (D2206; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was transfected into these stable cell lines. In parallel, shTRIM15 or control plasmids were cotransfected as indicated. After 24 h, the cells were stimulated with or without IL-1β (20 ng/mL for 9 h), and dual-luciferase reporter activity was measured to assess NF-κB transcriptional activity.

  The human TRIM15 promoter region (− 1900 to + 100 bp relative to the transcription start site), including the wild-type (WT) and mutant constructs, was amplified and cloned and inserted into the pGL3-Basic vector using the SacI and XhoI restriction sites. GES-1 cells pretreated with CDCA were plated in 24-well plates (3 × 104/well). Cells were then cotransfected with pGL3-TRIM15 promoter constructs, CDX2 expression plasmid or empty vector, and a Renilla luciferase internal control plasmid. After 48 h, relative luciferase activity was assessed using a dual-luciferase reporter assay system (Promega, WI, USA).


### Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay and ChIP‒qPCR
  ChIP assays were conducted using a ChIP Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Following immunoprecipitation, DNA was extracted and further purified using a PCR/DNA purification kit (Beyotime). The samples were subsequently analyzed by PCR and qPCR, and the results are expressed as a percentage of the input (% input). All antibodies and working concentrations are shown in Table S2.


### Coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP) and protein interaction LC‒MS/MS analysis
  Co-IP was performed using an assay kit (Beyotime, China). Flag-TRIM15 and HA-IκBα plasmids were individually transfected into or cotransfected into CDCA-treated GES-1 cells for 48 h. The cells were then lysed in lysis buffer, after which the lysates were centrifuged to collect the supernatant. The tagged proteins were extracted according to the manufacturer’s instructions and detected by Western blot (WB) analysis. The LC‒MS/MS workflow was as follows: loading buffer was added to the labeled protein samples, followed by boiling for 5 min and cooling to room temperature. The extracted Flag-TRIM15 protein was subjected to enzymatic digestion to generate peptides. The resulting peptides were separated and analyzed using a nanoflow Easy-nLC 1200 system. The results are provided in Tables S3–4.


### RNA extraction and real-time quantitative PCR (RT‒qPCR)
  Total RNA was extracted using RNA-easy™ Isolation Reagent (Vazyme, China). cDNA was synthesized using a Bioer system with HiScript III SuperMix II for qPCR (Vazyme, China). The resulting cDNA from patient samples was used for PCR amplification with 2 × Prefects Master Mix (Dye) (Bio Giant ATG, China). PCR products were separated on 2% agarose gels (Sigma, USA), and images were captured using a gel imaging system (Tanon, China).

  The same cDNA was used as a template for RT‒qPCR with ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China) on a Tian Neng detection system (China). The expression levels of genes were calculated using the comparative 2^−ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH used as the internal control. The primer sequences are listed in Table S5.


### Protein‒protein docking
  We used HDOCKlite to predict the binding mode between TRIM15 and IκBα. The structure file for TRIM15 was obtained from the Protein Data Bank database (PDB ID: 7QS2), and the structure of IκBα was obtained from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database. Both 3D structures were processed in PyMOL 2.5.3 by removing nonprotein components. Global rigid-body docking was performed using the default parameters of HDOCK. The top-ranked docking model was selected based on binding energy and visualized using PyMOL.


### Western blot analysis
  Proteins were extracted from tissues and cells using specialized protein extraction kits (KeyGEN Bio) and IP lysates (Beyotime) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Selleck, USA). The proteins were then transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA) following standard protocols. The membranes were blocked and incubated with primary and secondary antibodies in 3% BSA (Fdbio Biotech, China) blocking solution. The membranes were then visualized using an ECL Femto chemiluminescence kit, and the imaging results were captured using a fully automated chemiluminescence imaging system (TANNON, China). The protein bands were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH).


### Adeno-associated virus (AAV) production
  The shTRIM15 knockdown cassette (U6-shTRIM15-MCS-CAG-EGFP) was cloned and inserted into the GV478 vector (Genechem, Shanghai, China). A nontargeting shRNA (CGCTGAGTACTTCGAAATGTC) was used as the negative control. The TRIM15-targeting shRNA sequences used were as follows: shRNA-TRIM15-1, GGAGAGGGACAAGATTGAAGA; shRNA-TRIM15-2, GGTTGAAAGCAAGAA.

  GCAACA; and shRNA-TRIM15-3, GCAACCAGCAAGTGAGCTTCT. Recombinant AAV9 particles were generated by triple-plasmid cotransfection of HEK293T cells with GV478-shTRIM15, pAAV-RC9, and pHelper plasmids at a 1:1:1 molar ratio. At 48–72 h posttransfection, viral particles were collected from the supernatant, concentrated by iodixanol gradient ultracentrifugation, and titrated using real-time PCR (≥ 2 × 1013 vg/mL). The detailed protocol has been described previously (Nguyen et al. 2021).


### Animals and experimental design
  A multifactor rat model of GIM was established using bile acid exposure, a high-fat diet (HFD), and acid suppression, with or without AAV-mediated TRIM15 knockdown. The experimental protocol is illustrated in Fig. 7C.

  After a 2-week acclimatization period, male SD rats were randomly assigned to four groups: normal control (NC, n = 7), GIM model (GIM, n = 7), scramble shRNA AAV9 (SC, n = 7), and AAV9-shTRIM15 (shTRIM15, n = 7). All the animals were maintained under SPF conditions with free access to water and subjected to a cyclic fasting–refeeding regimen (1 day of fasting followed by 2 days of feeding) throughout the study.

  The rats in the NC group were fed a standard diet and received sterile saline via intragastric gavage every other day. The rats in the GIM, SC, and shTRIM15 groups were fed a modified diet containing 0.3% (w/w) CDCA (Song et al. 2011; Jones et al. 2015), 0.03% (w/w) ranitidine (Juan et al. 2017), and a high-fat diet (HFD, 45% kcal from fat) (Watanabe et al. 2025). The diet was provided under the same fasting-refeeding regimen. The rats were also gavaged with CDCA (20 mM) every other day, with the gavage volume adjusted according to body weight (< 200 g: 1.0 mL; 200–400 g: 1.5 mL; > 400 g: 2.0 mL). This combined intervention was maintained for up to 32 weeks to induce GIM.

  In the AAV-treated groups, the rats received tail vein injections of 1.2 × 1013 viral genomes (vg) of AAV9-shTRIM15 or scramble control virus (≤ 150 μL per injection) at the beginning of the experiment (week 0), according to the experimental schedule (Fig. 7C).

  To evaluate disease progression, one rat per group was sacrificed at 12 and 24 weeks, and gastric tissues were collected. At 12 weeks, three gastric tissue samples were randomly taken from each rat for assessment of AAV transduction efficiency. The terminal (32-week) cohort (n = 4 per group) was used for figure presentation, pathological evaluation, GIM risk stratification, and molecular pathway validation, and TRIM15 knockdown efficiency was further confirmed at the 32-week endpoint. At the end of the study, the rats were euthanized and analyzed as described above.


### Statistical analysis
  Statistical analyses of the experimental data were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc.). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally distributed data. Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t test. P values were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction for multiple t tests (Sedgwick 2014). Differences among multiple groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. For data that did not meet normality assumptions, nonparametric tests were applied (the Mann‒Whitney U test for two groups and the Kruskal‒Wallis test for multiple groups). Gene–gene correlations were assessed using Spearman’s correlation test. All the data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P value < 0.05.


## Results

  We subsequently validated these findings in an independent cohort of 37 paired human GIM and adjacent normal tissues. The RT‒qPCR confirmed the consistent upregulation of CDX2, KLF4, VIL1, and MUC2 in GIM samples, in agreement with the RNA-seq results (all P < 0.001; Fig. 1C–F). TRIM15 exhibited one of the greatest increases among the identified genes (P = 0.0003; Fig. 1G). WB analysis further confirmed elevated TRIM15 and CDX2 protein levels in GIM tissues (Fig. 1H).

  In addition, stratification analysis in the same cohort was performed. TRIM15 mRNA levels progressively increased from normal gastric tissues to low- and high-risk GIM tissues. Notably, a statistically significant increase was observed in the high-risk group compared with the normal group (P < 0.0001; Fig. 1I). Representative IF staining images and quantitative analysis demonstrated enhanced expression of TRIM15 and GIM markers. Notably, at the protein level, TRIM15 and CDX2 exhibited synchronized expression and prominent colocalization within intestinal metaplastic regions (all P < 0.0001; Fig. 1J, K). Importantly, TRIM15 levels increased stepwise across risk groups (all P < 0.01; Fig. 1L), which is consistent with the mRNA stratification results shown in Fig. 1I.

  Collectively, these results indicate that TRIM15 is closely associated with CDX2 expression and is progressively enriched during GIM progression, suggesting a potential role for TRIM15 in disease development.


### Establishing a bile acid–induced cellular model of GIM
  Targeted LC‒MS/MS analysis of 14 paired human GIM and normal gastric tissues revealed marked alterations in BA composition, with 38 BAs identified (Table S1). The OPLS-DA and Z score bubble plots clearly distinguished between the GIM and control groups (Fig. S1B, C), and the results of the heatmap analysis further highlighted distinct BA profiles in GIM tissues (Fig. 2A). Notably, CA, CDCA, lithocholic acid (LCA) and β-ursodeoxycholic acid (β-UDCA) were significantly increased in GIM tissues (all P < 0.05; Fig. 2B). In addition, total free BAs were elevated (P < 0.05; Fig. S1D), whereas conjugated BAs were decreased (Fig. S1E), indicating compositional remodeling in GIM with bile reflux despite comparable total BA levels (Fig. S1F). LCA and β-UDCA are secondary BAs derived from CDCA through gut microbial metabolism (Fleishman and Kumar 2024; Cai et al. 2022).   CA and CDCA induced significant dose- and time-dependent upregulation of GIM-associated markers, including CDX2, MUC2, VIL1, and KLF4 (Fig. 2C, D). CDCA (100 μmol/L, 24 h) had the strongest effect on GIM markers and was therefore selected for subsequent experiments. Consistent with these findings, CDCA treatment increased CDX2 and MUC2 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 2E, F). Importantly, TRIM15 expression was also markedly elevated following CDCA stimulation at both the mRNA and protein levels in GES-1 cells (Fig. 2H, I), and higher TRIM15 expression was observed in GC cell lines (Fig. 2G), suggesting the potential involvement of TRIM15 in BA-induced GIM. Together, these findings demonstrate that CDCA effectively induces a GIM-like phenotype in vitro and is accompanied by the upregulation of TRIM15 expression.


### TRIM15 drives cell proliferation while suppressing apoptosis
  The development of GIM is associated with dysregulated epithelial homeostasis (Willet and Mills 2016), driven by a pathological cycle of chronic inflammation and tissue repair (Tsubosaka et al. 2023), in which the proliferation and apoptosis of gastric epithelial cells are involved (Mills and Shivdasani 2011; Zhang et al. 2019). Based on these considerations, the functional role of TRIM15 in BA-induced GIM was next investigated. Efficient knockdown of TRIM15 was achieved by plasmid-based transfection (Fig. 3A), whereas overexpression was established using a lentiviral system (Fig. 3E) in CDCA-treated GES-1 cells. In addition, TRIM15 knockdown significantly reduced CDX2 and KLF4 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 3B–C), whereas TRIM15 overexpression had the opposite effect (Fig. 3F–G). Consistent with these findings, IF staining confirmed that TRIM15 positively regulates CDX2 expression and is colocalized with CDX2 (Fig. 3D, H), in agreement with the findings in human tissues.   We next evaluated whether TRIM15 functionally regulates cellular phenotypes in CDCA-induced GES-1 cells. TRIM15 knockdown increased apoptosis, which was partially reversed by the pancaspase inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK (Fig. 3I, J), indicating the involvement of caspase-dependent apoptotic pathways. ### CDX2 directly binds to the TRIM15 promoter and transcriptionally activates its expression
  To clarify the expression regulatory network of TRIM15, we systematically screened and validated its potential upstream TFs. We first used the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) 2.0 database (TCGA and GTEx datasets) to assess correlations between TRIM15 and GIM biomarkers in gastric tissues. The results revealed positive correlations between TRIM15 and CDX2 (R = 0.77), MUC2 (R = 0.75), and VIL1 (R = 0.61) in GC tissues (all P < 0.001; Fig. 4A–C). However, in 37 paired GIM and adjacent normal tissues, only CDX2 expression was significantly correlated with TRIM15 expression (R = 0.8037, 95% CI: 0.6821–0.9096; P < 0.0001), whereas no significant association was observed between CDX2 expression and MUC2 or VIL1 expression (Fig. 4D–F). CDX2 is a well-established nuclear TF and a key driver of GIM (Sugano et al. 2023; Sakamoto et al. 2011; Silberg et al. 2002). We hypothesized that CDX2 could bind to the TRIM15 promoter and regulate its transcription.   To further explore the underlying transcriptional mechanism, we constructed WT and mutant TRIM15 promoter luciferase reporters (Fig. 4I). Dual-luciferase reporter assays showed that CDX2 overexpression significantly enhanced TRIM15 promoter activity, which was abolished by mutation of the CDX2 binding sites (Fig. 4J). In addition, ChIP-qPCR analysis demonstrated significant enrichment of CDX2 at the TRIM15 promoter region compared with the IgG control in GES-1 cells (Fig. 4K-L), suggesting direct binding of CDX2 to the TRIM15 promoter. Putative CDX2 binding sites within the TRIM15 promoter were predicted using the UCSC Genome Browser and JASPAR database (Fig. S2; Fig. 4M). These results collectively demonstrate that CDX2 directly binds to the TRIM15 promoter and transcriptionally activates its expression in GES-1 cells.

  The RT‒qPCR analysis of 37 paired gastric tissues revealed significantly increased NF-κB p65 expression in GIM samples (Fig. 5D), which was positively correlated with TRIM15 expression (R2 R= 0.5575; P < 0.001; Fig. 5E). Moreover, TRIM15 further potentiated IL-1β-induced NF-κB activation (20 ng/mL, 9 h; Fig. 5G), whereas TRIM15 knockdown markedly suppressed NF-κB activity (Fig. 5H). Together, these results indicate that TRIM15 promotes NF-κB transcriptional activation in GES-1 cells under CDCA stimulation.

  Next, we explored the underlying mechanism. TRIM15 knockdown markedly decreased the phosphorylation of IKKβ, IκBα, and p65 in CDCA-induced GES-1 cells, but total p65 and p50 levels remained unchanged. Conversely, TRIM15 overexpression increased their phosphorylation (Fig. 5I, J). Consistent with these findings, fractionation analysis revealed parallel changes in nuclear p-p65 and p-IκBα levels (Fig. 5K, L), indicating that TRIM15 promotes NF-κB activation and p65 nuclear translocation.

  To assess the requirement of NF-κB signaling in this regulatory axis, both inhibition and activation approaches were applied. The NF-κB inhibitor JSH-23 abolished TRIM15-induced p65 nuclear accumulation, whereas TNF-α restored NF-κB activation in the TRIM15-deficient cells (Fig. 5M). IF and confocal microscopy further confirmed that TRIM15 promotes p65 nuclear translocation (Fig. 5N).

  We further investigated the downstream effectors regulated by NF-κB signaling. Modulation of NF-κB expression by TNF-α or JSH-23 reversed the effects of TRIM15 knockdown or overexpression on the expression of MMP9, IL-1β, Bcl-2, and cleaved caspase-3. Moreover, CDX2 and KLF4 regulation by TRIM15 was also dependent on NF-κB (Fig. 5O). Mechanistically, TRIM15 activates CDX2 expression via NF-κB signaling, whereas CDX2 reciprocally increases the transcription and expression of TRIM15, thereby forming a regulatory loop of TRIM15–NF-κB–CDX2 that sustains BA-induced GIM.

## Discussion
  This study demonstrated that TRIM15 is upregulated in bile reflux–induced GIM and is positively correlated with disease severity in both human tissues and animal models, providing the first evidence of its association with GIM severity. Mechanistically, we further identified a previously unrecognized TRIM15–NF-κB–CDX2 regulatory loop that sustains GIM progression. In vivo, we established an AAV-mediated TRIM15 knockdown rat model and demonstrated that TRIM15 silencing attenuated gastric mucosal injury and alleviated intestinal metaplasia. These findings identify TRIM15 as both a potential biomarker for risk stratification and a promising therapeutic target to halt GIM progression.

  CDX2, a key transcriptional regulator (Yu et al. 2019; 2019) and a well-established initiator of GIM (Mutoh et al. 2009; 2004), is expressed in GIM but not in normal gastric mucosa, as previously reported (Mutoh et al. 2009; He et al. 2022). BAs can upregulate CDX2 and its downstream intestinal markers in gastric cells (Wang et al. 2021; Ni et al. 2020; Li et al. 2019; Yu et al. 2019; Yue et al. 2021). In our study, the effects of CDCA and CA were similar, in line with these findings. However, these studies commonly selected BAs directly to examine the effects of bile reflux on GIM, whereas alterations in BA composition have received limited attention. To address this shortcoming, we performed targeted BA profiling and confirmed that the selective enrichment of CA and CDCA in GIM tissues was accompanied by increased total free BAs. In contrast, a previous LC–MS/MS study of gastric fluid from patients reported elevated CA, CDCA, and DCA levels in both bile reflux gastritis and GC cohorts (Wang et al. 2022). In our cohort, the DCA and total BA levels did not significantly differ, likely reflecting variations in sample type and patient population. Collectively, these findings suggest that specific BA compositions, rather than total BA levels, are more relevant to GIM development.

  The molecular characteristics of cells in gastric premalignant lesions remain incompletely defined, limiting our understanding of the mechanisms driving progression along the Correa cascade. TRIM15 is highly expressed in GIM cells and may serve as a potential biomarker for GIM (Liu et al. 2024). In our study, TRIM15 expression increased in a stage-dependent manner and was positively correlated with disease severity in both human and rat gastric tissues. Moreover, CDCA induced the upregulation of TRIM15. In addition to its role in GIM, TRIM15 has been implicated in multiple gastrointestinal malignancies. It has been reported to be highly expressed and associated with poor prognosis in gastric cancer (Zhou et al. 2021). In esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, knockdown of TRIM15 suppresses tumor growth and metastasis by inhibiting the activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway (Zhang et al. 2021). These observations suggest that TRIM15 is closely associated with disease progression and may contribute to the pathogenesis of BA-associated GIM. In addition, GIM is considered a potentially reversible outcome of injury and repair, whereas sustained inflammatory stimulation contributes to its persistence and malignant transformation (Graham et al. 2019). This process involves the remodeling of gastric epithelial cells, including alterations in proliferation and apoptosis mediated by downstream inflammatory signaling. TRIM proteins are important regulators of inflammatory processes, including transcription, cell proliferation, and apoptosis (Roy and Singh 2021). Previous work has also suggested that TRIM15 may regulate cell proliferation through ERK1/2 (Zhu et al. 2021). Similarly, our results demonstrated that TRIM15 promotes cell proliferation and inhibits apoptosis in CDCA-treated GES-1 cells. Collectively, these findings highlight TRIM15 as a potential biomarker of bile reflux-associated GIM progression.

  The NF-κB pathway is widely implicated in intestinal metaplasia and plays a critical regulatory role (Li et al. 2019; Yu et al. 2019). Li et al. demonstrated that the activation of NF-κB signaling mediated by the miR-92a-1–5p/FOXD1 axis plays a key role in CDX2 induction and the generation of the IM phenotype from gastric cells (Li et al. 2019). ALKBH5 promotes CDX2 expression through the ZNF333/CYLD axis and activation of NF-κB signaling in BA-induced GIM (Yue et al. 2021). The results of the results of our transcriptomic analysis suggested that the regulatory effect of TRIM15 in GIM is closely associated with NF-κB activation and ubiquitination-mediated regulation. Our subsequent experiments further confirmed that TRIM15 activates NF-κB signaling, leading to the upregulation of CDX2 and IL-1β expression in CDCA-induced GES-1 cells. These results are consistent with those of previous reports identifying TRIM15 as a critical upstream regulator of NF-κB signaling (Roy and Singh 2021; Roy et al. 2022), suggesting that TRIM15 regulates GIM through NF-κB–mediated transcriptional programs involved in CDX2 induction and intestinal differentiation.

  TRIM15 is an E3 ubiquitin ligase. The RING domain of TRIM15 serves as the catalytic core, mediating protein–protein interactions and driving ubiquitination, which regulates protein function and stability (Huang et al. 2022). In particular, K48- and K63-linked ubiquitin chains are involved in the activation of kinases in NF-κB signaling pathways (Hatakeyama 2017). A previous study indicated that TRIM15 inhibited NF-κB activation by interacting with TAK1 and reducing its K63-linked ubiquitination in psoriasis (Roy et al. 2022). In contrast, our data demonstrate that TRIM15 interacts with IκBα and promotes its K48-linked ubiquitination, leading to its proteasomal degradation. IκBα is a key negative regulator of NF-κB signaling, and its degradation promotes NF-κB pathway activation (Taniguchi and Karin 2018). RING domain mutations abolished TRIM15-mediated ubiquitination in an E3 ubiquitin ligase activity-dependent manner. These results support previous findings that TRIM proteins play both promoting and inhibitory roles in disease processes (Hatakeyama 2017). In addition, AAV9-mediated TRIM15 knockdown in vivo attenuated gastric mucosal injury and intestinal metaplasia, accompanied by reduced NF-κB activation and CDX2 expression. These findings suggest that TRIM15 modulates NF-κB signaling in GIM and represents a promising therapeutic target. Interestingly, we further found that CDX2 directly binds to the promoter region of TRIM15 and transcriptionally activates its expression in CDCA-treated cells. Together, these findings support a TRIM15–NF-κB–CDX2 regulatory circuit that drives epithelial reprogramming and sustains the progression of BA-associated GIM.

  Previous studies have shown that BAs can activate NF-κB through p50 nuclear translocation (Qu and Shi 2022; Yu et al. 2019; Debruyne et al. 2006), and other reports suggest that CDCA may exert anti-inflammatory effects via TGR5 signaling (Guo et al. 2015). In our study, although p-IKKβ levels were elevated in CDCA-treated GES-1 cells compared with those in the untreated group, no changes in downstream signaling molecules or p65 nuclear translocation were observed. Therefore, under our experimental conditions, CDCA did not induce detectable activation of the canonical NF-κB pathway (p65-dependent signaling) in GES-1 cells. Taken together, these results indicate that CDCA did not induce detectable activation of the canonical NF-κB pathway under our experimental conditions, which further supports the potential role of TRIM15 in the regulation of NF-κB signaling.

  This study has several limitations. First, the sample size of BA related-GIM tissues is relatively small, and serum-based data are lacking, limiting comparisons of TRIM15 with established biomarkers. Second, AAV9 lacks gastric tissue specificity, and potential systemic effects cannot be fully excluded. Third, owing to the multifactor induction model used in this study, the specific contributions of individual components may not be clearly distinguishable. These limitations underscore the need for further validation in large, multicenter cohorts. Future studies should evaluate circulating TRIM15 as a noninvasive biomarker and assess its performance in comparison with current standard-of-care strategies, including H. pylori eradication. In addition, elucidating the role of TRIM15 across the Correa cascade will further clarify its contribution to gastric tumorigenesis.

  Taken together, our findings implicate TRIM15 as a regulatory factor in BA-associated GIM. TRIM15 is linked to BA exposure, CDX2-driven transcriptional reprogramming, and NF-κB signaling and may contribute to a regulatory network involved in disease progression. These findings provide mechanistic insights into the role of TRIM15 in GIM and support further investigation of its potential utility as a biomarker and therapeutic target.
