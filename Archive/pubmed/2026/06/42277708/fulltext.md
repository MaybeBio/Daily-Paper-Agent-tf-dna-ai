# Genome-wide identification of WRKY gene family reveals its regulatory role in ABA/CBF-mediated cold adaptation in king grass (Pennisetum purpureum × P. americanum)


## Background
  King grass is a fast-growing C4 perennial with high biomass potential, yet its productivity is limited by cold stress. WRKY transcription factors (TFs) regulate abiotic stress responses, but their roles in king grass cold adaptation remain unclear.


## Methods
  WRKY TFs were identified genome-wide using BLASTP and HMMER, followed by phylogenetic classification, transcriptome profiling, promoter cis-element analysis, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), molecular docking, and yeast heterologous expression assays.


## Results
  Sixty-two PsiWRKY genes were identified and classified into three groups. Time-series transcriptome analysis across nine cold-stress time points revealed four expression patterns, with 29 genes significantly induced (> 2-fold), mainly during late acclimation (48–72 h). qRT-PCR confirmed RNA-seq trends (R² = 0.742). Functional enrichment and WGCNA analyses indicated that cold-responsive PsiWRKYs coordinate abscisic acid-gibberellin antagonism and sugar metabolism. Molecular docking predicted interactions between PsiWRKY proteins and Calvin cycle enzymes, with PsiWRKY16 and PsiWRKY55 showing the highest structural confidence (pTM > 0.75). Network analysis identified PsiWRKY16 and PsiWRKY50 as hub regulators linking GA signaling genes (GID1/DELLA) with sugar metabolism enzymes such as GAPDH and PRK. Yeast assays quantitatively confirmed enhanced cold tolerance, as heterologous expression of PsiWRKY16 reduced doubling time by 0.56 h and increased area under the curve and carrying capacity by 26.20 and 3.39, respectively, while PsiWRKY50 increased these values by 14.45 and 1.43. Promoter analysis showed enrichment of ABRE and DRE elements (p = 0.00061), and integrative target prediction identified 193 genes co-regulated by WRKY and CBF pathways, supporting ABA-CBF signaling convergence.


## Conclusions
  PsiWRKYs exhibit subgroup-specific and time-resolved responses to cold stress. PsiWRKY16, PsiWRKY50, and PsiWRKY55 are implicated in coordinating hormonal signaling and metabolic reprogramming, providing candidate targets for cold-tolerant breeding in king grass.

## Introduction
  King grass (Pennisetum purpureum Schumacher × P. americanum), also known as hybrid giant Napier grass, is a high-yielding C4 perennial forage crop in the Poaceae family. Owing to its vigorous growth, strong environmental adaptability, and excellent nutritional value, it is widely used in forage production, bioenergy development, and ecological restoration [1]. With superior CO₂ fixation efficiency and drought tolerance compared to C3 forages, king grass plays an increasingly important role in sustainable agriculture [2]. However, being native to tropical and subtropical regions, king grass has low cold tolerance, restricting its cultivation in temperate and high-altitude regions. Despite some degree of local adaptation following its introduction to Southwest China in the 1990s, cold temperatures, especially in areas above 1500 m altitude, continue to reduce growth rates, biomass yield, and survival, hampering both agricultural and industrial applications [3]. This limitation not only affects its availability as a forage resource for livestock but also hinders its industrial potential as a biomass energy crop [4]. Therefore, improving cold tolerance has become a key objective for the sustainable utilization of king grass.

  Plants have evolved complex regulatory networks to cope with low-temperature stress. Among these, the conserved ICE-CBF-COR signaling cascade functions as a central transcriptional module controlling cold acclimation by activating downstream cold-responsive genes [5]. In parallel, phytohormone-mediated signaling pathways, particularly abscisic acid (ABA) signaling, play essential roles in modulating physiological and transcriptional responses to low temperature [6, 7]. Increasing evidence suggests that cold adaptation involves coordinated regulation between transcriptional control and metabolic reprogramming, including carbohydrate metabolism, osmotic adjustment, and reactive oxygen species homeostasis [8, 9].

  The WRKY transcription factor family represents one of the largest regulatory protein families in higher plants and is characterized by a conserved WRKYGQK domain and zinc-finger motifs that bind W-box elements in target gene promoters [10]. Based on structural features, WRKY proteins are classified into three main groups (I, II, III), with Group II further divided into five subgroups (IIa-IIe) [11]. WRKY transcription factors participate broadly in plant responses to both abiotic and biotic stresses. In particular, multiple WRKY genes have been confirmed to enhance cold tolerance by directly or indirectly regulating the expression of cold-responsive genes. For instance, in Arabidopsis, AtWRKY33 positively regulates the CBF (C-repeat binding factor) signaling pathway to promote cold acclimation, whereas AtWRKY34 negatively regulates CBF expression, demonstrating functional diversification within the family [12, 13]. WRKYs also participate in ABA-mediated stress signaling and have been shown to enhance cold tolerance in diverse crops, including plum (PmWRKY57), grape (VvWRKY11), and peanut (MuWRKY3) [14–16]. These findings highlight WRKY transcription factors as important components linking stress signaling pathways and downstream adaptive responses.

  Despite these advances, the regulatory mechanisms underlying cold adaptation in king grass remain poorly understood. Although WRKY-mediated cold responses have also been investigated in napiergrass (Pennisetum purpureum) [17], king grass represents an interspecific hybrid between P. purpureum and P. americanum, possessing a distinct genomic constitution and regulatory background compared with its parental species or related grasses. Hybridization often reshapes transcriptional regulatory networks and stress-response coordination. Therefore, WRKY regulatory mechanisms characterized in napiergrass cannot be directly extrapolated to king grass, leaving a critical knowledge gap regarding WRKY-mediated cold adaptation in this important forage crop. Our previous transcriptomic analysis of king grass revealed activation of both the abscisic acid-activated signaling pathway and the conserved ICE-CBF-COR cold-response module during cold acclimation, indicating that hormonal signaling and CBF-mediated transcriptional regulation are both involved in the cold response of this species [18]. However, the upstream transcription factors coordinating these parallel pathways remain largely unknown. However, the upstream regulatory mechanisms coordinating these pathways remain largely unknown.

  Given the central regulatory roles of WRKY transcription factors in stress signaling networks, we hypothesized that WRKY proteins may function as integrative regulators linking hormonal signaling with cold-responsive transcriptional and metabolic programs in king grass. To test this hypothesis, we performed genome-wide identification and characterization of WRKY genes, combined with time-series transcriptome analysis, co-expression network construction, molecular docking analysis, and yeast-based heterologous expression. This study aims to elucidate the regulatory roles of WRKY transcription factors in king grass cold adaptation and to provide molecular targets for improving cold tolerance in tropical forage crops.


## Materials and methods

### Plant material treatment and transcriptome sequencing
  In this study, the king grass (Pennisetum purpureum Schumacher × P. americanum, Psi) cultivar Reyan NO.4 materials were collected from the experimental field of Xichang University, located in Xichang, Liangshan, China (coordinates: 27.8945°N, 102.2640°E). This cultivar has undergone over five years of natural cold acclimatization in the temperate zone of Sichuan Province, China, and was selected for its stable cold-tolerant traits previously characterized in our laboratory. The experimental procedures, including plant cultivation, cold stress treatment, and transcriptome sampling, followed protocols established in our earlier study [18]. Briefly, plants at three-leaf stage were subjected to cold stress at 4 °C, and leaf tissues were sampled at nine time points (0 h, 0.5 h, 1 h, 3 h, 6 h, 12 h, 24 h, 48 h and 72 h) after treatment. Total RNA was extracted from each time point sample and used for library construction. High-throughput sequencing was performed using both Illumina and PacBio SMRT platforms. The sequencing data were assembled to generate a reference transcriptome for king grass. Further details on experimental procedures including RNA extraction, sequencing, data processing, and raw data access are available in previous study [18].


### Identification of WRKY genes in king grass
  WRKY genes in king grass were identified using a multi-step approach integrating sequence homology search, conserved domain prediction, and cross-validation across multiple databases. WRKY gene sequences from sorghum (Sorghum bicolor), rice (Oryza sativa), maize (Zea mays), and wheat (Triticum aestivum) were retrieved from public databases including PlantTFDB (http://planttfdb.gao-lab.org/), Phytozome (https://phytozome.jgi.doe.gov), Ensembl Plants (http://plants.ensembl.org), and the International Wheat Genome Sequencing Consortium (IWGSC) (https://wheatgenome.org/). These sequences served as references for comparative identification.

  Candidate WRKY genes were detected by BLASTP searches of the king grass transcriptome and subsequently validated using InterProScan v5.52 [19] to confirm the presence of WRKY domains (Pfam ID: PF03106). Genes containing WRKY domains were retained, including king grass-specific sequences absent from related species. BLASTP hits lacking InterProScan annotations were curated manually to avoid missing potential novel candidates. Additional validation was performed with SMART [20] (accession: sma00774) to assess domain integrity, classify single versus duplicated WRKY domains, and determine zinc-finger types (C2H2 or C2HC). Final confirmation was achieved by cross-validation against Pfam (https://pfam.xfam.org/) and the NCBI Conserved Domain Database (CDD) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/bwrpsb/bwrpsb.cgi), ensuring robust annotation of PsiWRKY transcription factors.


### Phylogenetic analysis of PsiWRKY genes
  To investigate the evolutionary relationships and subgroup classification of WRKY transcription factors in king grass, a phylogenetic analysis was performed using the 62 identified PsiWRKY protein sequences. Multiple sequence alignment was carried out with the ClustalW algorithm implemented in MEGA v11. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Maximum-Likelihood (ML) method based on the JTT model. Branch support was assessed by bootstrap resampling with 1000 replicates, and clades with bootstrap values ≥ 70% were considered well supported.

  The resulting phylogenetic tree was visualized and annotated using FigTree v1.4.4 (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/), enabling the identification of WRKY subgroups and inference of their evolutionary divergence.


### Clustering and co-expression analysis of PsiWRKY genes
  Expression profiles of 48 PsiWRKY genes were clustered to reveal temporal patterns. A z-score normalized expression matrix of 26,839 genes (average FPKM > 1 across nine time points) was subjected to k-means clustering with K = 9 as determined by the Elbow method, using Euclidean distance and the Hartigan-Wong algorithm. Cluster validity was further assessed with STEM v1.3.11,with model profiles restricted to 10 and Bonferroni-corrected p-values < 0.05 considered significant.

  Co-expression modules were identified by weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) with the same dataset after linear normalization. A soft-thresholding power of 12 was selected to approximate scale-free topology (R² > 0.89). Modules were detected by hierarchical clustering of the topological overlap matrix (TOM) followed by dynamic tree cutting (minModuleSize = 30, deepSplit = 2). Closely related modules were merged based on eigengene dissimilarity using a threshold of MEDissThres = 0.25. Module eigengenes were then calculated. Module-trait relationships were evaluated by Pearson correlation, with significance assessed using Pearson correlation tests, and p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure (FDR < 0.05). Modules harboring PsiWRKY genes were extracted for downstream analysis.


### Conserved motif and gene structure analysis
  To investigate the structural diversity and evolutionary conservation of WRKY genes, conserved motif analysis was performed using MEME Suite v5.5.7 (https://meme-suite.org/meme/doc/meme.html), with the maximum number of motifs set to 10 and motif widths of 6–50 amino acids. Motifs with E-value < 1e-5 were considered significant. Conserved domain annotation was conducted using the NCBI Batch CDD tool to confirm the presence of WRKY-specific domains. Gene structure information, including exon-intron organization, was analyzed and visualized using the “Gene Structure View” functions in TBtools v2.154. These analyses revealed conserved structural features shared among WRKY groups, as well as subgroup-specific motif patterns, contributing to our understanding of the functional diversification and evolutionary dynamics of the PsiWRKY gene family.


### Chromosomal and subcellular localization analysis
  Due to the lack of a complete king grass genome, chromosomal positions of PsiWRKY genes were inferred from the reference genomes of elephant grass (Pennisetum purpureum) [21] and pearl millet (Pennisetum glaucum) [22]. PsiWRKY protein sequences were aligned against the elephant grass protein database using BLASTP, with matches considered significant at identity > 98% and E-value < 0.01 to minimize false positives. Orthologous hits were used to assign putative chromosomal positions, and distributions were visualized in TBtools v2.154. Subcellular localization of PsiWRKY proteins was predicted with WoLF PSORT (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/) using the plant-specific settings.


### Cis-acting element analysis and regulatory pathways prediction of PsiWRKY genes
  To investigate the upstream regulatory potential of PsiWRKY genes, promoter regions (2000 bp upstream of the start codon) of 26 elephant grass genes homologous to PsiWRKYs were extracted for cis-element analysis. Elements were identified using PlantCARE (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/), and enrichment significance was evaluated by Fisher’s exact test with Benjamini-Hochberg correction (adjusted p < 0.05). The types and frequencies of cis-elements were visualized using the ggplot2 package in R v4.4.1.

  To predict potential regulatory targets, PsiWRKY proteins were queried against the NCBI protein database using BLASTP. The top-matching sequences were further analyzed in PlantTFDB (https://planttfdb.gao-lab.org/) to predict putative target genes. In parallel, known targets of the CBF family in king grass were screened to evaluate possible WRKY-CBF regulatory crosstalk. Functional annotation of predicted target genes was performed in R using the GO.db package, and enrichment of Gene Ontology (GO) categories was tested by the hypergeometric test with Benjamini-Hochberg correction (adjusted p < 0.05).


### Molecular docking analysis of PsiWRKY proteins with key calvin cycle enzymes
  To explore potential protein-protein interactions between PsiWRKY transcription factors and key enzymes involved in the Calvin cycle, molecular docking analysis was performed. Three-dimensional structures of PsiWRKY proteins were predicted with AlphaFold3 (https://alphafoldserver.com/) using a PLDDT confidence threshold ≥ 90. Structures of Calvin cycle enzymes were retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/). Docking simulations were performed to predict potential interaction interfaces, and resulting complexes were analyzed in PyMOL v4.6 [23]. Docking scores were ranked by binding energy, and top-ranked conformations were considered biologically relevant. No additional hypothesis testing was applied to docking results.


### Low-temperature tolerance assay of yeast transformants
  The expression vectors pYES-PsiWRKY16 and pYES-PsiWRKY50 were constructed using a homologous recombinatio based cloning strategy and subsequently introduced into the yeast strain WAT11. Primers were shown in Supplementary Table S1. The resulting yeast strains, together with an empty-vector pYES-NTA control, were inoculated into SD/-Ura selective liquid medium containing 2% (w/v) glucose and cultured at 30 °C with shaking overnight. After measuring OD600, cells were collected by centrifugation and washed once with glucose-free SD/-Ura medium to remove residual glucose. The cell pellets were then transferred to induction medium (SD/-Ura supplemented with 2% (w/v) galactose), adjusted to an initial density of OD600 = 0.2, and incubated at 30 °C with shaking for 36 h to induce transgene expression. Following induction, cultures were normalized to OD600 = 1. Aliquots were incubated at 4 °C for 48 h to evaluate low-temperature tolerance, while incubation at 28 °C for 48 h served as the optimal-temperature control. After treatment, cultures were prepared as the undiluted suspension (OD600 = 1) and serial 10-fold dilutions (10− 1 to 10− 4). For spot assays, 3 µL of each dilution was spotted onto SD/-Ura agar plates containing 2% (w/v) glucose. Plates were imaged after incubation, and growth phenotypes were compared among transformants. All assays were performed with at least three independent biological replicates, and representative images are shown.

  Growth curves were measured at fifteen time points (0–14 h) with three biological replicates per treatment. From each individual growth curve, key growth kinetic parameters were calculated, including lag time, maximum specific growth rate (µmax), doubling time, area under the growth curve (AUC), and carrying capacity. Lag time was defined as the time required to reach 10% of the total increase in optical density and was estimated by linear interpolation. The maximum specific growth rate (µmax) was determined as the maximum slope of ln(OD) versus time between consecutive measurements, and doubling time was calculated as ln(2)/µmax. The AUC was calculated using the trapezoidal method over the entire experimental time window, and carrying capacity was approximated as the mean OD value at the final two time points.

  Statistical analyses were performed using a two-factor analysis of variance (ANOVA) with temperature condition (CK vs. Cold treatment) and gene treatment as fixed effects. When significant effects were detected, pairwise comparisons between cold treatment and CK were conducted within each gene using estimated marginal means. To account for multiple testing across growth parameters, p values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Differences were considered statistically significant at adjusted p < 0.05. All analyses were performed in R.


### Real-time qRT-PCR verification
  To validate the expression profiles of PsiWRKY genes obtained from transcriptome analysis, quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed. Total RNA was extracted from three independent biological replicates of same leaf samples used for RNA sequencing. Equal amounts of RNA from these replicates were pooled to generate a representative cDNA sample, then treated with DNase I (Takara, Japan), and reverse-transcribed using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, Japan). Gene-specific primers for PsiWRKY genes are listed in Supplementary Table S1. qRT-PCR was conducted using SYBR® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Takara, Japan) on a CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA), with Actin serving as the internal reference gene. Each pooled cDNA sample was analyzed with three technical replicates. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCT method. The qRT-PCR results were used to validate the expression trends observed in RNA-seq data. For validation, relative expression levels (2−ΔΔCT) were compared with RNA-seq expression (FPKM) values across the tested genes, and the consistency between the two datasets was assessed using Pearson correlation analysis (p < 0.05) [24].


### Statistical analysis
  All statistical analyses were performed in R v4.4.1 unless otherwise specified. Significance thresholds, correction methods, and specific statistical procedures are described in the corresponding sections above. Unless otherwise noted, p < 0.05 (or adjusted FDR < 0.05) was considered significant.


## Results

### Identification and physicochemical characterization of WRKY genes in king grass
  To comprehensively identify WRKY transcription factors in king grass, we performed BLASTP searches using WRKY proteins from sorghum (Sorghum bicolor), rice (Oryza sativa), maize (Zea mays), and wheat (Triticum aestivum) against the king grass reference transcriptome, yielding 177 candidate sequences (E-value < 1e-5). In parallel, a HMMER search using the WRKY domain model (Pfam: PF03106) identified 91 potential sequences. After removing redundancies, 107 unique candidates were obtained and further validated with InterProScan, which confirmed complete WRKY domains in 89 sequences. Of the 18 excluded sequences, nine borderline cases underwent manual curation; four were retained based on complementary evidence from SMART and zinc finger structures (C2H2 or C2HC), resulting in 93 candidates. These were cross-validated using Pfam and the NCBI Conserved Domain Database (CDD), leading to the removal of 31 redundant or incomplete sequences. Ultimately, 62 high-confidence, non-redundant PsiWRKY genes were identified and designated PsiWRKY1-PsiWRKY62 (Supplementary Figure S1). All PsiWRKYs contained at least one conserved WRKY domain, whose integrity was confirmed across multiple databases.

  The 62 PsiWRKY proteins showed considerable diversity in physicochemical properties. Protein lengths ranged from 166 aa (PsiWRKY49) to 613 aa (PsiWRKY31/59), with molecular weights from 17.94 to 65.76 kDa (average 38.27 kDa). Theoretical pI values varied between 4.79 (PsiWRKY14) and 10.98 (PsiWRKY22), classifying 28 proteins as acidic (pI < 7) and 34 as basic (pI > 7). All PsiWRKYs were predicted to be hydrophilic (negative GRAVY values), with PsiWRKY24 showing the highest hydrophilicity (-1.128). Based on the instability index, only PsiWRKY62 was predicted to be stable (< 40), while others were potentially unstable in vitro. The aliphatic index ranged from 42.71 (PsiWRKY27) to 79.97 (PsiWRKY43), suggesting variable thermal stability. These physicochemical profiles (Supplementary Table S2) provide a basis for further functional characterization and group-specific classification of the PsiWRKY family.


### Domain evolution and motif-based functional divergence of WRKY genes in king grass
  To investigate the structural features and evolutionary relationships of WRKY transcription factors in king grass, we analyzed the conserved WRKY domains and zinc finger motifs of 62 PsiWRKY proteins using the Pfam and SMART databases. Based on the number of WRKY domains and zinc finger types, these genes were classified into three groups: Group I (11 members) with two WRKYGQK motifs and a C2H2-type zinc finger, Group II (35 members) with one WRKYGQK motif and a C2H2-type zinc finger, and Group III (16 members) harboring one WRKYGQK motif and a C2HC-type zinc finger (Supplementary Figure S2), following the classification system of Eulgem et al. [25]. Most PsiWRKYs exhibited classical domain structures, although several sequences carried mutations in the conserved WRKY motif. Specifically, PsiWRKY36, PsiWRKY39, and PsiWRKY42 harbored a substitution from WRKYGQK to WRKYGKK. Despite such variations, 59 out of 62 (95.16%) PsiWRKY proteins retained the highly conserved WRKYG(X)K core and appropriate zinc finger structures.

  To confirm the subgroup classification, we reconstructed a phylogenetic tree including both PsiWRKY proteins and representative Arabidopsis WRKYs (Fig. 1). The PsiWRKY genes were distributed across all major groups and five canonical subgroups (IIa-IIe), consistent with the structural features described in Arabidopsis. This phylogenetic analysis further supported the classification, showing that Groups I and II clustered into adjacent clades with a relatively close evolutionary relationship, whereas Group III formed a distinct and separate lineage. These subgroups formed well-defined subclades in the phylogenetic tree, with IId and IIe showing the closest relationship as sister branches. IIa and IIb clustered together but were positioned farther from IIc, suggesting possible functional divergence of IIc from the other subgroups.

PsiWRKYs and AtWRKYs were classified into three main groups (I-III), with Group II further divided into five subgroups (IIa-IIe)

  To further investigate structural diversity, we analyzed motif composition and gene structures, identifying 10 distinct motifs. Motifs 1, 2, and 4 were nearly ubiquitous, whereas motifs 3 and 7 were specific to Group I, serving as distinguishing features. Motif 6 occurred in IIa, IIb, and select Group I and III members (e.g., PsiWRKY28 and 54), while motif 9 was unique to IIa, highlighting motif-driven divergence among WRKY subgroups (Fig. 2A). Beyond WRKY domains, several PsiWRKYs contained additional transcription factor superfamilies, suggesting multifunctionality. The bZIP superfamily appeared in PsiWRKY1, 13, 35, 58, and 60, four of which responded to cold stress, implying roles in abiotic adaptation. PsiWRKY21 and 23 carried the PRK12323 superfamily, PsiWRKY20 featured a Herpes_pp85 domain likely derived from viral horizontal transfer, and all Group IId members harbored the Plant_zn superfamily, suggesting unique structural and functional specialization (Fig. 2B).

B Motif composition of PsiWRKYs mapped onto a phylogenetic tree. Conserved motifs are represented by colored boxes. C Conserved domain architecture of PsiWRKY proteins.

  Subcellular localization prediction further revealed that 47 out of 62 PsiWRKY proteins (75.81%) were predominantly localized in the nucleus, consistent with their function as transcriptional regulators. In addition, 10 proteins (16.13%) were predicted to localize to the chloroplasts, 4 (6.45%) to the mitochondria, and 1 (1.61%) to the cytoplasm (Supplementary Figure S3 and Table S2). These non-nuclear WRKYs may be involved in organelle-specific stress signaling or retrograde regulatory mechanisms, providing additional layers of functional complexity.


### Expression profiling of PsiWRKY genes under cold stress
  To explore the temporal regulation of WRKY transcription factors in response to cold stress, we performed transcriptome sequencing of king grass at nine time points following exposure to 4 °C. Among the actively expressed genes (average FPKM > 1 across nine time points), 48 PsiWRKYs were selected for time-series clustering using Short Time-series Expression Miner (STEM), with the number of clusters set to 10. STEM grouped the 48 genes into four distinct expression modules. To validate this classification, we cross-referenced our previously published dataset in which 26,839 genes were clustered into nine modules using the k-means algorithm. Mapping the same 48 PsiWRKY genes to these clusters showed perfect alignment with the four STEM-defined modules, reinforcing the consistency and robustness of our clustering strategy.

  The 48 PsiWRKY genes exhibited four major temporal expression profiles (Fig. 3A). Cluster 1 showed early transient induction, peaking at 1 h and declining rapidly, suggesting potential involvement in early cold-responsive signaling. Cluster 2 followed a U-shaped pattern, with initial high expression, repression between 3 and 12 h, and recovery at 24–72 h, consistent with delayed transcriptional response patterns. Cluster 3 showed sustained upregulation throughout cold treatment, peaking at 48–72 h, consistent with potential roles in sustained cold response. Cluster 4 genes were strongly repressed from the onset and remained low, potentially reflecting transcriptional programs suppressed during cold stress.

Four distinct expression clusters were identified using STEM and K-means clustering, each representing a unique temporal expression profile.   Subgroup distribution among clusters revealed distinct functional patterns (Fig. 3B). PsiWRKY genes from Groups I and III were broadly distributed across all clusters, suggesting roles in multiple phases of the cold response, from early perception to late adaptation. Groups IIa and IIc were predominantly enriched in Cluster 3, exhibiting continuous upregulation and peaking at later stages, consistent with potential association with sustained defense-related transcriptional responses. In contrast, most genes from Groups IId and IIe were clustered in Cluster 4, with strong and persistent downregulation, possibly associated with growth- and metabolism-related programs suppressed under cold stress.

  To further clarify the roles of PsiWRKY genes in cold adaptation, we focused on those with marked transcriptional induction. Based on their structural classification and temporal expression trends, we hypothesized that strongly upregulated genes may represent candidate cold-responsive regulators. A total of 29 PsiWRKY genes were identified with at least two-fold upregulation at one or more time points compared to 0 h. Z-score standardized heatmap visualization (Fig. 3C) showed that most reached peak expression at 48 h (10 genes) or 72 h (15 genes), indicating coordinated late-stage activation during cold acclimation.

  To validate the RNA-seq data, we selected 15 PsiWRKY genes with distinct expression profiles across cold stress time points and performed qRT-PCR analysis. For each gene, the temporal expression trend obtained by qRT-PCR closely mirrored the RNA-seq-derived FPKM values, with a high overall correlation (R² = 0.742; Supplementary Figure S4). Most genes showed consistent upregulation or downregulation patterns across the time series, with peak expression typically observed at 48–72 h. This high concordance confirms the reliability of our transcriptome data and supports the robustness of the identified expression modules.


### Functional enrichment and regulatory pathways of PsiWRKYs
  GO enrichment analysis of these 29 highly upregulated PsiWRKY genes revealed significant overrepresentation in molecular functions related to transcriptional regulation. Notably, terms such as “nucleic acid binding transcription factor activity” and “transcription factor activity specific DNA binding” were enriched (Fig. 3D), consistent with the canonical transcription factor-associated functions of WRKY proteins. These findings suggest that strongly induced PsiWRKYs may represent candidate components of cold-responsive transcriptional regulatory networks. Interestingly, enrichment was also observed in biological process categories such as “negative regulation of gibberellic acid mediated signaling pathway” and “negative regulation of response to stimulus”. These results suggest that in addition to activating stress-related genes, certain PsiWRKYs may be associated with regulatory pathways linked to negative regulation of gibberellin (GA)-related signaling or other growth-associated pathways, potentially reflecting broader transcriptional shifts toward stress-responsive states under cold conditions. The enrichment of GA-related regulatory terms suggested a possible association between induced PsiWRKYs and hormone-responsive signaling networks, however, potential ABA-GA crosstalk under cold stress required further investigation.


### Network associations between cold-responsive PsiWRKYs, GA signaling, and sugar metabolism
  Given that cold-responsive PsiWRKYs were significantly enriched in binding-related GO terms, particularly nucleic acid binding, and showed a tendency to negatively regulate gibberellin (GA) signaling, we further integrated previous transcriptomic data to investigate their potential associations with cold-responsive regulatory networks. In our earlier analysis, k-means clustering revealed that genes upregulated during the late phase of cold treatment (after 12 h) were significantly enriched in glycolysis/gluconeogenesis pathways. Notably, PsiWRKYs classified into Cluster 3 in the current study exhibited expression profiles highly consistent with those late-phase genes. This pattern suggested that these PsiWRKYs may represent candidate nodes linking GA-related signaling and sugar metabolism-associated transcriptional responses, including genes involved in glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and Calvin cycle-related pathways.

  To further evaluate this possibility, we constructed a weighted gene co-expression network (WGCNA) using all 26,839 genes, with the soft-thresholding power set to 12. A focused subnetwork was then extracted, which included 29 late-upregulated PsiWRKY genes and functionally annotated genes involved in GA signaling and sugar metabolism. Among them, GA signaling-related genes were found in three distinct modules (Fig. 4A, C), comprising a total of 35 genes, while sugar metabolism-related genes were distributed across four modules (Fig. 4B, D, E), encompassing 131 genes.

A, C Modules within the PsiWRKY-GA signaling subnetwork, comprising a total of 35 genes. B, D, E Modules within the PsiWRKY-sugar metabolism subnetwork, encompassing 131 genes.

  Figure 4 illustrates the co-expression relationships among genes within these modules. In the GA-related modules, PsiWRKY16, PsiWRKY49, and PsiWRKY50 were densely connected with key GA signaling genes such as GID1 and DELLA, suggesting potential co-expression-based associations with GA-related signaling components. In the sugar metabolism-related modules, PsiWRKY50, PsiWRKY16, and PsiWRKY37 exhibited strong co-expression with genes encoding key metabolic enzymes, including GAPDH (glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase) and PRK (phosphoribulokinase). qRT-PCR validation of four representative GA signaling- and sugar metabolism-related genes showed expression trends consistent with the RNA-seq data (Supplementary Figure S5). These findings collectively supported a hypothesis that PsiWRKYs may be associated with broader cold-responsive co-expression networks linking hormone-related signaling and metabolic pathways.


### Yeast-based heterologous assessment of PsiWRKY16 and PsiWRKY50 under low-temperature stress
  Given the prominent co-expression connectivity of PsiWRKY16 and PsiWRKY50 within cold-responsive network modules, we next employed a yeast-based low-temperature tolerance assay to assess whether heterologous expression of these two candidates is associated with altered cellular performance under cold stress. PsiWRKY16 and PsiWRKY50 were individually cloned into the galactose-inducible expression vector pYES-his and transformed into the yeast strain WAT11, with WAT11 harboring the empty pYES-his vector used as the control. Under optimal growth conditions (28 °C), yeast transformants expressing PsiWRKY16 or PsiWRKY50 displayed growth comparable to the empty-vector control, with no detectable growth defects or proliferation differences. In contrast, following low-temperature treatment (4 °C for 48 h), spot assays showed that both WAT11 (pYES-his-PsiWRKY16 and pYES-his-PsiWRKY50) exhibited improved growth and recovery relative to the control WAT11 (pYES-his), as reflected by enhanced colony formation across serial dilutions (Fig. 5A). These observations suggested that heterologous expression of PsiWRKY16 and PsiWRKY50 improved yeast growth performance under low-temperature conditions.

  Fig. 5Yeast-based heterologous assessment of PsiWRKY16 and PsiWRKY50 under low-temperature stress. A Spot assays of yeast strain WAT11 expressing PsiWRKY16 or PsiWRKY50 and the empty-vector control under optimal temperature (28 °C) and low-temperature treatment. Serial 10-fold dilutions (10⁰~10-⁴) are shown. B OD600-based growth curves of PsiWRKY16- and PsiWRKY50-expressing yeast under optimal and low-temperature conditions. C Growth kinetic parameters derived from growth curves, including area under the curve (AUC), carrying capacity, doubling time, lag phase duration, and maximum specific growth rate (µmax). Data are presented as mean ± SD. Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < 0.05, *p < 0.01, **p < 0.001)

  Consistent with these observations, growth dynamics analyses revealed no significant differences among transformants at 28 °C, whereas PsiWRKY16- or PsiWRKY50-expressing yeast displayed improved growth performance under low-temperature stress compared with the empty-vector control (Fig. 5B). During the growth initiation phase, low-temperature treatment did not significantly affect the lag time of either transgenic strain (PsiWRKY16: padj = 0.165; PsiWRKY50: padj = 0.742), indicating that heterologous expression of WRKY genes did not markedly alter growth initiation in this system under cold conditions (Fig. 5C). With respect to growth rate-related parameters, the doubling time of PsiWRKY16-expressing yeast was significantly reduced under low-temperature conditions by approximately 0.56 h relative to optimal temperature (padj = 0.028), suggesting enhanced cellular proliferation efficiency in yeast under low-temperature conditions. In contrast, no significant differences were detected for PsiWRKY50-expressing yeast in either doubling time or maximum specific growth rate (µmax) (padj > 0.05). Importantly, low-temperature treatment significantly increased both the area under the growth curve (AUC) and carrying capacity of the two transgenic yeast strains. In detail, PsiWRKY16-expressing yeast displayed a significant AUC increase of 26.20 (padj = 3.65 × 10−⁹), accompanied by an increase in carrying capacity of 3.39 (padj = 5.85 × 10−⁶). Similarly, PsiWRKY50-expressing yeast exhibited a significant AUC increase of 14.45 (padj = 1.98 × 10−⁷), along with an increase in carrying capacity of 1.43 (padj = 0.0012). Collectively, these results indicated that heterologous expression of PsiWRKY16 and PsiWRKY50 is associated with enhanced yeast fitness under low-temperature stress in this heterologous system, providing indirect functional support for their potential involvement in cold-stress responses.


### Molecular docking between PsiWRKYs and key enzymes of the calvin cycle
  Given the essential role of the Calvin cycle in sugar metabolism, we investigated whether cold-responsive WRKY transcription factors exhibit structurally plausible associations with representative Calvin-cycle enzymes, thereby exploring a potential structural hypothesis linking WRKY-mediated cold responses and metabolic pathways. Based on heterologous yeast growth assays indicating altered low-temperature-associated fitness, we performed protein-protein docking between 48 expressed PsiWRKY proteins and three Calvin cycle enzymes (Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), fructose-bisphosphate aldolase (FBA), and Phosphoribulokinase (PRK) shown in Supplementary Figure S6.

  We emphasize that the pTM score primarily reflects the confidence of the predicted complex structure rather than binding affinity. Nevertheless, differentially expressed WRKYs (≥ 2-fold change at 48 h cold treatment) displayed significantly higher mean pTM scores (pTM mean = 0.60) than non-differentially expressed WRKYs (pTM mean = 0.49, t-test: t = 3.47, p = 0.0017, Supplementary Table S3), suggesting a greater prevalence of structurally plausible predicted complexes among cold-responsive WRKYs.

  Focusing on the 31 differentially expressed PsiWRKYs (Supplementary Figure S7), all exhibited predicted structural compatibility with at least one enzyme with pTM scores ≥ 0.5. PsiWRKY16 and PsiWRKY55 showed the highest structural confidence across PRK, GAPDH, and FBA with pTM scores of 0.75, 0.78, and 0.78 for PsiWRKY16, and 0.77, 0.79, and 0.79 for PsiWRKY55, respectively. PsiWRKY50, which was selected for yeast-based heterologous assessment based on its hub-like connectivity in co-expression networks, also exhibited moderate-to-high docking confidence with Calvin cycle enzymes (mean pTM = 0.63) (Figure 6).

  Fig. 6Molecular docking of cold-responsive WRKY transcription factors with key Calvin cycle enzymes in king grass. A-C Docking models of PsiWRKY16 with GAPDH (A), FBA (B), and PRK (C). D-F Docking models of PsiWRKY55 with GAPDH (D), FBA (E), and PRK (F). Left panels show overall docking conformations; right panels highlight key interacting residues at the binding interfaces. All models yielded pTM scores > 0.75, indicating high-confidence structural predictions based on AlphaFold2-Multimer

  Residue-level interface analysis provided additional structural context for these predicted associations. In the PsiWRKY16-GAPDH complex, residues within the WRKY DNA-binding domain of PsiWRKY16 (Arg127, Lys128, Glu133, Leu135, and Arg145) were predicted to interact with acidic residues at positions 280–282 of GAPDH. These residues lie within the tetramerization interface of GAPDH (172–290) rather than its catalytic pocket (155–183), suggesting that the predicted association is unlikely to involve the catalytic pocket, but may be related to oligomerization-associated interfaces. A similar pattern was observed for the PsiWRKY16-FBA complex, where Arg127, Lys128, Tyr129, Arg145, and His150 were predicted to contact residues between positions 146 and 187 of FBA, located within its intersubunit interface region (139–272), which is critical for oligomerization. This spatial arrangement indicates that PsiWRKY16 may associate with structural interfaces of Calvin cycle enzymes rather than directly occupying catalytic sites, raising the possibility that such predicted associations, if biologically relevant, could relate to enzyme complex assembly rather than direct catalytic-site occupation. For PsiWRKY55, predicted docking residues were primarily located within its second WRKY domain (358–430) and interacted with residues clustered around positions 113–169 of FBA, also within the intersubunit interface region, suggesting that PsiWRKY55 was similarly predicted to associate with structural interface regions rather than catalytic sites.


### Intrinsic disorder and MoRF analyses revealed structural flexibility of PsiWRKY16 and PsiWRKY55
  To further evaluate the structural basis underlying the potential multifunctionality of PsiWRKY proteins, we analyzed intrinsic disorder and Molecular Recognition Features (MoRFs) in PsiWRKY16 and PsiWRKY55 using two complementary PONDR predictors (VLXT and XL1_XT) together with IUPred2A-ANCHOR. Both proteins exhibited substantial intrinsically disordered regions (IDR), a structural feature commonly associated with regulatory flexibility in transcription factors, but their disorder-associated architectures differed markedly (Supplementary Figure S8).

  PsiWRKY16 exhibited a high-confidence intrinsically disordered segment spanning residues 186–226 (average PONDR-VLXT score = 0.7938), which overlapped with the ANCHOR-positive MoRF region at residues 207–230. This IDR-MoRF module was positioned immediately downstream of the WRKY DNA-binding core involved in the predicted docking interface (121–180 aa), suggesting a core-adjacent flexible recognition module. For PsiWRKY55, the highest-scoring IDR spanned residues 260–358 (average PONDR-VLXT score = 0.9013) and overlapped extensively with the ANCHOR-positive MoRF region at residues 275–382. Notably, this MoRF region extended from the IDR into the N-terminal portion of the second WRKY domain, where the predicted docking residues were primarily located (358–430 aa). Thus, PsiWRKY55 displayed a contiguous IDR-MoRF-WRKY2 domain architecture.

  Together, these results indicated that both PsiWRKY16 and PsiWRKY55 contain IDR-MoRF modules positioned adjacent to WRKY-domain-associated docking regions, although their spatial organization differs. PsiWRKY16 showed a WRKY core-adjacent IDR-MoRF module, whereas PsiWRKY55 showed a more continuous IDR-MoRF-WRKY2 docking-domain arrangement.


### Integrated Cis-Element and target gene analysis
  To explore the potential regulatory features of WRKY transcription factors in the cold stress response of king grass, we analyzed the chromosomal distribution, promoter cis-elements, and regulatory network integration of PsiWRKY genes. Using the reference genomes of both parental species, Pennisetum purpureum (elephant grass) and Pennisetum glaucum (pearl millet), we mapped homologous PsiWRKY sequences with > 98% identity. In P. purpureum, 26 PsiWRKY homologs were located, including three highly conserved gene pairs co-localized on homologous chromosomes. In P. glaucum, 25 PsiWRKY genes were identified, with eight exhibiting duplicated chromosomal localization, suggesting potential gene expansion or copy number variation (Fig. 7). In total, 37 PsiWRKYs were mapped in at least one parent, and 14 were consistently located in both genomes, indicating that the WRKY gene family is largely conserved during hybridization.

  Fig. 7Chromosomal localization of PsiWRKY homologs in the parental genomes of king grass. Homologous PsiWRKY gene sequences (>98% identity) were mapped onto the reference genomes of Pennisetum purpureum (elephant grass, top panel) and Pennisetum glaucum (pearl millet, bottom panel). PsiWRKY gene names are labeled, with gene pairs mapped at similar positions shown in parentheses

  Cis-element analysis of the 2,000 bp upstream promoter regions of these 37 genes revealed a total of 2,183 regulatory elements categorized into four functional groups: abiotic/biotic stress response (20.41%), phytohormone response (33.63%), plant growth and development (41.35%), and light responsiveness (4.61%) (Fig. 8A). Among the phytohormone response elements, the most abundant ABRE (27.49%, 207/753) was involved in the abscisic acid response, followed by CGTCA-motif and TGACG-motif (19.92%, 150/753 in both) in the methyl jasmonate (MeJA) response, and as-1 involved in the salicylic acid reaction (19.92%, 150/753). Importantly, DRE (dehydration-responsive element) motifs (key targets of the CBF cold response pathway) were detected in 18 of the 21 differentially expressed PsiWRKYs identified after 12 h of cold treatment. Statistical analysis using the chi-square test revealed that DRE elements were significantly enriched in the cold-induced group compared to non-differentially expressed genes (χ² = 11.75, df = 1, p = 0.00061). This non-random distribution strongly implicates DRE motifs in mediating cold-responsive transcriptional activation of PsiWRKYs, consistent with the known role of DRE as a core cis-element in the CBF signaling cascade.

  Building on this observation, we sought to determine whether WRKY-mediated ABA signaling and CBF cold response pathway converge on common downstream targets. Because PsiWRKY regulatory information is not available in PlantTFDB, we used Setaria viridis (foxtail millet) as a reference species. BLAST alignment confirmed that all 37 PsiWRKY proteins had high-confidence homologs in S. viridis (identity > 80%, E-value < 1e-5). Fifteen WRKYs associated with ABRE (ABA-responsive) elements were predicted to have 928 putative downstream target genes. Parallel analysis of 49 Setaria homologs of PsiCBF genes, previously characterized in our laboratory (unpublished data), identified 2,472 downstream target genes. A total of 193 genes were predicted as shared putative targets of WRKYs and CBFs. Hypergeometric testing demonstrated that this overlap was significantly higher than expected by chance (p < 0.001), suggesting a non-random overlap between putative WRKY and CBF target sets. GO enrichment analysis of the 193 overlapping targets revealed strong associations with functions relevant to cold stress tolerance, showing significant enrichment in the glucose metabolic process (GO:0006006), response to oxidative stress (GO:0006979), and lipid metabolic process (GO:0006629), all of which are known to be critical for cold stress adaptation. These findings supported a hypothetical regulatory framework in which cold-responsive PsiWRKYs may be associated with ABA- and CBF-related signaling pathways. The overlap between putative WRKY and CBF targets suggests potential convergence on genes involved in glucose metabolism, oxidative stress response, and lipid metabolism (Fig. 8B). This convergence suggested that PsiWRKYs may act as candidate nodes linking hormone-related and environmental stress-responsive transcriptional networks during cold acclimation in king grass.


## Discussion

### WRKY evolution reveals adaptive regulatory strategies in perennial grasses
  Perennial forage grasses originating from tropical regions frequently encounter fluctuating temperature regimes when cultivated beyond their native environments, imposing strong selective pressure on regulatory networks controlling stress adaptation. The WRKY transcription factor family represents one of the most evolutionarily dynamic regulatory systems enabling plants to cope with such environmental challenges [26]. Comparative analyses across angiosperms have demonstrated that expansion and diversification of WRKY genes commonly accompany ecological adaptation and stress specialization [27, 28]. In king grass, the identification of 62 PsiWRKY members places this species within the typical range reported for monocot plants. Although similar gene numbers have been reported in Triticum urartu of 62 [29], Prunus mume of 58 [30] and Prunus persica of 58 [31], the PsiWRKY repertoire exhibits distinctive subgroup enrichment and domain architectures, suggesting lineage-specific regulatory innovation.

  Unlike rice and Arabidopsis, where WRKY family diversification is largely associated with pathogen defense specialization [25, 32], our analyses indicate that WRKY evolution in king grass may be more strongly shaped by adaptation to abiotic stress conditions, particularly low temperature. The enrichment of cold-responsive genes within the Group IId clade suggests that this subgroup functions as a regulatory hub during cold acclimation. In addition, several Group IIa members retain the WRKY domain while acquiring an additional bZIP domain, a rare structural configuration implying enhanced capacity for integrating transcriptional regulation with hormonal signaling [11]. The presence of additional non-WRKY domains, including Plant_zn and viral-like Herpes_pp85 motifs, further supports the possibility of neofunctionalization driven by long-term environmental selection [33]. Similar evolutionary events have been documented in wheat, where motif gain or loss promotes regulatory innovation and stress-biased expression under drought and cold conditions [34, 35], indicating that structural diversification of WRKY proteins represents a conserved mechanism for environmental adaptation across crops.


### WRKY-associated hormonal signaling and metabolic adjustment during cold acclimation
  Cold acclimation in plants commonly involves coordinated suppression of growth-promoting pathways together with activation of protective metabolic programs. WRKY transcription factors have emerged as important regulators of this transition, frequently functioning as repressors of gibberellin (GA)-mediated growth pathways under abiotic stress, thereby facilitating a physiological shift from growth to survival mode [14, 36]. The close association between WRKY factors and DELLA proteins, key negative regulators of GA signaling, supports a conserved mechanism in which stress-induced DELLA accumulation redirects cellular resources toward stress protection rather than growth promotion [37, 38].

  In this context, PsiWRKY transcription factors in king grass may be associated with a regulatory framework linking hormonal signaling with metabolic adjustment during cold acclimation. In GA-related modules, PsiWRKY16, PsiWRKY49, and PsiWRKY50 were densely connected with DELLA. The co-expression association between several PsiWRKYs and DELLA-related GA signaling components is consistent with a possible growth-to-stress transition mechanism previously described in Arabidopsis and rice [39, 40]. Such coordination reflects a broader strategy whereby transcriptional regulators modulate hormonal balance to optimize stress tolerance.

  Beyond hormonal control, increasing evidence indicates that WRKY proteins also contribute to metabolic reprogramming under abiotic stress. Several highly expressed PsiWRKYs (particularly those in Cluster 3) exhibited strong co-expression with glycolysis and Calvin cycle genes, including GAPDH and PRK. WRKY-mediated regulation of sugar signaling and carbohydrate metabolism has been reported in multiple species, where modulation of glycolysis and carbon metabolism supports energy homeostasis during environmental stress [41, 42]. Similar regulatory patterns observed in grapevine and tea plant demonstrate that WRKY-controlled sugar metabolism enhances osmotic adjustment and stabilizes cellular energy status under adverse conditions [43, 44]. The observed co-expression patterns suggest a potential link between hormone-related regulation and carbohydrate metabolism rather than independent regulatory processes. The convergence of hormonal repression (via GA-DELLA) and metabolic reprogramming (via sugar pathway genes) within the WRKY-centered regulatory network points to a multifaceted mechanism in which WRKYs may contribute to balancing growth-related and metabolic responses under cold stress.


### Structural associations between PsiWRKYs and metabolic enzymes
  Traditionally, WRKY transcription factors have been regarded as DNA-binding regulators that function through recognition of W-box elements in target gene promoters. However, accumulating evidence indicates that TFs may also participate in regulatory processes through protein-protein interactions, potentially influencing regulatory processes [45, 46]. In the present study, molecular docking analysis predicted structurally plausible associations between cold-responsive PsiWRKY proteins, particularly PsiWRKY16 and PsiWRKY55, and key Calvin cycle enzymes including GAPDH, PRK, and FBA.

  Comparable findings in other plant species provide biological precedent supporting this expanded functional paradigm. In Arabidopsis, AtWRKY33 directly interacts with MAP kinases MPK3/MPK6 to regulate downstream defense-associated metabolic pathways [47], while AtWRKY53 participates in reactive oxygen metabolism through interactions with regulatory components affecting enzymatic activity and metabolic homeostasis [48]. In addition, WRKY proteins frequently form complexes with VQ motif-containing proteins, demonstrating that WRKY regulatory activity is extensively shaped by protein interaction networks [49]. These studies indicate that WRKY proteins can participate in protein-interaction networks; however, whether similar interactions occur between PsiWRKYs and metabolic enzymes in king grass remains to be experimentally tested.

  Although direct interactions between PsiWRKYs and metabolic enzymes have not yet been experimentally demonstrated in king grass, our results suggest that PsiWRKY proteins may be considered candidate transcriptional regulators with predicted structural compatibility with sugar metabolic enzymes, which provides a hypothesis for possible links between transcriptional regulation and metabolic adjustment under cold stress. Phylogenetic analysis further supports this functional hypothesis, as the closest rice homologs of PsiWRKY16 and PsiWRKY55 (OS01g74140.1 and OS05g39720.1) belong to WRKY clades associated with carbohydrate regulatory processes. In rice, WRKY proteins have been shown to regulate carbohydrate mobilization through protein-protein interactions with transcriptional regulators such as OsWRKY51 and OsGAMYB, which modulate α-amylase expression and starch degradation during stress responses [50]. This conserved interaction-based regulatory framework also suggests that PsiWRKY proteins may participate in monosaccharide regulation via protein interaction-mediated regulatory mechanisms.


### Hormonal and environmental signal convergence in PsiWRKY-associated regulatory networks
  Accumulating evidence indicates that plant cold acclimation is not governed by a single signaling pathway but instead relies on coordinated integration of environmental perception and phytohormone regulatory networks. In present study, the enrichment of ABRE and DRE motifs within the promoter regions of PsiWRKY genes suggests that these transcription factors may receive regulatory inputs from both ABA signaling and the canonical CBF-mediated cold response pathway. A comparable ABRE-DRE regulatory mechanism has been experimentally demonstrated in the A. thaliana RD29A promoter, where ABA-dependent and CBF-dependent pathways jointly determine stress-induced transcriptional activation levels [51, 52]. This regulatory configuration implies that WRKYs may function as convergence nodes linking multiple stress signaling pathways rather than acting solely as downstream responders within a single pathway.

  Similar integrative roles of WRKYs have been reported across diverse plant species. In Arabidopsis, WRKY members participate in the crosstalk between ABA signaling and cold-response regulatory networks [53]. In rice, WRKY proteins coordinate stress adaptation with growth regulation through modulation of phytohormone balance, thereby enabling dynamic adjustment between developmental processes and environmental stress responses [54]. Consistent with these observations, our analysis revealed predicted shared downstream targets between putative PsiWRKY and CBF regulatory pathways, with enriched functions primarily associated with carbohydrate metabolism, redox homeostasis, and membrane stability, which are considered as core determinants of cold tolerance. Compared with annual crops, tropical perennial forage grasses are likely to rely more strongly on maintenance of metabolic stability rather than rapid induction of defense responses during prolonged low-temperature exposure. Therefore, PsiWRKY-associated multi-signal convergence may represent a particularly important adaptive strategy in king grass.

  Notably, WRKY regulatory networks appear to participate not only in stress signal activation but also in growth repression mechanisms. Previous studies demonstrated that OsWRKY53 suppresses gibberellin biosynthesis in rice, thereby attenuating GA-dependent growth processes and enhancing stress tolerance [54]. Similarly, AtWRKY75 in Arabidopsis mediates a functional transition from growth to defense by coordinating nutrient acquisition and hormonal signaling balance [55]. Together, these findings supported the widely proposed growth-defense transition model, in which plants reallocate resources under stress conditions through antagonistic regulation of GA signaling pathways [56]. Our results suggested that PsiWRKYs may participate in a comparable regulatory framework in king grass, potentially linking cold adaptation and growth-related responses through putative ABA-GA signaling crosstalk.

  Collectively, PsiWRKYs may represent candidate regulatory nodes connecting environmental sensing, hormonal signaling, and metabolic regulation during cold stress. Although similar integrative regulatory strategies have been described in model plants and major crops, systematic evidence in tropical perennial grasses remains limited. The present study therefore provides new insight into how perennial forage species may achieve dynamic balance between growth persistence and stress resilience under fluctuating climatic conditions.


### Implications for cold-tolerant breeding of king grass
  Beyond mechanistic insights, our findings provide practical implications for breeding strategies aimed at improving cold tolerance in king grass. Traditional breeding approaches have focused primarily on morphological selection or physiological screening, which are often inefficient for complex stress traits. The identification of candidate hub-like genesf such as PsiWRKY16, PsiWRKY50, and PsiWRKY49 offers candidate molecular targets for marker-assisted selection, genomic prediction, or future gene-editing strategies.

  Importantly, our findings suggest that cold tolerance in king grass is unlikely to be governed by single stress-responsive genes but instead depends on coordinated regulation of hormonal signaling and metabolic homeostasis. This observation supports a shift from gene-centered breeding toward regulatory-network-based improvement, in which WRKY-associated modules may be further explored to simultaneously optimize stress resilience and biomass productivity. Such an approach is particularly relevant for tropical perennial forage grasses, where maintenance of growth capacity during episodic cold events is as critical as survival itself.

  From an applied perspective, WRKY-centered regulatory markers may facilitate early selection of genotypes with improved overwintering capacity, stable regrowth, and sustained biomass accumulation under marginal climates. Integrating these molecular regulators into breeding pipelines may therefore accelerate geographical expansion of king grass cultivation into subtropical and temperate regions, enhancing its utilization as both a high-yield forage resource and a bioenergy crop under changing climatic conditions.


### Limitations and future directions
  While this study provides a comprehensive analysis of WRKY transcription factors in king grass and proposes novel mechanisms of cold adaptation, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our conclusions regarding protein-protein interactions are based on in silico molecular docking, which, although informative, require experimental validation through biochemical or genetic assays such as yeast two-hybrid, co-immunoprecipitation, or CRISPR-Cas9-mediated gene knockouts. Second, the regulatory roles of PsiWRKYs in sugar metabolism and hormonal signaling were inferred from transcriptomic and promoter motif analyses; future work should include functional verification in vivo to confirm their direct targets. Additionally, due to the absence of a high-quality king grass reference genome, our reliance on elephant grass genome annotations may have introduced limitations in gene structure prediction and regulatory network reconstruction. Expanding genomic resources and establishing transformation systems for king grass will be critical for deeper functional studies in the future.
