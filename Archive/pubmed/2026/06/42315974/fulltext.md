# A four-gene signature for diagnosis of acute kidney injury following kidney transplantation


## Abstract
  Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a common and severe complication following renal transplantation, largely driven by ischemia–reperfusion injury (IRI). However, reliable biomarkers for post-transplant AKI remain unavailable. In this study, we analyzed transcriptomic datasets from multiple cohorts to identify robust gene signatures associated with transplant-related AKI. Weighted gene co-expression network and differential expression analyses in the discovery cohort revealed 222 candidate genes altered during early allograft IRI, which were subsequently refined in an integrated training cohort using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression and support vector machine–recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) to a four-gene signature comprising SOCS3, MYC, TGIF1, and LETM2. This signature showed excellent discriminative performance in the training cohort (10-fold cross-validation AUC = 0.969) and was validated in independent external datasets, including a large cohort (AUC = 0.942). Decision curve analyses indicated potential clinical utility for early AKI identification across a broad threshold-probability range, with favorable performance compared with several established biomarkers such as neutrophil gelatinase–associated lipocalin. Single-cell transcriptomics revealed cell type–specific expression of the four genes across renal compartments. Moreover, their protein levels were elevated at 24 h after IRI in mouse kidneys, and displayed high expression in human AKI biopsies. Additionally, serum protein levels of SOCS3 and LETM2 were elevated in patients with cardiac surgery-associated AKI, whereas TGIF1 and MYC did not reach statistical significance. Collectively, this study identified a four-gene signature with potential utility as an early diagnostic biomarker panel for post-transplant AKI. Further large-scale clinical trials are needed to validate its diagnostic efficacy.


## Introduction
  Acute kidney injury (AKI) is one of the most prevalent and severe complications following renal transplantation and is closely associated with adverse clinical outcomes. Post-transplant AKI often occurs concomitantly with delayed graft function (DGF), early hospital readmission, inferior long-term graft survival, and increased mortality [1–3]. Despite advances in donor management, organ preservation, and perioperative care, the incidence of AKI remains high [4]. Current diagnostic approaches, mainly relying on serum creatinine levels and histopathology from indication biopsies, are limited by low sensitivity for early detection, sampling bias, and procedural risks. These limitations contribute to a persistent gap between injury onset and actionable diagnosis [5,6].

  Ischemia–reperfusion injury (IRI) is an inevitable consequence of kidney transplantation [7] and a principal cause of AKI and DGF [8–10]. During procurement and implantation, kidneys undergo periods of cold and warm ischemia followed by abrupt reperfusion, leading to cell injury, mitochondrial dysfunction, metabolic disruption, and initiation of multiple forms of cell death [11,12]. Ischemia–reperfusion (IR) induces oxidative stress, endothelial activation, and release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) [13]. These DAMPs engage pattern-recognition receptors and rapidly activate complement, triggering a cytokine–chemokine cascade [11,14]. When inflammation fails to resolve, maladaptive repair leads to interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy, linking early IRI to chronic allograft dysfunction [15,16]. Given the similar pathophysiological features between murine IRI and human ischemia–reperfusion-related AKI, the murine IRI model is widely used as an experimental system.

  To improve early detection of post-transplant AKI, numerous molecular biomarkers have been evaluated. Injury markers such as neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL) and kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1) can rise before serum creatinine, thereby enabling earlier identification of tubular injury. In addition, inflammatory mediators such as C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 9 and interleukin-18 are associated with DGF and rejection risk [17,18]. Cell-cycle arrest markers such as tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases-2 (TIMP-2) × insulin-like growth factor binding protein 7 (IGFBP7) have shown predictive value in detecting AKI in critical care and transplant settings [19,20]. Despite these advances, translation to clinical practice remains limited. Many biomarkers lack robust external validation across diverse patient cohorts, and no single biomarker has proven sufficient for widespread adoption [21]. Thus, there remains an urgent need for integrative approaches capable of identifying robust molecular signatures generalizable across diverse clinical contexts.

  In this study, we systematically integrated transcriptomic data, machine learning, single-cell analysis, and experimental models to identify and validate potential molecular signatures of renal transplant–associated AKI. These signatures may contribute to AKI biomarker development and risk stratification, ultimately improving clinical decision-making and graft outcomes.


## Materials and methods

### Data availability
  Transcriptomic datasets were obtained from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih. gov/geo/). Dataset characteristics and phenotypic definitions are presented in Table 1. GSE30718 [7] was used as the discovery cohort for initial candidate-gene identification because it contained clinically annotated early post-transplant acute tubular necrosis (ATN) samples suitable for co-expression and differential-expression analyses. GSE126805 [10] and GSE90861 [22] were integrated as the training cohort because both captured early transplant-related IRI and together provided a larger sample size for stable feature selection and model development. GSE43974 [23] and GSE53769 [24] were reserved as independent external validation cohorts. GSE43974 captured very early IRI in a large cohort, whereas GSE53769 represented clinically defined early post-transplant ATN. This design allowed assessment of model robustness across different but biologically relevant transplant injury settings. Although sampling time points differed across datasets, all represented biologically related early post-transplant injury states characterized by ischemia–reperfusion stress, tubular injury, and early inflammatory responses, making them comparable for early AKI biomarker discovery. Additionally, single-cell RNA sequencing datasets (GSE210622 [25], human; GSE139107 [26], mouse) were analyzed to explore the cellular context of signature genes.


### Data preprocessing
  Raw expression data were downloaded from GEO and processed in R (version 4.3.1). For microarray datasets, raw or series-matrix expression data were background-corrected, log2-transformed, and quantile-normalized. Probe IDs were mapped to gene symbols according to the corresponding platform annotation files, and when multiple probes mapped to the same gene symbol, the mean expression value was used as the representative gene-level expression. For RNA-seq datasets, genes with counts per million l 1 in more than 50% of samples were excluded before normalization. For integrated analyses, only shared genes were retained across datasets. Batch effects were corrected using the ComBat function in sva package. The final matrix used for downstream bulk transcriptomic analyses was a normalized gene-by-sample expression matrix.

  The main R packages used in this study included limma (v3.58.1), sva (v3.50.0), WGCNA (v1.72.1), glmnet (v4.1.8), pROC (v1.18.5), Seurat (v5.4.0), harmony (v1.2.4), DESeq2 (v1.42.1), and clusterProfiler (v4.10.1) with default parameters unless otherwise specified.


### Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)
  WGCNA was performed using the WGCNA [27] package. The normalized gene-by-sample expression matrix from the discovery cohort (GSE30718) was used as the input for WGCNA. A soft-thresholding power was selected according to the scale-free topology criterion, and the minimum power achieving a scale-free topology fit index (R2) of 0.85 was chosen. After selecting a soft-thresholding power, genes were clustered into modules based on the topological overlap matrix. Module eigengenes were correlated with clinical traits, and modules showing the strongest associations were retained for downstream analysis.


### Differential expression and enrichment analyses
  Differential expression analysis was conducted using limma. Genes with p < 0.05 and |log2(FC)| > 0.25 were considered significant. Functional enrichment and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) were performed with clusterProfiler.


### Candidate gene selection
  Candidate genes were defined as the intersection of the top three AKI-related WGCNA modules and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from the discovery cohort. Univariate logistic regression on the training cohort excluded genes with p > 0.05 or inconsistent effect directions. Feature selection was performed using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression (glmnet [28], λ chosen by 10-fold cross-validation) and linear support vector machine–recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) [29] (5-fold cross-validation). Genes consistently retained by both algorithms across multiple repeated analyses were considered stable features and selected for the final molecular signature.


### Model construction and validation
  A logistic regression framework was constructed and internally validated by 10-fold cross-validation. After validation, the model was refitted on the full training dataset to obtain the final coefficients. Model discrimination was assessed by receiver operating characteristic (ROC). Calibration was evaluated with bootstrap resampling (500 repetitions). Clinical utility was quantified by decision curve analysis (DCA). For external validation, data were standardized with training-set parameters, and performance was evaluated using the same metrics. For biomarker benchmarking in GSE53769, NGAL, KIM-1, TIMP-2, and IGFBP7 were evaluated as single-gene classifiers using ROC analysis. Because detailed clinical covariates were not consistently available across the public transcriptomic datasets, clinical adjustment and combined clinical–molecular model construction were not performed.


### Single-cell transcriptomic analysis
  Single-cell RNA sequencing data were processed using Seurat with quality control and normalization, followed by dimensionality reduction via principal component analysis. Batch effects were corrected using Harmony [30], and the corrected embeddings were visualized with uniform manifold approximation and projection. Cell clusters were annotated based on canonical marker genes. Pseudo-bulk expression matrices were generated by aggregating gene counts per sample, followed by variance-stabilizing transformation using DESeq2.


### Transcription factors network analysis
  Predicted transcription factors–gene interactions were obtained from ChIPBase [31] database. Regulatory networks were visualized with Cytoscape.


### Animal and animal experiments
  Male C57BL/6 wild-type mice (8–10 weeks) were purchased from Chengxi Biotechnology and maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a 12-h light/dark cycle and free access to food and water. Mice were acclimatized for at least 7 days prior to the experiments. A total of 10 mice were included in the murine IRI validation experiment, with five mice in the IR group and five mice in the sham group. All mice were processed under the same experimental protocol. This animal experiment was conducted as an exploratory validation study, and no a priori sample size calculation was performed. All animals were included in the analysis, and no data points were excluded. Mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg, intraperitoneally). Bilateral renal pedicles were clamped for 30 min, followed by reperfusion. Sham-operated mice underwent the same procedure without clamping. Euthanasia was performed by cervical dislocation under deep anesthesia. Kidney tissues were collected 24 h after reperfusion for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, western blot, and immunohistochemistry analyses. Serum creatinine levels were measured using the QuantiChrom™ Creatinine Assay Kit (BioAssay Systems). All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Fudan University (approval number 2023-310) and were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.


### Human samples
  We studied six patients with AKI and six normal controls in Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University, between 2024 and 2025. Renal biopsy specimens were obtained from patients with biopsy-proven acute tubular necrosis (ATN), and the control samples were resected from normal portions of nephrectomy specimens for localized renal tumor, and were verified as histologically normal by light microscopy. All samples underwent H&E staining and immunohistochemistry.

  In addition, to further explore the potential noninvasive translational value of the candidate biomarkers, a clinical cohort from Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University, comprising 15 patients with cardiac surgery-associated AKI and 15 postoperative patients without AKI, was recruited for serum protein measurement. Peripheral blood samples were collected 6 h after surgery, and serum was isolated by centrifugation.

  The study protocol was approved by the Clinical Research Ethical Committee of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (approval number B2020-413). Patient information is available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.


### Western blot
  Western blot was performed as previously described in reference [32]. Primary antibodies included anti-suppressor of cytokine signaling 3 (SOCS3) (Wanleibio, WL01364), anti-MYC proto-oncogene (MYC) (Wanleibio, WL01781), anti-leucine zipper-EF-hand containing transmembrane protein 2 (LETM2) (Affinity, DF13128), and anti-TGFB-induced factor homeobox 1 (TGIF1) (ABclonal, A16984). Anti-Actin (KC-5A08, Aksomics) was used as a loading control.


### Immunohistochemistry
  Immunohistochemistry was performed as previously described in reference [33]. Primary antibodies against SOCS3, MYC, LETM2, and TGIF1 were the same as those used in the western blot. Detection was performed with HRP polymer systems (PV6001 for mouse, PV9001 for rabbit; ZSGB-BIO), visualized using DAB (ZLI-9018, ZSGB-BIO), counterstained with hematoxylin, and mounted.


### Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay
  Circulating levels of MYC, LETM2, SOCS3, and TGIF1 were measured using commercially available ELISA kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions, including Human MYC ELISA Kit (CSB-E09260h, CUSABIO), Human LETM2 ELISA Kit (EF010660, Wuhan Viccays Science and Technology Co., Ltd.), Human SOCS3 ELISA Kit (EF006002, Wuhan Viccays Science and Technology Co., Ltd.), and Human TGIF1 ELISA Kit (EK23399, Signalway Antibody). All samples and standards were assayed in duplicate, and optical density values were measured using a microplate reader. Protein concentrations were calculated according to the corresponding standard curves.


### Statistical analysis
  Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 27.0. Data were expressed as mean ± SD. Differences between two groups were analyzed by two-tailed, unpaired t tests or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as appropriate. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Feature selection, model construction, and validation were performed in R (version 4.3.1). This study followed the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) guidelines [34] for model development and validation (Table S1).


## Results

### WGCNA identifies post-transplant AKI-associated gene modules
  An overview of the study design and analytical workflow is shown in Figure 1. Multiple human renal transplantation cohorts were analyzed to identify and validate gene signatures associated with post-transplant AKI. The discovery dataset (GSE30718) consisted of early post-transplant biopsies exhibiting ATN without rejection (n = 28), together with corresponding stable (n = 11) and nephrectomy (n = 8) controls. GSE126805 and GSE90861 were integrated as the training cohort for feature selection and model development. These two datasets included preimplantation (n = 64) and post-reperfusion (n = 65) biopsies collected within hours after transplantation and represented biologically related early post-transplant injury states. External validation was performed in two independent renal transplant cohorts (GSE43974 and GSE53769), as summarized in Table 1.

  We performed WGCNA to identify co-expression modules associated with post-transplant AKI in the discovery dataset (GSE30718). A soft threshold of five was selected (Figure 2A), and hierarchical clustering categorized genes into distinct co-expression modules (Figure 2B). Module–trait correlation analysis identified three AKI-associated modules: red (r = 0.60, 45 genes), black (r = 0.59, 35 genes), and cyan (r = 0.50, 645 genes) (Figure 2C, Table S2). Functional enrichment further demonstrated that the red module was enriched in inflammation-related pathways (e.g., nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells signaling, myeloid leukocyte activation), whereas the black module was predominantly associated with fibrosis-related pathways (e.g., collagen binding, transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) receptor binding). The cyan module exhibited both pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic features (Figure 2D).


### Integrated transcriptomic analyses identify candidate genes related to post-transplant AKI
  Using a relatively permissive threshold (adjusted p < 0.05; |log2FC| > 0.25) to minimize the risk of omitting relevant genes, we identified 1,594 upregulated and 1,333 downregulated DEGs (Figure 3A; Table S3). GSEA revealed positive enrichment of inflammation-related pathways in post-transplant AKI, such as neutrophil degranulation, cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, and Toll-like receptor cascades, as well as fibrosis-related pathways including extracellular matrix–receptor interaction, platelet-derived growth factor signaling, and TGF-β signaling (Figure 3B, C). Conversely, negatively enriched pathways were predominantly metabolic, including amino acid metabolism, fatty acid metabolism, and tricarboxylic acid cycle (Figure 3D).

  Intersecting DEGs with the three AKI-associated modules yielded 222 overlapping genes (216 upregulated and 6 downregulated) (Figure 4A; Table S4), significantly enriched in immune and inflammatory processes, such as neutrophil migration and cytokine-mediated signaling (Figure 4B). Enrichment map analysis visualized pathway interconnections (Figure 4C). These 222 genes were defined as candidates for downstream machine-learning–based gene selection.


### Machine learning selection prioritizes four signature genes
  The integrated training dataset (GSE126805 and GSE90861) comprised 65 IRI samples and 64 controls. After probe annotation, batch correction, and normalization, expression distributions were well balanced (Figure 5A, B). Univariate logistic regression identified 59 significant genes (p < 0.05), and those with inconsistent effect directions across datasets were excluded, leaving 44 robust candidates (Table S5). We then applied two machine-learning approaches. LASSO regression with 10-fold cross-validation was used to tune the penalty parameter λ, retaining 13 genes at λ.min and 7 genes at λ.1se (Figure 5C, D). In parallel, SVM-RFE achieved the highest accuracy with a five-gene subset (Figure 5E). Intersection of the two methods consistently identified four signature genes—SOCS3, MYC, TGIF1, and LETM2 (Figure 5F).


### A four-gene signature demonstrates high discriminative performance in internal validation
  A four-gene signature was internally validated by stratified 10-fold cross-validation. Aggregated out-of-fold predictions demonstrated excellent discriminative ability (AUC = 0.969; 95%CI, 0.936–1) (Figure 6A). After cross-validation, the model was refitted on the full training dataset to obtain final coefficients (Table S6). The final logistic regression equation was: logit(P) = 0.18 + 1.88 × LETM2 + 1.34 × MYC + 1.26 × SOCS3 + 1.29 × TGIF1. A nomogram visualized the relative contribution of each signature gene (Figure 6B). Calibration assessed using bootstrap resampling showed good agreement between predicted and observed probabilities, indicating limited overfitting (Figure 6C). Furthermore, DCA revealed that the signature consistently achieved higher net clinical benefit than treat-all or treat-none strategies (Figure 6D). All four genes were significantly upregulated in IRI, with moderate-to-strong positive correlations (Figure 6E, F). Individual ROC curves demonstrated high discriminative power, with AUCs ranging from 0.925 to 0.953 (Figure 6G).


### The four-gene signature demonstrates discriminative ability across independent external cohorts
  External validation in kidney transplantation cohorts (GSE43974 and GSE53769) demonstrated generalizability of the four-gene signature across different post-transplant injury contexts. In GSE43974 (203 IRI biopsies and 163 controls), all four signature genes were significantly upregulated in IRI (Figure 7A). Individual ROC analyses showed good discriminative performance for MYC, SOCS3, and TGIF1, whereas LETM2 showed modest performance (Figure 7B). Importantly, the four-gene signature achieved excellent discriminative performance (AUC = 0.942; 95%CI, 0.919–0.965) (Figure 7C), with well-calibrated estimated probabilities (Figure 7D) and clear net clinical benefit (Figure 7E).

  In GSE53769 (8 post-transplant AKI samples with histologically confirmed ATN and 10 controls), LETM2 and MYC were significantly upregulated in AKI, whereas SOCS3 and TGIF1 did not reach statistical significance (Figure 7F). ROC analysis revealed good discriminative ability for LETM2, MYC, and TGIF1, while SOCS3 exhibited relatively weaker performance (Figure 7G). The four-gene signature achieved an AUC of 0.800 (95%CI, 0.535–1.000) (Figure 7H). Calibration demonstrated deviation at low (<0.3) and high (>0.8) probability ranges, with better agreement in the mid-probability range (0.4–0.8) (Figure 7I), likely attributable to the limited sample size.

  We further benchmarked the four-gene signature against established AKI biomarkers, including NGAL, KIM-1, TIMP-2, and IGFBP7, which are commonly used for early AKI detection. The AUCs and 95% confidence intervals of established biomarkers in GSE53769 are summarized in Table S7. Among single-gene classifiers, NGAL achieved the highest discriminative performance in GSE53769 (AUC = 0.887, 95%CI, 0.736–1.000) (Figure 7J; Table S7). DeLong test indicated no significant difference in AUC between the four-gene signature and NGAL (p > 0.05). Notably, DCA demonstrated net clinical benefit across a broad probability range (0.2–0.8), with superior benefit over individual established biomarkers between 0.20 and 0.65 (Figure 7K).


### Single-cell transcriptomic analysis reveals cellular landscape of signature genes in AKI
  To characterize the cellular context of gene expression, we analyzed single-nucleus RNA sequencing data from GSE210622 (8 AKI samples, 6 controls). Twelve major renal cell populations were identified (Figure 8A). Feature plots revealed distinct expression patterns: LETM2 was enriched in Henle’s loop segments, distal convoluted tubule, principal cells, and connecting tubule; TGIF1 was broadly expressed across tubular epithelial subtypes; SOCS3 was predominantly expressed in endothelial cells and principal cells; and MYC was selectively enriched in proximal tubule (Figure 8B). Pseudo-bulk analysis indicated that TGIF1 and SOCS3 were significantly upregulated across multiple cell types during AKI, whereas MYC induction was more restricted and most prominent in proximal tubule (Figure 8C–F).

  Analysis of the murine single-cell dataset GSE139107 (3 IR and 4 sham samples) yielded consistent findings. Eleven renal cell populations were identified (Fig. S1A). LETM2 showed an upward trend in intercalated cells, connecting tubule, and podocytes after IR (Fig. S1B). Meanwhile, TGIF1, SOCS3, and MYC were consistently upregulated across multiple cell types, with pseudo-bulk analysis confirming significant induction in representative populations (Fig. S1C–E).


### Regulatory network analysis implicates key transcription factors upstream of the signature genes
  ChIPBase-based transcriptional regulatory network analysis revealed that the four signature genes were interconnected within a dense regulatory network (Fig. S2). Notably, signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), a key transcription factor regulating inflammation in AKI, regulates expression of SOCS3 and TGIF1. Transcription factor Yin Yang 1 (YY1), involved in glucose and lipid metabolism, mitochondrial function, and cell proliferation [35], acts as an upstream regulator of the LETM2, MYC, and SOCS3.


### Signature genes are upregulated in a murine IRI model and human AKI samples
  In a murine renal IRI model, serum creatinine was significantly elevated, accompanied by histological evidence of tubular injury (Fig. S3A, B). Western blot and immunohistochemistry confirmed that LETM2, MYC, TGIF1, and SOCS3 were upregulated in IRI kidneys (Figure 9A, B; Fig. S4). Analysis of human renal biopsies showed consistent increases of these proteins in AKI samples compared with healthy controls (Figure 9C; Fig. S3C).

  To further explore the potential noninvasive translational value of the candidate biomarkers, we additionally examined serum concentrations of these proteins in a clinical cohort comprising 15 patients with cardiac surgery-associated AKI and 15 non-AKI patients. Blood was collected at 6 h after cardiac surgery. The concentrations of SOCS3 and LETM2 were significantly elevated in AKI patients compared to non-AKI patients, serum TGIF1 and MYC showed an increasing trend in AKI patients, but did not reach statistical difference (Fig. S5).


## Discussion
  In this study, we systematically integrated transcriptomic analyses, machine learning, external validation, and experimental assays to identify and validate molecular features of AKI following renal transplantation. By integrating WGCNA, DEG analysis and two machine-learning algorithms, we identified four robust signature genes—SOCS3, MYC, TGIF1, and LETM2. A logistic regression framework incorporating these genes showed strong discriminative performance in internal cross-validation and in a large external cohort, with supportive, though less pronounced, results in a smaller validation dataset. Importantly, validation in a murine IRI model, human kidney biopsies, patient blood samples, and single-cell transcriptomic profiling strengthened the biological plausibility of these findings. Our work provides a multilevel framework for reliable biomarker discovery in AKI.

  The four signature genes reflect distinct but interconnected facets of AKI pathophysiology. SOCS3, a canonical inhibitor of the Janus kinase–signal transducer and activator of transcription pathway, is often upregulated in inflammatory contexts. Its overexpression in tubular epithelial cells inhibits reparative proliferation and promotes classical macrophage activation [36]. MYC, a master regulator of proliferation and glycolysis-promoting metabolic reprogramming [37], contributes to maladaptive repair and fibrosis when persistently overactivated [38,39]. LETM2, a member of the LETM protein family, is a mitochondrial protein containing leucine zipper and EF-hand domains. LETM1, a better-characterized member of this family, is localized to the inner mitochondrial membrane and has been linked to ion exchange and calcium homeostasis [40]. In tumor settings, LETM2 has been reported to activate mTOR signaling and enhance glycolysis in gastric cancer [41]. However, the precise function of LETM2 in kidney injury remains unclear. Given its predicted mitochondrial localization, LETM2 may be involved in mitochondrial homeostasis during kidney injury. Finally, TGIF1, a known transcriptional corepressor of TGF-β/Smad pathway, is critical for coordinating repair response and fibrotic remodeling in AKI [42], and also promotes tumor necrosis factor-alpha–mediated cytotoxicity by facilitating Itch-dependent cellular FLICE-like inhibitory protein long isoform degradation [43]. Taken together, these genes are biologically relevant to inflammation, metabolic stress, and maladaptive repair; however, their exact roles in post-transplant AKI remain to be experimentally validated.

  From a translational perspective, the four-gene molecular signature demonstrated strong diagnostic performance and good calibration in the large GSE43974 cohort, underscoring its potential for generalization across diverse patient populations. In the smaller GSE53769 dataset, the four-gene signature showed more modest but still moderate discriminative performance. When benchmarked against established AKI biomarkers, NGAL had the highest AUC point estimate, whereas the four-gene signature remained within the performance range of the established biomarkers evaluated in GSE53769. The wide confidence intervals observed across these estimates indicate limited precision of performance assessment in this small dataset. DCA revealed net clinical benefit across a broad threshold-probability range and suggested greater net benefit than individual established biomarkers between 0.20 and 0.65, indicating potential clinical utility across a spectrum of decision-making preferences [44]. However, the ROC AUC of the four-gene signature was not significantly different from that of NGAL in GSE53769, indicating that the current data are insufficient to establish a clear discriminative advantage over NGAL. Given the limited sample size of GSE53769, these findings suggest that this cohort should be interpreted as exploratory external validation and biomarker benchmarking.

  Notably, in the cardiac surgery-associated AKI cohort, serum SOCS3 and LETM2 were significantly elevated in AKI patients compared to non-AKI patients. TGIF1 and MYC showed an increasing trend in AKI patients, but did not reach statistical difference, which may be due to the small sample size. Although this cohort involved cardiac surgery-associated AKI rather than kidney transplant recipients, both conditions involve IRI as a major underlying mechanism, providing a rationale for this preliminary assessment.

  However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, despite batch correction, residual heterogeneity across platforms and populations may remain. Second, the cohorts analyzed were not uniform: some compared post-transplant AKI with controls, while others examined IRI as a surrogate phenotype. Although IRI is a major driver of AKI after transplantation, it does not encompass the entire pathophysiological spectrum, which may also involve rejection, infection, or drug toxicity. Third, detailed clinical covariates, including recipient age, donor type, cold ischemia time, immunosuppressive regimen, serum creatinine, urine output, and other perioperative factors, were not consistently available across the public datasets. This precluded clinical adjustment and construction of an integrated clinical–molecular model, and may limit the assessment of model robustness and incremental value beyond standard clinical parameters. Fourth, this study identified and validated the four-gene signature as potential biomarkers for post-transplant AKI. Further functional studies are needed to clarify the roles of these genes in renal injury. Fifth, the exploratory findings from a limited number of renal biopsy samples require validation in larger cohorts. Finally, we examined the serum concentrations of these biomarkers in a small cardiac surgery-associated AKI cohort. The noninvasive diagnostic value of these markers remains to be validated in larger, transplant-specific biofluid cohorts.

  In summary, this study identified and validated a four-gene signature (SOCS3, MYC, TGIF1, and LETM2) associated with post-transplant AKI. These findings propose a molecular framework for the diagnosis and risk stratification of post-transplant AKI, suggesting the potential of these genes as biomarkers of AKI. Further prospective studies and experiments are needed to validate the diagnostic efficacy of these genes.
