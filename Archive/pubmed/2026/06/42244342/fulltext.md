# Functional and regulatory complexity: challenges and prospects for understanding rice WRKY transcription factors


## Abstract
  WRKY transcription factors (TFs) are central regulators of plant growth, development, and responses to biotic and abiotic stresses. In rice, the large size of this family, coupled with structural diversity, multifunctionality, redundancy, and extensive network integration, complicates efforts to define precise gene functions. While these features confer regulatory robustness, they limit the interpretability of single-gene analyses and obscure direct gene-trait relationships. This review examines the challenges underlying WRKY functional characterization in rice and highlights how they arise from the intrinsic properties of this gene family. We then present a challenge-centred framework in which experimental and computational approaches are mapped onto the four major barriers to WRKY functional resolution, such as context-dependent activity, multifunctionality, functional redundancy, and compensation. By emphasizing how these approaches resolve distinct layers of WRKY complexity, we provide a problem-driven synthesis of current methodologies. We further underscore the need for integrated WRKY-centric databases to support hypothesis generation and accelerate functional discovery. A systems-level understanding of WRKY TFs, grounded in their regulatory complexity, will be essential to harness their potential to improve stress resilience and productivity in rice.


## Introduction
  Transcription factors (TFs) regulate gene expression and signal transduction across diverse plant processes. Among the major TF families, the WRKY family is one of the largest (Tian et al., 2020), and is particularly notable for its roles in plant development and in responses to biotic and abiotic stresses (H. Wang et al., 2023b). WRKY TFs are defined by a ∼60-amino acid WRKY domain, which contains a highly conserved heptapeptide (WRKYGQK) DNA binding motif, as well as a zinc-finger (C2H2 or C2HC) motif. They are grouped based on the number of WRKY domains and the type of zinc-finger motifs (Fig. 1). Together, these domains direct the highly specific binding to cis-regulatory elements (CREs), primarily W-boxes (consensus sequence: TTGACC/T), in target gene promoters to regulate transcription (X. Chen et al., 2019; Jimmy and Babu, 2019b; Javed and Gao, 2023). Since the discovery of SPF1 in sweet potato (Ishiguro and Nakamura, 1994), WRKY TFs have been described across plant lineages, including major crop species such as rice (Zhang and Wang, 2005; Rushton et al., 2010; Huang et al., 2012; Rinerson et al., 2015, 2016; Chen et al., 2017; X. Chen et al., 2019; Goyal et al., 2023).

  With over 100 gene members, rice (Oryza sativa) contains one of the largest WRKY families, and they regulate plant defence against pathogens and pests, abiotic stress tolerance, and development (Jin et al., 2017; Jimmy and Babu, 2019b; Tian et al., 2020; T. Li et al., 2025). Additionally, recent studies demonstrate that WRKY-mediated regulation extends to the shaping of rhizosphere microbial communities, including nematodes and antagonistic bacteria (see Box 1). This gene family expansion underpins the regulatory robustness and adaptability of WRKYs, that is the resiliency to maintain stable functions or phenotypes through redundancy, gene duplication, feedback regulations, and gene network architecture, despite the environmental changes or disruptions of individual components (Shiu et al., 2005; Macneil and Walhout, 2011; Lachowiec et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2021; Iohannes and Jackson, 2023). However, this regulatory robustness complicates functional analysis.

  While several excellent reviews have summarized general WRKY functions in rice and other plants, they have generally emphasized cataloguing roles in development and stress, with less emphasis on identifying major barriers that limit functional characterization and on systematically linking those barriers to approaches for overcoming them (Li et al., 2020; Wani et al., 2021; Goyal et al., 2023; S. Li et al., 2024; T. Li et al., 2025; X. Chen et al., 2025a; Park et al., 2025; Shuaizu et al., 2025; L. Yang et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026). In this review, we address these challenges, including structural diversity, dual and multifunctionality, redundancy and compensation, and integration into complex regulatory networks. Focusing on rice, a staple food for much of the global population and a model for less genetically tractable cereals, we will illustrate how these complexities complicate functional dissection. Finally, we highlight already established and emerging strategies and technologies to overcome these challenges and outline future research directions to unlock the full potential of rice WRKY TFs for enhancing stress resilience and crop productivity.


## Structural and numerical diversity in rice WRKY TFs
  The 110 and 130 WRKY TF genes in indica and japonica rice, respectively (Jimmy and Babu, 2019b), display extensive sequence and structural variation (Fig. 1). Variants in the canonical WRKYGQK motif in rice include WRKYGKK, WRKYGMK, WSKYGQK, WRKYGEK, WRRYGQK, and WKKYGQK, with additional subspecies-specific forms such as WRICGQK, WRMTGQS, and WRMCGQS in japonica and WRMTGQS in indica (Jimmy and Babu, 2019b). Because the WRKY domain binds W-box elements (TTGAC/T) via a positively charged β strand (Yamasaki et al., 2005; Rushton et al., 2010; Mahiwal et al., 2024), even minor sequence changes can markedly alter DNA binding affinity and, consequently, transcriptional outcomes. Some variants may not even be functional, complicating predictions based on sequence homology alone. Further complexity arises from subspecies divergence driven by tandem duplication and gene loss, leaving certain WRKYs to be unique to one subspecies or present in different copy numbers, thus necessitating subspecies-specific validation (Yamasaki et al., 2008; Agarwal et al., 2011; Bakshi and Oelmüller, 2014).

  WRKYs are classified into four groups. Group I proteins contain two WRKY domains (an N-terminal and a C-terminal domain) and a C2H2 zinc finger structure. Group II and III proteins each contain only one WRKY domain but differ in zinc finger type (C2H2 versus C2HC). Group IV members contain incomplete WRKY or zinc finger motifs (Rushton et al., 2010; X. Chen et al., 2019). While this framework is useful, classification continues to evolve with expanded genomic datasets that reveal tandem duplications, gene losses, and sequence divergence, all of which reduce phylogenetic resolution (Yamasaki et al., 2008; Agarwal et al., 2011; Bakshi and Oelmüller, 2014). As an example, recent phylogenetic analyses based on refined amino acid sequence alignments have revised group membership. Wu (2005) reported 20 japonica WRKY gene members in Group Ib, but Jimmy and Babu (2019b) later reported no genes in that group. These discrepancies highlight how evolving analytical tools and expanding genomic resources continue to refine and complicate WRKY gene classification. As more resources become available, additional WRKYs and atypical members are being uncovered, including group IV proteins that lack complete WRKY or zinc finger motifs (Xie et al., 2005; Xu et al., 2016; Jimmy and Babu, 2019b). Such atypical members highlight the challenge of linking structural variation to functional roles and illustrate the ongoing complexity in fully characterizing WRKY diversity in rice. In summary, structural and numerical diversity complicate functional inference in three ways: they reduce the reliability of homology-based prediction, create subspecies-specific regulatory repertoires, and increase the likelihood that closely related WRKYs differ in DNA binding behaviour and biological output.


## Functional diversity: dual functions, redundancy, and compensation
  Rice WRKY TFs participate in diverse processes, including biotic and abiotic stress responses, developmental transitions, hormone signalling, and metabolic regulation (Banerjee and Roychoudhury, 2015; Jimmy and Babu, 2015; Finatto et al., 2018; Wani et al., 2021; H. Wang et al., 2023a; Huang et al., 2024). This functional versatility enables plants to fine tune physiological responses to a complex and changing environment. However, such pleiotropy complicates the task of assigning discrete functions to individual WRKY genes. The same WRKY gene can contribute to multiple, sometimes conflicting or even opposing biological outcomes, often through context-dependent interactions with hormone signalling networks.


### Dual or multiple functions
  Many WRKY operate in seemingly unrelated biological pathways. For example, in rice, OsWRKY11 is implicated in enhancing resistance to pathogens (Lee et al., 2018), and improving tolerance to drought and heat stress (Wu et al., 2009; Lee et al., 2018), but it also represses flowering and plant height (Cai et al., 2014). OsWRKY45-1 enhances rice blast resistance, but its activation compromises defence against bacterial blight and bacterial streak diseases (Tao et al., 2009).

  The paradoxical effects shown above often result from the TFs role in integrating signals from multiple hormone pathways. They combine inputs from key phytohormones, particularly salicylic acid (SA), jasmonic acid (JA), abscisic acid (ABA), and gibberellic acid (GA) to fine-tune responses depending on the context and stress conditions (Rushton et al., 2012; Banerjee and Roychoudhury, 2015; Wani et al., 2021). OsWRKY114 integrates biotic and abiotic stress responses by modulating phytohormone crosstalk. It enhances resistance to bacterial blight and bakanae diseases by suppressing ABA and GA signalling while activating SA- and JA-mediated defences. However, this same regulation impairs drought tolerance by limiting ABA-dependent stomatal closure (Anderson et al., 2004; Nahar et al., 2012; Cohen and Leach, 2019; Son et al., 2022; Song et al., 2023). OsWRKY114 can also directly bind to the promoters of pathogenesis-related (PR) genes to activate resistance against bacterial blight (Son et al., 2020). Likewise, OsWRKY28 shows comparable trade-offs. It represses blast resistance by suppressing defence-related genes but promotes salinity tolerance through activation of a salinity responsive gene (Delteil et al., 2012; Chujo et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2023). It also promotes metal homeostasis, root development, and yield (P. Wang et al., 2018; Hewedy et al., 2023). OsWRKY53 shows multifunctionality in regulating several stress responses, as well as growth and development (Fig. 2). It suppresses seed germination via ABA accumulation (Xie et al., 2022), reduces pollen fertility and seed setting during cold stress (Tang et al., 2022), but increases rice grain size by regulating genes involved in cell expansion (Tian et al., 2017). Similarly, OsWRKY53 plays contrasting roles in disease defence as it promotes resistance against rice blast (Chujo et al., 2007), but induces susceptibility to sheath blight (Fig. 2) (Peng Yuan et al., 2020). These examples highlight the complex trade-offs mediated by WRKYs at the interface of growth and defence. Several other rice WRKY TFs show such multifunctionality and are involved in various biological processes (Supplementary Table S1).


### Functional redundancy and compensation
  Redundancy among WRKYs enhances regulatory robustness but can mask gene-specific functions. Multiple WRKYs frequently converge on the same or similar phenotypes, providing resilience but obscuring individual contributions (Supplementary Table S2). Several WRKYs provide resistance against rice blast disease via multiple pathways, as represented in Fig. 3. OsWRKY19 and OsWRKY67 bind to the W-box in the promoter of PR genes, which increases their expression (Liu et al., 2018; Du et al., 2021). OsWRKY10 and an OsWRKY45-OsMED16-OsWRKY62 complex up-regulate the expression of phytoalexin biosynthesis genes, enhancing defence against rice blast. Furthermore, OsWRKY13 interacts with the W-box in the promoter of OsWRKY42, a suppressor of plant defence, to boost resistance (Fig. 3) (Cheng et al., 2015). In addition, OsWRKY9, OsWRKY65, and OsWRKY114 all regulate resistance against bakanae disease via different pathways. An OsWRKY9-OsWRKY65 module enhances resistance by controlling defence gene expression while OsWRKY114 confers resistance through down-regulation of GA and up-regulation of JA signalling (Song et al., 2023, 2024). OsWRKY28 and OsWRKY76 negatively regulate rice blast resistance by acting as transcriptional repressors of defence genes, like PR1 and PR10, during rice blast pathogen infection (Chujo et al., 2013; Yokotani et al., 2013). Additionally, overexpression of OsWRKY76 causes reduced biosynthesis of diterpenoid phytoalexins that are important for plant defence (Yokotani et al., 2013).

  Redundancy is also evident in plant growth regulation. OsWRKY21, OsWRKY36, OsWRK67, and others independently suppress plant height mostly through GA- and IAA-related pathways, often at the cost of yield (Lee et al., 2018; Vo et al., 2018; Wei et al., 2021). OsWRKY21 and OsWRKY36 suppress plant height by repressing cell wall biosynthesis genes and regulating plant architecture genes, respectively (Wei et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2025). The suppression of plant height in OsWRKY11 overexpressed lines is attributed to altered cell size in the internode region (Cai et al., 2014). Possible trade-offs between disease resistance and plant growth have been observed in OsWRKY7 (Zheng et al., 2024), OsWRKY89 (Wang et al., 2007), and OsWRKY31 (Zhang et al., 2008) overexpressed lines.

  Compensation between WRKYs further obscures their individual functions (Supplementary Table S3). For example, OsWRKY21 and OsWRKY108 redundantly activate the phosphate transporter OsPHT1;1. While single mutants (oswrky21 or oswrky108) exhibited no altered phenotype, the double mutant oswrky21oswrky108 showed significantly reduced root phosphate concentration and down-regulated OsPHT1;1 expression under high phosphate conditions (Zhang et al., 2021). Similarly, OsWRKY36 and OsWRKY102 jointly repress lignin biosynthesis in rice culms, with single mutants showing moderate lignin increases (28% and 32%, respectively) and a double mutant showing a 41% increase that extends to leaf tissues where single mutants showed no effect (Miyamoto et al., 2020).

  Taken together, the multifunctionality, redundancy and compensatory behaviour of WRKY TFs hinder straightforward gene-trait associations and limit the predictive power of single-gene approaches. The absence of a visible phenotype in single-gene knockouts can be deceptive, as it often masks a gene’s critical role when another compensates for its loss. Because WRKYs often regulate both growth and defence, overexpression or loss-of-function can lead to trade-offs, such as enhanced resistance but reduced biomass or yield (Wang et al., 2007; Zheng et al., 2024). This complexity necessitates higher-order mutant analyses, context-specific experiments, and system-level approaches to disentangle overlapping functions. Moreover, under field conditions, where plants face simultaneous stresses, WRKY-mediated responses are shaped by additive, antagonistic, or synergistic effects that cannot be predicted from single-stress studies (Liu et al., 2024; Zandalinas et al., 2024).


### Complex regulatory networks
  WRKY TFs operate within highly interconnected and dynamic regulatory networks, integrating diverse endogenous and environmental cues to modulate plant responses. In rice, this complexity arises from diverse mechanisms, including auto- and cross-regulation, DNA binding preferences, protein–protein interactions, and multiple layers of translational and post-translational control.

  At the core of WRKY function is binding to CREs, primarily the W-box (TTGACC/T), in target gene promoters. Many WRKY TFs activate defence responses by promoting pathogenesis-related (PR) gene expression through W-box binding (Peng et al., 2008, 2012; Koo et al., 2009; Choi et al., 2015; Yang et al., 2016; Choi et al., 2020; Son et al., 2020). In addition, many WRKYs autoregulate or regulate other WRKY genes, creating feedback and feedforward loops. For example, OsWRKY6 (Choi et al., 2015), OsWRKY13 (Xiao et al., 2013), OsWRKY70 (Li et al., 2015), OsWRKY62, and OsWRKY76 (Liu et al., 2016) autoregulate via W-box elements in their own promoters. Moreover, cross-regulation is frequent. OsWRKY13 regulates OsWRKY42 and OsWRKY45-1 (Fig. 4), while OsWRKY45 reciprocally activates OsWRKY13 (Xiao et al., 2013; Cheng et al., 2015). Other interactions include OsWRKY80 binding to the W-box in the promoter of OsWRKY4 (Peng et al., 2016), and OsWRKY30 potentially up-regulating OsWRKY45 (Han et al., 2013).

  In rare cases, WRKYs bind alternative CREs beyond W-boxes. OsWRKY13 can bind to both its own W-box as well as PRE4 (TACTGCGCTTAGT) elements (Fig. 4), expanding its regulatory reach during stress conditions (Cai et al., 2008; Xiao et al., 2013). This flexibility contributes to functional complexity and target specificity.

  Additional layers of control further complicate regulation. Epigenetic modifications (DNA methylation, histone acetylation), alternative splicing (e.g. OsWRK62/76 producing truncated isoforms), and splice variants with opposing functions (e.g. OsWRKY45-1 versus OsWRK45-2) expand regulatory potential (Tao et al., 2009; Ding et al., 2012; Garg et al., 2015; Xing et al., 2015; Liu et al., 2016). Small RNAs also regulate WRKYs. For example, drought-induced miRNAs regulate OsWRKY24 and OsWRKY72 (Shaik and Ramakrishna, 2012), while pathogen responsive small RNAs target OsWRKY40 variants (Dong et al., 2018).

  Protein–protein interactions add further complexity. WRKY TFs interact with mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs) and VQ proteins (protein containing a short VQ motif, FxxhVQxhTG, ‘h’ is hydrophobic residue) altering activity and stability. OsWRKY33 and OsWRKY31 are phosphorylated by MAPKs, enhancing PR gene expression (Fig. 3) (Koo et al., 2009; Li et al., 2012; S. Wang et al., 2023). Similarly, OsWRKY53 is activated via MAPK cascade during infection to induce defence responses (Chujo et al., 2014; Yoo et al., 2014). VQ domain proteins also modulate WRKY activity (Kim et al., 2013; Chen et al., 2022; Tian, 2024). VQ8 interacts with OsWRKY10, reducing its DNA-binding activity and enhancing thermotolerance (Chen et al., 2022).

  WRKY TFs stability and abundance are controlled by the ubiquitin-proteosome system (UPS), which degrades WRKY proteins. OsWRKY45 and OsWRKY6 are degraded in the absence of pathogen infection, preventing costly constitutive defences (Matsushita et al., 2013; Choi et al., 2015). WRKYs also form homo- or heterodimers to regulate crosstalk between signalling pathways (Zhang et al., 2015; Phukan et al., 2016).

  Together, these layers make WRKY networks exceptionally complex. Individual gene activity depends on expression levels, alternative splicing, post-transcriptional and post-translational modifications, interacting partners, and binding preference to a W-box, all of which vary across stress types, developmental stages, and genetic backgrounds. This context dependency hinders generalization and requires integrative, system-level approaches that combine genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics with high-resolution functional assays, all of which are time consuming and resource intensive.

  While WRKY TFs represent attractive targets for engineering stress tolerance or greater yield, network complexity means simple genome edits can cause pleiotropic effects or disrupt critical crosstalk, leading to undesirable traits. Unlocking their potential will require precise manipulation informed by comprehensive knowledge of their regulatory networks, moving beyond single-gene characterization towards integrated systems biology.


## Approaches to dissect the complexity in rice WRKY TFs
  The structural diversity, multifunctionality, redundancy, and complex regulatory diversity described above define the major barriers to resolving WRKY TF function in rice. These same features dictate the types of experimental and computational approaches required to study them. In the following sections, we organize these approaches as strategies to resolve these specific challenges. Different high-throughput, molecular, and genetic tools applicable to dissect the complexity in rice WRKY TFs are summarized in Supplementary Table S4.


### Resolving multifunctionality and context-dependent WRKY activity through system-level approaches
  The multifunctionality of rice WRKY TFs arises from their ability to regulate multiple, often interacting biological pathways, including responses to biotic and abiotic stresses, development, and hormone signalling. As a result, the function of individual WRKY TFs cannot be understood in isolation or under a single condition but instead requires approaches that capture their dynamic and context-dependent behaviour across biological states.

  Adopting genome-wide, comparative and systems level approaches is key to understanding the function of WRKY TFs across biological contexts. High throughput ‘omics’ technologies, including transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics analyses, have provided critical insights into how WRKY TFs operate within complex regulatory environments. Transcriptome profiling identifies differentially expressed WRKYs in response to biotic, abiotic, and combined stresses, revealing spatio-temporal patterns and hormonal cross-talk (Pradhan et al., 2019; Jeyasri et al., 2021; Nayyeripasand et al., 2021; Das et al., 2022; Hu et al., 2022). For example, RNA-seq identified 69 and 98 WRKY TFs during the early and late response phase, respectively, of chilling stress in rice seedlings (Pradhan et al., 2019). However, while transcriptomic analyses reveal dynamic WRKY expression patterns, they do not distinguish direct regulatory roles from indirect or compensatory effects, particularly in the context of extensive functional redundancy. This limitation underscores the need for integration of complementary approaches to resolve WRKY function more precisely. Transcriptomics also reveals involvement of distinct pathways in disease resistant and susceptible rice varieties under different stresses (Bagnaresi et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2017; Cohen and Leach, 2019; Wang et al., 2019). This integrative requirement is particularly important for WRKY TFs, where overlapping functions and network connectivity obscure direct gene-trait relationships. Phylogenetic and evolutionary analyses, including inter-species comparisons with model plants like Arabidopsis, complement these approaches by identifying WRKY TFs that have undergone divergent evolutionary trajectories, and may offer explanations for observed functional differences across species (Wu, 2005; Berri et al., 2009; Wang et al., 2015; Jin et al., 2016; Jimmy and Babu, 2019b; Wei et al., 2021).

  These approaches enable the identification of multifunctionality at the systems level. Because WRKY TFs often regulate multiple pathways simultaneously, transcriptomic analyses of overexpression or knockout lines provide a genome-wide view of downstream effects, helping to distinguish primary regulatory roles from indirect or pleiotropic consequences (Huang et al., 2022). Integration of proteomics and metabolomics data further links WRKY activity to protein abundance, post-translational modifications (Pradet-Balade et al., 2001; Yan et al., 2006; Cho et al., 2008; Singh and Jwa, 2013; X.-L. Chen et al., 2018; Vo et al., 2021; Feng et al., 2022), and hormone dynamics, particularly involving JA and SA pathways, thereby clarifying how WRKY TFs mediate trade-offs between growth and defence (Liang et al., 2017; Vo et al., 2021).

  A critical extension of these approaches is the incorporation of combined stress conditions. As rice plants are constantly exposed to multiple, often co-occurring, biotic and abiotic stresses, experimental designs must reflect combined stress conditions (Cohen and Leach, 2019; Zarattini et al., 2021; Roussin-Léveillée et al., 2024; Zandalinas et al., 2024; Mir et al., 2025). Single stress studies rarely capture the intricate regulatory mechanisms plants employ in nature, where signalling pathways can interact synergistically or antagonistically (Zarattini et al., 2021; Zandalinas et al., 2024). Similarly, identification of conserved co-regulatory networks under combined stress further clarifies WRKY roles in development and stress adaptation (Jeyasri et al., 2021; Mir et al., 2025). Notably, 30% to 60% of genes modulated under combined stress show unique expression patterns distinct from single stress responses, highlighting the importance of context-dependent regulation (Rasmussen et al., 2013; Farjad et al., 2018; Tonnessen et al., 2019; Zarattini et al., 2021). As some WRKY TFs oppositely regulate responses against different stresses, it is important to evaluate their net effects under combined stresses (Xiao et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2023). These integrative strategies, which uncover key regulatory nodes and functionally related gene clusters, are essential for understanding WRKY regulation in rice and for breeding climate-resilient crop varieties.

  At the population level, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have been pivotal in linking WRKYs to complex agronomic traits, capturing allelic diversity and enhancing resolution of quantitative trait loci (QTL) or quantitative trait nucleotides (QTNs) (Rebolledo et al., 2015; Pantalião et al., 2016; Wei et al., 2021). For example, GWAS revealed OsWRKY12 to be associated with salt stress tolerance in rice during early growth (Nayyeripasand et al., 2021) and OsWRKY53 as a negative regulator of salt tolerance in rice through OsMKK10.2 and OsHKT1;5 which promote ion homeostasis (Yu et al., 2023).

  Together, these system-level approaches move beyond single-gene analyses to resolve the context-dependent and multifunctional nature of WRKY TFs, providing a framework for understanding how these regulators co-ordinate complex biological responses in rice. However, resolving context-dependent activity alone is insufficient without addressing redundancy among WRKY TFs.


### Dissecting redundancy and compensatory WRKY functions using genetic and molecular perturbation
  Functional redundancy and compensatory interactions among WRKY TFs present a major barrier to resolving individual gene function in rice. Many WRKY TFs belong to expanded gene families with overlapping expression patterns and regulatory roles, often masking phenotypes in single-gene mutants. As a result, WRKY function requires perturbation strategies that uncover combinatorial and higher-order effects.

  Genetic analyses have shown that single-gene perturbations are often insufficient to reveal WRKY function, as redundant family members can compensate for loss of activity. In contrast, combinatorial perturbation approaches have demonstrated that higher-order disruption of WRKY genes can expose functional overlap and reveal regulatory roles that are not evident in single mutants. Genome editing strategies that enable multiplexed targeting have been particularly effective in uncovering these relationships. For example, while disruption of individual WRKY genes created using CRISPR Cas9 editing approaches has provided valuable insights into their roles in stress responses and development (Meng et al., 2017; Huang et al., 2022; Saeed et al., 2022; Son et al., 2024; Peng et al., 2025), strategies that enable multiplexed edits have been particularly important for revealing redundancy and compensation that are hidden in single gene studies (Wang et al., 2017; Gupta et al., 2024; Tan et al., 2025). Higher-order mutants reveal the combined impact of redundant WRKY TFs and highlight that WRKY function is often distributed across gene networks rather than confined to individual loci.

  Partial perturbation approaches further refine these insights by revealing dose-response dependent and context-specific regulatory effects. In cases where complete loss-of-function is lethal or masks subtle phenotypes, reduced WRKY TF expression achieved through RNAi has been used to uncover contributions of individual WRKY TFs to stress and developmental processes in rice (Chujo et al., 2013; Xiao et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2016; Vo et al., 2018). The findings from these studies emphasize that WRKY activity is not simply on or off but instead reflects quantitative contributions of multiple TFs acting together within a broader regulatory system.

  At the mechanistic level, redundancy among WRKY TFs is reinforced by their participation in complex protein interaction networks. WRKY TFs frequently interact with other WRKYs as well as additional transcriptional regulators and signalling components, such as MAPKs and VQ proteins, forming regulatory complexes that modulate transcriptional outputs. These interactions can mediate compensatory or cooperative functions, further contributing to the robustness of WRKY regulatory systems and complicating the attribution of function to individual TFs. Resolving these interactions requires approaches that can define both binding relationships and functional consequences. Experimental analyses of WRKY-containing complexes have identified specific protein–protein interactions and their roles in modulating transcriptional activity, providing insight into how redundancy is maintained at the molecular level. Studies that have advanced understanding in rice utilize approaches such as yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assays (Koo et al., 2009; Shen et al., 2012; Inoue et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2016; Du et al., 2021), bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) (Xie et al., 2006; Liu et al., 2016; J. Chen et al., 2018), co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) (Inoue et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2016; Du et al., 2021), and prime editing (Lin et al., 2020; Gupta et al., 2023; J. Li et al., 2023; X. Li et al., 2025). These interactions often mediate compensatory or cooperative functions, further contributing to the robustness of WRKY regulatory systems.

  In parallel, promoter-level analyses have shown that WRKY-mediated transcription depends on specific cis-regulatory elements, with mutational studies demonstrating how variation in these elements alters transcriptional activation. These studies include fusions of WRKY-responsive promoters to reporter genes, that allowed quantification of WRKY-mediated transcription in transient assays or stable transgenic lines (Wang et al., 2007; Liu et al., 2016; Amack and Antunes, 2020; Choi et al., 2020; Yuan et al., 2023; L. Wang et al., 2023; Bohrer et al., 2024; Prasad et al., 2024). Site-directed mutagenesis of CREs coupled with reporter assays was used to define the DNA elements critical for WRKY binding (Qiu et al., 2007; Xiao et al., 2013; Yokotani et al., 2013).

  Together, these approaches demonstrate that WRKY redundancy is not simply a consequence of gene duplication, but reflects coordinated regulation across multiple levels of organization. Dissecting these relationships requires integrated perturbation and mechanistic analyses that can resolve how overlapping WRKY functions are distributed, compensated, and ultimately translated into phenotypic outcomes.


### Defining WRKY target specificity and regulatory logic through chromatin and binding analyses
  A central challenge in WRKY biology is distinguishing direct transcriptional targets from indirect downstream effects, particularly given the widespread, context-dependent, and network-integrated roles of these TFs. As a result, identifying where WRKY proteins bind is not sufficient; resolving their function requires understanding how binding specificity, chromatin context, and regulatory state together determine transcriptional outcomes under specific biological conditions.

  Genome-wide studies now provide multiple layers of evidence to address this problem. Direct binding data have revealed that WRKY target specificity is shaped not only by recognition of canonical W-box elements but also by chromatin state and regulatory context. For example, analyses of WRKY-associated chromatin using ChIP-seq have shown that histone acetylation (H3K9ac) is linked to the regulation of seven WRKY genes in rice after Magnaporthe grisea infection, with enrichment across promoter, transcription start site, and gene body regions (Hu et al., 2022). These findings indicate that WRKY binding operates within an epigenetically defined landscape that modulates accessibility and transcriptional activity.

  Comparative binding analyses further demonstrate that WRKY proteins exhibit both shared and distinct DNA-binding preferences. High-throughput profiling of WRKY–DNA interactions using DAP-Seq has revealed that individual WRKY TFs can bind overlapping sets of target genes, while also displaying specificity driven by sequence variation and contextual factors. This is particularly important in rice, where closely related WRKYs often regulate similar pathways but produce different phenotypic outcomes, suggesting that subtle differences in binding contribute to functional diversity (M. Li et al., 2023; Gladman et al., 2024, Preprint).

  Chromatin accessibility provides an additional layer of resolution by identifying regulatory regions that are permissive for WRKY binding under specific conditions. In rice, dynamic changes in chromatin accessibility during stress have been associated with WRKY-mediated responses, including under pathogen infection or salt and water stress conditions (Reynoso et al., 2022; Qiu et al., 2023; M. Li et al., 2024; Q. Yang et al., 2025). Integration of chromatin accessibility (ATAC-seq) with transcriptional data (RNA-seq) has been especially informative. For example, combined analyses identified OsWRKY28 and OsWRKY77 to play important roles during rice stripe virus (RSV) infection in rice through condition-specific changes in accessible chromatin and downstream gene expression (M. Li et al., 2024). These integrative approaches move beyond correlation by linking binding potential to functional transcriptional changes (Shamimuzzaman and Vodkin, 2013; Muhammad et al., 2020; Hu et al., 2022; Qiu et al., 2023; M. Li et al., 2023, 2024).

  Because genome-wide analyses generate candidate targets, target validation remains essential to confirm regulatory relationships and define sequence specificity. Biochemical and molecular assays have shown that WRKY binding to W-box elements is highly sensitive to sequence variation, and mutational analyses have demonstrated how changes in these elements alter transcriptional activation (Qiu et al., 2007; Zhang et al., 2008; Xiao et al., 2013; Cheng et al., 2015, 2019; Huang et al., 2022; L. Wang et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025). Such studies are critical for distinguishing functional binding sites from non-functional occupancy and for defining the regulatory logic underlying WRKY-mediated gene expression.

  Together, these approaches establish a mechanistic framework for understanding rice WRKY target specificity by linking DNA binding, chromatin accessibility, and transcriptional output. They enable discrimination between direct and indirect regulation and clarify how WRKY TFs achieve functional specificity within highly interconnected networks. However, these insights into target specificity must then be integrated within broader regulatory networks to fully resolve WRKY function.


### Reconstructing WRKY-centred regulatory networks through integrated data analysis
  A defining feature of WRKY TFs is their integration into highly interconnected regulatory networks, where they participate in feedback loops, co-regulatory modules, and signalling cascades that collectively shape plant responses to environmental and developmental cues. As a result, the function of individual WRKY TFs cannot be resolved through single-dataset analyses, but instead requires approaches that capture relationships across genes, pathways, and regulatory layers.

  Network-level analyses integrate transcriptomic and epigenomic data to reveal how WRKYs co-ordinate complex biological responses. Co-expression studies, for example, have shown that WRKY genes often form tightly connected modules associated with stress responses, hormone signalling, and developmental processes. In rice, such analyses have revealed that WRKY TFs frequently share overlapping expression patterns, consistent with their functional redundancy and partial compensation, as in the case of OsWRKY13 which coordinates crosstalk of biotic and abiotic stresses (Fig. 4) (Qiu et al., 2008; Jimmy and Babu, 2019a). Network approaches facilitate the identification of co-expressed gene clusters containing phylogenetically related or distinct genes that function redundantly (Berri et al., 2009; Jimmy and Babu, 2019b; Jeyasri et al., 2021), and can help guide design of experiments that combine multiple mutants to overcome redundancy and reveal function.

  Importantly, integration of multiple data types enhances the resolution of these networks. Combining transcriptomic, chromatin accessibility, and binding datasets has enabled the identification of regulatory relationships that extend beyond simple co-expression, linking WRKY TFs to their direct targets and downstream signalling cascades under specific conditions in rice, including MAPK-WRKY modules (Jimmy and Babu, 2019a, b; Yu et al., 2020; Das et al., 2022; Edrisi Maryan et al., 2023; Qiu et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2023). Such integrative approaches are particularly valuable for distinguishing shared versus unique regulatory roles among closely related WRKYs and for resolving how network architecture shifts across environmental contexts. Tools like RiceNet provide predictive frameworks for linking WRKYs with developmental and stress response genes (Lee et al., 2011, 2015).

  Despite these advances, reconstructing WRKY-centred regulatory networks remains challenging due to the fragmentation of available datasets and the lack of unified analytical frameworks (Table 1). Data relevant to WRKY function are often distributed across multiple platforms and studies, limiting data integration and systematic comparative analyses across genotypes, developmental stages, or environmental conditions, thereby constraining the generation of testable hypotheses. Additionally, only a few databases specifically focus on TFs, including WRKY TFs, such as PlantRegMap/Plant TFDB (Jin et al., 2017), PlantPAN (Chow et al., 2024), and RicetissueTFDB (W. Chen et al., 2019). This fragmentation constrains the identification of higher-order regulatory relationships and slows the development of predictive models of WRKY function.

  Efforts to develop and maintain integrated platforms and predictive network models are therefore critical for advancing WRKY biology. By consolidating diverse datasets and enabling cross-comparison of regulatory features, such frameworks can support hypothesis generation, prioritize candidate regulatory interactions, and facilitate experimental validation. Ultimately, a systems-level reconstruction of WRKY regulatory networks will be essential for understanding how these TFs co-ordinate complex traits and for translating this knowledge into crop improvement strategies.


### Predictive modelling of WRKY–DNA and WRKY–protein interactions
  The combinatorial complexity of WRKY–DNA and WRKY–protein interactions, together with extensive redundancy and context-dependent regulation, limits the ability of experimental approaches alone to resolve WRKY function at scale, motivating the use of predictive computational methods. Experimental approaches such as ChIP-seq, DAP-seq, and RNA-seq have been invaluable for mapping WRKY–DNA binding sites and downstream transcriptional responses, while GWAS can link natural variation to differences in TF function or expression. Structural methods, such as X-ray crystallography, provide detailed snapshots of WRKY–DNA interactions, but are labour intensive and limited in their ability to capture dynamic conformational changes. Taken together, these approaches provide high-resolution insight but are not readily scalable across the full WRKY family or the range of biological conditions in which these TFs operate.

  Machine learning-based structure prediction is transforming studies of biomolecular interactions. Tools such as AlphaFold (AF), which utilize deep learning algorithms, achieve near atomic accuracy in predicting protein structures from amino acids (Jumper et al., 2021). Newer platforms, such as AlphaFold3 (AF3), Chai1, Protenix, and Boltz1 extend these capabilities to accurately model interactions among proteins, nucleic acids, ions, and other small molecules (Abramson et al., 2024; Boitreaud et al., 2024, Preprint; Wohlwend et al., 2024, Preprint; X. Chen et al., 2025b, Preprint). Applied to WRKY TFs, these approaches enable high-confidence prediction of both protein structure and interaction interfaces, providing a scalable framework for interrogating WRKY–DNA and WRKY–protein interactions.

  AF3 reproduced high-quality interaction predictions, such as those for OsWRKY76, OsWRKY10, and OsWRKY36 with promoter sequences, with strong confidence (Interface Predicted Template Modelling or ipTM values between 0.77 and 0.80), aligning with positive electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) results. Conversely, when nucleotide substitutions were introduced to mutate the conserved W-box sequences, a corresponding reduction in ipTM values was observed indicating likely failed predictions and mirroring the loss of physical binding seen in EMSA (Supplementary Table S5). Similarly, AF3 accurately predicted the presence of a second W-box in the promoter sequence of rice PAL6 when one W-box was mutated. It retained high confidence (high ipTM), while changing the interacting region in the new OsWRKY36-PAL6 complex (Fig. 5). This suggests that AF3 can effectively distinguish between functional and disrupted binding sites, demonstrating its utility as a scalable complement labour-intensive experimental assay.

  Applied to rice WRKYs, these tools generate highly accurate 3D structures encompassing both conserved WRKY domains and variable flanking regions. These models enable identification of interaction interfaces with DNA, protein partners, and post-translational modifications. Because subtle differences can strongly influence DNA binding affinity or regulatory partnerships, such predictions provide critical insight into functional divergence among WRKY TFs (Ciolkowski et al., 2008; Aamir et al., 2017). In addition, visualization of alternative binding surfaces and conformational states offers a mechanistic basis for understanding how individual WRKY proteins engage distinct targets under different conditions (Sielemann et al., 2021). Overall, deep learning tools have the potential to untangle the regulatory networks and complex crosstalk between biological pathways.

  Predictive modelling provides an efficient first layer of screening to guide experimental validation. In silico screening of WRKY–DNA and WRKY–protein interactions enables prioritization of candidate TFs, target promoters, and interacting partners before expensive, labour-intensive assays such as EMSA and yeast one-hybrid (Y1H). For example, predicted interactomes with protein partners such as VQs or MAPKs can reveal shared versus condition-specific partners (Dong et al., 2024), helping to explain functional redundancy, divergence, and compensation. Shared interactors among WRKY TFs may suggest overlapping roles, while condition-specific partners can signal bifunctionality or stress-specific activation. In short, computational predictions not only reduce experimental burden but also provide insight into the regulatory logic underlying WRKY network behaviour.

  Mutational modelling and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations extend these analyses by incorporating structural flexibility and energetic stability. Platforms like FoldX (https://foldxsuite.crg.eu/) and newer AI-driven stability predictors assess the impact of sequence variation on the stability of WRKY–DNA and WRKY–protein complexes (Asghar et al., 2025; Delgado et al., 2025; Gerasimavicius et al., 2025, Preprint; Hay Mele et al., 2025; Sutherland et al., 2025). This allows computational evaluation of natural or engineered alleles on the complex configurations and stability before experimental testing (Z. Chen et al., 2025; Sobitan et al., 2025). MD simulations further capture conformational changes over time, revealing how specific amino acid substitutions alter binding affinity and protein stability (Brand et al., 2013; Goyal et al., 2015; Verma et al., 2016; Dai et al., 2021). For example, MD simulations of WRKY variants in barley and pigeon pea have shown that single amino acid substitutions in the WRKYGQK motif can significantly alter DNA binding affinity and protein stability (Pandey et al., 2018; Singh et al., 2019).

  Together these computational advances enable precise modelling of WRKY structure and function, prediction of stress-responsive behaviour, and optimization of gene editing targets (Brand et al., 2013). For example, growth penalties associated with OsWRKY89 overexpression (Wang et al., 2007) may be mitigated by computationally guided tuning of interaction strengths with partner proteins, rather than abolishing its function entirely. Such integrative strategies are paving the way for engineering WRKY TFs with optimized properties for crop improvement programmes.


## Conclusion
  WRKY TFs in rice represent one of the most structurally diverse and functionally versatile gene families, acting as regulators of plant growth, stress responses, and developmental transitions. Their evolutionary expansion, structural diversity, and capacity to mediate a wide spectrum of biological functions position WRKY TFs as central nodes in the rice transcriptional regulatory network. As detailed in this review, WRKY TFs can simultaneously modulate multiple pathways, often with antagonistic or pleiotropic effects, while also exhibiting extensive functional redundancy and compensation. Although these features confer resilience and flexibility, they also complicate functional dissection and limit straightforward application in crop improvement.

  Ultimately, a systems-level understanding of WRKY TFs that is rooted in evolutionary context, mechanistic insight, and network integration will provide the foundation for improvement of stress-tolerant, high-yielding rice varieties. The central challenge is no longer recognizing that WRKYs are important regulators, but determining which WRKY functions are sufficiently robust, specific, and engineerable to be translated into resilient rice phenotypes.

  Overcoming these challenges requires an integrative and systematic framework that aligns experimental and computational approaches with the biological complexity of WRKY regulation. Advances in molecular genetics, high-throughput functional genomics, multi-omics data integration, and predictive computational modelling now provide the means to resolve WRKY function at multiple levels (Fig. 6). Genome editing approaches, including CRISPR multiplex editing strategies, enable dissection of redundant gene families, while transcriptomic, proteomic, and epigenomic analyses capture context-dependent regulatory activity. Complementing these, machine learning-driven structural prediction and network analysis offer unprecedented scalable clarity to model WRKY–DNA and WRKY–protein interactions and to prioritize candidate regulatory relationships.

  Equally important is the need to evaluate WRKY function under biologically realistic conditions. Because WRKY TFs integrate signals across multiple pathways, their regulatory roles cannot be fully understood under single-stress conditions. Experimental designs that incorporate combined biotic and abiotic stresses are therefore essential to capture context-dependent behaviour and to predict phenotypic outcomes more accurately. Such approaches will be critical for identifying key WRKY nodes that control critical trade-offs between stress tolerance, growth and yield.

  Progress in this area will also depend on improved integration of diverse datasets. The development of centralized, interoperable platforms that integrate multi-omics data, regulatory annotations, and predictive models will be essential for reconstructing WRKY-centred regulatory networks and accelerating both discovery and application.
