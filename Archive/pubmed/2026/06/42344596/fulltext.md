# AI-Enabled Mapping
of Structure-Hazard Relationships
for Emerging Contaminants


## Abstract
  Emerging contaminant hazards are widespread, yet evidence is fragmented across the literature, registries, inventories, and bioassays. This study presents an integrated, reproducible framework that aligns literature-derived signals with molecular structure and regulatory hazard dimensions within a persistence, bioaccumulation, mobility, and toxicity (PBMT) lens. A task-tuned large language model extracted 21,277 mentions from 9,557 publications; harmonization across CompTox, PubChem, and Wikipedia yielded 1,081 unique candidates with 94.6% name consistency against 17 inventories. The landscape shows abundant toxicity information but limited mobility data. Structure profiling identified a recurring motifan aromatic core with branched alkyl substituents and oxygenated groupsand directional associations with composite hazard (positive for halogenation and negative for several oxygenated groups). To extend pathway inference to list-naïve chemicals, AutoGluon models trained on Tox21/ToxCast were applied to 49 additional candidates. This produced end-point-level predictions concentrated at the protein-function level (receptors, enzymes, transcription factors), which is operationally useful for prioritization. Combining model scores with PBMT evidence density yielded an auditable prioritization, highlighting where mobility measurements and focused assays would most increase confidence. The framework links textual, structural, regulatory, and predictive streams to enable rapid screening, targeted data acquisition, safer substitution, and adaptive regulatory updating.


## Introduction
  Chemical production and use have expanded rapidly across pharmaceuticals, agriculture and animal husbandry chemicals, personal care products, raw materials and industrial chemicals, biogenic metabolites, and vehicle-related applications, delivering social and economic benefits while amplifying environmental and public-health pressures and uncertainties. ,  The rising number of registered chemicals and the growing complexity of co-use and substitution challenge “one-by-one” assessment paradigms in both breadth and timeliness. Under these constraints, building a traceable, updatable, and decision-oriented evidence system becomes a central scientific need for chemical governance.

  Despite notable progress through multilevel governanceexemplified by the Stockholm Conventionregulatory assessment and management have struggled to keep pace with emerging substances and uses, leading to delays in evidence acquisition, weak cross-source alignment, and difficulties quantifying mixture and coexposure contexts. −    High-throughput in vitro screening programs such as Toxicology in the 21st Century (Tox21) and the Toxicity Forecaster (ToxCast) have accelerated the recognition of hazardous properties and modes of action (MOA), yet the scale and heterogeneity of the chemical universe still outstrip the completeness and consistency of available data. −   Because regulatory determinations ultimately rely on auditable signals from the literature and public databases, the ability to transform dispersed textual evidence into structured, comparable information along regulatory dimensions, such as PBMT (persistence, bioaccumulation, mobility, and toxicity), is the connective tissue between scientific assessment and regulatory practice.

  Within current workflows, literature text mining is the primary gateway into the evidence system: it must continuously extract chemical entities, use/source contexts and PBMT-relevant signals at scale, then align them with structures and authoritative lists. In practice, this process is labor-intensive and repetitive. Keyword/rule-based approaches and traditional machine learning (ML) or semiautomated pipelines can improve screening and labeling efficiency, but they face shared bottlenecks: limited capacity for synonym/variant normalization and ambiguity resolution; insufficient context sensitivity that can inflate false positives when high-frequency methodological terms or niche experimental reagents dominate specific literatures; instability in cross-source mapping; difficulty projecting sentence-level cues into PBMT-comparable, regulator-ready representations; subjectivity in multicriteria weighting and thresholding; and inadequate auditability and updatability to keep pace with new publications.  −      In this setting, large language model (LLM) offers clear motivation: by leveraging contextual representations and relation extraction learned from large corpora, LLM can jointly perform entity/alias extraction, use/source attribution and evidence categorization in a unified semantic space, thereby strengthening the mapping from dispersed text to PBMT-aligned, regulator-relevant signals. ,

  Environmental risks from emerging contaminants arise from distributed sources, evolving chemistries, and evidence that is fragmented across documents and inventories. To enable a more connected, system-level understanding, this study develops an end-to-end, reproducible framework that integrates screening, classification, evaluation, and prediction within a single evidence-alignment pipeline. A task-tuned large language model is used to screen the literature and extract chemical entities, aliases, and contextual cues; the outputs are standardized through synonym normalization and validated against external registries to support classification by use and source. The consolidated set is then evaluated by expressing heterogeneous literature signals in PBMT dimensions and by analyzing structure groupings with functional-group features, which together reveal cluster-level tendencies that support prioritization and safer substitution in real-world applications. ,  To complement uneven documentary evidence, an end-point-level predictive layer is introduced: models trained with AutoGluon on Tox21 and ToxCast assays provide pathway-relevant activity forecasts for chemicals that are absent from inventories or sparsely documented, extending inference beyond the current evidence base. ,  Model scores are combined with evidence density to yield an auditable and updatable prioritization and identify where additional measurements would most improve confidence. In doing so, textual, structural, regulatory, and predictive signals are rendered into interoperable PBMT descriptors that connect molecular-level assays with management-level decisions and enable integrated monitoring, redesign, and regulatory updating across practical use contexts.


## Methodology
  The pipeline comprises four stages: (1) LLM-driven literature extraction and classification; (2) synonym normalization, identifier mapping, and reconciliation with external databases; (3) regulatory list cross-walk, PBMT encoding, and mechanism-use correlation; and (4) structural fingerprints and end-point-level toxicity prediction (AutoML). The corresponding definitions, search strings, and prompt templates are provided in the .


### Literature Corpus Construction and Standardized
Answers
  A literature corpus was assembled from the Web of Science Core Collection (English, 2009–December 2024). The query combined terms for classes/uses of suspected emerging contaminants with exposure-, risk-, and occurrence-related keywords and was restricted to environmental and aquatic contexts. Records were deduplicated and screened using a PRISMA-style workflow. The complete search string and inclusion/exclusion criteria are reported in Supporting Information 1, ; the corpus manifest is listed in Supporting Information 2, . The final corpus comprised 9,557 records.

  For evaluation, a reference standard (“standardized answers”) was compiled and used as the ground truth for both the extraction and use/source classification tasks. Human annotations and model outputs were aligned to this standard under harmonized entity-boundary rules and task-specific output formats, which were defined a priori for both tasks (, ). The reference labels were created by trained graduate annotators working under written guidelines, and disagreementseither between annotators or between human and LLM outputswere resolved through a blind review and adjudication procedure in which the source of each candidate label (human vs model) was not disclosed to the reviewers (, Note S9 and Table S5). Performance was then assessed using standard evaluation metricsprecision, recall, and F1-scorederived from a unified confusion-matrix definition (TP, FP, FN, TN), with formal definitions, formulas, and worked examples consolidated in , .


### LLM-Driven Chemical Name Extraction and Classification
  To perform extraction of specific organic chemical names, aliases, and the position of the sentence from the abstract, the Llama-3.2–8B-Instruct model was used and fine-tuned with a LoRA-based procedure. The initial model was trained on a seed corpus of 510 manually annotated mentions of emerging organic contaminants curated from the literature. An iterative human-in-the-loop scheme was then adopted: in each of 10 rounds, the fine-tuned model was applied to a new batch of n × 10 articles (n = 10, 20, ..., 100), its extraction outputs were compared with independent human annotations, and all discrepancies were manually corrected. The corrected annotations from each round were merged with the existing corpus and used as training data for the next round. Over these 10 rounds, the annotated corpus of extraction records progressively expanded from about 500 to about 1,250 mentions. Use- or source-based classification spanned eight domains: “pharmaceuticals, agriculture and animal husbandry chemicals, industrial chemicals, raw materials, personal care products, biogenic metabolites, vehicle-related chemicals and others” was implemented using Qwen2.5–14B-Instruct-GPTQ-Int8, fine-tuned with the same LoRA strategy on approximately 200 manually labeled PubChem and Wikipedia summaries over four iterative rounds, until macro-level performance stabilized (, ). Prompt templates and input–output formats are provided in , , whereas the LLM architectures, training data sets (including sample sizes and label distributions), and full hyperparameter settings are described in , ; prompt and fine-tuning were implemented with Llama Factory.

  Quality evaluation and error attribution were conducted at the reference level against the standardized answers. Four error categories were used: Omission, Hallucination, Misinterpretation, and Broad Terms. Representative snippets and category definitions are provided in , and the paired human-model error sets are listed in , . Counting rules were mention based; repeated occurrences of the same chemical within a single paragraph were deduplicated once; when a single output simultaneously satisfied multiple error definitions, repeated counting across categories was allowed. Aggregation to document or chemical levels was performed after mention-level attribution. Metric formulas, confusion-matrix conventions, regression slope specification, and p-value calculations are described in , , . Ambiguities between class labels and individual molecules were resolved with scripted rules and a fine-tuned Qwen model; unresolved cases were retained as class labels and excluded from individual-level mapping.


### Synonym Normalization, Identifier Mapping,
and External Database Reconciliation
  Candidate strings were synonym-clustered and normalized to unify salts, solvates, hyphenation variants, and common aliases. Automated scripts reconciled each record against external resources in a defined priority: PubChem for preferred names and InChIKey or SMILES verification, ChemExpo for industry and product context and alias checks, and Wikipedia as a supplemental common-name source. When lookups returned ambiguous hits such as homonyms, trademarks, or class names, a fine-tuned Qwen model performed short-text adjudication to distinguish class versus individual and to verify contextual consistency. The result is a one-record-per-chemical canonical layer including DTXSID, CAS, InChIKey, SMILES, and the assigned use or source label (, ). Field definitions and exemplar entries are provided in and .


### Regulatory Cross-Walk, PBMT Encoding and Mechanism-Use
Correlation
  Canonicalized chemicals were cross-referenced by name against multiple authoritative suspect or hazard inventories. Each hit was first mapped to hazard traits and then encoded into the PBMT dimensions. Combined labels in inventories were reconciled according to predefined rules. Trait-to-dimension scoring used an ordinal 0–2 scale (0 = no identified hazard, 1 = intermediate or provisional indication of hazard, 2 = confirmed high hazard) with a noncompensatory max aggregator within each dimension, so that a strong hazard signal in any trait within a dimension is not averaged away by weaker signals in others. The cross-inventory comparison, the alignment with mechanism–use correlations, and the definitions for mechanism routes of action and concentration fields are given in , , and robustness under alternative encodings and aggregation rules is examined in , .


### Structural Fingerprints and End Point-Level
Toxicity Prediction
  Chemical structures were represented as SMILES and transformed into ECFP fingerprints generated with DeepChem under a unified preprocessing configuration. End point labels were sourced from Tox21 and ToxCast panels covering nuclear receptor and stress-response pathways and were cleaned and deduplicated before modeling. AutoGluon was used for tabular model selection and ensembling to produce end-point-level activity predictions for candidates, including those not covered by inventories.


### Applicability-Domain Screening, Calibration,
and Uncertainty Characterization
  The applicability domain was assessed via nearest-neighbor (maximum) Tanimoto similarity: Morgan/ECFP fingerprints (radius 2; fixed-length bit vector) were computed for all Tox21/ToxCast training compounds and external candidates, and for each external candidate the maximum similarity to any training compound was recorded; candidates were classified as in-domain (≥0.7), borderline (0.3–0.7), or out-of-domain (<0.3). End point-level discrimination on held-out test sets was summarized using PR-AUC (with the prevalence baseline defined as the positive-class fraction) and ROC-AUC. Predicted probabilities were calibrated using Platt scaling, ,  and calibration was evaluated with a 10-bin reliability analysis comparing bin-wise mean predicted probability against the observed fraction of positives (with ECE/MCE computed from binned statistics). Predictive uncertainty was quantified using a bootstrap ensemble: models were refit on bootstrap resamples, and 95% percentile intervals were computed from the ensemble prediction distribution for each chemical–end point pair, with interval-width summaries reported across chemicals/end points.


## Results

### Data Corpus and Extraction/Alignment Quality
  A literature corpus comprising 9,557 records was assembled from the Web of Science Core Collection. The initial extractor returned 21,277 raw outputs (candidate strings). Manual screening indicated 5,231 outputs that did not denote discrete chemical substances (e.g., class labels, role, or process descriptors) and 9,683 duplicate strings. The remaining 6,363 strings (29.9% of all outputs) constitute the prenormalization candidate pool. This decomposition delineates the screening scale and motivates downstream consolidation to mitigate alias and spelling-variant inflation (Figure  , workflow and scope; , provides the literature source data and LLM extraction aggregates).


#### Human Extraction Versus LLM Extraction
  Evaluation under a shared gold-standard protocol showed that the two fine-tuned LLMs attained F1 values comparable to, or slightly exceeding, those of human annotators while maintaining high precision ,  ( and , ). To contextualize performance against established baselines, we benchmarked common chemical text-mining tools (ChemDataExtractor2 and SciSpacy , ) on a 20-abstract subset under the same postprocessing and normalization rules used for our pipeline. Under this shared evaluation, ChemDataExtractor2 achieved precision = 0.406, recall = 0.788 (F1 = 0.536), whereas SciSpacy achieved precision = 0.240, recall = 0.231 (F1 = 0.235); both were substantially below the task-tuned LLM under our task definition and matching rules (, ). Throughput scaled very differently across approaches ( and ). The manual workflow exhibited a steep increase in time with workload and substantially larger batch-to-batch variability, whereas the model pipeline scaled more gently with a narrow variability (Figure  B). An ANOVA confirmed a statistically significant separation between the two approaches (F = 18.9, p = 0.002). Under a fixed time budget, the manual slowdown implies lower effective recall at scale, even when average F1 remains comparable (Table  ).

  Workload sensitivity and failure modes. As batch size increased, the manual workflow’s processing time and variability rose sharply, accompanied by higher omission rates and more frequent annotator disagreements (, ), a pattern consistent with elevated omission risk under time pressure and cognitive load. ,  By contrast, the model pipeline maintained a gentler time–workload relation and narrower dispersion, conditions associated with a lower tendency toward omissions but a relatively higher exposure to misinterpretation when contextual cues for role or relevance are subtle. These qualitative links accord with the quantitative partitions reported below and are coherent with the distinct failure profiles typically observed for human reading versus machine extraction.


#### Error Taxonomy and Counts; Iteration and
Text-Complexity Control
  Errors were classified into four predefined categories: Omission (the target chemical was present in the text but not extracted), Hallucination (the extracted name was absent from the text), Misinterpretation (a nontarget substance was labeled as a target), and Broad Terms (a class or family name was treated as a specific molecule). On the model side, the aggregated composition was 40.0% (54/135), 34.1% (46/135), 21.5% (29/135), and 4.4% (6/135). On the human side, the corresponding composition was Omission 68.9% (93/135), Misinterpretation 20.0% (27/135), Broad Terms 10.4% (14/135), and Hallucination 0.7% (1/135) (Figure  ). These distributions indicate that both pipelines are dominated by recall-side failures, yet with distinct profiles: human annotation exhibits a markedly higher proportion of omissions, whereas the model’s errors are more evenly divided between omissions and misinterpretations, with a modest contribution from broad-term boundary violations. The very low hallucination rates observed for both human and model workflows suggest that the task framing and evaluation protocol effectively constrained nonevidenced extractions.

  Iteration analyses further indicated nonmonotonic behavior in misclassification counts: 4-fold cross-validation across later rounds yielded 70 iterations: 15.5 ± 1.3, 80:18.0 ± 1.6, 90:25.0 ± 1.6, and 100:23.0 ± 1.7 (, ). The increase from 70 to 90 and only partial reduction at 100 suggest that additional iterations or raw training volume, in isolation, do not ensure accuracy gains. These observations support a data-centric interpretation: once routine regularities are learned, further improvement depends principally on the quality and diversity of training and review examplesspecifically, coverage of aliases and variants tied to registry keys, cross-sentence and abbreviation cases with explicit boundaries, negative/contrastive contexts around role terms and methodological sections (to suppress misinterpretation), and explicit guidance for class-versus-instance policies (to reduce broad-term extractions). ,


### Overview of Use Classification and Mechanism/Regulatory
Lenses
  The pipeline screened 9,552 documents and extracted 9,602 chemical names. After synonym consolidation and alignment to public registries, we retained 1,081 unique chemicals (one record per entity). Registry anchoring was broad (overlaps allowed): PubChem 387, ChemExpo 729, and Wikipedia 169. By standardized primary use or source, the curated set displays a human-facing profile (Figure  A). Pharmaceuticals contribute ≈30.5% (≈330/1,081) of the consolidated set. Agriculture and Animal Husbandry Chemicals contribute ≈18.7% (≈201/1,081), and Personal Care Products contribute ≈17.7% (≈191/1,081). The remaining categories each contribute smaller shares (Figure  B and and ). This distribution is consistent with near field exposure in consumer and personal-care contexts and with the intensive application of agrochemicals documented in exposure and hazard frameworks. −


#### Regulatory Coverage and Pathway Patterns
  Cross-referencing at the name level shows high alignment across inventories (overall ≈ 94.6%). The U.S. Toxic Substances Control Act (TSCA) Inventory covers ≈ 44.1% of the curated set (≈ 477/1,081), the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) annotates ≈ 16.4% (≈ 177/1,081), and the European Chemicals Agency (ECHA) lists ≈ 1.8% (≈ 19/1,081). By contrast, the NORMAN Network flags ≈ 1,016 of the 1,081 entities (≈ 94.0%), and a substantial share of these remain outside the three authoritative inventories above, indicating that suspect platforms capture many chemicals not yet formally classified. This distribution is consistent with near field exposure in consumer and personal-care contexts and with the intensive application of agrochemicals documented in exposure and hazard frameworks.

  The pathway data clarify these contrasts. At the broad level, narcosis is the most frequent signal across lists, although its magnitude varies (TSCA 17.61% (n = 84); IARC 24.29% (n = 43); NORMAN 13.68% (n = 139); ECHA 10.53% (n = 2)). This prominence is consistent with baseline toxicity driven by hydrophobicity and membrane partitioning (Escher and Hermens, 2002). Neuro-related families form the next tier: AChE inhibition is present in IARC 5.08% (n = 9), NORMAN 4.92% (n = 50), and TSCA 1.89% (n = 9); the broader neurotoxicity tag contributes IARC 6.78% (n = 12), NORMAN 4.43% (n = 45), and TSCA 1.68% (n = 8). Every list contains a sizable “no record” fraction at the pathway layer (TSCA 74.63% (n = 356); NORMAN 74.11% (n = 753); IARC 55.93% (n = 99); ECHA 89.47% (n = 17)), reflecting incomplete annotation rather than absence of effect (Kienzler et al., 2017; Barron et al., 2015).

  Mechanistic detail sharpens these patterns. In both IARC and TSCA, most narcosis assignments fall under nonpolar narcosis (IARC 20.34%, 36; TSCA 12.37%, n = 59). Within the NORMAN subset, neuroactive pesticide classes are especially salient: organophosphates (3.84%, 39), carbamates (1.08%, 11), and pyrethroid sodium channel modulation (1.18%, 12), with smaller contributions from the sodium channel and gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) targets. These signals are consistent with established toxicodynamics for major pesticide families.

  Use-inventory coupling is also distinctive when it is mapped to the standardized categories. ECHA is centered on Raw Materials 52.63% (10/19), followed by Vehicle-Related Chemicals 21.05% (4/19) and Personal Care Products 15.79% (3/19). IARC is more even, with Pharmaceuticals 17.51% (n = 31), Personal Care Products 15.82% (n = 28), Raw Materials 15.82% (n = 28), and Agriculture and Animal Husbandry Chemicals 12.99% (n = 23); “others”contribute 25.42% (n = 45). NORMAN concentrates on Pharmaceuticals 30.81% (n = 313), Agriculture and Animal Husbandry Chemicals 19.29% (n = 196), and Personal Care Products 17.13% (n = 174). Within TSCA-matched chemicals, Personal Care Products (27.25%, n = 130) and Pharmaceuticals (19.92%, n = 95) are the largest contributors. Agriculture and Animal Husbandry Chemicals account for 17.61% (n = 84), followed by Raw Materials 11.53% (n = 55), Others 11.32% (n = 54), Biological Metabolites 7.76% (n = 37), and Industrial Chemicals 4.19% (n = 20). Taken together with the pathway counts, these profiles connect exposure proximity and use intensity to how chemicals enter suspect versus authoritative inventories. ,


#### Implications for Monitoring and Triage
  The screening results link use profiles, inventory status, and mechanistic signatures in ways that inform risk management. Entities already captured by authoritative inventories are concentrated in mature application domains, notably, Raw Materials and parts of Personal Care Products. This pattern is evident in ECHA (Raw Materials 52.63%, 10/19) and TSCA (Personal Care Products 27.25%, n = 130; Raw Materials 21.17%, n = 101). At the pathway level these subsets show comparatively high shares of nonpolar narcosis (for example, IARC 20.34%, n = 36; TSCA 12.37%, n = 59), consistent with baseline toxicity associated with hydrophobic solvents and formulation functions common to longstanding product streams. In contrast, entities found only on suspect platforms are more common in Agriculture and Animal Husbandry Chemicals and co-occur with classical neurotoxic mechanisms (organophosphate 3.84%, n = 39; carbamate 1.08%, n = 11; pyrethroid sodium channel modulation 1.18%, n = 12), in line with established toxicodynamic for major pesticide classes. Unlisted or weakly catalogued entities cluster in fast-moving consumer and technology-responsive uses, particularly Pharmaceuticals and Personal Care Products, where broad-level signals combine narcosis with neuro and stress tags, consistent with detection under near field exposure and the discovery orientation of research and suspect-merging platforms. Overall, the inventory status covaries with use-domain maturity and with characteristic modes of action that reflect historical selection pressures and monitoring focus.

  Operationally, these patterns support a differentiated triage. In near field domains such as Personal Care Products and consumer-facing Pharmaceuticals, where list shares are sizable (for example, TSCA Personal Care Products 27.25%) and nonpolar narcosis or neuro tags recur, targeted early warning panels with small confirmatory assays are warranted. In suspect-only pools enriched for organophosphate, carbamate, and pyrethroid mechanisms, expert review and matrix-specific sampling are priorities in settings with intensive application. For chemicals already present in the TSCA, ECHA, or IARC, existing oversight provides a benchmark for incorporating newly surfaced candidates from this pipeline and helps align analytical thresholds and interpretive criteria. Finally, the large “no record” fraction at the pathway layer indicates that improved mechanistic annotation would increase interpretability and enable joint consideration of the inventory status and mechanism in subsequent updates.


### PBMT Hazard Profiling and Functional-Group/Structure
Coupling

#### PBMT Hazard Profile and Regulatory Coverage
  Leveraging 17 curated authoritative lists (including five ECHA subclasses, four IARC groups, and other authorities), the screened emerging contaminants were evaluated against the PBMT criteria (Figure  B; , and ; , ). Overall, 48.8% of substances are supported by at least one authoritative source, and 26.8% are corroborated by two or more independent lists; by contrast, 51.2% lack any authoritative classification, within which 45.8% occur only on the NORMAN suspect list and 5.4% are unrecorded on all sources (, ). This stratification indicates that, in the present corpus, regulatory evidence already covers about half of the candidates, while a similarly large fraction remains outside formal classification. Within the PBMT-evaluable subset, assignments are uneven across PBMT, with toxicity (T) dominating and mobility (M) comparatively sparse under the present inventory-based PBMT mapping. Accordingly, the observed sparsity in M should be interpreted as a feature of the available categorical classifications used here, rather than being taken as a quantitative statement about mobility itself. To complement categorical inventory labels, we additionally report computed, chemical-level mobility proxies in and . Under the adopted trait-to-dimension encoding, toxicity-related classifications (CMR/STOT) are the most frequently represented and remain dominant across the alternative scoring schemes examined in the sensitivity analysis ( and ). Within the toxicity space, CMR flags 452 substances and STOT flags 227, alongside smaller contributions from EDC (39) and aquatic toxicity (49). Cross-criterion traits explain the pattern: PMT (73) simultaneously loads P and M, POP (44) loads P and B, and PBT (35) spans P, B, and T. These counts (overlap allowed) are consistent with human-health end points (CMR/STOT) being most frequently captured in existing inventories, whereas mobility-focused evidence (PMT/vPvM) is less systematically documented in current regulatory sources. ,,  The “suspect-only” and “unrecorded” partitions sharpen where evidence is thin: nearly half of the data set appears only in NORMANvisible to researchers but not yet reflected in authoritative classificationswhile a smaller unrecorded segment sits off both regulatory and suspect list. Taken together, these partitions mark a frontier for further evaluation: within the limits of current inventories and our hazard-based PBMT encoding, existing regulatory signals cluster predominantly around toxicity, whereas mobility and persistence–mobility combinations remain under-represented in the available classifications across the inventories considered among the emerging contaminants captured in this study. Thus, the PBMT findings should be interpreted as patterns in hazard classifications rather than as quantitative risk rankings.


#### Structure Grouping and Use-Functional-Group
Coupling
  To improve practicality for management and redesign, substances were grouped by structural fingerprints using curated SMILES and queried against an extended SMARTS library (aromatic ring, carbonyls, esters/ethers, amines/amides, halogens, branched alkyls, and selected heteroaromatics). Functional groups occurring >5 times in any use were retained. Across 8 general use categories and 18 retained groups, 121 nonzero “use → functional-group” links were observed. To assess whether the recurring motif could be disproportionately driven by a small number of scaffold families within major use categories, we summarized Bemis–Murcko scaffold diversity and motif prevalence by use category (, ). The major use categories show substantial within-category scaffold diversity, supporting the notion that the motif is not solely attributable to a single dominant scaffold family. Cumulative hits included aromatic rings 373 (18.0%), isopropyl 316 (15.2%), halogens 250 (12.0%), and ketones 227 (10.9%). A characteristic backbone recurs across usesaromatic scaffold + branched alkyl (isopropyl/tert-butyl) + oxygenated functions (ketone/ester/ether/phenol). Tight co-occurrence clusters appear in the heatmap (e.g., Ester-Ether r = 0.995, Secondary amine-Amide r = 0.982, Ketone-Ether r = 0.956, Ester-Isopropyl r = 0.950, Aromatic ring-Isopropyl r = 0.932), reflecting shared synthesis routes and formulation goals.

  Use-specific structure–function needs help to explain these clusters. Personal care product formulations often combine fragrance chemistry (aromatic/ester/ether), skin feel and spread (branched alkyl), photostability or UV filtering (aromatic carbonyl/phenol/halogen), and solubility or film formation (ether/ester), which enriches this “aromatic + branched + oxygenated” pattern and favors within-cluster substitutions. ,  Pharmaceuticals frequently pair aromatic or heteroaromatic rings with carbonyl or amine platforms and targeted halogenation to tune binding and ADME, giving the highest share of significant links (72.2%, p < 0.05). Raw materials preserve halogen handles and carbonyl platforms for downstream transformations (significant share of 55.6%). By contrast, industrial and vehicle-related chemicals cover fewer groups (11/18 and 12/18, respectively) and show lower significant fractions (36.4% and 25.0%), reflecting simpler backbone requirements (Figure  A).

  As an external reference, we also benchmarked motif prevalence against the PBMT-background inventory (17 lists; 6,909 unique chemicals; 4,803 with RDKit-parseable SMILES), where the motif occurs at non-negligible rates under both branching definitions (, ). To link structure with the integrated PBMT-derived hazard score, we compared the integrated hazard score between chemicals with vs without each functional group using Mann–Whitney U tests, reported effect sizes as point-biserial r, and controlled multiple testing with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR (n = 589; matched by CAS and structure). Oxygenated fragments were associated with lower scores (e.g., ketone: Δ = −1.012, r = −0.248, q = 1.75e-07; hydroxyl: Δ = −1.376, r = −0.214, q = 6.49e-07), whereas halogenation was associated with higher scores (Δ = 0.671, r = 0.167, q = 2.52e-03). Most effects were small to moderate (|r| < 0.1 in 64.5%; 0.1–0.2 in 28.9%), with a meaningful subset at |r| ≥ 0.2 (6.6%) concentrated along two axes: aromatic + oxygenated and amine + amide. Given these effect sizes and the partial coverage of both structural and regulatory data, the associations should be interpreted as indicative rather than causal. Nevertheless, together with the co-occurrence clusters, they suggest that management at the structural-cluster level and cross-cluster redesign (e.g., moving away from halogenated aromatics toward more oxygenated, polar motifs where feasible) may be a useful starting point for hazard-informed substitution.


### Predicting Chemical Toxicity Risks

#### Toxicity Prediction Modeling Based on Bioassay
Data
  In Tox21 and ToxCast, toxicity is annotated as binary activity (active = 1, inactive = 0) derived from dose–response curve fitting (e.g., AC50) with statistical calling, which is suited to classification rather than regression of exact thresholds. Data were split 70/30 into training/test sets and modeled with AutoGluon. ,  On the test sets, performance was Tox21: accuracy = 0.9463, ROC-AUC = 0.7943, precision = 0.7199; ToxCast: accuracy = 0.9163, ROC-AUC = 0.7421, precision = 0.6903. End point-level baseline comparisons indicate nontrivial skill beyond prevalence baselines in PR space for most end points (, ), while overall discrimination remains heterogeneous across end points; accordingly, this prediction layer is used for screening-level prioritization and triage rather than quantitative risk determination. To improve probability interpretability for triage, shared Platt calibration was applied, and the calibrated probabilities show good aggregate calibration over N = 1,588,158 test predictions (ECE = 0.0018; MCE = 0.0175; , ). For external screening, 49 chemicals not in the initial list were evaluated across 629 end points, yielding 30,821 predictions; 2.04% of all end point predictions were predicted active. Applicability-domain screening based on nearest-neighbor Tanimoto similarity to the training set shows median similarity = 0.731 (mean = 0.676), with 25/49 (51.0%) in-domain (≥0.7), 16/49 (32.7%) borderline (0.3–0.7), and 8/49 (16.3%) out-of-domain (<0.3) (, ). Model uncertainty was additionally quantified via bootstrap ensembles on a representative end point subset (56 end points; 3332 external predictions with intervals), reporting 95% percentile intervals with median CI width = 0.0148 (mean = 0.0180; p90 = 0.0388; p95 = 0.0540) and applicability-domain–stratified summaries (, ). The full end point prediction matrix and metadata are provided in , Tables S5–S6. Predicted active end points were further summarized by PCA to derive a low-dimensional mechanism landscape; the first 10 PCs explain 80.07% of variance and are interpreted as stress-, signaling-, and gene-regulatory axes (, Note S6).


#### Mechanistic Levels of Activated Pathways
and Toxicity Effects
  To resolve biological levels of action, predicted-active end points were grouped by target type (Figure  A). Across all predicted active end points, 77.13% of activated pathways localize at the protein-function level, including receptors, enzymes, and transcription factors. Higher-level annotations are less frequent, with cellular processes at 6.58%, organ and system processes at 2.41%, phenotypic outcomes at 0.65%, and chemical-physical properties at 0.16%. Taken together, this distribution indicates that many suspects exert risk primarily via receptor/enzyme/transcriptional regulation, perturbing neurotransmission, endocrine signaling, and metabolic homeostasis, with multilayer extensions into GPCR-mediated signaling and core cellular functions. −   When a chemical shows coactivation across multiple end points, the pattern is interpreted as a screening signal of convergent biological perturbation. This supports prioritization for follow-up testing and helps link structure, mechanism, and use in subsequent management and redesign. ## Discussion: An AI-Enabled Screening Framework for Environmental Governance
  Heterogeneous evidence on emerging contaminantstextual mentions, registry identifiers, inventory status, structural characteristics, and bioassay predictionswas aligned within a single, reproducible workflow that progressed from screening to classification, evaluation, and prediction. A task-tuned language model was applied to 9,557 records to extract chemical entities and aliases; subsequent synonym handling and registry reconciliation reduced 21,277 raw strings to 1,081 unique chemicals with one audited record per entity. Cross-referencing with inventories enabled expression of the evidence in the PBMT dimensions, while an end-point-level predictive layer trained on Tox21/ToxCast extended pathway inference to chemicals that are absent from inventories or sparsely documented. The resulting pipeline lowered manual burden while preserving provenance, thereby facilitating the transition from dispersed reports to actionable shortlists without specialized computing infrastructure.

  The observed composition and coverage patterns have direct implications for environmental management. The consolidated set is dominated by human-facing domainspharmaceuticals (∼30.5%), agrochemicals (∼18.7%), and personal-care products (∼17.7%)consistent with near-field exposure and high application intensity. Name-level concordance with the Toxic Substances Control Act (TSCA) inventory is ∼ 44.1%, with the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) ∼ 16.4%, and with the European Chemicals Agency (ECHA) ∼ 1.8%, whereas many entries appear only in NORMAN suspect lists. Mechanistic labels are led by narcosis across inventories, with neuro-related families (for example, acetylcholinesterase inhibition and broader neurotoxicity) forming a secondary signal; a substantial fraction in each list lacks recorded mechanism, which reflects gaps in annotation rather than evidence of safety. Taken together, these distributions support differentiated triage: near-field consumer domains warrant early warning panels and streamlined confirmatory assays centered on narcosis and neuro/stress end points; suspect-only pools resembling agrochemical use should receive expert review and matrix-specific sampling; and mature raw-material streams benefit from class-level stewardship to avoid like-for-like substitutions that preserve baseline hydrophobic toxicity. PBMT provides a compact, policy-relevant lens for such cross-list interpretation, hazard communication, and risk-informed prioritization.

  Structure-based patterns reinforce these directions. A recurring motif was identified across use categories: an aromatic core with branched alkyl substituents and oxygenated groups. Within the subset with matched structures and PBMT annotations, halogenation showed a positive association with the composite hazard score, whereas several oxygenated functionalities showed negative associations. Given the modest effect sizes and the incomplete coverage of both structural and regulatory data, these associations should be viewed as indicative rather than causal. Nevertheless, taken together with the co-occurrence clusters, they support management at the level of structural clusters rather than individual substances. Accordingly, monitoring panels should pair baseline hydrophobicity and narcosis screening with neurotoxicity assays where agricultural applications dominate and incorporate mobility-focused measurements along the pathway from surface water into drinking water to complement the evidence base for mobility-relevant properties in future extensions of the framework. For redesign, where performance constraints allow, shifting away from halogenated aromatics toward more oxygenated and more polar scaffolds may serve as a hazard-informed starting point for substitution to be evaluated case-by-case with confirmatory testing to avoid regrettable substitution.

  The prediction layer functions as a complement to documentary evidence rather than as a stand-alone verdict. AutoGluon models trained on Tox21/ToxCast achieved competitive discrimination on held-out data and generated 30,821 end point predictions for 49 unlisted candidates, with 2.04% of end points predicted active. Signals are concentrated at the protein-function level (receptors, enzymes, transcription factors), which is operationally useful because it prioritizes candidates whose mechanisms converge across target classes. Integrating model scores with an evidence-density view turns prediction into a prioritization aid: high scores with rich documentation indicate immediate targeted testing; high scores with sparse documentation highlight where small experiments can produce the largest information gain; low scores with strong documentation can be deprioritized while being retained for surveillance. Such triangulation is consistent with the broader experience that machine-learning outputs are most reliable when interpreted alongside independent evidence streams.

  Several limitations bound the interpretation and suggested targeted upgrades. Expert review remains the rate-limiting step for high-fidelity extraction and synonym adjudication. In-vitro assay panels cannot capture mixture effects, transformation products, or exposure dynamics; a negative prediction for a single compound does not preclude risk in complex matrices or over life cycles. Identifier heterogeneity (for example, multiple or class-level CAS RNs) can lead to duplicate or missed matches; maintaining versioned mappings to DTXSID and InChIKey and recording adjudication decisions reduce reconciliation drift. Iteration analyses showed nonmonotonic error reduction, indicating that extending training alone is insufficient. Gains depend primarily on the quality and diversity of examples, including coverage of aliases tied to registry keys, explicit boundaries for cross-sentence references and abbreviations, and negative contexts around role terms in method sections to restrain omission and misinterpretation where they arise. Because manual throughput declines and variability increases with the workload, hybrid human-in-the-loop designs should allocate expert time to the instances most likely to alter downstream decisions.

  Overall, the framework establishes a practical baseline for moving from literature to shortlists and mechanistic anchors with modest user effort while retaining auditability at each step. By consolidating identities, uses and sources, PBMT-relevant traits, and pathway-level predictions, earlier recognition and avoidance of problematic chemistries are enabled, together with integrated monitoring, redesign, and regulatory updating across applications. The emphasis on interoperable, hazard-oriented PBMT descriptors connects molecular-level assays with management-level choices in a screening context, and the modular design permits the incorporation of larger corpora, improved models, and additional evidence streams as they become available.


## Conclusion
  This study establishes an integrated, reproducible framework that aligns the literature, molecular structure, and regulatory hazard information within a PBMT lens. From 9,557 publications, 21,277 mentions were consolidated into 10081 chemicals, revealing abundant toxicity evidence within the inventory-based PBMT mapping adopted here. Structure profiling indicates a higher hazard with halogenation and a lower hazard with selected oxygenated functions. With a task-tuned large language model supporting scalable extraction and Tox21/ToxCast predictions guiding pathway activity, the framework delivers an auditable prioritization for monitoring, safer substitution, and adaptive regulatory updating.
