## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text (abstract, introduction, results, discussion, materials and methods) as provided
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific content, experimental design, data interpretation, and claims as presented in the provided text; no external data or supplementary figures were available for direct inspection
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors report that UV-DDB and AAG form a direct, high-affinity protein/protein complex in the absence of DNA (SPR KD ≈ 17.5 nM), that this interaction is maintained but weakened approximately 2.6-fold in the presence of AP site-containing dsDNA (SPR KD ≈ 46.2 nM), that BLI independently confirms the interaction with higher KD values (84.1 nM DNA-free; 128.9 nM DNA-bound), that EMSA shows UV-DDB-mediated ternary complex formation with redistribution of AAG/DNA species, and that AlphaFold3 modeling predicts a DNA-dependent repositioning of AAG from a DDB1-dominated interface toward a lesion-proximal configuration near DDB2. The authors propose a model in which DNA binding acts as a molecular switch that reconfigures the UV-DDB/AAG interaction, potentially enabling UV-DDB to function as a recruitment platform facilitating directional progression of AAG through the BER cycle.
- **Visible evidence base** Figures 1–5 (described in text), Tables 1–3 (described in text), Supplementary Figures S1–S4 and Supplementary Tables S1–S2 (referenced but not provided), Materials and Methods sections 4.1–4.9
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Supplementary figures and tables (S1–S4, S1–S2) containing raw sensorgrams, fitted curves, kinetic parameters with standard errors, and AlphaFold3 quality metrics (pTM, ipTM, PAE plots) were not provided; raw EMSA gel images and quantitative binding curves were not directly inspectable; AlphaFold3 prediction outputs (PDB files, confidence maps) were not available for independent assessment

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript addresses a timely and potentially important question: how BER and NER pathways may be functionally integrated through direct protein/protein interactions, specifically between UV-DDB and AAG. The authors combine multiple biophysical techniques (SPR, BLI, EMSA) with AlphaFold3-based structural prediction to characterize the UV-DDB/AAG interaction under DNA-free and DNA-bound conditions. The core finding, that AAG and UV-DDB form a high-affinity complex whose affinity is modulated by DNA, is potentially interesting. However, the manuscript has several significant limitations that prevent the case from being fully established. First, the AlphaFold3 predictions, which form the structural basis for the proposed "molecular switch" model, are entirely computational and lack experimental validation. The in silico alanine substitution analysis is presented as supporting evidence but is itself purely predictive. Second, the discrepancy between SPR and BLI KD values (approximately 4.8-fold in the DNA-free condition) is acknowledged but not mechanistically resolved; the authors attribute it to inter-platform variability, but this explanation is not fully satisfying given the magnitude of the difference. Third, the functional significance of the interaction is inferred rather than demonstrated; no experiments directly test whether UV-DDB enhances AAG activity in this system, despite the authors citing their previous work showing such stimulation. Fourth, the use of a non-hydrolyzable THF AP site analog limits the conclusions that can be drawn about post-catalytic release of AAG, a point the authors acknowledge but do not experimentally address. The manuscript is clearly written and the techniques are appropriate, but the gap between the correlative biophysical and computational data and the proposed mechanistic model is substantial. The work would be considerably strengthened by experimental validation of the predicted interface residues, direct measurement of AAG catalytic activity in the presence and absence of UV-DDB, and a more rigorous treatment of the SPR/BLI discrepancy.

- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in the DNA repair field, particularly those studying BER and NER cross-talk, would be the primary audience. The work may also interest biophysicists studying protein/protein interactions in DNA repair complexes, and structural biologists using AlphaFold for complex prediction. The potential link between UV-DDB and AAG has implications for understanding how cells coordinate repair of oxidative and alkylation damage with UV damage, which could be relevant to cancer biology and therapeutic development. However, the interest is somewhat tempered by the lack of functional validation; the work is more likely to be cited as a biophysical characterization than as a definitive mechanistic study.

- **Major strengths** The manuscript combines multiple orthogonal biophysical techniques (SPR, BLI, EMSA) to characterize the UV-DDB/AAG interaction, which is a methodological strength. The use of AlphaFold3 to generate testable structural hypotheses is appropriate and forward-looking. The authors are transparent about the limitations of their approach, particularly regarding the THF AP site analog and the computational nature of the structural predictions. The writing is clear and the logical flow from biophysical characterization to structural modeling to proposed mechanism is easy to follow. The acknowledgment of inter-platform variability in KD measurements and the attempt to contextualize this within the published literature is commendable.

- **Major Concerns**

- **Concern ID** R1-M1
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** Yes
- **Axis** Experimental validation of structural predictions
- **Claim pointer** The authors claim that AlphaFold3 predicts a specific interface between AAG and DDB1 in the DNA-free state, and that DNA binding repositions AAG toward the lesion near DDB2, with specific residue contacts (Arg145, Gln195, Lys229 on AAG; Glu800, Thr798, Glu342, Gln759 on DDB1) and active site residue distances to the AP site C1′ atom.
- **Evidence pointer** Section 2.3, Figures 4–5, Tables 1–3, Supplementary Figures S3–S4
- **Concern** The entire structural model rests on AlphaFold3 predictions that are not experimentally validated. The in silico alanine substitution analysis is itself a prediction and cannot serve as independent validation. No mutagenesis, crosslinking, or co-crystallization data are presented to confirm the predicted interface. The authors state that experimental validation is "planned as a key objective of future follow-up studies," which explicitly acknowledges that the structural claims are currently unverified. The predicted distances (e.g., 2.1 Å for Arg145 NE–Glu800 OE2) are presented with a precision that implies confidence, but AlphaFold3 confidence metrics (pLDDT, ipTM) are only referenced in supplementary materials not provided for inspection. Without experimental confirmation, the structural basis for the proposed "molecular switch" mechanism is speculative.
- **Why it matters** The central mechanistic claim of the manuscript, that DNA binding reconfigures the UV-DDB/AAG interaction to enable directional progression of AAG through the BER cycle, depends entirely on the predicted structural rearrangement. If the predicted interface is incorrect or the conformational change does not occur, the proposed model collapses. Computational predictions, however sophisticated, cannot establish a structural mechanism without experimental support.
- **Resolution test** Provide experimental validation of the predicted interface, for example through site-directed mutagenesis of the predicted contact residues (DDB1 Glu800, AAG Arg145, AAG Lys229) followed by binding assays (SPR or BLI) to demonstrate loss or alteration of interaction. Alternatively, crosslinking mass spectrometry or cryo-EM could provide direct structural evidence. At minimum, the authors should provide the AlphaFold3 confidence metrics (pLDDT, ipTM, PAE) for the specific interface residues to allow assessment of prediction reliability.

- **Concern ID** R1-M2
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** Yes
- **Axis** Functional significance of the interaction
- **Claim pointer** The authors claim that UV-DDB may function as a recruitment platform that facilitates directional progression of AAG through the BER cycle, and that the interaction has biological relevance supported by cellular and in vivo observations.
- **Evidence pointer** Section 3 (Discussion), references to previous work [7,14]
- **Concern** The manuscript does not present any functional experiments demonstrating that the UV-DDB/AAG interaction affects AAG catalytic activity, processivity, or BER efficiency. The authors cite their previous work showing that UV-DDB stimulates AAG-mediated lesion excision, but the present study does not replicate or extend these findings with functional assays. The discussion invokes cellular and in vivo observations (XP-E phenotypes, AAG deficiency) to support biological relevance, but these are circumstantial and not directly linked to the specific interaction characterized here. The claim that UV-DDB acts as a "recruitment platform" is an inference from binding data and structural predictions, not a demonstrated function.
- **Why it matters** The title and abstract promise a "dynamic regulator linking base excision and nucleotide excision repair." Without functional evidence that the interaction modulates AAG activity or BER progression, the manuscript delivers a biophysical characterization but not a mechanistic demonstration. The gap between "these proteins bind" and "this binding regulates repair" is substantial and is not bridged by the present data.
- **Resolution test** Include experiments that directly test the functional consequence of the UV-DDB/AAG interaction. For example, measure AAG glycosylase activity (cleavage of damaged base-containing substrates) in the presence and absence of UV-DDB, with varying concentrations and order of addition. Alternatively, use the predicted interface mutants to show that disrupting the interaction abolishes the stimulatory effect on AAG activity. If the authors intend to claim a role in BER progression, they should also test whether UV-DDB affects AAG product release or handoff to downstream BER enzymes (e.g., APE1).

- **Concern ID** R1-M3
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** No
- **Axis** Quantitative rigor of binding analyses
- **Claim pointer** The authors report SPR KD values of approximately 17.5 nM (DNA-free) and 46.2 nM (DNA-bound), and BLI KD values of approximately 84.1 nM (DNA-free) and 128.9 nM (DNA-bound), concluding that the interaction is high-affinity and maintained but reduced in the presence of DNA.
- **Evidence pointer** Sections 2.2.1–2.2.2, Supplementary Figures S1–S2, Supplementary Tables S1–S2
- **Concern** The discrepancy between SPR and BLI KD values is substantial (approximately 4.8-fold in the DNA-free condition and 2.8-fold in the DNA-bound condition). The authors attribute this to inter-platform variability and cite literature reporting 1.2- to 18-fold differences. While this range encompasses the observed discrepancy, the explanation is not fully satisfying. The two techniques measure binding under different immobilization geometries and mass transport conditions, and the observed differences could reflect genuine differences in the binding mechanism (e.g., avidity effects, conformational selection) rather than technical artifacts. The authors do not provide a detailed analysis of the kinetic parameters (ka, kd) from both platforms to assess whether the discrepancy arises from association or dissociation differences. Additionally, the SPR experiments used a different DNA substrate (40-mer) than the EMSA and BLI experiments (37-mer), which could affect the comparison. The KD values are presented as single numbers without confidence intervals or replicate statistics in the main text, despite the authors stating that three independent replicates were performed.
- **Why it matters** The quantitative claim that DNA reduces binding affinity approximately 2.6-fold is central to the "molecular switch" model. If the true affinity change is smaller or larger than reported, or if the effect is an artifact of the specific experimental conditions, the mechanistic interpretation changes. The lack of detailed kinetic analysis and the unexplained platform discrepancy weaken confidence in the quantitative conclusions.
- **Resolution test** Provide a detailed comparison of kinetic parameters (ka, kd) from SPR and BLI, including confidence intervals and replicate statistics. Discuss whether the observed KD differences arise from association or dissociation rate differences and whether these are consistent with the proposed conformational rearrangement model. Consider performing both SPR and BLI with the same DNA substrate to eliminate substrate length as a variable. If the platform discrepancy cannot be resolved, acknowledge this as a limitation and temper the quantitative claims accordingly.

- **Concern ID** R1-M4
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** No
- **Axis** Interpretation of EMSA data
- **Claim pointer** The authors claim that EMSA shows UV-DDB-mediated ternary complex formation accompanied by redistribution of binary AAG/DNA species, supporting cooperative interaction between UV-DDB and AAG on abasic DNA.
- **Evidence pointer** Section 2.1.2, Figure 2D–E
- **Concern** The EMSA experiments are described qualitatively in the text, with the claim that ternary complex formation is accompanied by redistribution of binary AAG/DNA species. However, the quantitative analysis (Figure 2E) is not described in sufficient detail to assess the rigor of the conclusion. The authors report an apparent KD of 4.06 nM from fitting the EMSA data, but it is unclear whether this represents the AAG-DNA interaction, the UV-DDB-AAG interaction, or a combined effect. The text states that "the proportion of DNA in the ternary complex increased with UV-DDB concentration, while the AAG/DNA complex decreased," but the stoichiometry of the ternary complex is not established. It is also unclear whether the "redistribution" reflects a true cooperative mechanism or simply competition between AAG and UV-DDB for DNA binding.
- **Why it matters** The EMSA data are presented as evidence for cooperative interaction, which is a key component of the proposed model. If the interpretation is incorrect, the model loses support. The lack of detailed quantitative analysis makes it difficult to evaluate the strength of this evidence.
- **Resolution test** Provide a more detailed description of the EMSA quantification, including how the identity of each band was assigned, how the ternary complex stoichiometry was determined, and what model was used to fit the data. Consider performing competition experiments with unlabeled DNA or using a catalytically inactive AAG mutant to distinguish between cooperative binding and competitive displacement. If possible, include a supershift experiment with antibodies against AAG or UV-DDB to confirm the composition of the ternary complex.

- **Concern ID** R1-m2
- **Severity** Minor
- **Axis** Discussion of alternative interpretations
- **Affected element** Section 3 (Discussion)
- **Evidence pointer** Section 3
- **Issue** The discussion presents the proposed model as the most likely interpretation but does not thoroughly consider alternative explanations. For example, the reduced affinity in the presence of DNA could be due to competition between AAG and UV-DDB for DNA binding rather than a conformational rearrangement. The authors also do not discuss whether the interaction could be an artifact of the high protein concentrations used in the biophysical assays.
- **Required correction** Add a paragraph discussing alternative interpretations and how the data distinguish between them. Acknowledge the limitations of the biophysical approach and the need for functional validation.

- **Concern ID** R1-m3
- **Severity** Minor
- **Axis** Consistency of terminology
- **Affected element** Abstract, Sections 2.2, 3
- **Evidence pointer** Abstract, Sections 2.2, 3
- **Issue** The terms "DNA-free" and "DNA-bound" are used throughout, but the abstract also refers to "DNA-independent" and "DNA-dependent" interactions. These terms are not precisely defined and could be confused. Additionally, the abstract states the interaction is "maintained but reduced approximately 2.6-fold upon binding to AP site-containing dsDNA," but the BLI data show a larger reduction (84.1 to 128.9 nM, approximately 1.5-fold), which is not mentioned in the abstract.
- **Required correction** Define the terms "DNA-free" and "DNA-bound" clearly in the introduction or methods. In the abstract, either report both SPR and BLI KD values or state that the reduction was observed by SPR and note the platform-dependent variation.

- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria**
  - **Originality**: The concept of BER/NER crosstalk through direct protein/protein interactions is not entirely new, as the authors themselves cite previous work showing UV-DDB stimulation of glycosylases. The specific characterization of the UV-DDB/AAG interaction under DNA-free and DNA-bound conditions, and the proposal of a DNA-dependent molecular switch, has some novelty. However, the originality is limited by the lack of functional validation and the reliance on computational predictions for the structural claims.
  - **Scientific importance**: The question of how DNA repair pathways are coordinated is important, and a mechanistic understanding of BER/NER integration could have implications for cancer biology and therapy. However, the present work does not fully deliver on this promise, as it provides binding data and structural predictions but not functional evidence. The importance is therefore potential rather than demonstrated.
  - **Interdisciplinary readership**: The work is primarily of interest to DNA repair specialists and biophysicists. The use of AlphaFold3 may attract computational biologists, but the lack of experimental structural validation limits the broader appeal. The manuscript is unlikely to attract readers outside the DNA repair and biophysics communities.
  - **Technical soundness**: The biophysical techniques are appropriate and generally well-executed, but the quantitative rigor is insufficient. The unexplained SPR/BLI discrepancy, the lack of statistical reporting, and the absence of functional validation are significant technical weaknesses. The AlphaFold3 predictions are presented with confidence that is not justified without experimental support.
  - **Readability for nonspecialists**: The manuscript is clearly written and the logical flow is easy to follow. However, the heavy reliance on specialized terminology and the lack of a clear take-home message for nonspecialists limit accessibility. The abstract is reasonably accessible, but the discussion assumes significant background knowledge in DNA repair.

- **Recommendation posture** Currently not established from the provided evidence. The manuscript presents a biophysical characterization of the UV-DDB/AAG interaction that is potentially interesting, but the central mechanistic claims are not supported by the data presented. The structural model is entirely computational, the functional significance is inferred rather than demonstrated, and the quantitative rigor of the binding analyses is insufficient. The work would be considerably strengthened by experimental validation of the predicted interface, functional assays linking the interaction to AAG activity, and a more rigorous treatment of the binding data. If these concerns are addressed, the manuscript could become a valuable contribution to the DNA repair field. As it stands, the case for a "dynamic regulator linking base excision and nucleotide excision repair" is not established.
