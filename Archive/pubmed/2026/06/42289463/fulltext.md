# Uncovering core regulators of multi-abiotic stress adaptation in Arabidopsis thaliana through integrative meta-analysis and machine learning with RT-qPCR validation


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58347-8.


## Introduction
  Plant growth, productivity, and reproductive success are fundamentally constrained by water scarcity, one of the most significant abiotic challenges. This state of water deficit is often a direct consequence of osmotic stress, which occurs under conditions of drought, and salt stress1–3. During these stresses, the accumulation of solutes—including salts, sugars, and various ions—reduces the water available for cellular functions4,5. A fundamental mechanism for plant acclimation to these conditions involves a rapid and extensive remodeling of their transcriptomic and proteomic landscapes, facilitating the adjustment of metabolic pathways and the production of protective molecules6,7. Central to this vast molecular reprogramming is the signaling cascade mediated by the phytohormone abscisic acid (ABA), which acts as a master regulator of stress-responsive gene expression and downstream physiological adaptations8,9. Therefore, elucidating the intricacies of the transcriptomic response to osmotic stress is crucial. It not only deepens our fundamental understanding of plant acclimation but also provides a powerful blueprint for identifying key regulatory genes and pathways essential for developing stress-resilient crops10.

  To this end, advancements in high-throughput genomic technologies, particularly next-generation sequencing (NGS) and microarray platforms, have profoundly enhanced our capacity to unravel the molecular mechanisms underpinning plant responses to osmotic stress11. NGS, through RNA-sequencing (RNA-Seq), enables comprehensive, quantitative profiling of the transcriptome, overcoming limitations of traditional methods by detecting low-abundance transcripts, alternative splicing events, and novel stress-responsive genes with unprecedented resolution and sensitivity12. Complementarily, microarray analyses provide robust, cost-effective tools for genome-wide expression monitoring, facilitating the identification of differentially expressed genes and microRNAs involved in osmotic signaling pathways, such as those modulating ABA-mediated responses and osmoprotectant biosynthesis13. By integrating these approaches, researchers can construct detailed regulatory networks, revealing key hubs like transcription factors and epigenetic modifiers that orchestrate acclimation to water deficit and salinity, thereby accelerating the translation of molecular insights into breeding strategies for resilient crop varieties14.

  However, the wealth of transcriptomic data generated by these technologies is often fragmented across numerous independent studies, each focusing on specific conditions, genotypes, or developmental stages. This study-specific variability can obscure the core molecular principles conserved across different forms of osmotic stress. Transcriptomic meta-analysis provides a powerful framework to surmount this fragmentation15,16. By statistically integrating data from multiple experiments, this approach transcends the limitations of individual studies, enhancing statistical power to distill a robust signature of differentially expressed genes17. This synthesis allows for the identification of conserved regulatory networks and core hub genes—such as those governing ABA signaling, osmolyte synthesis, and ROS scavenging—that represent high-confidence targets for developing multi-stress resilient crops through advanced breeding and biotechnological strategies18.

  However, while meta-analysis effectively consolidates data, the resulting high-dimensional datasets pose significant challenges for identifying the most critical regulatory genes from a vast pool of candidates19. To address this complexity, machine learning (ML) has emerged as a powerful paradigm for dissecting complex transcriptomic landscapes and accelerating the discovery of targets for crop resilience20. By leveraging supervised and particularly ensemble algorithms, ML enhances the precision of gene identification by recognizing nonlinear patterns, handling high-dimensional data, and predicting stress responses across diverse genotypes and conditions21. For instance, hybrid feature selection approaches combining methods like ReliefF and LASSO with classifiers such as Random Forest and XGBoost have successfully prioritized key osmotic stress-responsive genes in Arabidopsis, including those involved in galactinol synthesis essential for drought and salinity tolerance22. Similarly, in rice, Random Forest ensembles have achieved high-accuracy multiclass stress discrimination, revealing conservation of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for various abiotic stresses, including osmotic stress23. In wheat under drought conditions, an improved Random Forest-Boruta ensemble has effectively identified core genes within crucial pathways like ABA signaling and osmotic adjustment24. Furthermore, other methods like SVM clustering, when applied to meta-analysis data, have helped define core abiotic stress gene sets in Arabidopsis, highlighting shared early response mechanisms to osmotic and salt stresses, such as those mediated by ethylene signaling25.

  Building on the power of these approaches, the primary objective of this study is to identify and experimentally validate a high-confidence set of genes that are consistently involved in the response to drought, salinity, and ABA signaling. To achieve this, we implemented an integrative pipeline in which meta-analysis was first used to derive a robust set of differentially expressed genes across multiple independent datasets. These genes were subsequently analyzed using three established ensemble machine learning algorithms (Random Forest, XGBoost, and AdaBoost) to prioritize candidates based on their predictive relevance to stress conditions. Finally, selected genes were subjected to cis-regulatory analysis and experimental validation using RT-qPCR. Through this approach, our aim is not to develop new computational methods, but to leverage existing analytical tools to uncover biologically meaningful and potentially under-characterized regulators of the abiotic stress response.

### Dataset collection and pre-processing of microarray data
  To investigate the conserved transcriptomic responses across osmotic stresses, a systematic search was conducted on the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Our selection was strictly limited to Affymetrix microarray datasets from Arabidopsis thaliana wild-type (WT) plants at the seedling stage to ensure a homogenous developmental and genetic background. This curated selection comprised sixteen datasets (Table 1) categorized under salt stress (GSE39236, GSE53308, GSE71001, GSE79997), drought stress (GSE56583, GSE10643, GSE10670, GSE15577, GSE16474, GSE35258, GSE48474, GSE49418, GSE56642, GSE72050), and ABA stress (GSE28800, GSE271692). For each dataset, preprocessing was performed independently using the Robust Multi-array Average (RMA) algorithm in R for background correction, quantile normalization, and probe summarization26. Subsequently, for each stress type, the normalized expression profiles from multiple datasets were merged into a unified matrix based on common gene symbols via cross-platform normalization to ensure comparability. Batch effects arising from technical variations across studies were then mitigated using the ComBat function from the sva package in R, applied separately to each stress-specific merged matrix27. Drought and salinity stresses were selected as primary osmotic stressors, while abscisic acid (ABA) was included as the central hormonal mediator of osmotic stress signaling. This design allows the identification of both upstream environmental responses and downstream regulatory mechanisms within a unified osmotic stress framework.

## Differential gene expression (DEGs) analysis
  Subsequently, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for each stress condition were identified independently using the linear modeling approach implemented in the limma package. Genes were considered significantly differentially expressed if they met two stringent criteria: a Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-value ≤ 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change (|log2FC|) ≥ 1. Finally, to identify a core set of genes consistently responsive to osmotic stress, the lists of up- and down-regulated DEGs from the salt, drought, and ABA analyses were intersected.


## Gene ontology enrichment analysis
  To elucidate the biological significance of the identified DEGs, functional enrichment analysis was performed on the gene lists from each stress condition. The sets of up-regulated and down-regulated genes for salt, drought, and ABA treatments were analyzed separately using the ShinyGO web server42. This analysis identified over-represented Gene Ontology (GO) terms within the three principal domains: Biological Process (BP), Molecular Function (MF), and Cellular Component (CC). For the analysis, the Arabidopsis thaliana genome was selected as the background reference. GO terms were considered statistically significant if they met a False Discovery Rate (FDR) adjusted p-value threshold of ≤ 0.05, based on a hypergeometric test.


## Cis-regulatory analysis of candidate hub genes
  To identify potential regulatory motifs, we analyzed the 2000 bp region upstream of the transcription start site for each identified hub gene using the PlantCare database43. Subsequently, the TBtools software was employed to visualize the distribution of these cis-acting elements and to generate a heatmap illustrating their abundance44. To enhance the clarity of the visualization and focus on the most prevalent regulatory elements, motifs with a frequency of less than two per promoter were excluded from the final analysis. This filtering step minimizes the inclusion of low-abundance elements that may represent stochastic occurrences rather than robust regulatory signals.


## Identification of differentially expressed transcription factors (TFs)
  Transcription factors (TFs) are master regulators that control gene expression by binding to specific cis-regulatory elements, playing essential roles in orchestrating plant development and stress responses45. To identify the key regulatory players involved in the response to each stress, the sets of up- and down-regulated DEGs from our salt, drought, and ABA analyses were screened for genes encoding TFs. This identification was performed by cross-referencing our gene lists with the comprehensive collection of Arabidopsis thaliana TFs available in the Plant Transcription Factor Database (PlantTFDB, v4.0)46. The resulting lists provided a focused view of the regulatory networks activated or repressed under each specific stress condition.


### Machine learning-based classification and feature selection
  To classify samples into stress and control groups and to identify key predictive genes, we employed three robust ensemble machine learning algorithms: Random Forest (RF), XGBoost, and AdaBoost. These algorithms were selected over alternative approaches such as Support Vector Machines (SVM) or Neural Networks for three methodologically grounded reasons. First, tree-based ensemble methods provide native, model-intrinsic feature importance metrics — Gini impurity reduction for RF and AdaBoost, and information Gain for XGBoost — that are directly interpretable as gene-level predictive contributions without requiring post-hoc attribution approximations. Second, ensemble methods are well-established as robust classifiers for high-dimensional, small-to-moderate sample transcriptomic datasets, where their internal regularization through bagging or boosting substantially mitigates overfitting. Third, and most critically, the deliberate deployment of three algorithmically distinct paradigms — bagging (RF), gradient boosting (XGBoost), and adaptive boosting (AdaBoost) — transforms the feature importance analysis into an internal consensus validation: only genes consistently ranked as high-importance across all three independent frameworks are retained as hub candidates, providing a stringency of biomarker selection that no single algorithm, regardless of its classification accuracy, could achieve. The analyses were conducted on a dataset comprising 94 samples and 95 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), which served as features23,25,47,48. To ensure rigorous model evaluation and mitigate overfitting, a nested validation strategy was implemented. The dataset was partitioned into 80% for training and 20% for testing within a stratified 5-fold cross-validation framework, repeated 100 times to guarantee the stability of the results. The Random Forest model was configured with 100 estimators (n_estimators = 100), a maximum depth of 8 (max_depth = 8), and max_features set to ‘log2’, while using ‘balanced’ class weights to address potential class imbalances. The XGBoost model was trained with n_estimators = 100, max_depth = 8, a learning rate of 0.1 (learning_rate = 0.1), and subsample and colsample_bytree both set to 0.8. For AdaBoost, we utilized 100 estimators (n_estimators = 100) with a learning rate of 0.1 (learning_rate = 0.1).

  In addition to classification, the feature importance capabilities of these algorithms were leveraged to prioritize critical stress-responsive genes. For Random Forest and AdaBoost, gene importance was quantified using the Gini impurity index, which measures the average reduction in impurity brought about by each feature across all trees in the ensemble. Conversely, for the XGBoost model, feature importance was assessed based on the ‘Gain’ metric, representing the average improvement in accuracy brought by a feature to the branches it is on. For all three models, the final importance score for each gene was calculated as the mean importance value across all 500 trained models (100 repetitions × 5 folds). This aggregation across repeated cross-validation runs ensures that the resulting feature importance ranking reflects consistent patterns in the data rather than being driven by any single train–test split. Consequently, the derived ranking can provide a robust and reproducible identification of the most influential genes in the osmotic stress response.

  The predictive performance of the trained Random Forest, XGBoost, and AdaBoost classifiers was comprehensively evaluated using a suite of standard metrics derived from the aggregated confusion matrix. These included overall Accuracy, which measures the proportion of correctly classified instances; Precision, indicating the ratio of true positive predictions to the total positive predictions; Recall (or Sensitivity), representing the model’s ability to identify all relevant instances; and the F1-score, which provides a single score that balances the trade-off between Precision and Recall. Furthermore, to assess the models’ ability to discriminate between the stress and control classes across all classification thresholds, Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves were generated. The Area Under the Curve (AUC) was then calculated as a summary measure of the model’s overall diagnostic performance. All reported performance metrics represent the mean values computed across the cumulative results of all 500 test folds (100 repetitions × Five-folds), ensuring a robust and unbiased estimation of each model’s generalization capability.


## Plant material, growth, and stress treatment protocols
  All experiments were conducted using Arabidopsis thaliana (ecotype Columbia-0) within the controlled environment of the Biotechnology Laboratory at Shahid Beheshti University, Tehran, Iran. A rigorous surface-sterilization protocol was applied to the seeds, involving sequential immersion in 95% ethanol (1 min) and 3% sodium hypochlorite (5 min), followed by five extensive rinses in sterile distilled water (5 min each). The sterilized seeds were then sown on solid half-strength Murashige and Skoog (½ MS) medium, supplemented with 1% sucrose and 0.8% agar, and buffered to a pH of 5.7. Seedlings were cultivated in a growth chamber under a 16-h light (22 °C)/8-h dark (20 °C) photoperiod.

  At 10 days post-germination, seedlings were subjected to abiotic stress treatments. The specific concentrations and durations for each stress were carefully selected based on established protocols49,50 to elicit physiologically relevant transcriptomic responses. For salinity stress, seedlings were transferred to ½ MS plates supplemented with 200 mM NaCl for 24 h. Drought stress was simulated by placing seedlings on ½ MS medium containing 25% (w/v) polyethylene glycol 8000 for 24 h51. For hormonal treatment, seedlings were exposed to 50 µM abscisic acid (ABA) in ½ MS medium for 3 h. In parallel, corresponding control groups for each stress condition were transferred to fresh, unamended ½ MS medium for the same duration. All plant material was harvested immediately following the treatment period for subsequent RNA extraction.


## Validation of candidate genes by qRT-PCR
  To experimentally validate our in-silico findings, a subset of the eight high-confidence candidate genes was selected for quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis under drought, salinity, and ABA treatments. This selection was strategically prioritized following a comprehensive literature survey. Four genes—At5g50360, At1g73480, At3g46230, and At1g16850—were chosen for validation as they represent candidates with putative or less-characterized roles in osmotic stress responses. The remaining four candidates were not carried forward for experimental validation because their involvement in abiotic stress signaling is already well-documented in existing literature. This targeted approach allowed for the efficient allocation of experimental resources towards confirming the novel regulatory candidates identified by our computational pipeline, rather than re-validating genes with previously established functions.

  Using the TRIzol reagent supplied by Thermo Fisher Scientific (USA), we isolated total RNA from seedlings of Arabidopsis thaliana in accordance with the guidelines provided by the manufacturer. A NanoDrop spectrophotometer (also from Thermo Fisher Scientific) was employed to assess the purity and quantity of the isolated RNA. Before proceeding to cDNA synthesis, any residual genomic DNA was eliminated from the RNA samples through treatment with DNase I (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The AddScript Reverse Transcriptase kit (Addgene, USA) was then utilized to generate first-strand cDNA, adhering to the recommended procedures. For the four selected hub genes, specific primer sets were created via the online Primer 3.0 platform (available at https://www.primer3plus.com/index.html), as detailed in Table 2. As an internal control, we selected the gene encoding glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH, At1g13440). Normalization of the RT-qPCR results was performed relative to GAPDH levels, and the Livak approach was applied to analyze the data52. All experiments were conducted with three independent biological replicates, and each qRT-PCR reaction was performed in technical triplicate. Data are presented as means ± SEM, with statistical significance between treatment and control groups determined by Student’s t-test using GraphPad Prism version 8.0.

### Computational validation of candidate hub genes
  To computationally assess the predictive power of each of the eight identified hub genes, a machine learning-based validation was performed using Python (v3.9) and the scikit-learn library (v1.1.0). For each hub gene individually, a Logistic Regression model was trained to classify samples as either control or stress-treated based solely on that gene’s expression level.

  The robustness of each model was evaluated using a leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) strategy. In this approach, the model is iteratively trained on all samples except for one, which is held out for testing. This process is repeated until every sample has been used as the test set once. The ROC curves were then generated by plotting the true positive rate (sensitivity) against the false positive rate (1-specificity) aggregated from all LOOCV iterations. The AUC was calculated to provide a single metric of each gene’s ability to discriminate between the two conditions. An AUC value of 1.0 represents a perfect classifier, while a value of 0.5 indicates performance no better than random chance.


## Results

### Integration of multiple datasets and mitigation of technical variation
  Following normalization of the transcriptome data, we utilized Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to assess the variance between normal and stressed cells. When visualizing the first two principal components for each specific stress condition—drought, salt, and ABA—we identified prominent batch effects: samples clustered primarily by their experimental origin rather than their biological treatment group (Fig. 2a-c). By employing the ComBat function to mitigate inter-study variation, we successfully harmonized the datasets. A subsequent Principal Component Analysis confirmed the effectiveness of this correction, revealing a robust separation between the normal and stress cell populations and thereby unmasking the underlying biological signal (Fig. 2d-f).

PCA plots of integrated transcriptome data before (a–c) and after (d–f) batch correction for Abscisic acid, Drought, and Salt stress studies. In each plot, samples are colored by their source dataset (GEO accession number) and shaped by their biological state (circles for normal, triangles for stress). ### Differential gene expression analysis
  The meta-analysis revealed that while all three abiotic stresses induced significant transcriptional changes, the magnitude and direction of this response varied considerably, particularly under salt stress. The salt stress condition elicited the strongest response, yielding 957 DEGs with a clear bias towards upregulation (76.2% of DEGs). In contrast, the transcriptional landscapes for ABA and Drought stress were more balanced and comparable in scale, resulting in 608 and 634 total DEGs, respectively, with a modest majority of genes being upregulated (55.4% for ABA and 56.6% for Drought) (Supplementary File 1). These differential expression patterns for each stress are visualized in the volcano plots and summarized in the corresponding donut charts (Fig. 3).

  Our analysis of the differential expression profiles identified the genes with the most extreme responses to each stress. Under Abscisic Acid (ABA) stress, At2g38480 was the most highly upregulated transcript (log₂FC = 6.77), while At1g49860 was the most strongly repressed (log₂FC = −4.58). The response to salt stress was characterized by the prominent induction of At4g37370 (log₂FC = 4.08) and the significant downregulation of At4g19690 (log₂FC = −2.59). For drought stress, At1g52690 showed the highest level of induction (log₂FC = 4.65), whereas At1g22690 was the most repressed gene (log₂FC = −2.78) (Fig. 3). These genes, representing the apex of the transcriptional response in each case, exemplify the distinct regulatory dynamics of each stress and serve as high-priority candidates for further functional studies.

### Functional annotation and pathway enrichment of stress-responsive DEGs
  Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of DEGs under abscisic acid (ABA) stress revealed significant overrepresentation of biological processes (BP) such as response to chemical and response to oxygen-containing compound, suggesting a conserved core mechanism underlying stress adaptation. Enriched molecular functions (MF) included oxidoreductase activity and peroxidase activity, while cellular components (CC) such as the extracellular region and liquid vacuole were prominent. These results indicate that both up- and down-regulated DEGs contribute to modulating stress signaling, antioxidant defense, and intracellular compartmentalization during ABA stress (Fig. 4a).

  Under drought stress, DEGs were significantly enriched in BP terms such as response to oxygen-containing compound, response to chemical, and response to abiotic stimulus. Enriched MF terms included hydrolase activity, acting on glycosyl bonds and oxidoreductase activity, while CC terms were dominated by the plasma membrane and cell wall. These enrichments suggest that drought-responsive DEGs are involved in maintaining water homeostasis, regulating carbohydrate metabolism, and promoting structural adaptations through coordinated gene regulation (Fig. 4b).

  In the case of salinity stress, enriched BP terms included response to stress, response to chemical, and response to oxygen-containing compound, while MF terms such as DNA-binding transcription factor activity and sequence-specific DNA binding were prominent. The CC terms plasma membrane and cell periphery were also significantly represented, highlighting the role of DEGs in maintaining ion homeostasis, transcriptional regulation, and membrane integrity through the balanced up- and down-regulation of gene expressions (Fig. 4c).

### Identification of stress-responsive transcription factors within DEG sets
  Transcription factors (TFs) serve as key regulators of gene expression, functioning as master switches that coordinate complex transcriptional networks in response to environmental stimuli. Our analysis revealed a dynamic and stress-specific modulation of TF families across abscisic acid (ABA), salt, and drought stress conditions (Fig. 5) (Supplementary File 2). Under ABA stress, upregulated TFs were predominantly members of the ERF and NAC families (9 genes each), followed by MYB (6 genes). In contrast, downregulated TFs were mainly represented by the ERF (8 genes), MYB-related (6 genes), and bZIP (5 genes) families, suggesting that certain TF families exert dual regulatory roles under ABA-mediated signaling. During salt stress, TF induction was markedly enhanced, with the ERF family showing the highest enrichment (32 genes), followed by NAC (16 genes) and WRKY (14 genes). Downregulated TFs were primarily from the bHLH (7 genes) and bZIP (5 genes) families, reflecting a potential suppression of transcriptional repressors under salt exposure. Similarly, under drought stress, the NAC (9 genes) and MYB (8 genes) families were the most abundantly upregulated, whereas bHLH (8 genes) and bZIP (4 genes) dominated the downregulated group. Collectively, these findings underscore both conserved and stress-specific transcriptional regulatory patterns, highlighting ERF and NAC as key positive regulators. At the same time bHLH and bZIP often act as negatively modulated components in the plant response to abiotic stress.

### Machine learning models identify a core set of predictive biomarkers for abiotic stress
  To identify the hub genes that mediate the core transcriptional response to all three stress conditions, we employed three ensemble machine learning algorithms—AdaBoost, XGBoost, and Random Forest. Unlike conventional network-based hub identification approaches, machine learning models can capture complex, nonlinear dependencies among genes while directly linking expression patterns to phenotypic outcomes (e.g., stress versus normal conditions). This data-driven approach allows for the identification of key genes based on their predictive power—their ability to accurately discriminate between stressed and normal physiological states. By training models on the expression profiles of our core gene set, we are able to identify a minimal subset of genes that function as strong and consistent biomarkers of the overall abiotic stress response—an outcome that cannot be readily achieved through topological analyses alone.

  To construct a robust classification framework, three ensemble learning algorithms—AdaBoost, XGBoost, and Random Forest—were implemented and evaluated. Model performance was assessed using standard classification metrics, including precision, recall, F1-score, accuracy, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) (Fig. 8). Among the three models, XGBoost exhibited the highest predictive performance with an AUC of 0.9949, followed closely by Random Forest (AUC = 0.9943) and AdaBoost (AUC = 0.9877). These results demonstrate that XGBoost most effectively discriminated between stress-treated and control samples, highlighting its superior ability to capture complex and informative gene expression patterns.

  Feature importance analysis further identified the most influential genes contributing to model prediction. The top 10 genes identified by AdaBoost were At5g05410, At1g73480, At1g01720, At1g56600, At4g34230, At2g36330, At3g28530, At1g02850, At4g74300, and At2g43750. For XGBoost, the key contributing genes included At5g05410, At1g73480, At1g56600, At1g01720, At1g07430, At4g26080, At3g18070, At3g46230, At4g32430, and At1g73040. Similarly, Random Forest prioritized At5g05410, At1g73480, At1g56600, At1g01720, At1g78070, At1g07430, At2g36330, At3g46230, At3g69770, and At4g34230 as the most influential predictors.

  Notably, At5g05410 and At1g73480 consistently ranked among the top genes across all three algorithms, suggesting their pivotal and conserved roles as hub regulators in the general abiotic stress response network of Arabidopsis thaliana.

### Functional enrichment of top-ranked genes identified by ensemble machine learning models under abiotic stress conditions
  To provide functional context for the genes prioritized by the machine learning models, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) Biological Process enrichment analysis on the top features identified by each algorithm (Fig. 9). Despite differences in their underlying learning strategies, all three models (XGBoost, AdaBoost, and Random Forest) converged on a consistent set of stress-related biological processes. In the case of XGBoost, the prioritized genes were significantly enriched in regulatory pathways associated with abscisic acid (ABA) signaling and stress response modulation, including “negative regulation of ABA-activated signaling pathway” (FDR = 3.34E-05), “regulation of signal transduction” (FDR = 1.02E-04), and “cellular response to hypoxia” (FDR = 3.34E-05). Key genes such as ABI1 (At4g26080), ATAF1 (At1g01720), and HAI2 (At1g07430) were repeatedly associated with ABA-related regulatory terms, while DREB2A and HSP17.4 were linked to hypoxia and broader abiotic stress responses. These results indicate that the machine learning models consistently prioritized genes involved in core stress-response and signaling pathways, supporting the biological relevance of the identified features.

### A consensus machine learning approach to identify core predictive genes
  To identify the most consistently prioritized predictive biomarkers, we intersected the top 10 most important features identified by each of the three algorithms. This high-confidence set of predictive genes includes At5g50360, At1g07430, At1g73480, At3g02480, At5g05410, At1g56600, At3g46230, and At1g16850. he remarkable agreement across diverse machine learning frameworks suggests that these eight genes represent a highly consistent core predictive signature associated with abiotic stress responses in Arabidopsis. Their consistent high predictive power positions them as strong candidates for future functional validation.

Specifically, our subsequent analyses, including quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) and individual-gene machine learning validation, centered on four candidates: At5g50360, At1g73480, At3g46230, and At1g16850.   To complement the expression analysis, we evaluated the diagnostic potential of each of the four candidate hub genes using a machine learning approach based on ROC curves. ### Experimental validation of candidate hub genes under multiple stress conditions by RT-qPCR
  To experimentally validate the involvement of the prioritized hub genes in abiotic stress responses, their transcript levels were quantified using RT-qPCR in Arabidopsis seedlings subjected to salt, drought, and ABA treatments. As depicted in Fig. 12, all four selected genes—At5g50360, At1g73480, At3g46230, and At1g16850—exhibited a significant upregulation under all tested stress conditions compared to the untreated control.

### Cis acting element analysis of candidate hub genes
  To elucidate the transcriptional regulation underlying their stress-induced expression, an in-silico analysis of the promoter regions was conducted. This analysis revealed that all four genes possess a rich repertoire of stress-responsive cis-acting elements (Fig. 13a, b), providing a molecular basis for their observed expression patterns. Notably, the promoters of At5g50360 and At1g73480, which were strongly upregulated by ABA, salt, and drought stress, were found to be significantly enriched with ABA-responsive elements (ABREs) and G-Box motifs. The exceptionally high frequency of ABREs in the At1g73480 promoter strongly correlates with its robust transcriptional induction (over 4.5-fold) in response to exogenous ABA, confirming a direct link between cis-element architecture and ABA sensitivity.

  Conversely, the distinct cis-regulatory landscapes of the other two genes provide a clear rationale for their more specific expression profiles. For instance, At3g46230, which was preferentially induced by drought but showed no significant response to exogenous ABA, contained prominent MYB binding sites (MBS) and anaerobic responsive elements (AREs). This suggests a predominant activation via ABA-independent drought signaling, potentially coupled with hypoxia-related pathways commonly triggered under water deficit. Similarly, At1g16850, which exhibited its most pronounced upregulation under salinity, was particularly enriched in TC-rich repeats and MeJA-responsive elements (CGTCA/TGACG-motifs). This points towards significant crosstalk with jasmonate signaling and defense responses that may be specifically engaged during ionic stress. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that the distinct cis-regulatory architectures of these genes provide a compelling molecular explanation for their stress-specific expression amplitudes, highlighting the sophisticated regulatory diversification that allows for the fine-tuning of stress responses in Arabidopsis.

  To investigate potential regulatory links between the identified cis-regulatory motifs and upstream transcription factors, Pearson correlation analysis was performed using the normalized expression profiles of all biological replicates across ABA, drought, and salt conditions.   The correlation analysis revealed distinct co-expression patterns between the selected TFs and the four hub genes (Fig. 14c). Overall, all four hub genes showed strong positive correlations with the ten upregulated TFs (r = 0.38–0.90) and consistent negative correlations with the downregulated TF At5g28770 (r = − 0.47 to − 0.74), confirming their broad integration within the conserved stress-responsive transcriptional network. Among the hub genes, At1g73480 and At5g50360 — whose promoters are highly enriched in ABRE and G-Box motifs — displayed the strongest and most uniform positive correlations across the TF panel, with several TFs reaching r ≥ 0.80, supporting direct transcriptional co-regulation consistent with ABA-dependent activation. ## Discussion
  Abiotic factors such as drought, salinity, and abscisic acid exposure pose significant challenges to global agricultural productivity. In plants, the ability to cope with these stresses arises from a network of signaling routes, many of which converge through the activity of transcription factors and protein kinases that coordinate downstream responses. Gaining more profound insight into how this regulatory systems function is crucial for understanding plant resilience. Despite this, most existing research examines individual stress conditions in isolation. To address this limitation, we employed a meta-analysis framework that compiles transcriptomic datasets from multiple abiotic stress treatments. This integrative strategy allowed us to pinpoint shared genes and regulatory pathways linked to stress tolerance and to identify candidates that consistently contribute to plant adaptation under diverse environmental challenges.

  Our meta-analysis elucidates the distinct and overlapping transcriptional signatures of Abscisic Acid (ABA), salt, and drought stress, reflecting both shared signaling pathways and unique physiological challenges. The comparable number of DEGs and the balanced up/downregulation ratios observed under drought and ABA treatments are consistent with the well-established role of ABA as the primary phytohormone mediating the response to water deficit58. In contrast, the much larger and strongly skewed transcriptional response to salt stress, with over 76% of DEGs being upregulated, highlights the dual nature of this challenge. Salt stress imposes not only an osmotic stress component, which activates ABA-dependent pathways, but also a severe ionic toxicity component59. The massive upregulation of genes observed is likely attributable to the energetic and biosynthetic demands of mitigating this ion toxicity, which requires the robust expression of genes involved in ion homeostasis, such as ion transporters for Na⁺ exclusion (e.g., SOS1) and vacuolar sequestration (e.g., NHX1), as well as the synthesis of compatible osmolytes and cellular repair proteins60. This suggests that while a core stress-responsive network is shared, coping with salinity necessitates a far more extensive and specialized transcriptional reprogramming than dealing with drought or direct ABA application alone.

  Gene Ontology enrichment analysis reveals a stress response architecture defined by both a conserved oxidative core and stress-specific adaptive mechanisms (Fig. 4). The consistent enrichment of “response to chemical” and “response to oxygen-containing compounds” across all three conditions positions ROS and reactive nitrogen species (RNS) signaling as a central integrative hub rather than a passive stress consequence, reflecting convergent activation of NADPH oxidase-mediated H₂O₂ and nitric oxide bursts that prime downstream transcriptional reprogramming61. Beyond this shared foundation, each stress engages a functionally distinct adaptive strategy: under ABA, enrichment of oxidoreductase, peroxidase, and vacuolar compartment terms reflects redox homeostasis channeled through RBOH-generated H₂O₂ in guard cell signaling and vacuolar osmotic adjustment; under drought, a strong bias toward xyloglucan: xyloglucosyl transferase and apoplast-associated functions indicates structural cell wall reinforcement coupled with apoplastic H₂O₂-mediated cross-linking as the primary dehydration defense; and under salinity, the unique co-enrichment of “cellular response to hypoxia” alongside dominant transcription factor activity terms suggests that ionic toxicity-driven mitochondrial ROS/RNS bursts engage hypoxia-responsive transcriptional programs in parallel with canonical ABA- and SOS-pathway activation61. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that while ROS/RNS signaling operates as a universal regulatory node, it is mechanistically deployed in stress-specific modes — as a stomatal signal under ABA, an apoplastic structural mediator under drought, and a hypoxia-coupled transcriptional amplifier under salinity — underscoring the sophisticated integration of redox signaling within each adaptive strategy.

  The distribution of transcription factor (TF) families across stress conditions reveals a highly sophisticated regulatory architecture orchestrating transcriptional reprogramming in Arabidopsis thaliana (Fig. 5). A conserved core of master regulators is evident, with ERF, NAC, and MYB families dominating the upregulated TFs across all three treatments. However, their stress-specific deployment highlights distinct regulatory priorities. The massive and disproportionate induction of ERF members under salinity, for instance, underscores the critical role of ethylene- and ABA-integrated signaling in mitigating ionic toxicity59. In parallel, the strong enrichment of NAC TFs under drought and salinity aligns with their established function as master regulators of drought tolerance through the control of membrane stabilization, cuticle biosynthesis, and stomatal function62. In stark contrast, downregulated TFs showed a more conserved pattern, with bHLH and bZIP members being consistently repressed across stresses. This strongly supports a “growth-defense trade-off” strategy, where transcriptional programs associated with cell elongation and development—often driven by bHLH and bZIP TFs—are actively suppressed to conserve energy and reallocate resources toward survival63. This coordinated interplay, involving the induction of stress-specific master regulators and the simultaneous repression of growth-oriented pathways, demonstrates a tightly balanced transcriptional strategy for optimizing plant survival in harsh environments.

  The high predictive performance of the ensemble machine learning models indicates that abiotic stress in Arabidopsis thaliana is governed by a stable and coordinated transcriptional signature that can be captured through data-driven approaches. Among the algorithms tested, XGBoost achieved the strongest classification accuracy (AUC = 0.9949), demonstrating its exceptional ability to model nonlinear and interactive effects among genes—relationships that traditional statistical or network-based methods often fail to detect. The close performance of Random Forest and AdaBoost further supports the existence of a robust underlying gene expression pattern shared across ABA, drought, and salinity stresses.

  We identified eight candidate hub genes using three machine learning algorithms, of which four (At5g50360, At1g73480, At3g46230, and At1g16850) underwent subsequent experimental and computational validation. The consistent prioritization of At5g05410 (DREB2A) across all three ensemble models provides an internal consistency check for the analytical framework. Given its well-established role in ABA-independent and osmotic stress signaling pathways, its identification supports the biological relevance of the features selected by the model.

  To further validate our meta-analysis at the individual gene level, we performed RT-qPCR on At5g50360 (AITR5), a von Willebrand factor type A domain-containing ABA-induced transcription repressor (Fig. 12). AITR5 was strongly upregulated under all treatments (~ 4-fold with exogenous ABA, ~ 3-fold under drought, and ~ 3.5–4-fold under salt stress), with the induction under salinity being comparable to or exceeding that under pure osmotic stress. Although AITR5 induction by ABA and drought was previously reported64–66, To our knowledge, this study provides one of the first comparative analyses across exogenous ABA, drought, and salt stress, revealing the previously unrecognized robust activation of this negative regulator precisely under the most extensive transcriptional reprogramming (salinity).

  The signaling context of AITR5 induction can be interpreted in light of both ABA-dependent and ABA-independent inputs, as well as ROS-mediated regulation. Within the ABA-dependent cascade, AITR5 functions as a transcriptional repressor that targets key negative regulators of ABA signaling, including ABI2, a PP2C phosphatase that itself forms part of a negative feedback loop within the PYR/PYL–PP2C–SnRK2 core module64,66. ABI1 and ABI2 phosphatase activity is known to increase in response to ABA67, and by repressing ABI2 expression, AITR5 may act as a secondary feedback brake that counteracts PP2C-mediated desensitization of the ABA pathway. Importantly, ROS have been established as key second messengers in ABA signal transduction, acting downstream of NADPH oxidase (RBOH)-mediated H₂O₂ production at the plasma membrane68. The interplay between PP2C phosphatases and ROS signaling is well-documented: PP2C-type phosphatases are inactivated under oxidative conditions, allowing enhanced SnRK2 activity and downstream ABA-responsive gene expression69. In this context, AITR5 induction under salt stress—a condition that simultaneously generates substantial ROS bursts70—may reflect an intersection between ABA-activated transcription and ROS-mediated amplification of the ABA signal. Regarding ABA-independent inputs, osmotic stress is known to engage SnRK2 kinases independently of ABA, and these kinases can regulate both AREB/ABF factors and DREB family members at the same time71. The particularly strong AITR5 induction under salt stress, which activates both ABA-dependent osmotic components and ABA-independent ionic stress pathways, suggests that AITR5 may serve to fine-tune signaling output at precisely this convergence point. However, we note that direct experimental evidence linking AITR5 to ABA-independent pathway components or ROS-mediated regulatory mechanisms has not yet been established, and this model therefore represents a testable hypothesis warranting future investigation. This indicates that plants deploy AITR5 as a potent brake to prevent excessive ABA signaling and growth arrest costs under severe ionic toxicity, while still permitting the massive ion-homeostasis and repair responses required for survival — an insight supported by studies on aitr5 mutants exhibiting enhanced salt/drought tolerance and ABA hypersensitivity. Thus, AITR5 expression dynamics not only confirm the shared ABA-dependent core network but also highlight a sophisticated feedback mechanism particularly critical for salinity adaptation, significantly extending prior understanding of AITR function.

  Our analysis and experimental validation of At1g73480 (MAGL4), an alpha/beta-hydrolase superfamily protein, as a central hub gene suggests a critical intersection between stress perception and lipid-mediated cellular defense. The robust upregulation of MAGL4 under drought, salt, and most profoundly, ABA treatment (Fig. 12), implies a potential function far more dynamic than simple lipase activity. We propose that MAGL4 could act as a key effector in two concurrent and critical stress-response processes. First, it may play a pivotal role in membrane homeostasis. Abiotic stresses induce oxidative and osmotic damage, compromising membrane integrity. The lipase activity of MAGL4 could therefore be important for the rapid removal of peroxidized fatty acids from membrane lipids, a critical step in the repair cycle that preserves cellular compartmentalization and function72. Second, and perhaps more critically, MAGL4 might also function as a gateway to lipid-based secondary signaling. The enzymatic release of fatty acids from membrane glycerolipids is the rate-limiting first step in the biosynthesis of potent signaling molecules such as jasmonates and other oxylipins, which orchestrate a vast array of downstream defense gene expressions.

  With respect to the interplay between ABA-dependent and ABA-independent pathways73, the marked induction of MAGL4 by exogenous ABA, together with the enrichment of ABRE and G-box motifs in its promoter (Fig. 13), is consistent with direct transcriptional activation through phosphorylated AREB/ABF transcription factors downstream of the PYR/PYL–PP2C–SnRK2 axis74. At the same time, the fatty acid products of MAGL4 enzymatic activity could serve as precursors for jasmonate biosynthesis—a lipid-derived hormone pathway that operates substantially independently of ABA75. Jasmonate signaling has been shown to contribute to salt stress tolerance in Arabidopsis via mechanisms distinct from the canonical ABA cascade76, and it has been suggested that ROS-induced lipid peroxidation can itself generate oxylipin signals that feed into jasmonate-related defense networks. It is therefore conceivable that MAGL4 acts as an ABA-activated effector whose enzymatic products simultaneously feed into ABA-independent jasmonate-mediated and ROS-linked defense responses, potentially linking these pathways at the level of lipid metabolism. This hypothesis is biochemically plausible, but it has not yet been experimentally tested in the specific context of abiotic stress in Arabidopsis, and functional studies including lipid profiling in MAGL4 overexpression or loss-of-function lines would be required to validate this proposed mode of action.

  The identification of At3g46230, which encodes the cytosolic class I small heat shock protein HSP17.4C1, as a central hub in our analysis underscores its critical role in mediating abiotic stress responses. Our findings demonstrate a significant upregulation of At3g46230 under both salt and drought conditions, which strongly corroborates previous research establishing this gene as a key player in plant defense against a wide array of environmental stressors77. This consistency validates our experimental system and reaffirms the importance of At3g46230 in a broader stress-responsive network.

  The regulatory architecture of HSP17.4C1 reflects an integration, and of ABA-dependent, ABA-independent ROS-mediated signaling inputs—a multi-pathway convergence that likely underlies its responsiveness to diverse stresses. From an ABA-dependent perspective, exogenous ABA has been shown to induce HSP17.4C1 expression77, and the presence of ABRE elements in its promoter (Fig. 13) is consistent with direct transcriptional activation through the AREB/ABF axis. Regarding ABA-independent regulation, the DREB2A–HsfA3 cascade provides a well-characterized parallel route: under osmotic and thermal stress, the dehydration-responsive element binding protein DREB2A directly activates the heat shock transcription factor HsfA3, which in turn drives expression from heat shock elements (HSEs) in sHSP promoters78,79. This pathway operates substantially independently of ABA and is likely a major driver of HSP17.4C1 induction under drought and salt stress. Crucially, ROS represent a third, convergent regulatory input. HSP17.4C1 expression is strongly induced by exogenous H₂O₂ and by the superoxide-generating herbicide methyl viologen, and its expression is significantly elevated in catalase 2 (cat2) loss-of-function mutants that constitutively accumulate intracellular H₂O₂, demonstrating that ROS directly modulate its transcriptional activation independently of applied stress77. The molecular basis for this ROS responsiveness is consistent with established mechanisms: H₂O₂ promotes trimerization and DNA-binding activity of HSFA1a on HSEs80, and HSFA2 and HSFA4A are themselves induced by oxidative stress and implicated in H₂O₂ sensing81. Together, these three regulatory streams—ABA-dependent ABRE-mediated transcription, ABA-independent DREB2A–HSF cascade, and direct ROS-driven HSF activation—converge on HSP17.4C1, explaining why its expression is robustly induced across conditions that differentially engage these pathways.

  Interestingly, while a previous study reported transcriptional induction of HSP17.4C1 by exogenous ABA, we did not observe a statistically significant change in its expression under our specific ABA treatment conditions (50 µM, 3 h). Critically, this discrepancy does not reflect a failure of the meta-analysis but rather reveals an important mechanistic distinction between indirect ABA-associated induction and direct ABA-mediated transcriptional activation. the meta-analysis datasets may have captured conditions in which ABA signaling co-occurred with endogenous ROS accumulation and heat shock factor activation co-stimuli that are absent under controlled exogenous ABA treatment alone. This interpretation is further supported by the Pearson correlation analysis, which revealed that among the four hub genes, At3g46230 displayed its relatively strongest co-expression with At5g05410 (DREB2A, r = 0.73) compared to the weaker correlation observed for At1g73480 (r = 0.38), consistent with the possibility that the dominant transcriptional input driving HSP17.4C1 is more closely aligned with ABA-independent DREB2A–HSF cascade activity than with ABRE-mediated bZIP signaling. This is mechanistically consistent with the well-characterized DREB2A–HsfA3 route, which drives sHSP expression independently of ABA under osmotic and oxidative stress, and with the ROS-driven trimerization of HSFA1a on heat shock elements — signals that are robustly generated under drought and salinity but not under short-term exogenous ABA application. Therefore, while HSP17.4C1 is genuinely embedded within ABA-associated stress networks, its direct transcriptional activation requires the combinatorial convergence of ROS, heat shock factor activity, and osmotic signals that exogenous ABA alone cannot fully recapitulate under the experimental conditions employed.

  Our investigation into the expression of At1g16850 builds upon and significantly expands the foundational work establishing its role in salt stress tolerance, as evidenced by the defective root growth phenotype of T-DNA knockout mutants under increasing salinity82. Our results provide molecular-level corroboration of this function through significant transcriptional upregulation under salinity stress and further extend these findings by providing, to our knowledge, the first quantitative evidence of its induction under drought conditions, implicating At1g16850 as a broader osmotic stress-responsive regulator. Importantly, the absence of statistically significant induction following exogenous ABA treatment under our experimental conditions (50 µM, 3 h) does not contradict its identification as a core stress-responsive gene in the meta-analysis. Rather, this expression pattern suggests that ABA-independent regulatory pathways may play a predominant role in controlling its transcriptional activation.

  This interpretation is further supported by the cis-regulatory architecture of the At1g16850 promoter, which is enriched in MeJA-responsive elements (CGTCA/TGACG motifs) and TC-rich repeats rather than canonical ABRE motifs. Jasmonate biosynthesis is known to be activated under salt stress through partially ABA-independent mechanisms in Arabidopsis, while TC-rich repeat elements have been associated with defense- and stress-responsive pathways that frequently intersect with ROS-mediated signaling networks activated during ionic and osmotic stress83. Additional support is provided by the correlation analysis, in which At1g16850 exhibited its strongest co-expression relationships with stress-responsive TFs not exclusively associated with ABA-dependent signaling pathways. In contrast, At1g16850 displayed a comparatively moderate correlation profile with the broader TF panel relative to At1g73480 and AT5G50360, both of which possess ABRE-enriched promoters and demonstrated stronger multi-TF co-regulation patterns.

  Together, the convergence of promoter architecture and expression-level co-regulation patterns supports a model in which At1g16850 transcription is regulated primarily through jasmonate-mediated and osmotic stress-responsive pathways, whereas ABA signaling likely exerts a more modulatory than primary activating role. This interpretation may also explain why the short-term exogenous ABA treatment employed in this study was insufficient to induce a statistically significant transcriptional response. Although salt and drought stresses are both associated with substantial ROS accumulation, and TC-rich elements are frequently linked to ROS-associated defense signaling, a direct mechanistic connection between ROS signaling and At1g16850 activation has not yet been experimentally demonstrated. Furthermore, because the At1g16850-encoded protein contains a predicted transmembrane domain and remains functionally uncharacterized, its precise mechanistic integration within these signaling pathways remains unresolved. Therefore, the regulatory model proposed here should be considered a biologically supported working hypothesis that requires future functional validation. Collectively, our findings not only reinforce the previously established role of AT1G16850 in salt tolerance but also suggest a broader and previously insufficiently explored function in osmotic stress adaptation in Arabidopsis thaliana.

  The PPI network analyses further provided an integrative systems-level framework linking the four hub genes to broader abiotic stress regulatory modules. AITR5 was associated with kinase-related and transcription-associated proteins, supporting its placement within phosphorylation-dependent and transcriptional regulatory pathways. The MAGL4 interaction network was enriched with lipid metabolic enzymes including SDP1/SDP1L and NPC3, consistent with coordinated membrane lipid remodeling and lipid-derived signaling during osmotic and ionic stress. HSP17.4C1 formed a highly interconnected chaperone-associated module with HSP70/HSP90 proteins and stress-responsive heat shock transcription factors, reinforcing its role in proteostasis maintenance under stress conditions. In contrast, At1g16850 clustered with canonical dehydration-responsive proteins and transcription-associated regulators, suggesting possible involvement in osmotic stress adaptation and transcriptional modulation. Although these interactions remain computationally predicted, the overall network architecture consistently positions the identified hub genes within interconnected stress-responsive regulatory layers rather than isolated signaling pathways.

  Collectively, our gene-level analyses indicate that the identified hub genes function at the intersection of multiple signaling layers rather than within a single pathway. A consistent pattern across candidates such as AITR5, HSP17.4C1, MAGL4, and At1g16850 is the convergence of three major regulatory axes: the ABA-dependent PYR/PYL–PP2C–SnRK2 cascade, ABA-independent osmotic stress pathways (e.g., DREB-mediated signaling), and ROS-driven signaling acting as a secondary modulatory system. These pathways likely interact through shared cis-regulatory elements, overlapping transcription factor activity, and feedback regulation, enabling combinatorial control of gene expression. In this context, the identified hub genes can be interpreted as integrative nodes that coordinate signaling intensity with downstream adaptive responses, which may explain their consistent prioritization across different stress conditions and machine learning models.

  Beyond transcriptional regulation, the conserved stress-responsive signature identified in this study is likely further modulated by post-transcriptional and epigenetic mechanisms. At the chromatin level, abiotic stress-responsive genes are commonly associated with dynamic histone modifications, including enrichment of active marks such as H3K4me3 and H3K9ac, which facilitate rapid transcriptional activation and may contribute to transcriptional memory under repeated stress exposure84. In parallel, chromatin remodeling processes such as H2A.Z eviction have been linked to the activation of stress-inducible loci, particularly heat shock proteins, suggesting a plausible epigenetic contribution to the strong induction of genes such as HSP17.4C1 observed in this study85. At the post-transcriptional level, RNA stability, alternative splicing, and microRNA-mediated regulation are known to fine-tune stress-responsive transcripts downstream of ABA and osmotic signaling pathways. Although direct gene-specific evidence for the identified hub genes is currently lacking, these regulatory layers likely act in concert with transcriptional networks to ensure precise, dynamic, and reversible control of stress responses. Future studies integrating epigenomic and post-transcriptional analyses will be necessary to determine how these mechanisms specifically regulate the identified hub genes.

  Despite the robust identification of hub genes through our integrative machine learning framework, several limitations should be considered. First, our analysis was deliberately restricted to the seedling stage of Arabidopsis thaliana to ensure developmental and experimental homogeneity across integrated datasets. While this stage represents a critical window for early stress perception and signaling, the conservation and functional relevance of the identified hub genes in mature tissues, specialized organs, or reproductive stages remain to be established. Furthermore, because this study was conducted in a model species, direct extrapolation to crop plants should be approached with caution. Although many core components of abiotic stress signaling are partially conserved across plant species, the identified genes should be regarded as candidate regulators for further investigation, rather than universally conserved determinants of stress tolerance. Second, this study relied on bulk transcriptomic data, which captures averaged gene expression profiles and may obscure cell-type-specific heterogeneity. Recent advances in single-cell and spatial transcriptomics have revealed that plant stress responses are highly structured across distinct cell populations and tissue layers. However, as of 2026, high-resolution single-cell datasets for abiotic stresses—particularly drought, salinity, and ABA treatments in Arabidopsis—remain limited, restricting their integration into the present analysis. Future studies incorporating these approaches will be essential to resolve the spatial and cellular contexts in which the identified hub genes operate. Third, although the identified genes exhibited strong and consistent transcriptional responses and high predictive power in machine learning models, their causal roles in stress tolerance remain to be experimentally validated. Functional characterization using gene editing technologies (e.g., CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockouts) or transgenic overexpression systems will be necessary to determine whether these genes directly contribute to stress resilience phenotypes. Finally, the temporal dynamics of gene expression were not explicitly addressed in this study. The integration of datasets with heterogeneous sampling time points provides a robust overall signature but does not distinguish between early signaling events and late adaptive responses. Future research should employ controlled time-course experiments to dissect the dynamic regulatory roles of these genes. Integrating such temporal analyses with multi-omics approaches, including proteomics and metabolomics, will be critical for linking transcriptional changes to functional and physiological outcomes.


## Conclusion
  This study presents a comprehensive framework integrating meta-analysis, machine learning, and experimental validation to dissect the transcriptional programs underlying plant adaptation to abiotic stress. By first harmonizing multi-study transcriptomic data to identify a conserved set of core stress-responsive genes, we demonstrated that adaptation in Arabidopsis thaliana is governed by a stable and highly coordinated regulatory network. This signature was subsequently refined by ensemble learning models to pinpoint eight high-confidence predictive biomarkers. Experimental and computational validation of four key candidates confirmed their roles as central regulators and, crucially, revealed the molecular logic behind their stress-specific responses, linking their distinct cis-regulatory architectures to differential activation via ABA-dependent and independent pathways. Collectively, our findings not only advance the fundamental understanding of stress adaptation but also provide a prioritized set of candidate genes that warrant further functional validation in crop systems.
