# CCDC6, a ferroptosis-related gene, modulates stemness features of pancreatic cancer cells in vitro


## Objective
  Pancreatic cancer (PC) poses a significant threat to patient quality of life. Ferroptosis, a novel form of non-apoptotic cell death, is implicated in pancreatic tumorigenesis and therapy resistance. This study aims to identify key ferroptosis-related genes (FRGs) as prognostic biomarkers, to construct a risk model for predicting patient outcomes, and to explore the underlying biological mechanisms.


## Methods
  We integrated transcriptomic and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data to develop a prognostic model. We used cross-validation across three machine learning algorithms to identify core FRGs with high prognostic value. A risk prediction model was then constructed and validated using independent datasets. Immunological profiling was conducted to characterize the tumor microenvironment of different risk groups. In vitro experiments, including gene knockdown and spheroid formation assays, were performed to functionally validate the role of a key gene.


## Results
  Cross-validated analysis identified YAP1, CCDC6, and CIRBP as pivotal prognostic FRGs. The risk prediction model based on these genes demonstrated clinically satisfactory performance, particularly for long-term survival, and was robustly validated. Immunological profiling revealed that high-risk patients exhibited elevated expression of immune checkpoint molecules CEACAM1 and LGALS3, whereas the low-risk group was enriched for myogenesis features. Experimentally, we confirmed CCDC6 overexpression in pancreatic cancer cells. CCDC6 knockdown suppressed the stemness markers NANOG and SOX2 and reduced cancer cell self‑renewal. It also downregulated GPX4 and SLC7A11. Treatment with the ferroptosis inhibitor Ferrostatin-1 partially restored the stemness markers, suggesting that CCDC6 sustains stemness-associated traits at least in part through ferroptosis regulation.


## Conclusion
  This study establishes a robust FRGs-based prognostic model for pancreatic cancer. In vitro, CCDC6 knockdown downregulated GPX4 and SLC7A11, and ferroptosis inhibition partially rescued stemness marker loss, suggesting that CCDC6 may link ferroptosis defense to stemness maintenance. These findings warrant further in vivo validation.

## Introduction
  Pancreatic cancer (PC) is an extremely lethal malignancy and the seventh leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide in both sexes, with an alarmingly low five-year survival rate [1, 2]. Diagnosis is challenging because early symptoms are inconspicuous, leading to low resection rates and high recurrence [3]. Despite decades of research, therapeutic outcomes for PC have not significantly improved. Thus, an urgent need is to pinpoint effective molecular markers for accurate prognosis prediction and deeper insight into the mechanism underlying PC progression to improve patient outcomes.

  Interest in iron metabolism has surged in recent years because of its central role in cancer and disease progression [4]. Iron is essential for DNA synthesis, energy transport, erythropoiesis, and cell signaling. However, dysregulation of iron metabolism leads to excessive ROS production, causing gene mutations and cellular damage [5]. Ferroptosis, a newly identified form of regulated cell death, results from inactivation of the lipid repair enzyme glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) and subsequent accumulation of lipid hydroperoxides [6]. Ferroptosis is well established to be involved in various pathological conditions, particularly carcinogenesis [7]. Ferroptosis has distinct morphological and bioenergetic features. Thiostrepton (TST) treated pancreatic cancer cells show intracellular iron overload. TST induces ferroptosis via the STAT3/GPX4 pathway, thereby decreasing cell viability and clonogenicity [8]. n pancreatic cancer, the tumor suppressor FBW7 promotes ferroptosis and regulates lipid peroxidation. FBW7 activates both ferroptosis and apoptosis, enhancing gemcitabine cytotoxicity by inhibiting SCD1 expression via NR4A [9]. These findings suggest potential ferroptosis‑based therapeutic strategies for pancreatic cancer.

  In our study, three FRGs (YAP1, CCDC6, and CIRBP) possessed significant prognostic value for pancreatic cancer patients. The FRGs were associated with the pathological stage of the cancer. These FRGs were used to develop a prognostic model that effectively predicted long-term survival. External validation further supported the accuracy of the model. In vitro experiments revealed that CCDC6 was highly expressed in pancreatic cancer cells, and its knockdown led to a decrease in NANOG and SOX2 protein expression and the formation of cancer cell mammospheres. These results provide insight into the molecular pathways and potential targets for pancreatic cancer therapies.


## Methods
  The Basic & Clinical Pharmacology & Toxicology policy’s guidelines for experimental and clinical studies were followed in this study [10].


### Acquisition and processing of transcriptomic data
  The model was constructed using RNA expression profiles of pancreatic cancer (n = 177) from the TCGA database. The PACA_CA (n = 186) and PACA_AU_Seq (n = 88) datasets from the ICGC database were used as validation sets. Additionally, the GSE28735 (n = 42), GSE57495 (n = 63), GSE62452 (n = 65), GSE85916 (n = 79) datasets from the GEO database and the PACA_AU_Array (n = 260) pancreatic cancer microarray data from the ICGC database were used as validation sets. Data correction was performed on the microarray data using the normalizeBetweenArrays function of the limma package. The immune therapy data was from IMvigor210 (with additional data predicted by TIDE), obtained from the R package IMvigor210CoreBiologies.


### Acquisition and processing of scRNA-seq data
  The single-cell dataset was obtained from the GEO database (GSE242230) with 25 samples. The Seurat package and R software (version 4.1.3) were used for data analysis. The Seurat package’s NormalizeData, FindVariableFeatures, and ScaleData functions were used to perform data normalization, select highly variable genes (2000), and transform the data (with parameters vars.to.regress = c(“S.Score,” “G2M.Score”) to eliminate the influence of the cell cycle. Batch effect correction was performed using harmony. Dimensionality reduction methods (UMAP, t-SNE) and clustering algorithm (Louvain) were employed using functions provided by Seurat. The FindAllMarkers function with parameters pvalue < 0.05, log2FC > 0.25, and expression > 0.1 was used to calculate DEGs between clusters and cell types.


### Cell annotation analysis
  We began cell annotation by using specific markers for various cell types: fibroblasts (“DCN,” “THY1”, “COL1A1”, “COL1A2”), epithelial cells (“EPCAM,” “KRT18”, “KRT19”, “CDH1”), endothelial cells (“PECAM1”, “CLDN5”, “FLT1”, “RAMP2”), NK cells (“NKG7”, “GNLY,” “NCAM1”, “KLRD1”), T cells (“CD3D”, “CD3E”, “CD3G”, “TRAC”), B cells (“CD79A”, “IGHM,” “IGHG3”, “IGHA2”), macrophages (“KIT,” “MS4A2”, “GATA2”), and myeloid cells (“LYZ,” “MARCO,” “CD68”, “FCGR3A”). Based on this cell annotation, various plots, such as TSNE and violin plots for cell markers, were generated.


### Subgroup analysis of immune cell clusters
  The immune cell clusters were isolated and subjected to cell annotation analysis using the Sc-Type software for automated annotation.


### Acquisition of FRGs
  A total of 235 ferroptosis_driver-related mRNA genes (after removing duplicates) were selected. These genes were sourced from http://www.zhounan.org/ferrdb/current/operations/download.html.


### Analysis of methylation data and generation of methylation tumor subtypes
  We used 450 K methylation data (Beta values, n = 177) from the TCGA database for pancreatic cancer, removed sites with NA, and non-autosomal sites. We selected data from sites with a variance in Beta values greater than 0.2 among samples and performed ConsensusClusterPlus clustering analysis on the methylation data using the km algorithm and Euclidean distance metric. In order to determine the best subtype using a consistency matrix and the Proportion of Ambiguous Clustering (PAC), we performed 100 bootstraps with 80% of the samples and tested 2–9 clusters [11].


### m6A-related subtyping and calculation of m6A level
  We used expression data for 24 m6A regulators and performed ConsensusClusterPlus clustering analysis on the expression data using the km algorithm and Euclidean distance metric. We performed 100 bootstraps containing 80% of the samples and tested 2–9 clusters to determine the best subtype (m6A Cluster) based on the Proportion of Ambiguous Clustering (PAC) and a consistency matrix. We calculated m6Alevel by subtracting the z-score values of 4 regulators (expressed at high levels and significant in non-cancerous samples) from the z-score values of 20 other regulators.


#### m6A gene=∑zscore 20 genes-∑zscore 4 genes
  Using the ConsensusClusterPlus package, we performed consistency clustering and subtyping (m6A subgroup) based on the expression levels of these genes, and we estimated the DEGs between m6A clusters. We performed a single-factor Cox analysis using the differential genes to screen out genes strongly correlated with prognosis (P < 0.05). Then, we divided the prognosis-related genes into two groups based on HR > 1 (Risky) and HR < 1 (Protect), respectively, and calculated the z-score values for each group. Finally, by subtracting the z-score values, we obtained the m6A value.


#### m6Avalue=∑zscore1-∑zscore 2
  zscore1 represents the score value for the risky group, while zscore2 represents the score value for the protect group [12].


### Identification of tumor subtypes related to ferroptosis
  Through univariate Cox analysis, 235 genes linked to ferroptosis were selected, and 75 prognoses-related genes (P < 0.05) were identified. Based on the expression matrix of these 75 genes, consensus clustering on the TCGA data was carried out using the ConsensusClusterPlus package. Using the Euclidean distance, km was the clustering algorithm. 100 bootstraps containing 80% of the samples were included in the analysis. 2–9 clusters were tested, and the optimal subtype was determined based on the proportion of ambiguous clustering (PAC) and the consistency matrix.

### GSVA analysis and functional annotation
  Using the GSVA package, GSVA analysis was carried out on each TCGA tumor sample to identify the functional characteristics of high-risk and low-risk subtypes. The signatures used were from the Hallmark collection of the MsigDB database. Using the limma package, a differential analysis of the gsva data from the two groups was carried out. Functional enrichments with t > 1 were considered enriched in the high-risk group, whereas in the low-risk group, functional enrichments with t < 1 were considered enriched. An Upset plot was used to study the union and intersection of functional enrichments across different data sets.


### Tumor Immune Infiltration Analysis
  Using the ESTIMATE algorithm, immune infiltration analysis of the TCGA-PAAD dataset was performed out to determine Tumor Purity, StromaScore, ImmuneScore, and ESTIMATEScore. Moreover, the composition of immune cells in PAAD was assessed using CIBERSORTx, which predicts the immune cell composition of bulk samples based on single-cell data. To compare groups across groups, the Kruskal test was used.


### Prediction of immune therapy/chemotherapy response
  The oncoPredict package in R was used to estimate the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) for traditional chemotherapy drugs by analyzing the response to these therapies. The ssGSEA algorithm was used to determine cytolytic activity (CYT). The TCIA database acquired scores for IPS, IPS-PD1, IPS-CTLA4, and IPS-PD1 + CTLA4. The TIDE online analysis predicted the TCGA and Meta-Seq datasets’ immune responses and scores. The immune checkpoint genes were subjected to differential analysis using the Kruskal test, with p adj < 0.05 considered significant.


### SNV analysis
  Biological information about SNV mutations was gathered from the TCGA database, and statistical analysis of the gene mutations found in each sample was carried out using the maftools package. With p < 0.05 considered significant, the Wilcoxon test was used for a differential analysis between the wild and mutant groups.


### Comparison of risk model with other classification results
  Comparison was made with other immune, PAM50, methylation, and m6A classification results reported in the literature. In order to compare the proportions of high-risk and low-risk subtypes in various classes, the chi-square test was employed, along with survival curves and Sankey diagrams for visualization.


### Cell line and culture condition
  AsPC-1, HPAC, and HPDE6-C7 cell lines were obtained from the Chinese Academy of Sciences Cell Bank of Type Culture Collection (CBTCCCAS, Shanghai, China). Standard cell culture conditions were maintained at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) for cell lines such as AsPC-1, HPDE6-C7, and HPAC.


### RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT–PCR)
  The RNA procurement and quantitative determination were conducted according to established protocols [13]. An RNA isolation system called Fastgen2000 (Fastgen, Shanghai, China) was used to extract total RNA from different types of cells. TaKaRa reverse transcription kits were utilized to generate complementary DNA (cDNA). To examine the qRT-PCR results, the 2-ΔΔCt method was used. Additional file 1 contains detailed information on the primers used in this study. All qRT-PCR experiments were performed with at least three independent replicates. Statistical comparisons between groups were performed using Student’s t-test, with *p* < 0.05 considered statistically significant.


### WB analysis
  The WB assay was performed to analyze protein expression levels. For cell lysis, RIPA buffer was used. Following electrophoresis on SDS-PAGE gels, the total protein samples were subsequently transferred to PVDF membranes. Subsequently, the proteins were detected utilizing an enhanced chemiluminescence kit provided by NCM Biotech in Suzhou, China. The primary antibodies employed in this study were ab109250 for anti-NANOG and ab92494 for anti-SOX2 (Abcam). anti-β-Actin (ab8226; Abcam). For ferroptosis inhibitor rescue experiments, CCDC6-knockdown cells were treated with Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1, 1 µM) for 72 h prior to protein extraction. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software and normalized to β-actin. All Western blot experiments were repeated independently at least three times. Statistical comparisons between groups were performed using Student’s t-test, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.


### CFA analysis
  HPAC pancreatic cancer cells were seeded at a density of 1000 cells per 6 cm dish. Following treatment, the cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and stained for 15 min with crystal violet solution. After that, colonies were counted under a microscope in five randomly selected fields. Colony formation assays were performed in three independent experiments. Colonies containing > 50 cells were counted manually. Statistical significance was determined using Student’s t-test.


### Mammosphere formation assays
  Briefly, the pancreatic cancer cells treated with siRNA transfection were seeded into 6-well ultralow attachment plates at densities of 2,000 per well. The medium was refreshed every other day with the previously mentioned medium supplemented with 20 ng/ml EGF (Sigma). Mammosphere formation assays were conducted in three independent replicates. Mammospheres with a diameter > 50 μm were counted under a microscope. Student’s t-test was used for statistical comparison.


### Statistical analysis
  The R language was utilized for doing statistical analysis. Using the relevant R packages, the Cox regression analysis was performed. A p-value criterion of less than 0.05 was used to identify prognostic variables and assess statistical significance.


## Results

### Establishment of a prognostic model based on ferroptosis-related genes
  In order to study the FRGs with prognosis stratification value, we used a single-factor Cox analysis, which was shown in the form of a correlation heatmap, displaying the expression patterns of 75 prognostic FRGs (P < 0.05) in pancreatic cancer samples from the TCGA dataset (Fig. 1A). YAP1, CCDC6, and CIRBP are the three cross-validated FRGs with high prognostic stratification value for pancreatic cancer patients that were identified using three machine learning algorithms for survival analysis: LassoCox (Fig. 1B-C), random forest (Fig. 1D-E), and CoxBoost (Fig. 1F) (Fig. 1G). The CoxBoost algorithm was shown to have the greatest performance out of the 20 ML algorithms when they were used to generate a thorough C index for external validation datasets. The expression levels of the three prognostic FRGs in pancreatic patients of different clinical features of age, pN, and pT were also analyzed using statistical methods (Fig. 1H-J). Significant differences could be observed in the expression levels of all 3 FRGs in pancreatic cancer patients of different pathological N stages. Except for YAP1, the other 2 FRGs showed no significant expression differences in the elderly (age > 60) pancreatic cancer population.

  We used the three identified FRGs to perform consensus clustering of pancreatic cancer samples from the TCGA dataset and found that k = 2 was the optimal clustering number based on the PAC plot (Fig. 2A-C). The survival outcomes of the pancreatic cancer patients of two subtypes, C1 and C2, were shown (Fig. 2D), highlighting C2 with a poorer prognosis (P < 0.05). 11 distinct types of immune cells were identified from our analysis of pancreatic cancer single-cell data (Fig. 2E). Pancreatic cancer patients in the C2 subgroup, characterized by poorer clinical outcomes, were found with an activated immune microenvironment, indicated by the 3 indices. The results contradicted the common knowledge, suggesting certain immune evasion mechanisms underlying the pancreatic cancer progression.

  We compared our risk groups with other immune subtypes reported in the literature. We used Sankey diagrams and survival curves to present the results and performed chi-square tests to compare the proportions of high-risk and low-risk groups in other subtyping systems. We found significant composition differences between the FRGs-based and immune subtyping models. The combination of the two models showed C1 + high risk group with the worst prognosis (Supplemental Fig. 1A, C, D). The c1-c6 immune subtyping were different with regard to the specific stratification strategy of the pancreatic cancer patients of our model. The combination of two models suggested a high+C6 subgroup with the worst prognosis and a low+other subgroup with the best prognosis in the TCGA cohort (Supplemental Fig. 1B, E, F).

  We conducted chi-square tests and showed waterfall plots of the top 30 gene mutations across the two groups, demonstrating significant differences in KRAS and TTN mutations (Supplemental Fig. 2A). We showed the differences in methylation subtypes based on risk values (Supplemental Fig. 2B-C), highlighting the C1 methylation site with lower risk values. KRAS and TTN mutations were also significantly associated with risk values in the wild and mutant subgroups (Supplemental Fig. 2E-F). Sankey diagrams and chi-square tests revealed significant differences in the composition of methylation subtypes and risk groups, and survival curves were plotted based on the combined subtype results (Supplemental Fig. 2D, G).

  We calculated the m6A level and m6A value in TCGA pancreatic cancer data and found a negative correlation between m6A level and risk values and a positive correlation between m6A value and risk values (Supplemental Fig. 3A-D). ### Construction of the FRGs-based nomogram model
  Gender, Age, Stage, pM, pN, pT, and Risk score were all subjected to multivariate Cox analysis (Figs. 7A–B), revealing the Risk score value as the only independent risk factor for patients with pancreatic cancer. The nomogram was shown by DCA analysis to be superior to the risk score and other clinical indicators (Fig. 7C). The calibration curves of different time points were shown (Figs. 7D-F). We also plotted the survival and corresponding timeROC curves for high- and low- points at 1, 3, and 5 years (Figs. 7G-H). The 3-year and 5-year AUC values were higher than 0.7, suggesting an optimal role of our nomogram model in prognosis prediction for pancreatic cancer patients.

### In vitro validation of key gene expression and identification of CCDC6 in regulating cancer cell stemness
  To investigate if the expression levels of the key genes were changed in the pancreatic cancer cells, we used qRT-PCR to examine the mRNA levels of YAP, CIREP, and CCDC6 in the AsPC-1 and HPAC cancer cell lines in comparison to the HPDE6-C7 normal pancreatic epithelial cell lines. We found significant up-regulation of the CCDC6 in both AsPC-1 and HPAC cancer cell lines (Fig. 8A). We knocked down the expression of CCDC6 using siRNA, which was verified by qRT-PCR (Fig. 8B). Little to no colonies were formed with the same dilution of HPAC cells. We asked whether CCDC6 knocks down diminished cancer cell stemness of HPAC cells. We applied WB and mammosphere formation analysis. The protein levels of NANOG and SOX2, both well-established cancer cell stemness markers, were significantly down-regulated in CCDC6-KD HPAC cells (Fig. 8C), which was consistent with markedly reduced mammosphere volume formed by the CCDC6-KD HPAC cells (Fig. 8D). The absence of the CCDC6 expression rendered the HPAC cells incompetent of forming tumor mammosphere. Collectively, we observed CCDC6 to be highly expressed in pancreatic cancer cells and the critical role of CCDC6 in the regulation of cancer cell stemness. Through CFA analysis, we observed a distinguished reduction in the colony number in CCDC6-KD HPAC cells (Fig. 8E).

### CCDC6 knockdown suppresses the SLC7A11–GPX4 ferroptosis defense axis
  To determine whether CCDC6 functionally intersects with ferroptosis pathways, we examined the expression of GPX4 and SLC7A11, two core components of the canonical ferroptosis defense system, following CCDC6 knockdown. Western blot analysis revealed that CCDC6 depletion significantly reduced the protein levels of both GPX4 and SLC7A11 in HPAC cells (Fig. 8C). To further establish the functional relevance of this regulation to stemness, we treated CCDC6-knockdown cells with the ferroptosis inhibitor Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) and assessed the expression of stemness markers. Fer-1 treatment partially restored NANOG and SOX2 protein levels that had been suppressed by CCDC6 depletion (Fig. 8E). These results indicate that CCDC6 sustains stemness-associated features, at least in part, through maintaining the SLC7A11–GPX4 anti-ferroptotic axis, and that pharmacological inhibition of ferroptosis can compensate for CCDC6 loss.


## Discussion
  Patients with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) had higher survival rates when GPX4, a key modulator of ferroptosis, was highly expressed, according to the survival analysis [14]. This suggests that one important prognostic marker for PDAC may be GPX4. In addition, certain drugs like sulfasalazine, sorafenib, and glutamate have been found to inhibit the relevant transporters, thereby inducing ferroptosis [15]. Another study suggests that the widely used antiviral drug zalcitabine may cause ferroptosis, stopping the growth of Human pancreatic cancer cells, both primary and immortalized. Zalcitabine-induced stress in mitochondrial DNA triggers the STING1/TMEM173-mediated DNA sensing pathway, which is how this effect is achieved. Furthermore, zalcitabine has been shown to enhance MFN1/2-dependent mitochondrial fusion, promoting ferroptosis in human pancreatic cancer cell lines [16]. Additionally, the study showed that the simultaneous inhibition of cystine import, GSH synthesis, and GPX4 can cause ferroptosis [17]. These results prove the significance of ferroptosis in pancreatic cancer. In our study, we investigated the role of FRGs in the prognosis stratification of pancreatic cancer patients by combining regression analysis and ML algorithms. QRT-PCR further determined the FRGs identified with prognostic value to study their mRNA expression in pancreatic cancer in vitro.

  YAP1 was one of our study’s 3 key genes found with prognosis stratification value. YAP1, a robust transcriptional coactivator, plays crucial roles in development, stem cell maintenance, normal tissue homeostasis, and regeneration [18]. The initiation, progression, invasion, and development of cancer resistance to therapy have all been linked to dysregulation and persistent activation of YAP1. These functions are predominantly attributed to the interaction of YAP1 with TEAD transcription factors [19, 20], which triggers a transcriptional promotion of cell survival and proliferation. Notably, elevated levels of nuclear YAP1 staining have been observed in numerous cancer types. In pancreatic cancer cells, YAP1 functions as a powerful transcriptional coactivator. It forms a complex with ZEB1 and interacts with the TEAD binding sites to activate the transcription of ITGA3. The protein ZIP4 increases the expression of YAP1 by activating miR-373 and inhibiting the YAP1 repressor LATS2. Via the ZEB1/YAP1-ITGA3 signaling axis, elevated ZIP4 levels also improve PANC cancer cells’ and models’ organogenesis, spheroid formation, cell adhesion, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) plasticity [21]. However, we found no significant differences in the mRNA expression level of YAP1 in pancreatic cancer cells. This raises the question of whether a particular YAP1 post-transcription modification plays a role in the development of pancreatic cancer, which requires further discussion.

  CCDC6 encoding coiled-coil domain-containing protein was widely taken as a tumor suppressor by exerting apoptotic functions. In lung neuroendocrine tumors, CCDC6 stability is regulated by USP7, and its degradation sensitizes cancer cells to PARP inhibitors and cisplatin [22]. Similarly, in high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma, loss of CCDC6 disrupts the CCDC6-PP4 complex, impairs homologous recombination repair, and confers PARP inhibitor sensitivity [23]. Paradoxically, emerging evidence also supports a tumor-promoting role for CCDC6 in certain contexts. For instance, high CCDC6 expression correlates with unfavorable outcomes in hepatobiliary carcinoma [24], and in gastric cancer, circDNA2 upregulates CCDC6 by sponging miR-149-5p, thereby promoting tumor progression [25]. Consistent with this oncogenic aspect, we observed markedly elevated CCDC6 mRNA levels in pancreatic cancer cell lines compared to normal pancreatic epithelial cells, and its knockdown suppressed proliferation, reduced stemness marker expression (NANOG and SOX2), and impaired mammosphere formation.

  To explore the mechanism underlying CCDC6-mediated stemness regulation, we investigated its impact on the ferroptosis defense pathway. Notably, CCDC6 knockdown significantly downregulated the expression of both GPX4 and SLC7A11, two core components of the canonical ferroptosis defense axis [26]. More importantly, treatment with a ferroptosis inhibitor partially restored the expression of the stemness markers NANOG and SOX2 that had been suppressed by CCDC6 depletion. These findings suggest that CCDC6 supports stemness features, at least in part, by maintaining the SLC7A11-GPX4 axis. Accumulating evidence indicates that ferroptosis plays a critical role in the maintenance of cancer stemness: GPX4 overexpression has been shown to promote stemness maintenance in pancreatic cancer [27], and SLC7A11 enhances cystine uptake to replenish glutathione and activate GPX4, thereby not only conferring ferroptosis resistance but also cooperating with GPX4 to sustain cancer cell stemness [28]. Collectively, our data establish a functional link between CCDC6 and this essential ferroptosis defense module in the context of pancreatic cancer stemness. Further investigation will be required to delineate the precise molecular mechanisms through which CCDC6 regulates GPX4 and SLC7A11 expression.

  In our study, three FRGs (YAP1, CCDC6, and CIRBP) were found to have significant prognostic value for pancreatic cancer patients. The pathological stage of the cancer and these FRGs’ expression levels were related. A prognostic model was developed using these FRGs, and it effectively predicted long-term survival. External validation further supported the accuracy of the model.

  The high-risk group was characterized by elevated macrophage infiltration, increased expression of CEACAM1 and LGALS3, yet no significant changes in other immune cell populations or immunotherapy response predictors. This immune profile should be interpreted in the context of pancreatic cancer’s characteristic tumor microenvironment. PC is typified by a fibroinflammatory stroma and pronounced immunosuppression, with tumor associated macrophages (TAMs) representing the most abundant immune cell population and serving as the central drivers of immune suppression [29, 30]. Within this context, LGALS3, encoding galectin-3, is highly expressed by tumor cells and macrophages and promotes macrophage recruitment and M2 polarization; its upregulation may further reinforce TAM dominance in the high-risk group [31–33]. CEACAM1 can act as a myeloid-recognized danger signal, further reinforcing macrophage activation [34]. Meanwhile, CEACAM1-mediated inhibition of T and NK cells occurs through functional silencing—such as impairing cytotoxicity or marking suppressive Treg subsets—rather than altering their already limited infiltration numbers [35, 36]. Furthermore, the CEACAM1–LGALS3 axis operates largely independently of the PD-1/CTLA-4 pathways targeted by current immunotherapies, which are themselves largely ineffective in pancreatic cancer. Collectively, these features are consistent with the well-established “cold” tumor phenotype of PDAC and may explain the disconnect between elevated immune checkpoint molecule expression and unchanged immunotherapy response predictors, suggesting that CEACAM1 and LGALS3 indicate a macrophage-dominated, immune-excluded state rather than actionable immune activation.

  Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, clinical annotations, including pathological stage, treatment regimens, and ethnicity, were not uniformly available across the external validation datasets, precluding a systematic comparison of baseline characteristics with the training cohort. Although the prognostic model performed robustly across multiple independent cohorts, potential population or treatment related confounding effects could not be fully excluded. Future prospective validation in demographically diverse and clinically well-annotated populations is warranted to further confirm the model’s generalizability.Second, the ESTIMATE and CIBERSORTx algorithms rely on bulk transcriptome deconvolution to infer immune and stromal infiltration. Given that pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma is characterized by a profoundly desmoplastic stroma that can constitute up to 80% of the tumor mass, stromal cell contamination may lead to overestimation of stromal and immune scores, and the deconvolution resolution remains inherently constrained even when single-cell-derived signatures are used as a reference. Third, although our model demonstrated robust long-term predictive performance, whether it provides additional prognostic value beyond existing clinical staging systems remains to be formally evaluated. Furthermore, all functional validation experiments were performed in a single pancreatic cancer cell line (HPAC). Although CCDC6 overexpression was confirmed in both AsPC-1 and HPAC cells, the downstream phenotypic and mechanistic experiments were not independently replicated in a second cell line. Validation in additional pancreatic cancer cell lines is needed to confirm the generalizability of these findings. Finally, the functional role of CCDC6 was assessed primarily in vitro; in vivo tumorigenesis assays and gain-of-function studies are warranted to further confirm its role in stemness regulation. Future studies incorporating prospective cohort validation, patient-derived organoid models, and spatial transcriptomics will be essential to address these limitations and advance clinical translation.


## Conclusion
  A FRGs-based prognostic model was established and extensively validated in pancreatic cancer, showing high long-term prognosis stratification efficacy. Our data demonstrate that pancreatic cancer cells exhibit high expression of CCDC6, and its depletion impairs in vitro stemness features. These findings reveal a role for CCDC6 in sustaining in vitro stemness-associated phenotypes, warranting further validation in in vivo tumorigenesis models.
