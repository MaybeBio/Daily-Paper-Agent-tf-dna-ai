# Single-cell mapping of regulatory DNA-protein interactions


## SUMMARY
  Gene expression is controlled by transcription factors (TFs), whose genome binding is shaped by chromatin accessibility and histone modifications, yet mapping these interactions, particularly those with weak affinity or a transient nature, in single cells remains technically challenging. To address this gap, we developed docking and deamination followed by sequencing (D&D-seq), a single-cell immuno-tethering technology for profiling DNA-protein interactions. D&D-seq couples an antibody-binding nanobody to a cytosine base editor, a combination that enables detection of weak or transient factor binding through targeted cytosine-to-uracil editing at protein-bound genomic sites. This approach is compatible with standard single-cell multi-omic workflows and therefore allows integrated analyses of gene regulation. Using assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq) and single-cell ATAC-seq (scATAC-seq), we assessed chromatin accessibility as a functional readout of TF activity, and by coupling D&D-seq with whole-genome sequencing, we captured CTCF binding in both active and inactive chromatin compartments.


## INTRODUCTION
  Regulatory control orchestrated by chromatin-binding factors, including transcription factors (TFs) and chromatin remodelers (CRs), underlies the gene expression programs that maintain cell identity, execute cellular functions, and respond to environmental stimuli. These DNA-protein interactions are directed through epigenetic features1 that establish chromatin landscapes, modulating the binding of specific factors to sculpt the functional genome according to cellular needs. Epigenetic dysregulation is linked to cellular dysfunction in disease, cancer, and aging, where aberrant chromatin landscapes transform TF-binding landscapes,2 thereby altering normal cellular processes.3 Furthermore, >90% of genome-wide association study (GWAS) hits fall within non-coding regions,4 which underscores the need to define cis-regulatory elements for insights into human genetic diseases or traits. Thus, understanding the complexity and dysregulation of TF binding across different cell types and cell states remains a central challenge in human biology.

  DNA-binding motif sequence information, obtained from in vitro assays, bulk genomic datasets, or computational models, is available for ~1,100 TFs,5 but motif information alone is often insufficient to accurately predict binding in vivo.5,6 Chromatin accessibility interplays with TF binding: many TFs preferentially bind accessible regions,6 while binding activity of others, particularly pioneer factors, shapes chromatin states. TF-TF interactions,7 TF-nucleosome competition,8 and the spatial syntax of motifs within enhancers9 also influence binding specificity.

  Thus, profiling TF binding in native chromatin contexts is crucial for unraveling the physiological regulation, and disease-associated dysregulation, of gene expression. However, current methods for mapping DNA-protein interactions in single cells have technical limitations. Chromatin immunoprecipitation with sequencing (ChIP-seq) and cleavage under targets and tagmentation (CUT&Tag) map genome-wide DNA interactions of target proteins through sequencing of crosslinked or tagmented DNA fragments. Additionally, DNA adenine methyltransferase identification (DamID)-based molecular-footprinting techniques harness DNA methyltransferase activity to map TF binding.10,11 However, these methods cannot be easily incorporated into high-throughput single-cell workflows, limiting applications to bulk analysis or to single-cell profiling of only the strongest interacting chromatin factors. Single-cell profiling of TF binding in primary samples has been mainly restricted to inferential approaches based on expression levels of downstream TF target genes12–14 or through motif analysis of assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq) peaks,15,16 but identification of specific TF-binding sites requires more direct methods.16,17

  To address these limitations, we developed docking and deamination followed by sequencing (D&D-seq) to map DNA-protein interactions in low-input samples or single cells from primary samples. We fused the base-editing deaminase DddA, which catalyzes C-to-U changes, to secondary nanobodies targeting an antibody-labeled DNA-binding protein of interest, amenable to integration into single-cell genome-accessibility workflows, combinatorial-barcoding Paired-seq18 or simultaneous high-throughput ATAC and RNA expression with sequencing (SHARE-seq),19 or whole-genome sequencing (WGS) approaches (direct library preparation [DLP]+20 or primary template-directed amplification [PTA]21). We applied D&D-seq to primary cells, profiled multiple TFs and CRs, integrated it with single-cell genotyping,22 and combined it with 10× Multiome to co-profile TF binding, the transcriptome, and chromatin accessibility of individual cells across erythropoiesis. We envision that these tools can be broadly applied to map TF or chromatin-remodeler binding to chromatin landscapes directly from primary samples.


## RESULTS

### D&D-seq maps DNA-protein binding via molecular footprinting
  CUT&Tag-derived approaches23–27 profile stable and abundant chromatin binders, such as histones, in single cells, generating libraries by fragmenting DNA surrounding an antibody-bound chromatin factor, facilitated by a protein A (pA)-Tn5 fusion protein. pA binds to antibodies and Tn5 transposase fragments and tags DNA with sequencing-ready adapters. High-affinity Tn5-DNA binding necessitates stringent conditions (e.g., 300–500 mM NaCl) to prevent open-chromatin signal from direct binding of Tn5 to DNA. Such stringency limits pA-Tn5 antibody-tethering activity, resulting in inefficient tagmentation at chromatin-factor-bound sites. Additionally, high-salt conditions disrupt weak interactions, such as TF binding,28 or prevent target binding by low-affinity antibodies.

  To address this challenge, we developed D&D-seq to map TF binding in single cells. As an alternative to tagmentation, we reasoned that precisely tethering a base-editing enzyme to target proteins would capture binding. We fused the double-stranded DNA deaminase DddA29,30 to secondary nanobodies that recognize species-specific immunoglobulin Fc domains (nb-DddA), or to IgG-binders, including protein A/G. Fusion enzymes bind to primary antibodies, converting cytosine to uracil near the targeted TF-or chromatin-remodeler-binding sites revealed upon sequencing, such as using standard ATAC-seq (Figure 1A).

  For fusion-enzyme design, we selected engineered DddA11, with higher activity and more versatile DNA-editing context than the original DddA.30 The split-enzyme design enabled switch-like control over enzyme activity (Figures 1B, S1A, and S1B), where the D&D enzyme is inactive upon DNA binding, avoiding non-specific deamination due to random DNA interactions. Addition of the small C-terminal peptide reconstitutes catalytic activity29–31 (STAR Methods).

  In a deamination assay, recombinant nb-DddA_NT (N-terminal) was only active when DddA_CT (C-terminal) was present, and Zn2+ addition increased activity (Figures 1C, S1C, and S1D). Consistent with previous studies,30,31 nb-DddA preferentially deaminated cytosines in a TpC context (Figures 1D and S1E), with high activity at 37°C (Figures S1F–S1H), confirming that our D&D enzyme recapitulates the base-editing activity of the unmodified enzyme.

  For bulk D&D-seq, isolated cells are fixed and permeabilized to allow reagent entry, followed by incubation with a primary antibody specific to the targeted DNA-binding protein. After washing unbound antibody, the sample is incubated with nb-DddA_NT, localizing the deaminase to the target. After washing excess nb-DddA_NT, we activated the deaminase by incubating samples with DddA_CT and Zn2+. Following washing, the sample can be processed with standard ATAC-seq methods, and analysis of the multi-modal data allows profiling of protein-binding events on accessible genomic DNA (Figure 1A).

  To evaluate nb-DddA activity in cells, we next tested nb-DddA in a bulk D&D-seq experiment targeting either CTCF, GATA1, or GATA2 TFs in K562 cells. We identified an increased C>T (or G>A) mutation signature in reads overlapping respective ENCODE ChIP-seq peaks versus remaining reads (Figures 1E–1G and S1I–S1K). We observed that nb-DddA deaminates in TC as well as CC contexts (Figure S1L), likely due to high local DddA concentration around targeted TFs. Deamination footprint analysis of edit events in a 200-bp window around all genome-wide, HOCOMOCO-defined32 CTCF-, GATA1-, or GATA2-binding sites showed the expected bimodal distribution of edit events, where target-binding sites are at the center of the two modes, with low editing frequency in background-accessible regions lacking binding sites (Figures 1H–1J). The same analytical framework applied to ENCODE K562 ATAC-seq controls showed no footprint structures or motif enrichment, reinforcing D&D signal dependence on active editing events (Figures S1M and S1N). Peaks called from reads with DddA-specific edits showed strong CTCF and GATA motif enrichment in the respective peaks (Figures 1K–1M), confirming specificity. CTCF and GATA motifs were consistently enriched as the top motifs identified by de novo discovery tools (Figures 1N–1P). Only the Tn5 consensus motif ranked higher, which is expected due to open-chromatin tagmentation. Comparing our results with ENCODE33 CTCF or GATA1 ChIP-seq data from K562 cells showed that D&D-seq data recapitulated ChIP-seq-identified peaks (Figure 1Q).

  We next explored profiling TF-binding sites beyond accessible chromatin regions. To identify genome-wide binding events, we coupled D&D-seq with WGS, profiling CTCF binding as before, except that after the CTCF-specific deamination reaction, we used PTA to amplify and obtain a WGS library. WGS confirmed the expected karyotype of K562 cells (Figure 1R), indicating that the D&D workflow does not affect the quality of whole-genome amplification. We identified 2,045 peaks outside open-chromatin regions, expanding applications to profile binding to regions that do not overlap ATAC assays. We generated CTCF peak tracks that were comparable to high-quality ENCODE ChIP-seq, regardless of peak accessibility (Figure 1S). To validate that D&D-seq is not restricted to accessible chromatin, we examined histone post-translational-modification patterns of non-ATAC D&D peaks, finding 196 inaccessible peaks overlapping H3K9me3 or H3K27me3 heterochromatin marks (Figure 1S), consistent with CTCF binding to inactive chromatin.34 Edit events within a 200-bp window encompassing all known CTCF-binding sites showed that footprints in both accessible and non-accessible regions were significantly higher than those observed in CTCF-negative regions (Figure 1T).

  We next evaluated D&D-seq performance on chromatin-remodeling factors, which typically exhibit more transient interactions and lack specific consensus DNA-binding sequences. We focused on p300, a histone acetyltransferase that localizes to active enhancers through interaction with coactivators, and found that D&D-seq successfully captured deamination footprints around p300-bound regions. We observed strong enrichment of deamination signal across p300 ChIP-defined peaks (ENCODE) (Figures S1O and S1P). Notably, these footprints were broader than sequence-specific TF footprints, consistent with the more diffuse occupancy of chromatin-remodeling complexes. We performed de novo motif analysis on the D&D-seq-positive peaks identified at p300-bound regions (Figures S1Q and S1R). Given that p300 does not bind DNA directly but is recruited through interactions with sequence-specific transcriptional coactivators, we searched for de novo motifs enriched in frequently edited peaks. TFs in the top three motifs enriched in the highly edited peaks matched previously reported recognition sequences of TFs that interact with p300, including GATA, NF-Y, and KLF1 TF family members35–38 (Figure S1R), and the D&D editing profile reproduced strong p300-binding signal (Figure S1S). Top edit peaks were annotated as promoter or enhancer loci, consistent with known p300 interaction partners, with active chromatin signature (H3K27ac) enrichment and closed chromatin signature (H3K27me3 and H3K9me3) depletion (Figures S1T and S1U).

  We additionally profiled other TFs, including JUN, SP1, and TAL1 (Figures S1V and S1W), providing evidence that D&D-seq can analyze the binding patterns of a range of TFs.


### D&D-seq profiles single-cell DNA-protein interaction
  We next tested the ability of D&D-seq to record TF binding in single cells when incorporated into the 10× Genomics single-cell ATAC-seq (scATAC-seq) workflow using cell-line mixing experiments with the lymphocyte cell line CA46 and the erythroblast cell line K562, as well as two TF targets (CTCF and GATA1). The two cell lines were individually crosslinked, permeabilized, and stained with a CTCF antibody for CA46 and a GATA1 antibody for K562. Cells were equally mixed and incubated in the D&D-seq buffer containing the activating C-terminal peptide of DddA and the Zn2+ cofactor, allowing us to record CTCF or GATA1 presence on genomic DNA in each cell line. After this step, samples can be processed with custom or commercially available scATAC-seq workflows.

  We profiled K562 (n = 1,732 cells) and CA46 (n = 4,108 cells) (Figure 2A), with high-quality tagmentation data (mean fragments per cell ± standard deviation: K562, 5,263 ± 2,633; CA46, 5,437 ± 2,588) (Figures 2B and S2A). Cell lines were identified by marker gene accessibility scores (Figure 2C). Two distinct clusters showed clear separation between CA46 and K562 cells, defined by lymphoid (Figure 2D) and erythroid (Figure 2E) markers, demonstrating that the D&D-seq reaction does not affect scATAC-seq performance.

  We mapped protein-binding events in all cells at the single-cell level, indicating high efficiency and specificity (Figure S2B; STAR Methods). The relatively low number of edits per cell (Figures 2F–2I) is comparable to established tagmentation-based single-cell TF binding inference methods, and data integration through pseudobulk or metacell analysis can enable robust downstream interpretations. Assessing the presence of cross-contamination during droplet encapsulation, barcoding, and library preparation showed that CTCF and GATA1 signals are retained exclusively in the subpopulation stained with the respective antibody, demonstrating cell-level specificity (Figures 2F–2I).

  We performed de novo motif discovery on pseudobulk D&D-seq peaks (Figures 2J–2M), observing significant enrichment of the CTCF-binding site in CA46 cells (Figure 2J) and the GATA1-binding site in K562 cells (Figure 2L). We orthogonally validated our findings using a motif-centric rather than de novo approach. Here, total reads from the single-cell experiment are used to calculate the frequency of D&D events in a 200-bp window around all genome-wide, HOCOMOCO-defined32 CTCF- or GATA-binding sites, compared with the frequency of C-to-T events in all identified accessible peaks. Consistent with the bulk experiment, single-cell pseudobulk analysis revealed the expected bimodal distribution of deamination events (Figures 2K and 2M top). However, the same analysis for accessible regions lacking known binding sites showed negligible edit event frequency lacking clearly defined distribution patterns (Figures 2K and 2M, bottom). Analysis of the non-targeted factor for each cell type showed no GATA1-binding signal in CA46 cells and no CTCF-binding signal in K562 cells (Figure S2C).

  We next evaluated deamination specificity at target protein-binding sites. We collapsed reads from specific clusters to obtain pseudobulk alignments, then extracted reads in which we identified C-to-T conversion events, generating D&D coverage plots. Peaks obtained with in situ footprinting showed high concordance with bulk ChIP-seq reference ENCODE data33 (Figure 2N), suggesting minimal off-target activity. Comparing scD&D-seq and ENCODE reads showed high correlation between CTCF signals and between GATA1 signals from each data type but low correlation across CTCF and GATA1 signals (Figure 2O). These correlation values are comparable to CUT&Tag benchmarking results on histones H3K27me3 or H3K4me327 (Figure S2D).

  The lack of similar single-cell technologies limits direct benchmarking of D&D-seq with current state-of-the-art DNA-protein interaction profiling methods. However, ultra-low input CUT&Tag (uliCUT&RUN)39 was used to profile CTCF binding in 10–500,000 mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs).40 To further benchmark scD&D-seq, we iteratively randomly subsampled (n = 100) CA46 single cells, stained with CTCF antibody, to generate matching datasets with the same cell number as uliCUT&RUN. As negative control, the same number of K562 cells, not stained with CTCF antibody, was subsampled to match the uliCUT&RUN negative control cells that were not stained with antibody. We calculated the fraction of the reads in the peaks (FRiP) from ChIP-seq, where high FRiP indicates that most reads are located in regions of interest, and low FRiP indicates that most reads are in non-specific regions, reflecting low specificity. scD&D-seq data showed that most base-edited reads map to regions where CTCF was previously mapped, with ~60% FRiP across analyses. Signal-to-noise ratio was largely unaffected by cell number, showing similar values for the 5,000- (56.75%,), 500- (56.78%), 50- (56.83%), and 10-cell (57.00%) sample sizes (Figure 2P, top). As expected, the cell line not stained with CTCF antibody showed low FRiP values across input conditions, indicating that most edited reads were not CTCF specific. scD&D-seq outperformed uliCUT&RUN, which scored 13.69%, 13.69%, 13.89%, and 14.04% FRiP for the 5,000-, 500-, 50-, and 10-cell sample sizes, reflecting most reads mapped to genomic regions not bound by CTCF (Figure 2P, bottom).

  We also analyzed publicly available scCUT&Tag datasets profiling Olig2 and Rad21 in mouse brain cells,23 benchmarking FRiP against ChIP-seq reference tracks from mouse brain. Olig2 scCUT&Tag exhibited low FRiP scores (Olig2 scC&T median FRiP = 0.22; CTCF uliC&R = 0.13–0.16; CTCF D&D = 0.57, GATA1 D&D = 0.43), consistent with the lower specificity we observed for uliCUT&RUN (Figures S2E and S2F). As a negative control, Olig2 FRiP calculated on Rad21 ChIP-seq showed similar amounts (~0.22) of reads aligned to the Rad21 peaks. scCUT&Tag for cohesin subunit Rad21 showed comparable FRiP scores to CTCF scD&D-seq and performed better than Olig2 (Figure S2E). Compared with scCUT&Tag, both CTCF (median FRiP = 0.57) and GATA1 (median FRiP = 0.43) scD&D-seq showed high FRiP scores on matching ChIP-seq peaks, with lower FRiP scores on unmatched ChIP-seq peaks (Figure S2F). D&D thus provides superior separation of target and control FRiP compared with scCUT&Tag, where separation was poor.

  To further evaluate specificity, we compared peak overlap between scCUT&Tag and scD&D-seq datasets using ChIP-seq as a reference. Although ChIP-seq peaks minimally overlapped for Rad21/Olig2 and CTCF/GATA1 pairs (Figure S2G), there was extensive overlap between Rad21 and Olig2 scCUT&Tag peaks (Figure S2H left), consistent with the low FRiP scores. In contrast, scD&D-seq peaks for CTCF and GATA1 had more restricted overlap, closer to ChIP-seq behavior (Figure S2H right). De novo motif discovery on scCUT&Tag datasets revealed that Rad21 scCUT&Tag peaks were dominated by CTCF motifs, consistent with the known Rad21 and CTCF interaction on chromatin (Figure S2I). However, Olig2 scCUT&Tag peaks failed to recover the Olig2 motif (80th rank in enrichment), highlighting poor factor specificity (Figure S2I). The top 3 ranked motifs in scCUT&Tag showed similar enrichment scores, indicating lack of motif dominance (Figure S2I). In contrast, scD&D-seq peaks showed higher enrichment for the cognate GATA1 and CTCF motifs, which were the top-ranked features in GATA1 and CTCF scD&D-seq peaks, respectively (Figures 2J and 2L).

  Next, we compared scCUT&Tag Rad21 and Olig2 datasets with our scD&D-seq CTCF and GATA1 datasets using the same motif-based approach to remove non-specific peaks, enabling fairer comparison. After filtering Rad21 scCUT&Tag peaks for CTCF motifs, 84% (11,662/13,933) overlapped with Rad21 ChIP-seq peaks, whereas Olig2 scCUT&Tag peaks filtered for Olig2 motifs showed only 43% (1,963/4,601) overlap with Olig2 ChIP-seq (Figures S2J and S2K). By comparison, 89% (10,024/11,296) of CTCF D&D peaks and 74% (1,065/1,442) of GATA1 scD&D-seq peaks with expected motifs overlapped with their respective ChIP-seq references. Swapping motifs to test cross-specificity showed that the Olig2 motif+ peaks in Rad21 scCUT&Tag and CTCF motif+ peaks in Olig2 scCUT&Tag exceeded the number of true Olig2 peaks, indicating signal bleed-through in open chromatin (Figure S2L left). In contrast, scD&D-seq showed no inflated overlap when cross-motif filters were applied (Figure S2L right). Thus, even after motif filtering, scCUT&Tag peaks are strongly influenced by open-chromatin accessibility, limiting factor-specific interpretation, whereas scD&D-seq achieves higher motif-driven specificity consistent with direct binding events. Downsampling analysis revealed robust aggregate footprint signals emerging with sample sizes of ≥250 cells (STAR Methods; Data S1).

  Given high D&D-seq specificity, we developed a de novo classification of on-target peaks using a logistic regression model built from five bulk CTCF and GATA1 D&D-seq datasets to collect features related to D&D edits for each peak (Table S1). Integrated D&D-seq data were randomly split into train and test datasets in a 70:30 ratio and fitted to a linear regression model with varying alpha parameters to predict the probability of on-target peaks (STAR Methods). Model evaluation on the test dataset showed both high area under the curve (AUC = 0.84) and precision-recall AUC (PR AUC = 0.70) values, indicating strong performance with the lasso regression model (Table S1). The maximum number of edits in a 100-bp sliding window (max edits [100-bp window]) was the most influential feature, indicating that clustered D&D edits in a small area represent the real signals induced by D&D-seq. This aligns with the footprinting in the D&D target peaks, where the majority of edits are ±50 bp from the motif sequence center due to the physical constraints of nanobody tethering. Although the model was trained on bulk CTCF and GATA1 D&D-seq samples, the classification generalized well to different target proteins and scD&D-seq with sparse D&D signals in the validation datasets (Table S1; AUC 0.73–0.89). For example, SP1 in the validation set had a distinct binding motif sequence and D&D edit patterns in target peaks (Figures S1V and S1W), where the overall performance was comparable to that of bulk CTCF and GATA1 D&D-seq. The model effectively classified on-target CTCF scD&D-seq peaks with high AUC (0.83) and specificity (0.97), comparable to bulk D&D-seq. However, GATA1 scD&D-seq performance was moderate due to low sensitivity caused by sparse single-cell D&D signals.


### Single-cell DNA-protein interaction profiling in primary human cells
  Next, we tested D&D-seq in primary human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), targeting CTCF due to its ubiquitous genomic presence across cell types and states with a defined consensus DNA-binding sequence.41 Mobilized PBMCs from healthy donors were crosslinked, permeabilized, and stained with a CTCF-specific polyclonal antibody. The sample was then processed with D&D, followed by standard 10× Genomics scATAC-seq with minimal modification (STAR Methods).

  We obtained high-quality single cells (n = 5,358 cells), with 15,534 ± 5,934 (mean ± standard deviation) fragments per cell. We projected cells into low-dimensional space using latent semantic indexing (LSI) and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP), clustering cells using the chromatin accessibility data (Figure 3A). We assigned cell states based on chromatin accessibility profiles of established marker genes, retrieving all expected subpopulations after quality control (QC) (Figures S3A and S3B). We mapped CTCF-binding sites in this complex primary tissue (Figures 3B and S3C), extracting reads with C-to-T edits and identifying D&D footprints as well as CTCF motifs (Figures 3C and 3D).

  Chromatin accessibility and CTCF binding information from the same single cells could inform 3D chromatin structure prediction using machine-learning-based approaches developed for bulk data, extending these to single-cell genomics. We explored using the C.Origami pipeline, which integrates chromatin accessibility with CTCF ChIP-seq data to train a deep neural network for de novo prediction of cell-type-specific 3D genome organization. We generated D&D-seq CTCF binding profiles for C.Origami input, together with matched scATAC-seq data, to produce pseudobulk Hi-C-like contact maps. The single-cell-derived input data (scATAC-seq and scD&D-seq) generated contact matrices that qualitatively resembled publicly available Hi-C maps, recovering both short- and long-range chromatin interactions in human primary CD8 T cells (Figure 3E). C.Origami predicted 3D chromatin structure across 100 random 2-Mb genomic regions in immune cell types including B cells and monocytes (Figures 3F and S3D–S3G). We compared CTCF ChIP-seq and D&D-seq inputs, using predictions without CTCF input as a negative control. Predictions using scD&D-seq data yielded comparable performance to those using ChIP-seq (Figures 3F, S3E, and S3G). We note that these findings are preliminary and call for further optimization of algorithms specifically tailored for single-cell data.


### Linking genotype to DNA-protein interaction profiles and chromatin landscapes in single cells
  To study the effects of somatic variants on gene regulation, we next integrated single-cell genotyping with D&D-seq. Given the mixture of mutant (MUT) and wild-type (WT) cells within clonally mosaic tissues, single-cell multi-modal methods that capture both genotype and phenotype are required to uncover mechanisms of clonal outgrowth in normal, aging or diseased tissues.42 We previously developed genotyping of transcriptomes (GoT)43 and genotyping of targeted loci and chromatin accessibility (GoT-ChA)22 to enable single-cell genotyping together with transcriptome or chromatin accessibility profiling. Leveraging this conceptual framework, we incorporated D&D-seq into GoT-ChA to link somatic mutations to changes in TF binding and applied this workflow (Figure S4A; STAR Methods) to a human sample with clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP).

  To test D&D-GoT-ChA, we profiled CTCF binding in PBMCs from a CHIP patient with an IDH2R140Q mutation at variant allele frequency 0.15 identified by targeted panel sequencing, to analyze the genotype of individual cells, along with accessible chromatin status and CTCF binding pattern.

  Targeted genotyping and D&D-deamination did not affect the quality of the chromatin accessibility data, which we used to identify all major cell subtypes expected (Figure 4A). After quality check and batch correction, data from two technical replicates were merged into a single dataset of 15,807 cells, showing appropriate QC metrics, with a mean of 7,592 fragments per cell (±3,971, standard deviation), enrichment around transcriptional start sites, and expected library size distribution (Figure S4B). Marker gene accessibility was used to annotate cells, identifying all major cell types (Figure S4C).

  We successfully genotyped 33% (5,291/15,807) of single cells, identifying IDH2R140Q-MUT and WT cells (STAR Methods). Genotyping data were assigned to individual cells and projected into the accessibility latent space to visualize the distribution of WT and MUT cells (Figure 4B), revealing enrichment of MUT cells in the CD8 T cell subcluster (Figures 4C and S4D). To validate this finding, PBMCs from the same donor were immunolabeled and fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)-sorted into the main subpopulations (STAR Methods; Data S2). Genomic DNA was isolated from natural killer (NK), monocytes, CD8 T cells, CD4 T cells, and B cells and processed for standard genotyping by bulk nanopore sequencing, which matched the single-cell genotyping data obtained with D&D-GoT-ChA (Figure 4D).

  Analysis of CTCF binding patterns showed that CTCF D&D edits were present in all single-cell clusters (Figures 4E, 4F, and S4E). We identified 5,631 CTCF-positive peaks, and motif enrichment analysis of footprinted reads showed significant enrichment of CTCF-binding sites in proximity of editing events (Figures 4G, S4F, and S4G). We performed differential analysis of CTCF edit signals between IDH2 WT cell type clusters, additionally including unique edit sites in each cell type. For validation, we analyzed publicly available immune cell CTCF ChIP-seq data, identifying differential ChIP-seq peaks using DiffBind. CD4 and CD8 T cells were grouped due to high similarity. Although most CTCF-binding sites are conserved across PBMCs,44 66 CTCF sites supported by ChIP-seq data were enriched in specific cell types (Figure S4H, monocyte = 32, NK = 2, B cell = 8, T cell = 24 CTCF sites). Several monocyte-specific CTCF peaks were at promoters of genes selectively expressed in monocytes, including the essential myeloid differentiation gene MAFB.45 Similarly, we observed CTCF binding at WNT7A specifically in T cells,46 and at GDF7 in B cells, matching ENCODE ChIP-seq data (Figure S4I).

  IDH-MUT cells have high 2-hydroxyglutarate levels that interfere with TET family 5′-methylcytosine hydroxylases,47 which catalyze a key step in DNA methylation removal.48 IDH-MUT cells exhibit hypermethylation at CTCF-binding sites in vitro, compromising binding.49,50 Reduced CTCF binding is associated with loss of insulation between topologically associating domains (TADs) and aberrant gene activation,49 although the significance of IDH-MUT-mediated alterations of epigenetic states remains unclear in vivo. We analyzed CTCF binding differences between primary IDH WT and MUT cells, focusing on MUT-enriched CD8 T cells (Figures 4B and 4H).

  CD8 T cells formed two distinct clusters differentially enriched for WT or MUT cells (Figures 4H and S4J). We compared CTCF D&D binding signals (C>T edit read counts) between IDH2 WT and MUT (IDH2R140Q). As edited reads are only detected in ATAC-seq fragments, a lower number of scATAC-seq fragments results in a lower editing signal (Spearman’s rho correlation = 0.66) (Figure S4E right). Therefore, we compared D&D edit counts after regressing out the effect of total ATAC fragment counts in CTCF peaks, which can confound the number of D&D edits. We fitted D&D edit counts and ATAC fragment counts to extract residuals as normalized edit counts. After we regressed out ATAC fragment counts, D&D edits of WT were significantly higher than those of MUT (Figure S4K), consistent with hypermethylation in IDH2-MUT cells interfering with CTCF binding.49 Most differentially bound CTCF-binding sites had decreased CTCF binding signal between MUT and WT cells (Figure 4I), corroborating reports suggesting that CTCF binding is reduced in IDH-MUT glioma and acute myeloid leukemia, mediated by DNA hypermethylation at CTCF-binding sites.49,50

  To evaluate the potential alterations in 3D genomic interactions between MUT and WT cells, we once again utilized the C.Origami algorithm51 to generate single-cell pseudobulk Hi-C genomic contact maps based on our CTCF D&D-seq profiles. At the GIT1 locus, which is involved in T cell function52–55 and showed the strongest reduction in CTCF binding, we observed a significant predicted alteration of 3D genome contacts in MUT-enriched T cells compared with WT-enriched T cells. WT cells formed structures resembling canonical TADs, while the interactions in MUT cells were more evenly distributed throughout the region (Figure 4J). Given that these predicted contact maps are model-based and not a direct measurement of 3D genome architecture, we next sought an orthogonal analysis to further assess whether MUT cells exhibit altered chromatin interactions. Specifically, we utilized the Cicero algorithm56 to assess regulatory element co-accessibility near perturbed CTCF elements. We expanded the GIT1 genomic window to include up- and downstream CTCF-binding sites bound by CTCF in both WT and MUT cells. Comparison of WT and MUT T cell co-accessible peaks showed substantially reshaped chromatin architecture in MUT T cells (Figures 4K and 4L) across four main accessible regions. For further visualization, we plotted smoothed co-accessibility scores of all the possible pairwise interactions of ATAC peaks in the GIT1 locus. In WT cells, these interactions appeared more well-defined, while co-accessibility patterns appeared more disorganized in MUT cells (Figures 4M and 4N). Additional interactions formed with an upstream peak within NUFIP257 locus in MUT T but not WT cells (Figures 4K–4N), suggesting formation of a new gene regulatory network upon loss of CTCF binding (Figure 4L). Observed co-accessibility periodicity in WT cells appears compromised in MUT cells, potentially reflecting a less-defined TAD organization due to CTCF binding and insulator function loss promoting more random interactions (Figure 4N).

  We observed the same behavior at the CNIH2 locus, where well-defined co-accessibility in WT cells became disorganized in MUT cells due to the loss of CTCF binding (Figures S4L–S4P), implying that IDH2 mutation-driven disruption of chromatin organization is not limited to a specific locus, aligning with in vitro acute CTCF depletion studies showing that CTCF is essential for looping between CTCF target sites and TAD insulation.58


### Mapping TF binding during erythroid differentiation
  GATA1 is a master TF that is essential for megakaryocytic-erythroid differentiation from hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs). GATA1 expression levels are tightly regulated during erythroid lineage commitment, reaching their highest levels in proerythroblasts, where GATA1 directly activates erythroid-associated genes, such as HBB and ALAS.59,60 To characterize the temporal dynamics of the GATA1 regulatory network during erythropoiesis, we performed single-cell multi-omics profiling integrated with D&D-seq to map GATA1:DNA interaction dynamics. Specifically, we performed ex vivo differentiation experiments of human CD34+ hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells and profiled cells along the erythropoietic trajectory at multiple time points (differentiation days 0, 5, 8, 18, and 24) for simultaneous assessment of transcriptome, chromatin accessibility, and GATA1 binding. After filtering out low-quality cells, the dataset exhibited an average of 1,429 (±1,034, standard deviation) unique genes per cell and contained a mean of 5,461 (±4,802, standard deviation) ATAC fragments per cell (Figure 5A). These values are consistent with standard gene expression and chromatin accessibility quality metrics reported for 10× Genomics single-cell multiome assays, indicating that the additional D&D-seq steps do not affect overall assay performance.

  We used transcriptome data to project cells into a low-dimensional space using UMAP and defined eight progenitor and differentiated cell types in erythroid and myeloid lineages based on marker gene expression (Figures 5B and 5C). GATA1 binding was defined using D&D edit signals in ATAC peaks harboring the GATA1 motif (Figure S5A) and showed strong enrichment in the erythroid lineage, particularly in proerythroblast (Proery) and early erythroid clusters (Figures 5D and 5E top). In contrast, GATA1 binding was largely absent across all cells in the myeloid populations (Figure S5A), consistent with lineage-specific regulation by alternative TFs such as PU.1 or IRF8 (Figure 5E bottom). Finally, to relate TF binding and downstream gene expression, we defined 48 putative GATA1 target genes that are closely located within a 10-kb window of GATA1 binding peaks harboring both motif and D&D edit signals (n = 48 peaks). Notably, a number of erythrocyte-related genes, such as SLC4A1 and RHAG, exhibited strong correlation and were located in proximal regions to GATA1 binding peaks (Table S2).61,62 Expression of GATA1 target genes showed high concordance with GATA1 binding patterns, exhibiting the highest overall expression in early erythroid cells (Figure 5F). Notably, GATA1 binding and target gene expression displayed partially decoupled temporal patterns across erythroid differentiation, highlighting the capacity of D&D-seq to capture dynamic TF:DNA interactions that are not readily inferred from RNA measurements alone.

  Collectively, these data demonstrate that D&D-seq enables scalable, single-cell-resolved interrogation of TF:DNA interactions within a multi-omic context, facilitating direct linkage between regulatory binding events and transcriptional output.


## DISCUSSION
  Single-cell mapping of chromatin factors has the potential to define gene network regulation directly within individual cells, but there are critical gaps in the single-cell toolkit in mapping TF and chromatin regulator binding across different cell types and biological contexts. To address these challenges, D&D-seq overcomes existing limitations and allows us to profile chromatin-associated factors across a range of affinities. D&D-seq integrates seamlessly into common single-cell workflows and can profile binding activity in a motif- and ChIP-independent manner.

  The flexibility of D&D-seq enables integration with other single-cell modalities, such as cellular indexing of transcriptomes and epitopes by sequencing (CITE-seq) or ATAC with select antigen profiling by sequencing (ASAP-seq) for surface protein expression63,64 or SHARE-seq, Paired-Tag, or combined assay of transcriptome and enriched chromatin binding (CoTECH) for transcriptomic measurements.19,65,66 Integrating D&D-seq with single-cell whole-genome amplification methods (e.g., PTA, DLP+, and DNA and expression following nucleosome depletion sequencing [DEFND-seq]) holds promise for genome-wide mapping of DNA-protein interactions.20,21,67 When implemented in a WGS-only configuration, D&D-seq enables cell clustering at the genotypic level (e.g., using shared single nucleotide variants [SNVs] or copy number variation [CNV] breakpoints to reconstruct clonal lineages), whereas phenotypic clustering can be achieved either through upfront cell sorting or by combining WGS-based D&D-seq with joint genome- and transcriptome-profiling approaches.

  As with other chromatin profiling assays, D&D-seq peak intensities should be interpreted cautiously. Importantly, the observed sparsity of TF binding signal is not unique to D&D-seq and likely reflects a combination of intrinsic TF biology (including transient or low-occupancy binding), antibody performance, and cell-to-cell heterogeneity, rather than a limitation of the D&D-seq chemistry itself. In particular, only the same genomic loci across samples are directly comparable, as the observed signal at a given site reflects a combination of factors, including local chromatin accessibility, antibody labeling efficiency, editing kinetics, and sequencing depth; consequently, a higher signal at one peak than another within a sample does not necessarily imply stronger or more frequent binding.

  In conclusion, D&D-seq represents a versatile approach for multi-omics chromatin profiling, capable of measuring genome-wide distribution of non-histone chromatin-binding factors in bulk and single-cell samples to provide biological insights into DNA-protein interactions.


### Limitations of the study
  A key limitation of D&D-seq is the relatively low number of editing events per cell. Although our method allows the first demonstration of capturing DNA-protein interactions in single cells, the lower per-cell resolution makes it less suitable for studying individual cells with high granularity. The number of mapped binding events per cell may arise from antibody efficiency, circumvented in bulk methodology by using thousands to millions of cells as input. Despite this current limitation, D&D-seq excels in analyses that aggregate data across cells, such as pseudobulking or meta cells,68 which enable the discovery of complex biological patterns that were previously inaccessible. For example, the single-cell application allowed us to characterize a small subpopulation of CD8 T cells enriched in IDH2-MUT cells in a primary CHIP sample. These cells exhibited a global reduction in CTCF binding, which perturbed chromatin architecture and reshaped the co-accessibility of specific TADs. However, we envision that future developments could further enhance the number of events per cell. For example, employing base editors with enhanced catalytic efficiency and minimal sequence-context preference will likely increase both the number and diversity of detectable binding events, thereby improving sensitivity and reducing dropout rates at low-abundance targets.69 Additionally, incorporating a secondary antibody staining step could boost the signal by increasing the local concentration of the base editor, allowing for a more comprehensive capture of DNA-protein interactions. As a preliminary test, we have shown that adding secondary antibodies increased the signal in bulk D&D-seq targeting CTCF and GATA1 (Figures S5B–S5E). Specifically, we used rabbit primary antibodies for CTCF or GATA1 as a control and then compared this to a reaction using the same primary antibodies with the addition of anti-Rb mouse secondary antibodies, in this case using both anti-mouse and anti-rabbit nanobodies for D&D genomic targeting. For fair comparison of D&D footprint signals, reads were downsampled to ~40 million or ~50 million reads for CTCF and GATA1 samples, respectively, and footprint analysis was performed on the peaks found in both conditions (CTCF = 10,462 peaks, GATA1 = 9,977 peaks; Figures S5B and S5C). Incorporating secondary antibodies boosted the overall D&D edit signals about 3-fold (CTCF = 3.06×, GATA1 = 2.88× on average), while preserving the same footprint shape surrounding the motif sequence (Figures S5B–S5E). Importantly, we also found that this increased signal facilitated the recovery of weaker or noncanonical CTCF motifs (Figure S5F). When secondary antibodies were used, a greater fraction of recovered peaks corresponded to lower-scoring CTCF motifs, suggesting that enhanced local concentration of the enzyme complex improves the detection of low-affinity or suboptimal binding events. Specifically, although the core CTCF motif is still maintained in the 3,155 CTCF secondary antibody-specific peaks (Figure S5F top), the motif features in the secondary antibody-specific peaks (e.g., T/A instead of G/C at motif position 9; Figure S5F bottom) more closely align with inaccessible CTCF-binding sites in humans, where CTCF binding is weaker than in accessible regions.70 We believe that this improvement arises from increased editing efficiency at these weaker sites, which would otherwise fall below the detection threshold. We anticipate that using secondary antibodies would be especially useful for more weakly or transiently binding proteins. These enhancements would further broaden the scope of D&D-seq, enabling its application to even more detailed and high-resolution studies at the single-cell level.

### METHOD DETAILS

#### Differentiation of human HSPCs
  Cells were thawed and cultured in StemSpan SFEM II medium (Stemcell Technologies, 09655), supplemented with StemSpan CD34+ Expansion Supplement (10X) (Stemcell Technologies, 02691). After 2 days of culture, the cells were transferred to a three-phase erythroid culture medium to initiate erythroid differentiation as previously described by Grevet et al.86

  Briefly, cells were maintained at a concentration of 200,000–1,000,000 cells/mL, and the medium was changed every 2 days to keep the cell concentration constant. Phase I (day 0–8): IMDM (MilliporeSigma, FG0465) supplemented with 3% human AB serum (Sigma-Aldrich, H4522), 2% fetal bovine serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 10–437-028), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Life Technologies, 15140–122), 10 μg/mL insulin (Sigma-Aldrich, 9278), 3 IU/mL heparin (Sigma-Aldrich, H3149), 200 μg/mL holo-transferrin (Sigma-Aldrich, T0665), 10 ng/mL stem cell factor (SCF) (Peprotech, 300–07), 3 IU/mL erythropoietin (EPO) (Stemcell Tech, 78007) and 5 ng/mL IL-3 (Peprotech, 200–03). Phase II (days 8–13): IL-3 was removed from the medium. Phase III (days 14–21): IL-3 and SCF were removed, and holo-transferrin concentration was adjusted to 1mg/mL. Cells were collected at different timepoints (0, 5, 8, 18, and 24 days post differentiation) for the single-cell multiome experiment.


#### Cloning of nb-DddA constructs
  Previously published sequences coding for the DddA11 enzyme were split at position 1397, and the N terminus of DddA11 (1290–1397) was synthesized as a gene fragment (Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT), Table S3) flanked by restriction enzyme sites EcoRI and SpeI. We chose the split site with the longest N terminus and linked the nanobodies with the N terminal protein (nb–DddA_NT) so that the C terminal peptide (DddA_CT) is 25 amino acids long (Figure 1B), short enough to be synthesized. We truncated the last 5 amino acids of DddA_CT in the original construct because it is a structurally disordered region (PDB 6u08) that is functionally redundant.31 The gene fragments were digested with EcoRI and SpeI for 1 hour at 37°C, ligated for 1 hour at room temperature with pTXB1-alnbOc-Tn5 (Addgene, 184285), pTXB1-nbMmKappa-Tn5 (Addgene, 184286), or 3×Flag-pA-Tn5-Fl (Addgene, 124601) digested with the same enzymes. The ligated product was transformed into competent cells (New England Biolabs, C2987) per the manufacturer’s instruction. The final plasmid products (pTXB1-nb-DddA_NT) were confirmed by Sanger sequencing (Eton Bio) or Whole Plasmid Sequencing using Oxford Nanopore Technology with custom analysis and annotation (Plasmidsaurus).


#### nb-DddA production
  The pTXB1-nb-DddA_NT vectors were transformed into BL21(DE3)-competent Escherichia coli cells (NEB, C2527), and nb-DddA_NT was produced via intein purification with an affinity chitin-binding tag.25,87 First, transformed bacterial colonies were grown overnight in 5 mL Luria broth (LB). Next day, 5 mL of LB culture was added to 400 mL and grown at 37°C to optical density (OD600) = 0.6. nb-DddA expression was induced with 0.5 mM isopropyl-ß-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) at 30°C for 4 hours. After induction, cells were pelleted and then frozen at −80°C overnight. Cells were then lysed by sonication in 30 mL HEGX (20 mM HEPES-KOH pH 7.5, 0.8 M NaCl, 10% glycerol, 0.2% Triton X-100) with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, 04693132001). The lysate was pelleted at 10,000g for 20 minutes at 4°C. The supernatant was transferred to a new tube, and 600 μL of neutralized 10% polyethyleneimine (Sigma-Aldrich, P3143) was added dropwise to the bacterial extract, gently mixed and centrifuged at 12,000g for 40 minutes at 4°C to precipitate DNA. The supernatant was loaded on a 2-mL chitin column (NEB, S6651S), followed by washing with 12 mL of HEGX. Then, 3 mL of HEGX containing 100 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) was added to the column with incubation for 48 hours at 4°C to allow cleavage of nb-DddA from the intein tag. After incubation, 2 mL HEGX was added to elute nb-DddA directly into a 10-kDa molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) spin column (Thermo Scientific, 88527). Protein was dialyzed three times using 15 mL of 2× dialysis buffer (100 HEPES-KOH pH 7.2, 0.2 M NaCl, 0.2 mM EDTA, 2 mM DTT, 20% glycerol) and concentrated to 0.5–1 mL by centrifugation at 5,000g. The protein concentrate was transferred to a new tube, mixed with an equal volume of 100% glycerol, and stored at −20°C. After purification, the protein was denatured at 95°C for 5 minutes, analyzed by SDS-PAGE gel (BIORAD, 4561085) and imaged by BIORAD ChemiDoc Touch Imaging System (Figure S1B).


#### Deamination assay
  DNA substrates, including lambda phage DNA (NEB, N3011) or 5′ 6-FAM-labeled 30-mer dsDNA oligonucleotides (IDT, Table S3) were used for testing nb-DddA deamination activity. Deamination reactions of 250–1000 ng DNA were performed in 50 μL of deamination buffer (40 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 50 mM KCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, 20 μM ZnCl2) with 50 μM nb-DddA_NT and 100 μM DddA_CT (Eton Bio, HPLC-purified, purity >95%, Table S3). The reactions were incubated at 37°C for 1 hour unless otherwise indicated. For lambda phage DNA, the products were then purified with 1.6X Ampure beads (Beckman Coulter, A63881), treated with 1 μL of T7 endonuclease (NEB, M0302) in 30 μL T7 buffer, further incubated at 37°C for 15 minutes per the manufacturer’s instructions, and analyzed by an agarose gel (Invitrogen, A42135). For 6-FAM-labeled dsDNA oligonucleotides, 1 μL USER enzyme (NEB, M5508) was added to the deaminase-treated products, incubated at 37°C for 1 hour, denatured at 95°C for 5 minutes, and analyzed by 15% polyacrylamide TBE-Urea gel (BIORAD, 4566055). The cleaved DNA fragments were imaged by BIORAD ChemiDoc Touch Imaging System.


#### Cell fixation and antibody binding
  For all the following centrifugations, cells were centrifuged in a swing-bucket centrifuge at 300g for 5 minutes before fixation and 600g for 5 minutes after fixation. About 200K to 1 million cells were collected and resuspended in 400 μL PBS. Then, 16% methanol-free formaldehyde (Thermo Fisher Scientific, PI28906) was added for fixation (final concentration 0.1%) at room temperature for 5 minutes. Glycine (final concentration 125 mM) was added to stop the cross-linking, followed by a wash with 1 mL of PBS. Fixed cells were permeabilized in 200 μL permeabilization buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 0.1% NP40, 0.1% Tween-20, 1% BSA, 1× protease inhibitors) on ice (7 minutes for cell lines, 5 minutes for primary cells), followed by a wash with 200 μL cold wash buffer (20 mM HEPES pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM spermidine, 1% BSA, 1× protease inhibitors). After cell counting, 100K-400K cells were transferred to PCR tubes and resuspended in 100 μL antibody buffer (20 mM HEPES pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM spermidine, 1% BSA, 1× protease inhibitors), with 1 μL (1:100 dilution) of the antibodies. The cells were incubated at 4°C with slow rotation overnight. For optional secondary antibody staining, cells were washed once with 200 μL wash buffer, resuspended in 100 μL antibody buffer containing 1 μL secondary antibody, and incubated at room temperature with slow rotation for 1 hour.


#### D&D binding and activation
  The cells were washed once with 200 μL wash buffer, and another time with 200 μL D&D binding buffer (Table S3). The cells were resuspended in 50 μL D&D binding buffer (Table S3) containing 1–5 μL (10–50 μM) nb–DddA_NT, and incubated at room temperature with slow rotation for 1 hour. The cells were then washed with 200 μL D&D binding buffer, resuspended in 50 μL D&D activation buffer containing 100 μM DddA_CT (Table S3), and incubated at 37°C for 1 hour for deamination.


#### Bulk ATAC
  Tn5 adaptors were purchased from IDT. Adaptors (100 μM) (Table S3) were annealed in TE buffer to form mosaic-end, double-stranded (MEDS) oligos by incubating at 95°C for 5 minutes and then cooling at 0.2°C per second to 12°C. MEDS-A and MEDA-B were mixed 1:1, and 2 μL was transferred to a new tube and mixed with 18 μL of TnY (in-house) enzyme after 1 hour at room temperature to allow for transposome assembly.88

  After D&D activation, the cells were washed once with 200 μL 1x Tris-TD buffer and resuspended in 50 μL 1x Tris-TD buffer with 9 μL loaded TnY (TnY volume depends on its concentration and needs to be titrated to get the optimal tagmentation). To initiate tagmentation, the reaction was incubated at 37°C for 1 hour. To extract DNA, 1 μL 10% SDS, 2 μL proteinase K (NEB, P8107S), and 3 μL 0.5M EDTA were added to the reactions, which were then incubated at 55°C for 1 hour, followed by column-based DNA purification per the manufacturer’s instruction (Zymo, D5205). For library preparation PCR, we used uracil-tolerant DNA polymerase (NEBNext Q5U Master Mix, NEB M0597S) and Nextera-compatible indexing primers (Table S3) to amplify the purified DNA. To increase the efficiency of the initial gap fill-in step, we spiked in 1 μL of non-hot-start polymerase Bst 3.0 (NEB, M0374). The PCR steps are: 72°C for 5 min; 98°C for 30 s; 12 cycles of 98°C for 10 s, 55°C for 30 s and 72°C for 30 s; followed by 72°C for 5 min.


#### Bulk PTA
  Bulk PTA was performed using the ResolveDNA Whole Genome Amplification Kit (BioSkryb Genomics, 100136) per the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, after D&D activation, cell lysis was performed to denature DNA, followed by PTA amplification and DNA Cleanup. WGS libraries were generated using KAPA HiFi HotStart Library Amp Kit (Roche, 07958960001). Libraries were sequenced to 3–5x genome coverage (paired-end 100bp).


#### Single-cell ATAC
  After D&D activation, the cells were resuspended in 1x nuclei buffer and counted using trypan blue and a Countess II FL Automated Cell Counter. For the remaining steps, we follow single-cell ATAC-seq per 10x protocol (version CG000209 Rev F, 10x Genomics) with the following modification: during the GEM generation and barcoding reaction (step 2.1), 2 μL of uracil-tolerant DNA polymerase (NEBNext Q5U Master Mix, NEB M0597S) was added to the barcoding reaction mixture to facilitate the first few rounds of PCR, where we expect to have some uridine in the reaction.


#### Single-cell Multiome
  Protector RNase inhibitor (Roche, 3335402001) was added to all buffers to prevent RNA degradation. After D&D activation, the cells were resuspended in 1x nuclei buffer and counted using trypan blue and a Countess II FL Automated Cell Counter. For the remaining steps, we follow single-cell Multiome per 10x Genomics protocol (version CG000338 Rev G, 10x Genomics) with the following modification: during Pre-Amplification PCR (step 4.2), 2 μL of uracil-tolerant DNA polymerase (NEBNext Q5U Master Mix, NEB M0597S) was added to the barcoding reaction mixture.


#### Sequencing
  The sequencing libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq6000, NovaSeqX Plus, or NextSeq2000 with dual indexed, paired-end 100 or 150-bp settings. Specifically, i5: 8 bp (16 bp for single-cell sequencing), i7: 8 bp, read1: 100 or 150 bp, read2: 100 or 150 bp.


#### GoT–ChA
  We performed GoT–ChA according to the previously published paper with minor modifications.22 Briefly, we performed whole cell fixation, permeabilization, antibody incubation, D&D binding and activation as aforementioned bulk D&D-seq. After D&D activation, the cells were resuspended in 1× diluted nucleus buffer (10x Genomics) and counted using trypan blue and a Countess II FL Automated Cell Counter. Afterwards, the cells were processed according to the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell ATAC Solution user guide (version CG000209 Rev F, 10x Genomics) with the following modifications:

  During the GEM generation and barcoding reaction (step 2.1), 1 μL of 22.5 μM GoT–ChA primer mix was added to the barcoding reaction mixture. The primers used are IDH2 locus-specific primers IDH2_R140_F1 (or IDH2_R140_F2) and IDH2_R140_R1 (or IDH2_R140_R2) (Table S3). These primers allow for exponential amplification of the GoT–ChA fragments relative to the linear amplification of ATAC fragments. In addition, to facilitate the first few rounds of PCR, where we expect to have some uridine in the reaction, we spiked in 2 μL of uracil-tolerant DNA polymerase (NEBNext Q5U Master Mix, NEB M0597S) into the barcoding reaction mixture.

  During the post-GEM incubation clean-up (step 3.2), 45.5 μL of elution solution I is used to elute material from SPRIselect beads. A total of 5 μL is used for GoT–ChA library construction, and the remaining 40 μL is used for ATAC library construction as indicated in the standard protocol.

  To generate the GoT–ChA library, two additional PCRs were performed on the 5 μL set aside during step 3.2. The first PCR aims to amplify genotyping fragments before sample indexing and uses P5 and IDH2_R140_N1 (or IDH2_R140_N2) primers (Table S3) with the following thermocycler program: 95°C for 3 min; 15 cycles of 95°C for 20 s, 65°C for 30 s and 72°C for 20 s; followed by 72°C for 5 min and ending with hold at 4°C. After a 1.2× SPRIselect clean-up, biotinylated PCR product is bound and isolated using Dynabeads M-280 streptavidin magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 11206D). In brief, the beads are washed three times with 1× sodium chloride sodium phosphate-EDTA buffer (SSPE, VWR, VWRV0810–4L), added to the purified PCR product and incubated at room temperature for 15 minutes. The beads are then washed twice with 1× SSPE buffer and once with 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) before resuspending in water. The bead-bound fragments are then amplified and sample indexed using P5 and RPI-X primers (Table S3) with the following thermocycler program: 95°C for 3 min; 6–10 cycles of 95°C for 20 s, 65°C for 30 s and 72°C for 20 s; followed by 72°C for 5 min and ending with hold at 4°C.

  Final libraries were quantified using the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Q32854) and the High Sensitivity DNA chip (Agilent Technologies, 5067–4626) run on a Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies) and sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 or NovaSeq X system at the Weill Cornell Medicine Genomics Resources Core Facility with the following parameters: paired-end 100 or 150 cycles; read 1N, 100 or 150 cycles; i7 index, 8 cycles; i5 index, 16 cycles; read 2N, 100 or 150 cycles. ATAC libraries were sequenced to a depth of 25,000–35,000 read pairs per cell and GoT–ChA libraries were sequenced to 5,000 read pairs per cell. A list of the primer sequences used in this study is provided in Table S3.


#### Cell sorting and genotyping
  Cryopreserved peripheral blood mononuclear cells from a CHIP donor with IDH2R140Q mutation were thawed and stained. Briefly, the cells were resuspended in staining buffer (BioLegend, 420201) and incubated with Human TruStain FcX (10 min at 4°C; BioLegend, 422302) to block Fc receptor-mediated binding. Then, the cells were stained with CD8-BV650 (Biolegend, 344730), CD4-FITC (Biolegend, 344604), CD19-PE (BD biosciences, 561741), CD14-APC (Thermo Fisher, 47–0149-41), CD56-BV786 (BD biosciences, 564058) (1:100 for up to 106 cells in a final volume of 100 μL) for 20 minutes at 4°C, and DAPI (Invitrogen, D1306). The samples were then sorted for DAPI− and single-marker positive cells (CD8 for CD8+ T cells, CD4 for CD4+ T cells, CD19 for B cells, CD14 for monocytes, CD56 for NK cells) using the BD FACSymphony S6 Cell Sorter (Data S2). The genomic DNA of the sorted cells was extracted using Puregene cell kit (QIAGEN, 158043) per the manufacturer’s instruction, and PCR-amplified using KAPA 2X mix (Roche, 07958927001) and IDH2_R140_F2 + IDH2_R140_R2 (Table S3) primers. The linear PCR amplicon sequencing was performed by Plasmidsaurus using Oxford Nanopore Technology with custom analysis, and the genotype ratio was quantified by the number of reads mapped to IDH2 wild type and IDH2R140Q.


### QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

#### Preprocessing of bulk ATAC-seq data
  Nextera transposase sequence and homopolymer G were removed using CutAdapt with “CTGTCTCTTATACACATCTCCGAGCCCACGAGAC” for R1 and “CTGTCTCTTATACACATCTGACGCTGCCGACGA” for R2 and “G{12}” parameters whenever identified in the FastQC report.71 After trimming adapter and polyG sequences, processed FASTQ files were re-analyzed using FastQC and aligned to the Human reference genome (GRCh38) using BWA-MEM2.72 Read alignments were subsequently sorted and indexed using samtools. To call D&D edits in bulk datasets, bam files were analyzed as described in the following Extraction of D&D signal section.

  For TF motif discovery, genomic regions of 400 bp to 1 kb (indicated in the figures) centered at the peak summits were used to query the genome using MEME.77 To visualize the genomic tracks on IGV, bigwig files were generated using the DeepTools bamCoverage function with the –normalizeUsing BPM option set.76 ChIP-seq peak coordinates for CTCF, GATA1 and GATA2 for K562 cells were downloaded from ENCODE.33


#### Copy number alteration analysis
  Bam file was transformed to bed format using bedtools bamtobed with default parameters. The copy number was predicted by binning read counts into 500 kb non-overlapping, variable-width windows using Ginkgo.79 Briefly, genome data were built using the hg38 human reference genome, and a config file for the analysis was generated with following modifications from the provided config file, binMeth=variable_500000_101_bwa and chosen_genome=hg38. Copy number plot was generated by running analyze.sh script with the bed and config files. K562 copy number profile reflects the female origin of the cell line, showing two copies of the X chromosome and absence of the Y chromosome. Most autosomes display copy number gains (red) consistent with a triploid state, while some regions exhibit tetraploidy (e.g., chromosome 1q). In contrast, certain chromosomes retain the cell line’s germline ploidy, such as chromosomes 13 and 14, in agreement with previously reported karyotypes.89


#### Preprocessing 10x scATAC-seq data
  Raw scATAC-seq datasets were aligned to the Human reference genome (GRCh38) and quantified using CellRanger-ATAC (version 2.1.0). The resulting peak/cell matrix was filtered for low quality cells and normalized using Signac (version 1.13.0).80 In detail, cells having 1) reads aligned to the blacklist region more than 0.05%, 2) less than 20% of reads aligned to peaks, 3) transcription start site enrichment score less than 3, 4) nucleosome signal higher than 4, 5) outliers having too many (average of peak counts + 2*standard deviation of peak counts) or fewer peaks (average of peak counts – 1*standard deviation of peak counts) were filtered out (Figures S2A, S3A, and S4B). Multiplets were additionally annotated using AMULET and removed.81 The preprocessed data were normalized by latent semantic indexing analysis, projected to low dimensions UMAP, and clustered using the smart local moving (SLM) algorithm. Gene activity based on chromatin accessibility was inferred in order to annotate markers of each cluster. Finally, motif activity score was computed using ChromVAR with the JASPAR2020 core database for Homo sapiens.82,90

  For the cell line data, eight clusters were defined with a clustering resolution of 0.6, and annotated as one of two cell lines based on the marker gene activity. Cluster 7, which was found in both K562 and CA46 clusters, was removed. The remaining number of cells was 5,840 cells (CA46, n = 4,108 and K562, n = 1,732). For PBMC data, 18 and 16 clusters were identified with a clustering resolution of 0.8 in replicate 1 and 2, respectively. Clusters were annotated based on marker gene activity and cell type prediction using Azimuth with a Human PBMC reference dataset.26,91 For replicate 1, two clusters were additionally removed because two different cell types were mixed (cluster 7, CD8 T cell and monocyte) or it had overall low quality and lacked markers (cluster 13). The total of 5,413 cells in replicate 1 and 10,394 cells in replicate 2 remained.


#### Cell number downsampling analysis
  While scD&D-seq captures TF binding with high specificity at low cell numbers, sparsity of scATAC-seq data and base editing signal can impact global detection of aggregated footprint signals. We benchmarked the number of cells required to detect aggregated footprint signals, downsampling the cell line dataset and analyzing different subsets, from 50–4,000 cells for CA46 or 1,500 cells for K562, with 100 replicates. The number of peaks called in each downsample set was associated with D&D edit recovery. On average, at 250 cells, 217.9 (CA46) and 127.9 (K562) peaks were identified with corresponding motifs and D&D edits, where we observed visible footprint signals (Data S1). Similarly, when all ATAC peaks with C>T conversions were analyzed, the corresponding binding motifs (CTCF or GATA family) were only recovered with significant E-value in ≥250 cells (Data S1). To evaluate peaks recovered in downsampling analysis, we calculated the recall of D&D target peaks. Average recall values reached 0.81 (4,000 CA46 cells) and 0.75 (1,500 K562 cells) (Data S1). Reads supporting the D&D target peak became depleted at <250 cells in both CTCF and GATA1 (Data S1). Footprint signals were clear with 250 cells but disappeared below 100 cells due to limited peak identification (Data S1). Thus, robust aggregate footprint signals emerge with sample sizes of ≥250 cells, whereas signal detection becomes limited below this threshold due to reduced numbers of confidently called peaks (Data S1).


#### GoT–ChA genotyping of IDH2 mutation
  Raw FASTQ files were first analyzed using FastQC to examine overall data quality and mutant allele at the targeted locus.92 Based on a FastQC report, position of mutant allele, a read file with mutant allele, and primer sequence were identified. Input FASTQ files were then processed using the GoT–ChA analysis pipeline (https://github.com/landau-lab/Gotcha).22 In detail, raw data were first split into smaller files and quality filtering was performed with the “FastqFiltering” function. The R1 FASTQ file for replicate 1 and R3 FASTQ file for replicate 2 were used for genotyping with c(37:39) as a mutation site. After quality control of raw data, mutation state is annotated with the “BatchMutationCalling” function based on the provided wild-type/mutant and primer sequences. For both replicates, the primer sequence was set as “GATGGGCTCCCGGAAGACAGTCCCCCCCAGGATGTT”, and “CCG” and “CTG” were set as wild-type and mutant sequences, respectively. Results from each split data were merged into a single barcode and read count matrix using “MergeMutationCalling”. Genotype of each cell was assigned by manually setting thresholds based on distribution of log-transformed read counts (Figure S4D).


#### Extraction of D&D signal
  To summarize genome edits introduced by D&D in scATAC-seq data, a bam file produced by CellRanger was split into each cell type based on barcode sequence and cell annotation using sinto. For bulk ATAC-seq data, bam files aligned to the genome using BWA-MEM2 were analyzed.72 After splitting bam files, the following five steps were performed to analyze D&D-mediated genomic variants (Figure S5G). All steps are integrated in the D&D analytic pipeline.

  The final results produced by the D&D analytic pipeline include 1) D&D edits in vcf, 2) motif centered D&D target peaks in bed, and 3) ATAC reads with D&D edits in bam format, for users to freely explore and visualize the edit sites. To visualize D&D signals, QNAMES (read id) of reads with D&D edits were extracted from alignment files (bam) using pysam. Briefly, D&D variants and positions are searched in individual reads and QNAMES of reads with matching variant status and position were extracted and saved in a text file. Edited reads were then extracted from the read alignment using samtools with view -N <qnames.txt>. D&D bam was then converted into bigwig using bamCoverage from the DeepTools package with bin size 50bp. Bigwig files were visualized using the trackplot package in R. Extraction of D&D reads is also integrated in the D&D edit calling pipeline.


#### Evaluation of D&D signal
  To compare D&D edit counts between target and background regions, edit counts per peak were summarized and signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) were calculated by dividing the number of C-to-T and G-to-A by the number of other variants. First, edits per peak were calculated by dividing SNV counts by the number of peaks in resized target and background peak regions. We assume that the frequencies of non-D&D edit (edits other than C-to-T) are consistent across the target and background regions, so this can be used to normalize the D&D edit counts. In this manner, SNR was then calculated by dividing the D&D edit counts by the mean of non-D&D edit counts.

  Footprint analysis for D&D edits was performed by counting the number of D&D edits in each base pair from randomly sampled target and background peaks, +−100 bp from the center of the motifs. The random sampling was repeated for 10 times and mean and standard deviation were used for visualization. For both the cell mixing experiment and primary blood cells, we used 200 randomly selected peaks. The number of subsampled peaks can be set by the user.


#### Benchmark analysis with uliCUT&RUN
  Raw uliCUT&RUN data for CTCF and negative controls with no primary antibody were downloaded from GEO (GEO: GSE111121).40 The adapter sequence in both reads was trimmed using CutAdapt71 with -a AGATCGGAAGAG -A AGATCGGAAGAG -m 21 parameters. Trimmed reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome (mm10) using BWA-MEM2 with 10 as the minimum seed length (-k 10).72 Read alignments with high mapping quality Phred score (>= 20), primary alignment, reads aligned to intact chromosomes, and those with properly aligned mates were retained from the resulting bam files using samtools (version 1.19). The processed bed file of CTCF ChIP-seq was downloaded from an independent study which used the same cell line (E14 mouse embryonic stem cells, GEO: GSE11431).94 Because the bed file was aligned to mm8, the genomic coordination was updated to mm10 using LiftOver,84 sorted and merged for overlapping coordination using bedtools,74 and modified to SAF format to use it as a custom reference for alignment. Processed bam files were aligned to the SAF using FeatureCounts (Subread package version 2.0.4) with -p –countReadPairs -F SAF parameters.95 Fraction of fragments in peaks was calculated by dividing the assigned fragment counts by the total fragment counts.

  The total of 4,105 CA46 cells with D&D edits from the cell mixing experiment were sampled to 10, 50, 500, and 5,000 cells with replacement and repeated for 100 times. K562 cells with D&D edits (n = 1,290) were downsampled to 10, 50, 500, and 1,000 cells in the same manner. To compare D&D to uliCUT&RUN, all reads harboring C-to-T or G-to-A tentative D&D edits with matched downsampled cell barcodes were collected. The fraction of fragments in peaks was calculated as described above using K562 CTCF ChIP-seq from ENCODE.


#### Target prediction linear regression model
  For each D&D-seq experiment, ATAC peaks were annotated for the class label using ENCODE ChIP-seq peaks with a matching cell line and a transcription factor. ATAC peaks overlapped with ChIP-seq peaks were annotated as on-target peaks (class label = 1) and the remaining peaks were annotated as off-target peaks (class label = 0). To characterize peaks using D&D edit features, following variables were summarized for each peak: total C>T conversion counts, maximum C>T conversion counts in a 100bp sliding window, C>T conversion counts per read, proportion of reads with C>T conversions, proportion of converted cytosines, binary indicator of C>T conversions, robust Z-score of C>T conversion counts, and percentile rank of robust Z-score. Total C>T conversion counts and maximum C>T conversion counts in the 100bp sliding window were log-transformed with a pseudocount of 1. These features were fitted to predict the class label with interaction between features using 10-fold cross-validation on a linear regression model using glmnet package in R differing alpha parameters from 0.0 to 1.0. Train and test datasets were generated by splitting the five integrated bulk D&D-seq data with strong D&D edit signals (one CTCF sample in Figures 1E and 1H and two samples each for CTCF and GATA1 from the primary antibody vs with the secondary antibody experiment in Figures S5B and S5C) into a 70:30 ratio. The remaining bulk and single-cell D&D-seq datasets were tested as an unseen validation set. The decision threshold of the model was selected using Youden’s index, and the same threshold was used to measure the performance in the validation set. We chose to use alpha parameter 1.0 (Lasso regression) which showed the best AUC and specificity. Detailed model performance and features are summarized in Table S1.


#### CTCF binding in IDH2 wild-type immune cells
  To summarize cell type-specific CTCF binding sites, cells annotated as wild-type using GoT-ChA genotyping were retrieved because IDH2-mutant cells showed significantly decreased D&D edits which may skew the analysis. CD4 T cells and CD8 T cells were integrated to form a collective T cell group due to low variability. Using CTCF D&D edit counts, differential binding analysis was performed across cell type clusters using FindMarker method in Seurat with following parameters, only.pos = T, min.pc = 0.0001, logfc.threshold = 0.01, method = “LR”. Uniquely edited sites but did not reach statistical significance in each cell type were additionally included. True CTCF binding sites were collected by analyzing publicly available ENCODE CTCF ChIP-seq data generated from sorted immune cells with three technical replicates (monocyte = GEO: GSE209089, NK = GEO: GSE209376, B cell = GEO: GSE208512, CD4 T cell = GEO: GSE209390, and CD8 T cell = GEO: GSE208887). Raw fastq files were downloaded from GEO and aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using BWA-MEM2 as described in the bulk data analysis. Peak calling was performed using MACS2 and blacklist regions were excluded. Replicate 2 for CD8 T cell and replicate 1 for CD4 T cell were excluded from the analysis due to low sequencing depth (4~5 million reads compared to 30~40 million reads in other replicates), low mapping rates (76.60% and 70.74%, respectively) and low number of called peaks. Differential ChIP-seq peaks were defined using DiffBind. Each cell type clustered well with each other identifying a large number of cell type-specific CTCF ChIP-seq peaks (B cell = 775, T cell = 1467, monocyte = 5354, NK = 4984 ChIP-seq peaks). Differential CTCF binding sites from D&D-seq were validated by comparing to differential CTCF ChIP-seq peaks identifying a total of 66 CTCF sites (Figure S4H).


#### Evaluation of CTCF binding in CD8 T cells
  In our integrated PBMC dataset, CD8 and CD4 T cells formed a continuous cluster without a clear separation (Figure 4A). Because the IDH2 mutation in these samples was specifically enriched in the CD8 T cell population (Figures 4B–4D), we re-clustered CD4 and CD8 T cell populations to extract highly confident CD8 T cells and removed clusters expressing CD4 T cell or non-T cell markers and mixed CD4 and CD8 T cells. The remaining CD8 T cell cluster (n = 7,546) consisted of 2,132 (534 WT, 329 MUT, and 1,269 not genotyped) cells from replicate 1 and 5,414 (853 WT, 486 MUT, and 4,075 not genotyped) cells from replicate 2. These cells were further grouped into MUT-enriched (n = 6,437 cells) and WT-enriched (n = 1,109 cells) based on the clustering and genotype proportions (Figure 4H). To compare differential CTCF binding between MUT and WT CD8 T cells, we leveraged the linear mixture model (LMM) from the GoT-ChA analytic pipeline which considers sample-specific batch effect and variability in cell numbers. LMM analysis was applied to 1,102 CTCF peaks harboring more than 5 D&D edits and found in more than 5 cells. We used a permissive false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of FDR ≤ 0.25, which identified 104 CTCF peaks with differential CTCF bindings between MUT and WT (Figure 4I).

  Co-accessible peaks were identified using Cicero.46 First, ATAC peaks in two replicates were merged using GenomicRanges::reduce and new count matrices were generated using Signac workflow for both replicates. Count data were loaded in R using the Monocle 3 workflow. Batch effect was normalized by aligning two replicates using align_cds with preprocess_method = “LSI”, alignment_group = “Dataset” and reduce_dimension with preprocess_method = “Aligned” parameters. Cicero object was generated with batch corrected CellDataSet and UMAP coordinates from Monocle 3 and co-accessible peaks were inferred using the run_cicero function as described in the Cicero workflow (https://cole-trapnell-lab.github.io/cicero-release/).


#### Preprocessing of 10X Multiome data
  Raw scATAC-seq datasets were aligned to the Human reference genome (GRCh38) and quantified using CellRanger-ARC (version 2.0.2). The resulting gene/cell and peak/cell matrices were filtered to remove low-quality cells and subsequently normalized using Seurat (version 5.0.3)91 and Signac (version 1.13.0).80 In detail, cells having 1) fewer than 200 expressed genes, 2) outliers in RNA data with excessively high UMI counts (average of UMI counts + 2*standard deviation of UMI counts), 3) mitochondrial gene expression exceeding 10% of total gene expression, 4) more than 0.1% of reads aligned to blacklist regions, 5) transcription start site enrichment score below 1, 6) nucleosome signal higher than 4, 7) outliers in ATAC data having excessively high peak counts (average of peak counts + 2*standard deviation of peak counts) were filtered out. After preprocessing, gene expression data were normalized using the “LogNormalize” parameter and scaled while regressing out gene counts. The number of principal components (n = 12, P <= 0.001) was determined by jackstraw analysis, projected to low dimensions using UMAP, and clustered using the shared nearest neighbor algorithm. A total of 1,291 cells were clustered into ten clusters at a resolution of 0.8 and annotated based on marker gene expression. Monocle 3 was used to infer differentiation trajectories, and cells were ordered in pseudotime using the hematopoietic stem cell cluster as the root node.85 The inferred pseudotime scores were extracted from the Monocle object and integrated into the Seurat metadata. For lineage-specific footprinting and trajectory analyses, cell types were grouped into distinct lineages; proerythroblast, early erythroid, and late erythroid clusters were grouped as the erythroid lineage; granulocyte-monocyte progenitor and monocyte/dendritic cell clusters were grouped as the myeloid lineage. GATA1 target genes were identified using the LinkPeaks method to summarize correlations between gene expression and chromatin accessibility of each peak within a 10kb distance parameter. Peaks harboring the GATA1 motif were annotated using the AddMotifs method with the JASPAR2020 core database for Homo sapiens and filtered to retain those linked to gene expression. These peaks were overlaid with D&D signals for GATA1 binding. Total of 48 putative target genes linked to 48 GATA1-bound peaks were identified (Table S2), and a module score was calculated using the AddModuleScore method.


#### Hi-C contact map predictions
  To generate Hi-C contact map predictions, we used the pseudobulk ATAC reads and D&D reads for each cell type as the input for C.Origami.51 The bam files of ATAC-seq and D&D reads were first converted to bigwig files using DeepTools76 with the command bamCoverage –normalizeUsing RPKM –binSize 1 –bam $bamfile -o $bigwig. The bigwig file for D&D reads was then normalized against the bigwig of the ATAC and log2-transformed with a pseudocount of 1 using the command bigwigCompare –binSize 1 -b1 $D&D.bigwig -b2 $ATAC.bigwig -o $D&D.normalized.bigwig. The ATAC bigwig files and the normalized D&D bigwig files were used as the inputs for inference of HiC contact maps using C.Origami. For predictions generated without incorporating CTCF binding information, the ATAC bigwig files, along with a bigwig file containing zeros for all regions, were used as input.
