# Organ‐Specific Cancer‐Associated Fibroblast Subtypes Across the Digestive System Linked to Cancer Hallmarks


## ABSTRACT
  Cancer‐associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are major stromal components of the tumor microenvironment (TME) and play diverse roles in gastrointestinal (GI) cancer progression, immunity, and therapeutic resistance. However, the heterogeneity, tissue specificity, and clinical relevance of CAFs across GI cancers remain incompletely defined. We integrated 14 independent single‐cell RNA‐sequencing datasets comprising 239 GI adenocarcinoma samples to define the subtypes of CAFs. Machine learning–based deconvolution was applied to 18 bulk transcriptomic cohorts (3396 patients) to estimate CAF subtype abundance and assess prognostic associations by meta‐analysis. Functional states, differentiation trajectories, cell–cell communication, and spatial organization were analyzed using gene‐set enrichment, transcription factor activity inference, pseudotime modeling, ligand–receptor analysis, and spatial transcriptomics. We identified eight CAF subtypes with distinct transcriptional programs and organ‐specific distributions. Two myofibroblastic CAF subtypes (myCAF1 and myCAF2) were consistently associated with poor prognosis, characterized by extracellular matrix remodeling, TGF‐β signaling, hypoxia adaptation, and close crosstalk with immunosuppressive macrophages, as well as tumor cells displaying epithelial‐mesenchymal transition and hypoxia signatures. In contrast, inflammatory CAF (iCAF) 1 was associated with a favorable prognosis and increased infiltration of antitumor immune cells. Spatial transcriptomic analyses further revealed that distinct CAF subtypes preferentially occupied discrete spatial domains within the GI TME. Our study demonstrated that the functional state and spatial context of CAFs jointly affect tumor progression and outcomes in patients with GI cancers. myCAF‐driven stromal niches promote immune suppression and poor prognosis, whereas a balanced inflammatory CAF program may support antitumor immunity.


## Introduction
  Tumors are complex ecosystems made up of more than just cancer cells; they also include other cell types, such as fibroblasts and immune cells, that surround cancer cells. These cells interact closely within the tumor microenvironment (TME), and their crosstalk plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of cancer [1]. Fibroblasts, one of the main components of the TME, are referred to as cancer‐associated fibroblasts (CAFs) [2, 3, 4]. Decades of research have revealed that CAFs support tumor progression through various types of extracellular matrix (ECM) and soluble factors, which facilitate tumor invasion and metastasis through promoting epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT) [5]. Recent findings from our group and others have indicated that CAFs also contribute to the suppression of antitumor immunity by recruiting immunosuppressive cells, such as myeloid‐derived suppressor cells, tumor‐associated macrophages, and regulatory T cells (Tregs), via the secretion of chemokines and thereby leading to poor prognosis [6, 7]. On the other hand, some studies suggest that CAFs restrain tumors by promoting antitumor immunity [8] or inhibiting cancer cell stemness [9] and angiogenesis [10]. These different possibilities make understanding the complex functional heterogeneity of CAFs difficult.

  Recent advances in single‐cell omics technologies, including single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq), have revealed phenotypic subtypes of CAFs [11]. Representatively, myofibroblastic CAFs (myCAFs [12]), inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs [12]), and antigen‐presenting CAFs (apCAFs [13]) have been identified in human and mouse pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), and these subtypes are widely recognized as conserved across various cancer types. However, accumulating evidence indicates that the functional properties and prognostic relevance of these CAF subtypes are highly dependent on tissue context and disease state [8, 14]. Recent integrated large‐scale data analyses have proposed conserved CAF subtypes and their characteristics across diseases and organs [15, 16]. While these studies provide a useful framework for cross‐tumor comparisons, the marked variation in the abundance and dominance of specific CAF subsets across cancer types reveals inherent limitations. This variability highlights the risk of overgeneralization when a unified CAF classification across diverse tissues is applied.

  Gastrointestinal (GI) cancers account for almost one‐third of cancer‐related mortality worldwide [17]. Among GI cancers, stomach cancer, colorectal cancer, pancreatic cancer, and biliary tract cancer are prevalent and lethal types of cancer, and approximately 90% of these cancers are adenocarcinomas [17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. Previous evidence has indicated that increased CAF abundance is associated with poor prognosis in these GI cancers [22, 23, 24]. Nevertheless, the relationships between specific CAF subtypes and the hallmarks of cancer remain unclear. In addition, a systematic evaluation of the CAF subtype–specific prognostic impact in multiple GI cancer cohorts based on the unified CAF subtype annotation framework has not yet been performed.

  Here, we combined 14 independent scRNAseq datasets, including 239 samples from patients with stomach, colorectal, pancreatic, and biliary tract cancer, and performed a comprehensive analysis of CAFs. We identified eight CAF subtypes with distinct phenotypic characteristics and organ‐specific patterns of CAF composition. A machine learning‐based model was developed to predict the proportions of each CAF subtype in multicohort transcriptomic GI cancer datasets, and a meta‐analysis revealed the specific CAF subtypes associated with poor or good patient prognosis across tissue types. Furthermore, the integration of spatial transcriptomics and scRNA‐seq datasets confirmed the existence of unique cellular communication and spatial domains associated with cancer hallmarks. These findings provide a clinically relevant classification of CAF subtypes, which can serve as targets in the TME for precision GI cancer therapy.


## Methods

### Curation of scRNA‐Seq Data
  Previously published scRNA‐seq datasets of stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD), colorectal adenocarcinoma (COAD), pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), and biliary tract cancer (BTC) that contain fibroblast populations were obtained from public repositories, including the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) (Table S1). For each dataset, the gene count matrices and accompanying metadata, including clinical information and original cell‐type annotations, were obtained and curated for downstream analysis.


### Preprocessing, Batch Correction, and Cell Annotation
  An AnnData object was constructed for each dataset from the gene count matrix and metadata using Scanpy (v1.11). Raw count data were normalized to a fixed total count per cell and log‐transformed using scanpy.pp.normalize_total and scanpy.pp.log1p, respectively. Highly variable genes (HVGs) were identified using scanpy.pp.highly_variable_genes, and the top 2000 HVGs were selected for downstream analyses. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the HVGs using scanpy.tl.pca. Cell‐type identities were assigned on the basis of the original annotations provided in the metadata. Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) was used for dimensionality reduction and visualization. All the datasets were subsequently concatenated into a single AnnData object and renormalized, log‐transformed, and subjected to PCA using the top 2000 HVGs. Because the gene expression patterns varied across datasets owing to technical and biological differences (batch effects), batch correction was performed using the Harmony [25] algorithm implemented in scanpy.external.pp.harmony_integrate. To annotate cell types in the integrated dataset, we applied the built‐in CellTypist [26] models “Immune_All_Low” and “Immune_All_High” using the celltypist.annotate function with “majority_voting = True”. Following prediction, closely related immune cell clusters were merged to facilitate downstream analyses, including ligand–receptor interaction analysis and correlation analysis.


### Subset Analysis
  Fibroblast and epithelial cell subsets were extracted from the integrated dataset and processed independently as described above. Following batch correction, the cells were clustered using the Leiden algorithm implemented in scanpy.tl.leiden, with a resolution of 0.2 for fibroblasts and 0.05 for epithelial cells. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using scanpy.tl.rank_genes_groups with the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test and used to annotate subclusters.

  For the analysis of the proportion of different CAF subtypes, the number of cells belonging to each CAF subtype was counted for each organ, and the relative proportion of each subtype was calculated by dividing the subtype‐specific cell count by the total number of fibroblasts within the corresponding organ. The resulting proportions were visualized as stacked bar plots. The sample‐level CAF subtype proportions were calculated to compare subtype abundance across organs. For each sample, the proportion of each CAF subtype was calculated by dividing the subtype‐specific cell count by the total number of fibroblasts in that sample. Subtype proportions were visualized using boxplots with overlaid individual sample points.

  Finally, subcluster annotations were merged back into the parental dataset and used for downstream analyses. Methods for gene ontology analysis, pathway analysis, transcription factor activity analysis, pseudotime analysis, ligand‐receptor interaction analysis, and cell correlation analysis are described in detail in the Supporting Informations and Methods Doc S1.


### Survival Analysis
  To evaluate the association between CAF subtypes and patient prognosis, we estimated the relative abundance of each CAF subtype in bulk transcriptomic datasets using the CIBERSORTx web tool [27]. Briefly, 10,000 cells were randomly subsampled from the fibroblast subset of the scRNA‐seq data to generate a reference matrix containing gene expression counts and subcluster annotations. This reference matrix was uploaded to CIBERSORTx as a Single‐Cell Reference Matrix and used to construct a signature matrix via the Create Signature Matrix module with the scRNA‐seq option and default parameters.

  Bulk transcriptomic datasets with available survival information for patients with STAD, COAD, and BTC were obtained from public repositories, including The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and the GEO (Table S2). Bulk transcriptomic data for PAAD were obtained from curated datasets provided by the pdacR [28] package. Gene expression matrices from each dataset were uploaded to CIBERSORTx as Mixtures, and the CAF subtype abundances were estimated using the Impute Cell Fractions module in absolute mode with S‐mode batch correction.

  For survival analysis, multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were fitted separately for each dataset using the CoxPHFitter function from the lifelines package (v0.30.0). The inferred abundance of each CAF subtype was log‐transformed and standardized prior to analysis and was included as a covariate together with patient sex, age, and tumor stage. Hazard ratios and corresponding confidence intervals derived from each dataset were subsequently integrated using random‐effects meta‐analysis implemented with the metagen function in the meta package (v8.2‐1). The meta‐analysis results were visualized using forest plots generated by the forest function.


### Spatial Transcriptomics Analysis
  We utilized previously reported spatial transcriptomics (ST) datasets generated using the 10× Genomics platform to evaluate the spatial context of the CAF subtypes. A total of 19 ST samples were obtained from GEO (Table S5). For each dataset, an AnnData object was constructed from the gene count matrix and spatial coordinates using Scanpy. Quality control was performed by filtering spots on the basis of the total counts, number of detected genes, and mitochondrial gene fraction. All the datasets were subsequently concatenated into a single AnnData object for integrated analysis. The Tangram [29] package (v.1.0.4) was used to predict the cell–type composition in each Visium spot by leveraging the integrated scRNA‐seq dataset as a reference. On the basis of the inferred cell‐type proportions, the spots across all the samples were clustered using the scanpy.tl.leiden function with a resolution parameter of 0.1. GO analysis was conducted as described above. For spatial CAF scoring, we used the spatial CAF subtype gene signatures reported in a previous study [16], which defined four conserved spatial CAF programs, designated S1–S4, across multiple cancer types using single‐cell spatial multiomics. For each Visium spot, enrichment scores for the S1, S2, S3, and S4 CAF signatures were calculated using the scanpy.tl.score_genes function. To characterize the spatial CAF programs associated with each domain, we calculated the mean score of each CAF signature within each spatial domain. The domain‐level mean scores were then Z‐score normalized across domains and visualized as a heatmap to compare the relative enrichment of the four spatial CAF programs among the domains. In addition, the distribution of each CAF signature score across domains was visualized using raincloud plots, allowing the assessment of both domain‐level trends and spot‐level heterogeneity in spatial CAF programs.


### Statistical Analysis
  Differences between pairs of groups were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one‐way analysis of variance followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test. Correlations were evaluated using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10, Python, and R. A two‐sided p value less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.


## Results

### Integration of Multicohort GI Cancer scRNA‐Seq Datasets and Profiling of CAF Subtypes
  To comprehensively characterize CAFs in GI cancer, we curated 14 publicly available scRNA‐seq datasets focusing on GI adenocarcinoma (Figure 1A, Table S1). After preprocessing, batch correction, and unsupervised clustering, we obtained an integrative dataset comprising 239 samples and more than 700,000 cells, with distinct cellular populations, including fibroblasts, tumor cells, endothelial cells, and various immune cell types (Figures 1B and S1A–C). To further elucidate CAF heterogeneity, we reclustered the fibroblast population and identified eight CAF subtypes, including previously known myCAFs, iCAFs, and apCAFs (Figure 1C). These CAF subtypes recapitulated the transcriptomic hallmarks defined by subtype‐specific curated gene sets (Figure 1D, Table S3), supporting the accuracy of the clustering and annotation. Furthermore, differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis revealed representative genes for each CAF subtype (Figure 1E), and in addition to an established myCAF subtype (myCAF1) characterized by LRRC15 [30, 31] expression, we identified an unexplored myCAF subtype (myCAF2) that expresses the peroxiredoxin 6 (PRDX6) gene and three distinct iCAF subtypes, iCAF1, iCAF2, and iCAF3, characterized by the gene expression of HSD17B2, COL7A1, and ADAMDEC1, respectively (Figure 1F).

  We next examined the organ‐specific composition of each CAF subtype. Notably, while the CAF subtypes exhibited significant heterogeneity in STAD and COAD, PAAD and BTC displayed a more restricted CAF landscape dominated by myCAF1 (Figures 1G and S1D). STAD was characterized by a high abundance of iCAF1, pericyte‐like CAFs, and myCAF2. Among these, myCAF2 was identified as an STAD‐specific subtype. COAD exhibited contracted myCAFs along with expanded iCAFs, with a particularly marked abundance of iCAF2 and iCAF3. Compared with STAD and COAD, pancreaticobiliary cancers presented a distinct CAF composition, with predominant accumulation of myCAF1 and relative depletion of inflammatory CAF subtypes (Figure 1H). Overall, we defined eight conserved CAF subtypes across GI cancer and the organ‐specific pattern of CAF composition.


### Distinct CAF Subtypes Related to GI Cancer Patient Prognosis
  Next, we analyzed the impact of CAF subtypes on the prognosis of patients with GI cancer. First, we developed a machine learning‐based deconvolution model to infer the abundance of each CAF subtype from bulk transcriptomic data using CIBERSORTx [27]. Next, we applied this model to 18 cohorts of bulk transcriptomic datasets of GI cancer patients, each linked to clinical metadata, including survival information (Table S2) and estimated the proportion of each CAF subtype across the 3396 patients. Finally, a multivariate Cox proportional hazards model was used to calculate the hazard ratio for each CAF subtype per cohort, and the combined results were subjected to meta‐analysis (Figure 1A and Methods).

  We detected correlations between shorter overall survival (OS) and both myCAF1 (hazard ratio [HR]: 1.16; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.01 to 1.33; p = 0.0374; Figure 2A) and myCAF2 (HR: 1.09; 95% CI: 1.01 to 1.19; p = 0.0302; Figure 2B). In contrast, iCAF1 was correlated with longer OS (HR: 0.90; 95% CI: 0.81 to 0.99; p = 0.0382; Figure 2C). Other CAF subtypes were not statistically correlated with OS (Figure S2A–E). In summary, our meta‐analysis revealed specific CAF subtypes with distinct clinical outcomes: both myCAFs were associated with poor prognosis, whereas iCAF1 was correlated with favorable prognosis (Figure 2D).


### Characterization of Functional Heterogeneity and the Trajectory of CAF Differentiation
  To further characterize each CAF subtype, we analyzed its molecular signatures. Gene set analysis by MSigDB [32, 33] revealed that gene signatures related to ECM remodeling were enriched in both myCAF1 and myCAF2, whereas the activity of hypoxia‐associated signatures was markedly increased in myCAF2 (Figure 3A and Table S4). In contrast, inflammatory response‐related signatures were preferentially enriched in the iCAF subtypes. Notably, while iCAF3 expression was selectively enhanced in terms of inflammatory response signatures, iCAF1 and iCAF2, especially iCAF2, exhibited concurrent enrichment of both ECM remodeling and inflammatory signatures, suggesting a hybrid transcriptional state bridging the myCAF and iCAF phenotypes. As expected, signatures related to antigen presentation were specifically enriched in the apCAF subtype.

  Next, we inferred transcription factor (TF) activity by using the collection of transcription regulation interactions (CollecTRI) [34] to identify key regulators underlying CAF subtype–specific phenotypes. TFs associated with myofibroblast differentiation, including TCF21 [35] and YAP1 [36], increased activity in myCAF1 and myCAF2, respectively. Consistent with the enrichment of hypoxia‐associated signatures, the activity of the hypoxia‐responsive TF ELK3 [37] was also preferentially activated in myCAF2. In addition, histone deacetylases (HDACs), including HDAC1 and HDAC7, exhibited elevated activity in the myCAF subtypes, suggesting that epigenetic regulation contributes to the maintenance of the myCAF phenotype. Inflammatory regulators were activated across all the iCAF subtypes; however, each subtype displayed distinct dominant TF programs, including activator protein 1 (AP1 [38]) in iCAF1, RELA [39], an NF‐κB‐related TF, in iCAF2, and signal transducer and activator of transcription 2 (STAT2 [40]) together with interferon regulatory factor 1 (IRF1 [41]) in iCAF3 (Figure 3B). These findings indicate that subtype‐specific inflammatory signaling programs may underlie the functional diversity of iCAFs.

  Finally, with a focus on prognosis‐associated CAF subtypes, we compared the pathway activities among myCAF1, myCAF2, and iCAF1. We observed strong activation of the transforming growth factor beta (TGF‐β) pathway in myCAF1 cells, whereas hypoxia and vascular endothelial growth factor pathways were preferentially enhanced in myCAF2 cells. These upregulated pathways are established inducers of epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT), invasion, and metastasis [42, 43], suggesting the protumorigenic roles of these myCAF subtypes. In contrast, iCAF1 activated inflammatory pathways, such as the NFkB, TNFa, and JAK–STAT pathways, suggesting a potential role for iCAF1 in promoting the antitumor immune response (Figure 3C). In addition, we found that Meflin (ISLR), previously reported as a marker of tumor‐restraining CAFs in pancreatic cancer [44, 45], was highly expressed in iCAF1 (Figure S3A), suggesting that iCAF1 may correspond, at least in part, to a tumor‐restraining CAF population.

  Furthermore, to investigate the differentiation trajectories of CAF subtypes, we applied multiple pseudotime inference methods, including scFates [46], Palantir [47], and CellRank [48]. Interestingly, all these approaches consistently identified pericyte‐like CAFs as a putative source population from which other CAF subtypes emerged (Figures 3D and S3B,C). In contrast, myCAF2, iCAF1, and apCAF were inferred to be in terminally differentiated states (Figure 3E,F). Consistent with these trajectory predictions, lineage marker genes exhibited progressive and subtype‐specific expression changes over pseudotime (Figure 3G). These results suggest a model in which pericyte‐like CAFs serve as a potential origin for prognosis‐associated CAF subtypes.


### Cell–Cell Communication Network Across CAF Subtypes, Immune Cells, and Cancer Cells
  To elucidate the intercellular communication between prognosis‐linked CAF subtypes and other cells in the TME, we reannotated the integrative dataset (Figure 1B) according to CAF subtypes, immune cell populations, and tumor cell states (Figure S4A–C). Correlation analysis across all cell types revealed that the proportion of myCAF1 was positively associated with the abundance of M2 macrophages (Figures 4A and S4D). Consistent with this finding, ligand–receptor interaction analysis revealed ECM ligands expressed by myCAF1 and their corresponding receptors on M2 macrophages (Figure 4B), which are known to exhibit anti‐inflammatory and immunosuppressive functions within the TME [49]. Furthermore, compared with iCAF1, myCAF1 and myCAF2 exhibited substantially stronger ligand–receptor interactions with M2 macrophages (Figure 4C). In contrast, the proportions of myCAF1 and iCAF1, or myCAF2 and iCAF1, were negatively correlated (Figures 4D and S4E), suggesting opposing roles of these CAF subtypes in regulating immune activity within the TME. To further assess the immunological impact of myCAF1 and iCAF1, we performed immune cell deconvolution using TCGA bulk transcriptomic data and stratified patients on the basis of the abundance of these CAF subtypes. Notably, compared with the myCAF1‐low/iCAF1‐high subgroup, the myCAF1‐high/iCAF1‐low subgroup exhibited increased infiltration of M2 macrophages and Tregs (Figure 4E). Conversely, tumors classified as myCAF1‐low/iCAF1‐high presented higher levels of CD8+ T cells and B cells (Figure 4F). These results indicate that myCAF1 is associated with an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, whereas iCAF1 is linked to enhanced immune activation.

  Next, we investigated cell–cell communication between CAF subtypes and tumor cells. Within the integrated dataset, we categorized tumor cells into subpopulations defined by cancer hallmarks, including enhanced glycolysis and EMT programs (Figure 4B,C). CellChat [50] analysis revealed that several tumor‐promoting signaling pathways, such as thrombospondin (THBS) [51], tenascin [52], and insulin‐like growth factor (IGF) [53] signaling, were actively transmitted from myCAF1 and myCAF2 to glycolytic tumor cells and EMT‐like tumor cell subpopulations (Figures 4G and S4F). Consistent with these results, ligand–receptor interaction analysis demonstrated increased interactions involving THBS, tenascin, and IGF ligands expressed by myCAF subtypes and their corresponding receptors, including members of the syndecan (SDC) and integrin (ITG) families, on tumor cell subtypes compared with those of iCAF1 (Figure 4H). Collectively, these findings indicate that myCAF subtypes engage in close crosstalk with aggressive tumor cell subsets and may facilitate tumor progression.


### Distinct Spatial Domains Consisting of Different Cellular Components in the GI Cancer TME
  Accumulating evidence suggests that cellular localization and spatial topology critically influence cell function within the TME [54]. To assess the spatial colocalization of CAF subtypes with other cell populations, we analyzed 10× Visium ST datasets of GI cancers (Table S5) and inferred the cellular composition of each spatial spot by integrating the scRNA‐seq dataset as a reference (Figure S5A). Clustering spatial spots based on inferred cellular composition revealed five distinct spatial domains across 19 ST datasets: tumor cell–rich domain 1, tumor cell–rich domain 2, the CAF‐rich domain, the immune cell–rich domain, and the tertiary lymphoid structure (TLS) domain (Figures 5A and S5B). These domains were segregated within tumor sections (Figure 5B), and their relative abundance varied across cancer types. Notably, PAAD exhibited a high proportion of CAF‐rich domains, which is consistent with its characteristic desmoplastic stroma (Figure 5C). Cooccurrence analysis revealed preferential enrichment of myeloid lineage cells within tumor cell–rich domain 1, whereas lymphoid lineage cells were enriched in tumor cell–rich domain 2 (Figure 5D). The CAF‐rich domain consisted predominantly of myCAF subtypes and M2 macrophages, suggesting an immunosuppressive microenvironment (Figure 5D). In contrast, the immune cell–rich domain was enriched for dendritic cells (DCs) and lymphocytes together with the iCAF subtypes, which is consistent with an immune‐reactive phenotype (Figure 5D). The TLS domain was primarily composed of B‐cell lineage populations and apCAFs, in agreement with previous studies reporting the association of apCAFs and TLS [8, 16] (Figures 5D and S5C).

  Pathway enrichment analysis further revealed domain‐specific transcriptional programs (Figure 5E). For instance, hypoxia‐related pathways, which are known contributors to tumor progression [55], were strongly activated in the CAF‐rich domain (Figure 5F), whereas interferon‐γ response pathways, reflecting immune activation and antitumor immunity [56], were significantly enriched in the immune cell–rich domain (Figure 5F). Consistent with these findings, in the CAF‐rich domain, myCAF1 was the most abundant CAF subtype, whereas iCAF1 was predominant among CAFs in the immune cell–rich domain (Figure 5G), which is in line with their respective associations with unfavorable and favorable prognosis.

  Finally, we scored each spatial domain on the basis of the enrichment of recently reported spatial CAF subtypes associated with TME features and clinical outcomes [16] (Figure 5H). The CAF‐rich domain, dominated by myCAF subtypes, was preferentially enriched for s1‐CAFs, a spatial CAF subtype linked to poor survival. In contrast, the immune cell–rich domain, characterized by iCAF1 predominance, was enriched in s4‐CAFs and was associated with improved survival (Figure 5I). Together, these findings demonstrate that CAF subtypes occupy spatially distinct niches within GI cancers and colocalize with specific immune and tumor cell populations, thereby shaping divergent microenvironmental programs that may promote either protumorigenic or antitumorigenic states.


## Discussion
  In this study, we performed a comprehensive, multicohort analysis of CAFs across major GI adenocarcinomas and established a clinically relevant CAF subtyping framework. By integrating single‐cell transcriptomics, bulk transcriptomic deconvolution, survival meta‐analysis, cell–cell communication inference, and spatial transcriptomics, we delineated eight CAF subtypes characterized by distinct transcriptional programs, tissue‐specific distributions, prognostic associations, and spatial contexts.

  Strong correlations between CAF abundance and poor prognosis in patients with GI cancers have been reported [22, 23, 24]. Our analysis revealed that two myCAF subtypes, myCAF1 and myCAF2, were associated with poor patient survival across GI cancer cohorts through distinct biological programs. myCAF1 exhibited classical myofibroblastic features and close crosstalk with EMT‐like tumor subtypes via ECM‐ and TGF‐β‐related pathways, such as THBS [51]. In addition, myCAF1 is characterized by the expression of LRRC15, which recently emerged as a novel CAF marker associated with immunosuppression [30, 31]. Indeed, myCAF1 is positively correlated with M2 macrophage infiltration, which is known to suppress antitumor immunity [49]. In contrast, myCAF2 represented a previously uncharacterized myCAF population marked by PRDX6. PRDX6 is known for its protective role against hypoxia‐induced cell death [57] and is associated with tumor progression [58]. Therefore. The hypoxia‐adapted transcriptional profile of myCAF2 suggests that it plays a specialized role in supporting tumor growth under hypoxic stress. Spatial transcriptomic analyses further demonstrated that both myCAF1 and myCAF2 preferentially localized within CAF‐rich domains characterized by hypoxia, dense stromal architecture, and enrichment of M2 macrophages, highlighting a spatially organized stromal niche that supports tumor progression.

  In contrast, a recent study revealed that iCAF populations are associated with favorable prognosis in non–small cell lung cancer [59]. However, the detailed classification and functional heterogeneity within iCAF states remain unclear. In the present study, we identified three iCAF subtypes. Among them, iCAF3 cells exhibited the strongest inflammatory phenotype. iCAF2 cells displayed enhanced ECM remodeling, suggesting a more myCAF‐like phenotype. iCAF1 cells exhibited a balanced phenotype and were able to model the ECM remodeling program and inflammatory pathways. Among these iCAF subtypes, only iCAF1 was correlated with a good prognosis in patients with GI cancers through the enhancement of antitumor immunity [56]. On the basis of prior evidence that chronic or excessive inflammation can promote tumor progression [60], this intermediate, balanced inflammatory program of iCAF1 may contribute to antitumor immune activation. Moreover, by applying multiple pseudotime and lineage inference approaches, we identified pericyte‐like CAFs, including myCAFs and iCAFs, as a putative progenitor population from which diverse CAF subtypes emerge. These observations are supported by the findings of other studies reporting that pericytes are among the origins of other CAF subtypes [15] and that vascular pericytes promote cancer progression via pericyte–fibroblast transition [61]. Nevertheless, to clarify the cellular origins, differentiation trajectories and causal roles of these CAF subtypes in tumor progression in GI cancers, lineage‐tracing approaches and functional validation will be needed.

  A key observation from our integrated analysis is that the CAF subtype composition differs markedly across organ types. Pancreatobiliary cancers, including PAAD and BTC, were characterized by a predominance of myCAF1 and few iCAF subtypes, which is consistent with the desmoplastic stromal reaction observed in these tumors and with findings from previous studies of human and mouse PAAD [13, 62] and BTC [63]. This enrichment of myCAF1 likely contributes to the aggressive clinical behavior of PAAD and BTC, as well as their resistance to immunotherapy. In contrast, patients with COAD exhibited a relative enrichment of inflammatory CAF subtypes, particularly iCAF2 and iCAF3, and a lower proportion of myCAF populations. STAD displayed the most diverse CAF composition, with prominent iCAF1 and unique myCAF2 subtypes, suggesting that organ‐specific differentiation cues shape CAF fate decisions. These findings indicate that CAF heterogeneity is strongly influenced by tissue context and that organ‐specific stromal ecosystems must be considered when interpreting CAF function or designing CAF‐targeted therapies. Given the very limited availability of publicly accessible spatial transcriptomics datasets for BTC, the integrated spatial analysis in this study was performed using the STAD, COAD, and PAAD datasets. Therefore, future studies incorporating BTC ST datasets will be necessary to further validate and extend the spatial characteristics of CAF subtypes within the pancreatobiliary system.

  Together, our findings establish a unifying framework that links CAF subtype identity, tissue context, immune interactions, spatial organization, and clinical outcome in GI cancers. This framework provides important insights indicating that indiscriminate CAF targeting may yield unpredictable results, whereas selectively modulating specific CAF subtypes may offer more precise and effective strategies.
