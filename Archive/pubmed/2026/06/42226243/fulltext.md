# CA9 targets ITGB1 to accelerate the progression of colorectal cancer by promoting the neutrophil extracellular traps formation


## Background
  The immunological role of carbonic anhydrase IX (CA9) in colorectal cancer (CRC) progression remains undefined. This study reveals a CA9-driven mechanism promoting tumor progression through neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs).


## Methods
  CA9 was identified as ‌a candidate oncogene in CRC through public single-cell RNA sequencing data screening. Endogenous modulation of CA9 in vitro and in vivo was performed to elucidate its function. Flow cytometry and immunofluorescence assays were employed to assess how CA9 promotes neutrophil infiltration and NETs formation in tumor tissues. Finally, molecular docking, co-immunoprecipitation, and cytokine arrays were integrated to investigate the specific mechanisms of CA9-driven CRC progression.


## Results
  Serum CA9 expression level in CRC patients exhibited significantly elevated relative to controls, correlating with poor clinical prognosis (P < 0.05). Interestingly, CA9 mainly played a significant pro-tumor role in CRC mouse models, promoting tumor progression by regulating neutrophil recruitment and NETs formation. Mechanistically, CA9 binds to ITGB1, activating the NF-κB pathway and triggering increased secretion of C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 8 (CXCL8), which subsequently promoted neutrophil recruitment and NETs formation. Thus, the CA9-ITGB1-CXCL8 axis induces neutrophil reactive oxygen species production and NETs formation, thereby promoting CRC tumor progression.


## Conclusion
  The CA9-driven ITGB1-CXCL8-NETs signaling axis significantly promotes CRC progression, thereby establishing CA9 as a novel potential therapeutic target for CRC.

## Introduction
  Colorectal cancer (CRC) has become an urgent global health issue in recent years. Its incidence ranks third and mortality ranks second globally, affecting millions of patients annually [1]. A large percentage of patients received their diagnosis only when the disease had reached an advanced phase, resulting in suboptimal treatment outcomes. Even for early-stage patients, the risk of postoperative recurrence and metastasis remains a concern [2]. The continuously rising disease burden coupled with the shortcomings of existing therapies underscore a pressing demand for innovative therapies. Identifying novel targets is imperative to effectively curb the progression of CRC.

  The tumor microenvironment (TME) acts as a key driver underlying oncogenesis, disease advancement, metastatic spread, and therapeutic failure [3]. Tumor cells actively shape and exploit their surrounding microenvironment by releasing extracellular signals and remodeling the extracellular matrix (ECM). Concurrently, the stromal components within the TME not only provide nutrients and support for tumor growth, but also profoundly influence tumor progression and treatment response [4–6]. These complex intercellular interactions and dynamic changes within the microenvironment constitute the core driving forces of tumor progression. Immune cells serve as an indispensable component within the TME. Mechanistically, cytokines within the microenvironment can regulate immune function, inhibit immune reactions, and in turn facilitate the advancement of tumors [7]. Consequently, targeting particular immune cell types has risen as a very promising fresh strategy.

  Neutrophils assume a pivotal role in clearing extracellular pathogens and suppress tumor progression in the TME by producing antitumor factors such as nitric oxide and TSP-1 [8]. Notwithstanding these potential inhibitory capacities, clinical evidence indicated that intratumoral neutrophil infiltration correlates positively with malignant tumor progression phenotypes [9, 10]. Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) are net-shaped structures formed via the expulsion of nuclear and cytoplasmic components from neutrophils, encompassing DNA scaffolds, decondensed chromatin fibers, and granular enzymes like myeloperoxidase (MPO). Researches have established that NETs are implicated in driving the proliferation of malignant cells [11, 12]. Simultaneously, they remodel the immune microenvironment by suppressing T-cell activity, collectively forming key drivers of tumor-promoting immune reactions [13]. Notably, immune-related features inherent to the TME are primarily shaped by malignant cells. However, the core molecular mechanisms through which cancer cells regulate neutrophil infiltration and intercellular interactions remain to be systematically elucidated.

  Carbonic anhydrase IX (CA9) exhibits marked overexpression in solid neoplasms. Its overexpression correlates with poor clinical prognosis and treatment resistance [14]. CA9 creates a favorable acidic microenvironment that promotes tumor progression, while simultaneously reducing the efficacy of chemotherapeutic drugs and suppressing antitumor immune responses [15–17]. Nevertheless, the molecular mechanisms by which CA9 drives tumor progression and its distinct role as a treatment target require further investigation. Elucidating the mechanisms through which CA9 promotes cancer progression is crucial for establishing it as an effective intervention target.

  This study reveals a novel mechanism by which CA9 mediates cancer cell-neutrophil interactions in CRC. Specifically, CA9 potentiates malignant progression by promoting neutrophil infiltration and inducing NETs formation. The “CA9-ITGB1-CXCL8-NETs” signaling axis represents a novel pathway in tumor progression, and targeting CA9 emerges as a promising novel therapeutic approach for CRC.


## Results

### Elevated CA9 in serum and tissues predicts poor prognosis in CRC
  Investigating public single-cell RNA-seq databases for CRC revealed that the samples were divided into 12 subpopulations (Fig. 1A). Figure 1B depicted the distribution of cell clusters across samples, revealing that epithelial cells constitute a significantly higher proportion than other cell types in all samples. This distribution pattern suggested epithelial cells may play a dominant role in CRC progression. Differential expression analysis between malignant and normal epithelial cells identified 424 significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs), comprising 140 downregulated and 284 upregulated genes (Fig. 1C). Notably, CA9 emerged as a key candidate gene exhibiting significant epithelial-specific enrichment in its expression profile (Fig. 1D). The CRC group had a greater proportion of CA9-positive epithelial cells than the normal group (Fig. 1E).

  We further investigated CA9 expression in CRC based on data retrieved from GEPIA (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn), which showed elevated CA9 levels in CRC specimens versus normal specimens (Figure S1). Immunohistochemical analysis of CA9 in tissue microarrays comprising 52 CRC specimens and 52 adjacent non-cancerous tissues revealed markedly elevated CA9 expression in CRC specimens (Fig. 1F-G). Concurrently, Western blot (WB) analysis demonstrated that markedly higher CA9 protein levels in CRC specimens relative to their matched non-tumor counterparts, consistent with the aforementioned findings (Fig. 1H-I). To evaluate serum CA9 expression in CRC patients, we enrolled 184 patients and classified the patient population into high-CA9 or low-CA9 expression groups in accordance with serum CA9 levels on the median serum CA9 level as the critical value. Patient characteristics were presented in Table S1. Elevated CA9 expression correlated positively with more advanced disease stages and unfavorable clinical outcomes in CRC patients (Fig. 1J-K, Table S2-S3).

  Furthermore, WB and real-time quantitative PCR experiments demonstrated that compared with the normal colonic NCM460 cell, HT29 and SW620 cells exhibited a pronounced up-regulation of CA9, whereas no statistically significant difference in CA9 expression was demonstrated in SW480 and Lovo cell lines (Fig. 1L-M). Our findings revealed that CA9 expression is notably upregulated in CRC, implying that CA9 may function as an oncogenic driver in this cancer.


### CA9 promotes colorectal tumor growth in vivo
  To clarify the oncogenic contribution of CA9, we silenced CA9 expression in HT29 and SW620 cell lines using CRISPR/Cas9 technology (Fig. 2A). Both cell lines exhibit high endogenous CA9 expression (Fig. 1L). As shown in Fig. 2B-E, CCK8, EdU, colony formation, and Transwell assays revealed that CA9 knockout did not affect the in vitro proliferation or migration capacity of CRC cells. Concurrently, as depicted in Figure S2A, we constructed CA9 overexpression in SW480 and LoVo cell lines, which exhibit low endogenous CA9 expression (Fig. 1L). CA9 overexpression did not promote in vitro proliferation or migration of CRC cells (Figure S2B-E). These results indicated that CA9 exerts limited direct effects on CRC cells in vitro.

  Unexpectedly, we observed that endogenous suppression of CA9 expression significantly inhibited tumor progression in CA9 knockout nude mouse models (Fig. 2F). Tumor growth was markedly inhibited in the CA9 knockout nude mouse group compared to controls, resulting in significantly reduced tumor volume at the experimental endpoint, with tumor weight nearly halved (P < 0.001, Fig. 2G). These findings indicated that CA9 effectively promotes colorectal tumor growth in vivo, but not in vitro.


### Neutrophil involvement in the pro-tumorigenic activity of CA9 in vivo
  CA9 exhibits significant pro-tumor effects in vivo experiments, suggesting it may promote colorectal tumor progression by relying on non-cancerous cells within the TME. We conducted intercellular communication profiling of the single-cell sequencing database GSE221575 from the GEO database. Epithelial cells were annotated as CA9-positive and CA9-negative (Fig. 3A), and cell communication analysis was conducted on CA9-positive and CA9-negative epithelial cells in both normal intestinal tissue and colorectal cancer tissue. Results revealed that CA9-positive epithelial cells exhibited communication with multiple cell types compared to CA9-negative epithelial cells (Fig. 3B-C). Previous studies have indicated that myeloid immune cells may participate in tumor-promoting effects [18–20]. Subsequent flow cytometric assays demonstrated that the percentage of neutrophils was notably decreased within tumor tissues of CA9 knockout nude mice compared to controls, while other immune cell populations remained unchanged (Fig. 3D). These findings indicated that CA9 expression in tumor cells promotes neutrophil infiltration into tumor tissues.

  To confirm neutrophil involvement in CA9-promoted CRC progression, an in vitro co-culture system was established between CRC cells and dHL60 cells, and the resulting cell proliferation was assessed via CCK-8 assays. Statistical analysis of the results demonstrated that, relative to the vector group, the CA9-overexpressing cell lines (SW480-CA9-OE and LoVo-CA9-OE) exhibited enhanced proliferative capacity, whereas the CA9-knockout cell line (HT29-Sg-CA9) showed attenuated proliferative capacity (Figure S3). Furthermore, we conducted in vivo experiments using neutrophil scavengers to deplete Ly6G+ neutrophils in nude mice. Neutrophil depletion significantly attenuated the CA9 overexpression-induced promotion of colorectal tumor growth (Fig. 3E). As shown in Fig. 3F, tumor weight and volume in CA9-overexpressing nude mice exceeded those of controls by more than twofold (P < 0.001). Neutrophil depletion significantly inhibited the progression of colorectal tumors caused by CA9 overexpression. In summary, neutrophils are involved in CA9-mediated colorectal tumor progression.


### NETs contribute to CA9-mediated progression of colorectal tumors
  Studies indicated that NETs participate in tumorigenesis, progression, metastasis, and cancer-associated thrombosis [21–23]. Immunofluorescence experiments revealed significantly increased NETs formation (CitH3/MPO colocalization) in tumor tissues of CA9-overexpressing nude mice compared to controls (Fig. 4A-B). Concurrently, both tumor tissue homogenate and plasma MPO-DNA levels were markedly elevated in CA9-overexpressing nude mice compared to controls (Fig. 4C), consistent with the aforementioned findings. To further investigate the correlation between CA9-promoted colorectal tumor progression and NETs formation, we used a classic model employing dHL60 cells to induce NETs formation in vitro. After evaluating the cytotoxic effect of the CA9 inhibitor (U104) on SW480 cells (Figure S4), we treated SW480-CA9-OE cells with different concentrations of the U104 (1, 5, and 50 nM), then collected the conditioned medium (CM) to induce NETs formation in dHL60 cells, and assessed NETs formation using flow cytometry. In the U104-50 nM group, both MPO+ neutrophils and ROS production were markedly reduced relative to all other groups (Fig. 4D). We then extended our investigation by assessing the inhibitory effect of U104 on NETs formation through the use of CitH3/MPO immunofluorescence colocalization staining (Fig. 4E). These results indicated that high CA9 expression in CRC cells may promote NETs formation through soluble molecules released from the TME.

  To further validate NETs involvement in CA9-mediated colorectal tumor progression, we applied DNase Ⅰ to degrade released NETs. As shown in Fig. 4F-H, DNase I significantly inhibited SW480 tumor growth in nude mice and markedly attenuated CA9 overexpression-induced tumor growth. Following DNase Ⅰ, the MPO-DNA levels enhanced by CA9 overexpression in SW480 tumors were significantly suppressed in both mouse plasma and tumor tissue homogenates (Fig. 4I-J). Given that DNase I may produce off-target effects, we used a PAD4 inhibitor (GSK484, 10 µM) [24] to inhibit NETs formation in order to further validate the above findings. We co-cultured CRC cells with dHL60 cells. GSK484-induced inhibition of CA9-driven CRC cell proliferation was observed in the CCK-8 assay when compared with the vector group (Figure S5). Therefore, CA9 expression in CRC cells primarily promotes tumor progression by inducing NETs formation.


### CA9 interacts with ITGB1 in CRC cells to promote NETs formation
  To uncover how CA9 drives the molecular mechanism underlying NETs formation, we constructed the CA9 overexpression plasmid. SW480 cells transfected with Flag-CA9 were collected and lysed, followed by co-immunoprecipitation (CO-IP) using anti-CA9 antibody, silver staining analysis, and IP-MS analysis (Fig. 5A). Subsequently, by integrating the IP-MS dataset with RNA-seq databases (RNA-seq analysis of SW480-CA9-OE versus SW480-Vector cells), we identified three proteins: ITGB1, MAGEB2, and DSC3 (Fig. 5B-C). Molecular docking confirmed ITGB1 as the CA9-binding target protein with a combined binding free energy amounting to -56.92 ± 3.24 kJ/mol (Table S4). Potential interaction sites include SER-178, ARG-234, THR-451, GLU-281, ARG-181, PHE-375, ARG-272, ASN-269, THR-378, ASN-305, ARG-151, SER-263, and CYS-156 (Fig. 5D).

(B-C) Flowchart of the combined IP-MS and RNA-Seq analysis workflow identifying ITGB1 as an interacting protein of CA9. (D) Molecular docking results for CA9 and ITGB1 proteins, with hydrogen bonds indicated by connecting lines. (E-I) Molecular dynamics simulation results show: Root mean square deviation (RMSD) (E). Root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) (F). solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) per residue over the trajectory (G). Radius of gyration (Rg) (H). Number of hydrogen bonds (I)

  Molecular dynamics simulations revealed that the CA9 and ITGB1 protein complex displays potent binding affinity and conformational stability. As shown in Fig. 5E-I, analytical metrics encompassing root mean square deviation (RMSD), radius of gyration (Rg), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) per residue over the trajectory, and hydrogen bond formation indicated minimal structural deviation, high rigidity, consistent hydrophobic interactions at the interface, and sustained intermolecular interactions in the CA9-ITGB1 complex. These data further confirmed the formation of a stable CA9-ITGB1 complex. Furthermore, we confirmed the targeted binding of CA9 to ITGB1 via CO-IP and immunofluorescence colocalization (Fig. 6A-C). As shown in Fig. 6D-E, the binding between the two proteins was significantly reduced upon intervention with the U104.

  To further investigate the correlation between CA9-ITGB1 interaction-mediated promotion of colorectal tumor progression and NETs formation, we continued to employ the in vitro NETs induction model using neutrophil-differentiated dHL-60 cells. After evaluating the cytotoxic effect of the ITGB1 inhibitor (Cilengitide) on SW480 cells (Figure S6), we treated SW480-CA9-OE cells with varying concentrations of the Cilengitide (0.5, 1, and 5 µM), then collected the CM to induce NETs formation in dHL60 cells, and assessed NETs formation using flow cytometry. We found that neutrophil infiltration (MPO+ neutrophils) alongside lower ROS production, was detected in the Cilengitide-5µM group compared with other groups (Fig. 6F). Concurrently, co-administration of U104 with Cilengitide did not further inhibit NETs formation in dHL60 cells compared to either U104-treated or Cilengitide-treated groups alone (Fig. 6G).

  Finally, we conducted rescue experiments using CA9 knockdown cells to assess the effects of ITGB1 recombinant protein. The CA9 knockdown cells (HT29-Sg-CA9 or SW620-Sg-CA9) were co-cultured with dHL60 cells. CCK-8 assays showed that recombinant ITGB1 failed to reverse the reduced proliferation capacity of colorectal tumor cells caused by CA9 knockout (Figure S7). These findings further confirmed that CA9-ITGB1 interaction promotes colorectal tumor progression by inducing NETs formation.


### CA9 and ITGB1 interact to promote NETs formation by increasing CXCL8 expression
  To better elucidate how CA9 promotes NETs formation in CRC cells, we utilized an 80-cytokine proteome array to compare the expression of cytokines in CM derived from SW480-CA9-OE versus SW480-Vector cells. Results revealed significantly higher CXCL8 expression in the CA9-overexpressing group (Fig. 7A, Figure S8). Subsequently, ELISA assays demonstrated that CA9-overexpressing cells secreted more CXCL8, while CA9-knockdown cells secreted less CXCL8 than controls (Fig. 7B).

(A) Microarray analysis of 80 cytokines in cell culture supernatants from CA9-overexpressing (SW480-CA9-OE, LoVo-CA9-OE) and control (SW480-Vector) cells. (B) ELISA analysis of CXCL8 levels in the culture supernatants of CA9-overexpressing cells, CA9-knockout cells, and their respective control cells. (C) Mice implanted with SW480 cells were treated with CXCR1/R2 inhibitor (Navarixin) or Vehicle. After 28 days, the mice were euthanized and tumors were dissected. (D-E) Comparison of tumor growth curves and weights in SW480-CA9-OE and SW480-Vector tumors in Navarixin- or Vehicle-treated mice (n = 6). (F-G) MPO-DNA levels in tissue homogenates from tumors treated with Navarixin or Vehicle and in mouse plasma (n = 6). (H) ELISA assay to measure CXCL8 levels in the culture supernatants of CA9-overexpressing and control cell lines before and after treatment with U104 or Cilengitide. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001

  CXCL8 ranks as the most powerful neutrophil chemoattractant in humans, serving as a key mediator of infection and tissue damage responses, and is essential for neutrophil recruitment and NETs formation [25]. The functionality of CXCL8 relies on its interactions with CXCR1 and CXCR2 [25]. Although mice lack a homologous protein of human CXCL8, human CXCL8 can act on cells through murine CXCR2 [26]. In this study, we employed Navarixin, an inhibitor of CXCR1/R2, to interrupt CXCL8-mediated signaling in subsequent experiments. As shown in Fig. 7C-E, Navarixin administration in mice significantly attenuated the tumor-promoting effects of CA9. Relative to the control group, the MPO-DNA levels in the plasma and tumor tissue homogenate of CA9 overexpression group mice were significantly inhibited after Navarixin intervention (Fig. 7F-G). Additionally, treatment with U104 or Cilengitide alone significantly reduced CXCL8 levels in the supernatant of CA9-overexpressing cells, whereas combined treatment did not result in a further decrease in CXCL8 expression levels (Fig. 7H).

  Besides that, to further explore the above results, we applied the CXCL8-neutralizing antibody (10 µg) to further verify pivotal involvement of CXCL8 [27]. The results showed that, compared with vehicle, the CXCL8-neutralizing antibody inhibited the pro-proliferative effect of CA9 overexpression on colorectal tumor cells (Figure S9A-B). These findings indicated that CXCL8 is a key contributor to NETs formation induced by CA9 in CRC, and the interaction between CA9 and ITGB1 promotes NETs formation by increasing CXCL8 secretion.


### CA9-ITGB1 promotes CXCL8 secretion through the NF-κB pathway
  Based on the above experimental results, U104 or Cilengitide inhibited CXCL8 secretion, whereas combined treatment did not produce a synergistic effect. We hypothesized that CA9-ITGB1 may promote CXCL8 secretion through a common downstream signaling pathway. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using RNA-seq data obtained from SW480-Vector and SW480-CA9-OE cells, revealing that CA9 overexpression may lead to the NF-κB pathway activation (Fig. 8A). Previous experimental evidence has established that NF-κB acts as a central node in ITGB1-mediated regulation of CXCL8 secretion, a process closely associated with the tumor microenvironment [28, 29]. More importantly, upstream transcription factors of CXCL8 include NF-κB [30], NFATc1 [31], NFATc2 [32], ATF3 [33], and WT1 [34]. Therefore, we hypothesize that CA9-ITGB1 promotes CXCL8 secretion in CRC via NF-κB pathway.

  Fig. 8CA9-ITGB1 promotes CXCL8 secretion through the NF-κB pathway. (A) KEGG enrichment analysis of RNA-Seq data from SW480-Vector and SW480-CA9-OE cells. (B-C) Western blot analysis of NF-κB pathway proteins in SW480-CA9-OE cells following treatment with U104 or Cilengitide, including grayscale images and semi-quantitative histograms. (D) ELISA assay measuring CXCL8 levels in the cell culture supernatants of SW480-CA9-OE and control cells following treatment with U104 + Cilengitide or TNF-α. ***P < 0.001

  Subsequently, our research results demonstrated that, relative to the control group, p-p65 expression was inhibited after treatment with U104 or Cilengitide alone, co-administration of the two compounds did not elicit a synergistic decrease in the expression level of p-p65 protein (Fig. 8B-C). Furthermore, we further validated the above results using NF-κB pathway activator (TNF-α) (10 ng) [35, 36]. ELISA detection showed that TNF-α intervention could reverse the inhibitory effect of U104 and Cilengitide combined intervention on CXCL8 expression levels (Fig. 8D). These results established that the NF-κB pathway plays an indispensable role in CA9-ITGB1-mediated regulation of CXCL8.


## Discussion
  This study revealed a new mechanistic pathway through which CA9 promotes the progression of CRC by regulating cancer cell-neutrophil interactions. Our findings demonstrated that CA9, specifically overexpressed in epithelial cells, interacts with ITGB1 to secrete CXCL8, thereby recruiting neutrophils and promoting NETs formation, which accelerates colorectal tumor progression. These findings expand the knowledge of CA9 function and lay the groundwork for CRC treatment targeting tumor-immune interactions.

  Catalyzing CO₂ hydration is a classic function of CA9, leading to acidification of the TME and driving malignant invasion and distant metastasis. This study identified a novel tumor-promoting mechanism independent of enzymatic activity: CA9 reshapes the immune microenvironment by mediating interactions between cancer cells and neutrophils. Previous researches indicated that CA9 is markedly up-regulated in various solid tumors and links to progressive disease course [14, 37]. Clinical statistical scrutiny linked elevated CA9 abundance to markedly worse survival outlooks among CRC cases. In vivo experiments confirmed that its deletion markedly inhibits tumor progression, contrasting sharply with its lack of direct in vitro influence on cancer cell proliferation and migratory behavior. This suggested that the key role of CA9 relies on the stromal components in the TME.

  Our research further indicated that NETs formation contributes to the pro- colorectal tumor activity of CA9. In vitro assays showed that GSK484 curbed the enhanced proliferation driven by CA9-overexpressing cancer cells. Systemic dissolution of NETs in tumor-bearing mice blocked CA9-overexpression-induced tumor growth. These findings provided compelling evidence that NETs are the key factor in the pro-tumor function of CA9. ITGB1, as a transmembrane adhesion receptor, mediates interactions between cells and the ECM, activates downstream signaling pathways, and regulates cell migration, proliferation, and survival. Studies indicated that ITGB1 systematically drives malignant tumor progression across multiple cancer types by enhancing tumor cell invasiveness and mediating immune evasion [38–40]. This study revealed a direct interaction between CA9 and ITGB1, demonstrating that inhibiting ITGB1 significantly reduces NETs formation. Furthermore, the CA9-ITGB1 interaction recruit neutrophils and induces ROS bursts, leading to NETs formation that promotes tumor progression.

  Our study identified CXCL8 as the central mediator driving CA9-mediated interactions between CRC cells and neutrophils. It mediates tumor progression by regulating tumor angiogenesis, cell migration, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition [41–43]. Our research revealed that CA9 interacts with ITGB1 to induce high levels of CXCL8, which directly drives the infiltration of neutrophils into the TME. CXCL8 binds to CXCR2 receptors, triggering a neutrophil ROS burst cascade that drives the NETs process. Existing researches revealed that ITGB1 regulates CXCL8 expression through multiple signaling axes. In pancreatic cancer models, ITGB1-mediated activation of NF-κB signaling elicited significant CXCL8 release from pancreatic stellate cells. Blocking this pathway significantly inhibited the growth and metastasis of in situ tumors [28]. This regulatory role extended to colorectal cancer: ITGB1 facilitated the activation of TNFAIP3-mediated NF-κB signaling via extracellular vesicles, prompting cancer-associated fibroblasts to release inflammatory factors like CXCL8 that drive metastasis. This suggested ITGB1 may regulate CXCL8 through the NF-κB core node [29]. Collectively, these researches indicated that CXCL8 may serve as a key downstream inflammatory mediator of ITGB1, consistent with the CA9-ITGB1 interaction promoting CXCL8 secretion observed in this study. These findings suggested that CXCL8 serves as the core effector in the CA9-ITGB1-NETs axis, whose dual functions cascade interactions between cancer cells and immune cells. Although the CA9-ITGB1-CXCL8-NETs axis emerged as pivotal in our study, additional mechanisms may still contribute to CA9-driven tumor progression.

  A key consideration in xenograft models is the cross-species interaction between human tumor-derived CXCL8 and murine host neutrophils. Human CXCL8 has been shown to exert conserved chemotactic functions by binding to the mouse CXCR2 receptor. This cross-reactivity of the human-mouse CXCL8/CXCR2 signaling pathway enables CXCL8 secreted by human CRC cells in this study to effectively recruit mouse neutrophils to the tumor microenvironment, thereby supporting the validity of this model for investigating mechanisms of human tumor-associated neutrophil infiltration. Although this cross-species system does not perfectly replicate the human TME, it remains biologically valid for assessing the directionality and function of CA9-ITGB1-driven neutrophil recruitment and NETs formation. The observation that Navarixin attenuates CA9-promoted tumor growth in our model further supports the validity of this xenograft model.

  Nevertheless, this study has certain limitations, but it also points the way for future research. First, whether the enzymatic activity of CA9 contributes to the CA9-ITGB1-CXCL8-NETs axis requires further validation. Comparing the effects of CA9 enzymatic activity mutants and wild-type CA9 on NET formation will help elucidate the relative contributions of its non-enzymatic functions and enzymatic activity, providing a basis for developing selective targeting strategies. Second, in vivo experiments currently rely solely on immunodeficient nude mouse models, which lack functional T cells and thus cannot assess the suppressive effects of NETs on adaptive immunity. Future validation in immunocompetent syngeneic models and primary immune cells would not only address these limitations but also assess the translational value of integrating CA9-targeted therapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors. Finally, the correlation between CA9 and NETs markers has yet to be validated using human tissue samples. Future validation in independent clinical cohorts or multicenter studies will provide stronger evidence for its use as a prognostic marker or predictor of treatment response. In addition, functional analysis of CA9-ITGB1 through site directed mutagenesis or domain mapping experiments is a key focus of future research to confirm binding specificity and functional relevance. These limitations do not diminish the originality of this study’s contribution to elucidating the new CA9-ITGB1-CXCL8-NETs axis; rather, they provide clear scientific questions and feasible technical pathways for further in-depth exploration.


## Conclusion
  This study highlighted the pro-tumor role of CA9 in CRC, elucidating the pivotal function of the CA9-ITGB1-CXCL8-NETs axis in cancer progression across multiple levels including molecular interactions, cellular functions, and animal models. The discovery of this pathway not only deepened our understanding of cancer cell-neutrophil interaction mechanisms but also reveals the novel therapeutic potential of CA9 as a target in CRC. Combined intervention strategies targeting the CA9-ITGB1-CXCL8-NETs axis may represent a breakthrough approach to overcoming immune suppression in the colorectal tumors. We further propose that serum CA9 levels could act as an innovative indicator for screening CRC patients, demonstrating significant clinical relevance.


## Materials and methods

### Bioinformatic analysis of publicly accessible datasets
  The raw gene expression datasets obtained from the GSE221575 cohort, which was sourced from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) at https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/. Subsequently, clustering analysis was conducted following data normalization. Automated cell type annotation was achieved by combining the Single R algorithm with the Human Primary Cell Atlas reference database, validated using target gene sets specified in the CustomGene.txt file. To decipher the cellular composition across distinct pathological microenvironments, we visualized the annotation results separately. Concurrently, we systematically analyzed core functional genes in epithelial cells, setting thresholds of |Log2FC| ≥ 2 and FDR < 0.05, followed by cell communication analysis. The GEPIA database provided bioinformatics analysis of CA9 differential expression in normal and colorectal cancer tissues.


### Clinical data
  A total of 184 CRC cases contributed peripheral blood samples for the study between 2018 and 2020 at Northern Jiangsu People’s Hospital. Inclusion Criteria: Isolated malignant tumors situated exclusively in the colon or rectum; anatomical position validated by colonoscopy and CT scans; malignant tumors confirmed by colonoscopy and histopathological examination; patients who underwent radical operation. Exclusion criteria: Emergency surgery for acute abdominal symptoms; Patients with metastatic disease at distant sites or multiple primary neoplasms; patients who underwent preoperative neoadjuvant therapy. Diagnoses were double-verified by two certified pathologists, and AJCC TNM criteria guided staging. Clinical variables—age, sex, TNM category, plus vascular or perineural invasion—were extracted from electronic charts. The hospital’s Ethics Committee sanctioned the protocol, and all steps followed the Institutional Review Board rules and Helsinki principles; every participant gave written consent before donation.


### Reagents and antibodies
  A complete roster of reagents and antibodies employed in the experiments is supplied in Tables S5 and S6.


### Immunohistochemistry
  Tissue microarrays were constructed containing 52 pathologically confirmed colorectal cancer specimens and their matched adjacent normal tissues. Sections were processed by xylene dewaxing, graded alcohol rehydration, and sodium citrate antigen retrieval, followed by the application of 3% hydrogen peroxide to suppress endogenous peroxidase expression. Primary rabbit anti-human CA9 antibody (1:200; Novus Biologicals, USA, NB100-417) was incubated. Subsequently, the sections were subjected to treatment with a biotin-conjugated secondary antibody (ZSGB-BIO, China, PV-9000). Finally, hematoxylin counterstaining was applied after the sections were stained with DAB. For each specimen, five random × 400 fields were captured. A combined score was calculated by multiplying the proportion of labeled cells (scored on a 0–4 scale: 0 for < 5%, 1 for 5–25%, 2 for 26–50%, 3 for 51–75%, and 4 for > 75%) by the staining intensity (0–3: 0, absent; 1, faint; 2, moderate; 3, intense). Scores ≥ 5 defined “high” expression, whereas scores ≤ 4 denoted “low” expression.


### Immunofluorescence
  Fixation of cells on confocal-grade dishes (NEST, China) with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilization with 0.01% Tween X-100 (10–15 min), and overnight primary antibody incubation at 37 °C in a humidified dark chamber: rabbit anti-human CA9 (1:200, Novus Biologicals, USA, NB100-417) or mouse anti-human ITGB1 (1:100, Thermo Fisher, USA, 14-0299-82). Subsequently, cells were treated with the corresponding secondary antibody and DAPI-stained for nuclei. Fluorescence patterns and signal strength were subsequently evaluated with a Carl Zeiss LSM 880 confocal laser-scanning microscope (Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany).


### Western blot and CO-IP
  Total protein extracts were obtained from CRC tissues or cells using RIPA buffer (Solarbio, Beijing, China), resolved on 10–12% SDS-PAGE gels, followed by electrophoretic transfer onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, Boston, USA). A 1-hour blocking step was performed using 5% non-fat milk. Next, they were incubated with primary antibodies, followed by a 1.5-hour incubation with matched HRP-linked secondary antibodies. Finally, protein bands were detected via an ECL chemiluminescent reagent. Signals were acquired on a Tanon chemiluminescence imager (Tanon, Shanghai) and quantified with ImageJ v1.51 (NIH).

  Co-IP was carried out with the Pierce cross-linked magnetic bead immunoprecipitation reagents following the vendor’s protocol. Antibodies: anti-CA9, anti-ITGB1, anti-rabbit IgG mAb isotype control (Sepharose-conjugated), and anti-GAPDH. Immune complexes were recovered in 6 × SDS buffer and analyzed by SDS-PAGE.


### Immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP/MS) analysis and protein identification
  After Co-IP, equivalent protein quantities were resolved on SDS-PAGE gels, silver-stained per kit instructions, and target bands were excised. Gel pieces were destained, dehydrated, reduced, alkylated, and digested overnight using trypsin. Peptide samples were analyzed by LC-MS/MS using a Q Exactive Plus mass spectrometer, and subsequent protein identification was performed by BGI (Shenzhen, China).


### RNA extraction and RNA-SEQ
  RNA was extracted from samples using TRIzol (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), subsequently reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a matching commercial kit. qPCR was run on an Applied Biosystems StepOnePlus thermocycler using SYBR Green Master Mix (Aibotek, Wuhan, China). GAPDH was employed as an internal reference for normalization of gene expression data, with relative expression quantified via the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method. All experimental reactions were assayed in triplicate runs to validate result consistency. Primer details are given in Table S7.

  For RNA-seq, equal amounts of total RNA from SW480-NC and SW480-OE cells were sent to BGI (Shenzhen, China). mRNA libraries were constructed and sequenced on a BGI-SEQ platform; differential expression analysis was performed in R (DESeq2).


### Molecular dynamics simulation
  The GROMACS 2022.3 computational package was employed to conduct all-atom molecular dynamics calculations. Small molecule preprocessing involved GAFF force field parameterization via AmberTools22 and RESP charge calculation using Gaussian16W, with potential data embedded into topology files. We carried out steady-state simulations at 300 K and 1 bar, utilizing the TIP3P water model and introducing Na⁺ ions to achieve charge neutralization throughout the system. Upon completion of energy minimization with the steepest descent approach, the system was subjected to equilibration within both canonical (NVT) and isothermal-isobaric (NPT) ensembles. Each equilibration process consisted of 100,000 simulation steps, with a coupling constant set to 0.1 picoseconds and a total duration of 100 picoseconds. This was followed by a 100 ns free simulation (5 million steps, 2 fs step size). Trajectory analysis utilized native tools to extract parameters including RMSD, RMSF, radius of gyration, and MMGBSA free energy.


### ELISA assay
  Quantitative detection of CA9 expression levels in serum samples from colorectal cancer patients was performed using the Human CA9 ELISA Kit (#ml104928, Mlbio) with a sandwich ELISA technique. Three milliliters of venous blood were gathered from fasting participants, left undisturbed at ambient temperature for half an hour, and subsequently spun at 3,000 ×g for a quarter of an hour to extract serum. The separated serum was then preserved at -80 degrees Celsius for subsequent analysis. Pre-coat the ELISA plate with CA9-specific capture antibody. Incubate samples/standards at 37 °C to form solid-phase antigen complexes. Wash to remove unbound components. Sequentially incubate with biotin-labeled detection antibody and streptavidin-HRP to establish a “capture antibody-CA9 antigen-biotinylated secondary antibody-SA-HRP” cascade signal amplification system. TMB chromogenic solution (subjected to a 15-minute incubation at room temperature with full light exclusion) produces a concentration-dependent blue product catalyzed by HRP. After termination with 2 M H₂SO₄, absorbance is measured at 450 nm (OD value correlates positively with CA9 concentration). Final CA9 concentration (ng/ml) is quantified via four-parameter fitting of the standard curve. Three washing steps are performed throughout the experiment.

  Additionally, the IL-8 ELISA kit (#ml025983, Mlbio) was used to assess CXCL8 abundance in tumor extracts and spent cell-culture fluid.


### Cell culture
  The CRC cell lines HT29, SW620, SW480 and LoVo, together with the promyelocytic leukemia cell line HL-60, were all obtained from the Shanghai Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology (Shanghai, China). The NCM460 cell line was obtained from Shanghai Zhongqiao Xinzhou Biological Technology. HT29, LoVo, SW620, SW480 and NCM460 were cultured using McCoy’s 5 A, F-12 K, L-15 or DMEM, respectively, each with 10% FBS; HL-60 were grown in IMEM plus 20% FBS and primed for 4 d with 1.5% DMSO plus 25 ng/ml G-CSF (#300-03, PeproTech). All cells were maintained in humidified conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂).


### Gene overexpression and knockdown
  CRISPR/Cas9 system (General Biol, Anhui, China) [44, 45] achieved stable CA9 knockdown in HT29 and SW620 cells using sgRNA sequences listed in Table S8. Stable CA9 overexpression in SW480 and LoVo cells was accomplished by infecting cells with recombinant lentivirus carrying the human CA9 gene (General Biol, Anhui, China), with empty vector-infected cells serving as controls. All procedures strictly followed the manufacturer’s protocols.


### Cell proliferation assay
  A cell density of 3 × 10³ per well was established in 96-well plates, followed by incubation at 37 °C for the predetermined intervals. Cell proliferation was examined employing the CCK-8 assay kit (#CA1210, Solarbio). To examine CRC cell growth in the presence of neutrophils, CRC cells were first plated and allowed to adhere for 10–12 h, after which dHL-60 cells (2,500 cells per well) were added. Before measurement, the wells were rinsed twice gently with serum-free medium to eliminate the dHL-60 cells, and absorbance was then recorded.


### EdU assay
  EdU incorporation was detected with a kit sourced from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). The protocol involved pulsing cells with 10 µM EdU for 2 h, fixing them in 4% neutral paraformaldehyde (30 min), permeabilizing with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS (20 min), and performing three subsequent washes. EdU detection was then carried out for 30 min followed by three additional washes, and nuclei were counter-stained with Hoechst supplied in the kit before microscopic examination.


### Transwell-based cell migration/invasion assays
  Migration and invasive capacities were evaluated using Transwell assays based on the method in Reference [34]. Briefly, 1 × 10⁴ cells in serum-deprived medium were seeded into the upper Transwell chamber, with 500 µL of 10% FBS medium placed in the lower compartment. For invasion experiments, the Transwell membranes were pre-coated with 100 µL of chilled Matrigel (Vazyme GL201) at a 1:8 dilution in serum-free medium. Following a 24-hour incubation, cells on the lower surface were subjected to fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (10 min), followed by staining with 5% crystal violet (5 min) and imaging across six randomly selected microscopic fields. The mean cell number from these fields was used for statistical comparisons.


### Xenograft experiments and drug intervention
  CA9-knockout or control HT29 cells and CA9-overexpressing or control SW480 cells (5 × 10⁶ per mouse) were suspended in serum-free medium and subcutaneously transplanted into the left axillary fossa of 5-week-old male BALB/c nude mice (Animal Experiment Center, Yangzhou University). Body mass and tumor volume (length × width² × 0.5) were recorded twice weekly. On day 28, mice were deeply anesthetized and euthanized by cervical dislocation; death was verified by pupillary dilation. Tumors were excised and weighed in accordance with institutional animal-welfare guidelines.


### DNase I treatment
  Starting 2 days post-tumor implantation, the tumor-bearing group received intravenous injections of DNase I (50 µg/mouse) every 3 days, and the controls were given an identical volume of physiological saline.


### Systemic clearance of Ly6G⁺ cells
  To achieve systemic clearance of Ly6G⁺ cells, intraperitoneal injections commenced 2 days prior to tumor implantation: the experimental group received anti-Ly6G antibody (#BE0075-1, Bioxcell) (200 µg/mouse), while the control group received isotype control antibody. Dosing was repeated q3d until the experimental endpoint.


### CXCR1/2 inhibitor intervention
  Mice received intraperitoneal injections of Navarixin (100 µg/mouse; #HY-10198, Med Chem Express) every other day.


### Tumor-infiltrating immune cell isolation
  Tumor infiltrating immune cells were separated the Precoll density gradient centrifugation method, based on a previously published protocol [30].


### Flow cytometry
  Prepare a single-cell suspension from mouse tumor tissue as previously described. Cell suspensions were labeled with anti-CD45.2, anti-CD11b, anti-Ly6G, anti-F4/80, anti-CD11c, and anti-NKp46.

  Collect dHL60 cells, incubate with the above surface antibodies, then fix, lyse, wash, then resuspend the cells in PBS. Incubate cells with anti-MPO antibody (clone EPR 20257). Detect cellular ROS levels using the dihydroethidium (DHE) fluorescent probe. Cells were incubated with DHE solution for 30 min in a light-protected environment. Wash cells with PBS, stain with the aforementioned surface antibody, wash, then resuspend the cells in PBS. After washing cells with PBS, resuspend them, process on the Beckman DxFlex, and analyze/evaluate using CytExpert software.


### Assessment of NETs formation
  NETs were visualized in vitro by fixing dHL-60 cells with 4% paraformaldehyde, washing with PBS, and incubating them with anti-CitH3 and anti-MPO. The cells underwent three PBS washes, followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor–conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at 37 °C, and subsequent DAPI staining for 5 min at 37 °C.

  For in situ NETs detection, mouse tumors were fixed, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 5 μm. Slides were processed for antigen unmasking in EDTA solution, and stained with anti-MPO and anti-CitH3. The sections were then washed three times, incubated with secondary antibodies, and counterstained with DAPI. Imaging was performed using a Leica TCS SP8 STED confocal microscope. Quantitative analysis of the images was carried out using LAS X software.


### MPO-DNA detection
  MPO-DNA complexes were quantified in mouse plasma and tumor-homogenate supernatants by sandwich ELISA. A 96-well plate was sensitized with 5 µg/mL anti-MPO antibody, and treated with 4% BSA for 2 h. Samples were added after five washing steps and incubated for 2 h. The plate was then probed with anti-DNA antibody for 2 h. After a further five washes, TMB substrate was added, the reaction was terminated by adding stop solution, and the optical density was measured at 450 nm. Each plate was fitted with its own standard curve, from which MPO-DNA values were derived.


### Cytokine array
  When the SW480-Vector group and SW480-CA9-OE overexpression group attained 70%–80% confluency, the cells underwent three washes, after which the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium. The culture system was then renewed and continued for 36 h. After harvesting the conditioned medium, semi-quantitative detection of 80 cytokines was performed using the Human Cytokine Antibody Array C5 (RayBiotech, #AAH-CYT-5), strictly adhering to the kit protocol. Signal intensity was captured via a chemiluminescence system, and data processing was completed using Fiji image analysis software. All cytokine expression levels were normalized against the array’s built-in positive control.


### Data analysis
  Experimental results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or as box-and-whisker plots depicting the median. Between-group comparisons were assessed using Student’s t-tests or one-way/multivariate ANOVA unless otherwise specified. Survival outcomes were visualized using Kaplan-Meier curves, while univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models were applied to identify predictors of OS and RFS. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P value < 0.05 for all tests.
