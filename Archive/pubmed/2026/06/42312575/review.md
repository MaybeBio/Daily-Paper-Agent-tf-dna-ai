## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text and abstract
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific claims, logical coherence, evidence quality, and alignment with stated aims; no assessment of formatting or editorial scope
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The author argues that transcription occurs predominantly in clusters of RNA polymerases (transcription factories, condensates, hubs), and that this cluster-based view offers a better framework than the traditional independent-gene model for understanding how genomic sequence determines cell fate. The author proposes that AI tools, despite their power, suffer from the curse of dimensionality, data sparsity, and lack of interpretability, and suggests that incorporating DNA:DNA contact data (especially higher-order and trans contacts) into AI models could improve predictions. A minimalist experimental approach using PRO-cap and long-read Pore-C data is proposed to generate information-rich inputs for AI.
- **Visible evidence base** Abstract, Introduction, sections on noise, Waddington landscapes, cis/trans cooperative effects, AI use, and a proposed experimental approach; references cited but not individually verified
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** No figures, tables, or supplementary data were provided; primary experimental data cited from other studies were not accessible; no code or detailed methods for the proposed approach were given

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This is a thought-provoking perspective that synthesizes existing evidence for clustered transcription and proposes a novel integration with AI-based approaches. The author's central claim that clusters are the primary sites of transcription is supported by cited literature, though the strength of that evidence is not fully assessable from the provided text alone. The proposal to use higher-order contact data as AI inputs is interesting and potentially valuable, but the manuscript lacks a concrete, testable framework or preliminary validation. The discussion of noise as a functional element in developmental transitions is conceptually appealing but remains speculative. The writing is generally clear, though some sections assume substantial background knowledge. Overall, the manuscript presents a coherent alternative viewpoint but does not yet establish a rigorous case for its most ambitious claims.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in transcriptional regulation, chromatin organization, and developmental biology would find this relevant, as it challenges the conventional independent-gene model and offers a cluster-centric perspective. Computational biologists working on AI applications in genomics would also be interested in the proposal to integrate contact data into predictive models. The speculative nature of the noise-based mechanism for lineage choice may appeal to those studying stochasticity in cell fate decisions.
- **Major strengths** The manuscript provides a clear and accessible summary of the cluster-based transcription model, drawing on a wide range of cited evidence. The proposal to use higher-order and trans contacts as AI inputs is a creative and potentially impactful idea that addresses a real gap in current approaches. The discussion of noise as a constructive force in development is conceptually novel and could stimulate new thinking. The author is transparent about the limitations of both AI and minimalist approaches, which adds credibility.
- **Major Concerns** 
  - R1-M1: The central claim that "clusters of RNA polymerases make most RNA" is presented as established fact, but the supporting evidence is not critically evaluated. The author cites reviews and specific studies, but does not address potential counterarguments or limitations of the cluster model. This weakens the foundation for the subsequent proposals.
  - R1-M2: The proposed minimalist approach using PRO-cap and Pore-C data lacks specificity. The author does not provide details on how the data would be analyzed, what statistical thresholds would define a "repeated" contact, or how the approach would be validated against existing methods. This makes the proposal difficult to evaluate or implement.
  - R1-M3: The claim that "overexpressing a factor >2-fold in simulations simplifies small-world networks" is cited to a simulation study, but the relevance to biological reality is not explained. The leap from simulation to experimental prediction is underdeveloped.
  - R1-M4: The discussion of noise as a mechanism for facilitating lineage choice is speculative and lacks direct experimental support. The author acknowledges this indirectly but does not propose a clear test of the hypothesis.
- **Minor Comments** 
  - R1-m1: The term "promoter" is defined broadly to include non-genic sites, which is useful, but the distinction between genic and non-genic transcription units could be clarified earlier to avoid confusion.
  - R1-m2: The section on cis/trans cooperative effects introduces a new terminology (cis vs. trans contacts) that is not consistently used throughout the manuscript. A brief summary table or figure would help.
  - R1-m3: The reference to "AlphaGenome" and other AI tools is timely, but the author does not discuss potential limitations of these tools beyond the curse of dimensionality, such as biases in training data or interpretability issues.
  - R1-m4: The concluding remarks mention the three-body problem as an analogy, which is evocative but may distract from the main argument. Consider shortening or removing.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** R1-M1 (uncritical acceptance of cluster model), R1-M2 (lack of specificity in proposed approach), R1-M3 (unexplained simulation-to-biology leap)
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** 
  - Originality: High. The integration of cluster-based transcription with AI inputs and the noise-based mechanism for lineage choice are novel perspectives.
  - Scientific importance: Moderate to high. If the cluster model is correct, it has broad implications for gene regulation and development. However, the importance is not fully realized without a testable framework.
  - Interdisciplinary readership: Moderate. The manuscript bridges molecular biology, computational biology, and developmental biology, but may be too specialized for a general audience.
  - Technical soundness: Weak to moderate. The cited evidence is not critically assessed, and the proposed approach lacks methodological detail.
  - Readability for nonspecialists: Moderate. The writing is clear but assumes familiarity with transcription, Hi-C, and AI concepts.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The manuscript has merit as a perspective piece, but the central claims need stronger critical evaluation and the proposed approach needs more specificity to be convincing.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that ">92% of all nascent RNAs made by polymerases II and III are concentrated in extra-nucleolar clusters" is cited but not independently verifiable from the provided text.
- The assertion that "most genomic contacts involve active units" is presented as a general finding, but the cited evidence is from specific cell types and may not be universally applicable.
- The proposal that "segments from essentially all windows in the genome should be seen repeatedly in higher order concatemers" is a strong prediction that is not justified by existing data.
- The claim that "overexpressing a factor >2-fold in simulations simplifies small-world networks" is not explained in sufficient detail to assess its biological relevance.
- The statement that "evolution might have 'played with' only a tiny fraction of available sequence space" is speculative and not supported by direct evidence.
