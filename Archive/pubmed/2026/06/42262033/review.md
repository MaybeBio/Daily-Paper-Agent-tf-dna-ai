## Review setup
- **Input scope** Abstract only
- **Assessment boundary** Claims and conclusions as presented in the abstract; no evaluation of full-text data, figures, or methods
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The abstract claims that transcription factors (TFs) represent a promising third-generation core sensing element for low-cost, on-site rapid detection of food contaminants, owing to intrinsic transcriptional signal amplification, suitability for small molecules and heavy metal ions, inexpensive microbial production, and integration with synthetic biology and AI-assisted analysis. It further claims to summarize TF architecture, discovery, and engineering, introduce a proximal mutagenesis strategy, and outline a coordinated optimization approach.
- **Visible evidence base** Abstract text only; no figures, tables, methods, or experimental data provided
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Full manuscript text, all figures and tables, experimental protocols, validation data, comparison with existing sensing elements, and any AI-related methodology or results

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** The abstract presents a plausible and timely perspective on transcription factor-based biosensors for food safety. The concept of TFs as a third-generation recognition element is intellectually appealing and builds on established strengths of transcriptional amplification. However, the abstract is largely programmatic and lacks specific evidence, quantitative benchmarks, or critical comparison with existing technologies. The scientific case for the "promising" designation is asserted rather than demonstrated. The review's novelty and practical impact cannot be assessed from the abstract alone.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in biosensor development, food safety diagnostics, synthetic biology, and analytical chemistry would find this review relevant. Practitioners seeking low-cost, field-deployable detection methods for small-molecule contaminants and heavy metal ions would also be interested, particularly if the review provides actionable engineering guidance and performance comparisons.
- **Major strengths** The abstract identifies a genuine gap in recognition element diversity for food contaminants. The emphasis on intrinsic signal amplification and microbial production cost advantages is well founded. The proposed integration of TF engineering with AI-assisted analysis is forward-looking and could appeal to a broad interdisciplinary audience.
- **Major Concerns** The abstract does not provide any comparative performance data against antibody- or aptamer-based sensors. The "proximal mutagenesis strategy" is mentioned but not explained, leaving its novelty and utility unverifiable. The claim that AI-assisted analysis identifies "performance-defining functional sites" is unsupported by any methodological detail or example. The scope of the review, including how many TFs are covered and for which contaminants, is not stated.
- **Minor Comments** The abstract would benefit from a clearer statement of the review's scope, including the number of TF families and target analytes covered. The term "third-generation" is used without defining the first and second generations, which may confuse nonspecialist readers. The phrase "coordinated approach" is vague and could be replaced with a more specific description of the optimization workflow.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** None can be identified from the abstract alone, as no technical data are presented. However, the absence of any quantitative evidence means the central claim of "promising" performance is currently not established.
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** Originality: Moderate. The concept of TF-based sensors is not new, but framing TFs as a distinct "third-generation" class is a useful organizing idea. Scientific importance: Potentially high for applied food safety, but the abstract does not demonstrate impact beyond assertion. Interdisciplinary readership: The topic bridges molecular biology, analytical chemistry, and food science, which is attractive, but the abstract lacks sufficient technical detail to engage specialists. Technical soundness: Cannot be assessed from the abstract; no methods or data are provided. Readability for nonspecialists: The abstract is generally clear but uses jargon such as "proximal mutagenesis" and "cognate DNA-binding sites" without explanation.
- **Recommendation posture** Currently not established from the provided evidence. The abstract outlines a plausible review, but the scientific case for TF-based sensors as a "promising third-generation" platform requires full-text evidence, including performance comparisons, engineering case studies, and validation data.
