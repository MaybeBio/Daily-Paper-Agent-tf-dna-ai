# Language model-based self-training reduces labeled data requirements by 99% for biological sequence classification


## Abstract
  The prediction of a biological sequence’s function is an exploding field that could prove to be an invaluable tool in deciphering molecular mechanisms of diseases and genetic variation. However, existing techniques suffer from a very low number of experimentally verified labels as well as the complexity of modeling sequence context. Previous work only focuses on semi-supervised learning (SSL) or pre-trained language models (PLMs) individually and does not consider their complementarity: whereas PLMs capture syntactic conservatism and long-range dependency from huge unlabeled sequence data as features, SSL updates label-level certainty via confidence-weighted pseudo-label selection. To realize integrating these benefits, we make a creative use of PLMs as strong feature extractors to make the sequence semantics extracted, and meanwhile leverage the SSL to constrain the decision boundary by selecting reliable pseudo-labels. We show that our framework achieves competitive performance compared to full supervision while using far fewer labeled samples, on two biological prediction tasks: DNA-binding protein (DBP) and non-coding RNA (ncRNA) detection. In general, our approach, developed here, not only offers an efficient solution for discovering new DBP and ncRNA in low-resource settings where experimental verification is infeasible, but also lays a solid methodological foundation for biological sequence classification in computational biology.

## Introduction
  Recent decades have witnessed an accelerating explosion of biological sequence data induced by rapid progress of high-throughput sequencing techniques, which has generated unrivaled possibilities for the mechanism elucidation of complex diseases through molecular pattern recognition. Novel computational approaches to accurately identify regulatory features–such as DNA-binding proteins (DBP) and non-coding RNA (ncRNA) functional domains–can now be leveraged to systematically formulate genotype-phenotype association models elucidating pathogenic drivers in cancer and cardiometabolic conditions.

  Most of the existing computational methods for biological sequenceclassification imitate the supervised learning (SL) mode; theyusually consist of the following major steps: (i) sequenceencoding and feature selection and (ii)model training and testing. In the first stage, sequencefunction-specific features are designed from prior knowledge aboutthe functional features in the biological sequences. For example,sequence composition statistics count the occurrences of thenucleotides (for DNA/RNA) or amino acids (for proteins) and can discriminate between the sequence types by representing the intrinsic biases in the compositional profiles. It is one of the basic features in DBP/non-DBP discrimination. Methods such as DBPPred [1] exploited both amino acid frequency and hybrid feature selection for more discriminative power. iDNA-Prot [2] goes a step further and uses amino acid distance-pairs and reduced alphabet profiles, capturing both compositional biases and spatial proximities of amino acid residues important for DNA binding. In mRNA/ncRNA discrimination, methods such as CPPred [3] exploit such compositional characteristics via its Composition, Transition, and Distribution (CTD) descriptors, representing nucleotide composition, transition between nearest-neighbor nucleotides, and distribution of nucleotides located at particular sites in a sequence. Also, two other popular potential coding predictors, CPAT [4] and CPC2 [5], incorporate hexamer usage bias and Open Reading Frame (ORF)-related compositional features to identify coding sequences, because they both are connected with the biological process of protein synthesis. PLEK [6] utilizes a redefined k-mer-based approach to extract sequence compositional patterns in the absence of reference genomes, and is able to tolerate the noisy indel error character of the sequencing data. After the extraction of the features, feature selection is applied to select the most discriminative features for classification. Methods like CPPred employ the minimum Redundancy Maximum Relevance-Incremental Feature Selection [7] method to select discriminating features, which can be used to produce a refined representation to reduce noise and focus on the most biologically informative patterns for coding potential prediction. In addition, DPP-PseAAC [8] ranks feature importance contributions to the classification accuracy by Random Forest for DBP/RBP prediction and selects the positive-value feature importance contributions. It is followed by the application of the support vector machine with recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) algorithm [9], whose steps are to successively eliminate the least important features and select an optimal set that trades off predictive power and computational efficiency. The second step consists of building predictive classifiers in the form of machine learning models, trained on the extracted features. Well-known techniques are SVMs, artificial neural networks, and random forests. For the mRNA/ncRNA classification, CPC2 also uses an SVM with an RBF kernel trained from sequence intrinsic features using LIBSVM [10]. For DBP/non-DBP prediction, the iDNA-Prot—dis [11] employs an SVM with an RBF kernel, and the model is trained on amino acid distance-pairs and the reduced alphabet profiles to learn spatial and compositional patterns. iDNAProt-ES [12] indicates that the linear kernel SVMs with RFE-evaluated evolutionary and structural information are effective in DBP prediction. TargetDBP+ [13] is a DBP prediction SVM-based approach that employs a weighted multi-view feature set to improve the discriminative properties through learning the feature weights with a differential evolution algorithm and makes use of the RBF kernel for final classification. We select ones suited to high-dimensional biological features that generalize well over diverse sequence datasets. However, despite their popularity, these SL-based methods for biosequence classification have several main limitations: (i) Complexity of sequence representation. They need not only a complicated feature extraction process, but also abundant prior knowledge of biological sequence. (ii) Scarcity of sequence labels. The economic expense and technical constraints for experimental verification pipelines, like chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing [14] for studying chromatin interactions or Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats (CRISPR)-based functional screening [15] for elucidating gene functions, are considerable. These methods require specialized equipment and training infrastructure and are expensive to implement, which can constrain the quantity of data collected. This double-barreled constraint imposes a paradoxical “data gap,” limiting the operational scalability of SL approaches in biological sequence analysis.

  The semi-supervised learning (SSL) paradigm [16] has been a key tool used to break the label scarcity bottleneck for supervised biological sequence prediction. At the heart of SSL is the assumption that the distribution of the unlabeled data carries some latent structural properties that can be effectively leveraged to improve model generalization. It takes advantage of a few labeled samples and many unlabeled samples for performance boosting. However, despite the success of this approach, the literature lacks notable research that applies this to the task of biological sequence classification. Reference [17] presents a SSL based on cross-species pseudo labeling for learning the regulatory DNA sequences for predicting, the evolutionary conservation to increase the number of training data. Models trained using small SSL outperform large language models (e.g. DNABERT2 [18]) and enhance prediction of SNP effects, especially in the case of low-data TFs. In addition, other work [19] presents an active semi-supervised setting with random-diversity-based sampling for bioinformatics data. It focuses on the boundary points via a simple linear function to save expenses of annotation, gaining 20% higher accuracy with less annotated samples and computation faster than the traditional algorithms.

  At the same time, the development of PLMs has brought newprospects to address the label-lacking problem. By self-SL on gigantic unlabeled datasets (e.g. GENCODE, RefSeq), PLMs capture hierarchical representations of deep syntax-semantics relationships from trillions of unlabeled biological sequences without the need for expensive labeled data, and thus they can achieve state-of-the-art (SOTA) in biological sequence classification. Examples of note are RNABERT [20], which uses a convolutional sequence encoder to transform nucleotide sequences into position-preserving representations, together with a Transformer backbone to encode long-range interactions, achieving a gain of 28% in area under the ROC curve (AUC) for long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) subcellular localization prediction. In protein informatics, the models are like ProtTrans [21] that use a variety of transformer models (BERT [22], T5 [23], etc.) and learn residue-level embeddings by pretraining on billions of sequences obtained from UniRef50, where UniRef50 embeddings pass the evolutionary constraints, physical chemical properties, structural motifs, as verified by their dominance in tasks like secondary structure prediction or pathogenicity variant annotation. Similarly, ProteinBERT [24], a BERT-style protein embedding model, is also shown to be competitive when it comes to annotating protein sequences, predicting protein structure, etc., by incorporating the context of amino acids. All these results indicate the potential of PLMs to capture transferable biological knowledge from unlabeled data.

  In view of the promising abilities of SSL and PLMs to alleviate the problems associated with label scarcity and sequence complexity in biological sequence classification, we are motivated to build a general and effective approach for biological sequence classification by marrying SSL and PLMs, which takes advantage of the best of both models above. SSL produces and iteratively refines high-quality pseudo-labels using confidence-weighted filtering based on the unlabeled samples to optimize decision boundaries, and PLMs offer stronger initial features to help generate reliable pseudo-labels. By updating the model parameter in a self-closed loop, the architecture is gradually evolved towards target domains, driving a scalable and powerful solution for biological sequence classification that alleviates label scarcity yet maximizes feature extraction effectiveness. ## Materials and methods

### Datasets

#### DBP/non-DBP
  Three benchmark datasets that have been used in TargetDBP, TargetDBP+, and DBP2Vec [25] are also used here: (i) DBP1: Curated from the Protein Data Bank (PDB, May 2018), containing 1052 DBPs and 1052 non-DBPs for training (PDB-Tr) and 148 pairs are used for testing (PDB296), filtered for 25% sequence identity. (ii) DBP2: Derived from UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot (October 2020), with 4500 DBPs/non-DBPs for training (UniSwiss-Tr) and 381 pairs for testing (UniSwiss-Tst), processed identically to PDB. (iii) DBP3: 76 novel DBPs that are experimentally validated are downloaded from UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot from November 2022 to April 2023 and filtered to remove sequences with 25% identity to UniSwiss-Tr.


#### mRNA/ncRNA
  Thestudy employed three benchmark datasets for mRNA/ncRNAclassification, which has been used in CPPred and CPPVec [26]: (i) CPP1: Composed of 33 360 human mRNAs (NCBI RefSeq) and 24 163 ncRNAs (Ensembl) for training, and 8557 mRNAs/8241 ncRNAs for testing. (ii) CPP2: Combined multi-species data (human, mouse, zebrafish, fruit fly, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, nematode, and Arabidopsis) with 52 530 mRNAs and 27 600 ncRNAs for training, and a balanced testing set of 13 903 mRNAs/ncRNAs. (iii) CPP3: 34 novel lncRNAs from the EVlncRNAs database [27], experimentally validated by low-throughput experiments (e.g. qRT-PCR, knockdown [28]), then filtered by CD-HIT [29] tool to exclude sequences with 80% identity to training data.


### Language models-based self-training
  In order to mitigate the generalization of sequence classifiers in the few-shot labels, we introduce a language model-based self-training model that takes advantage of PLMs and self-training. The approach generates this filtered subset through iterative updating of predictions (by including high-confidence unlabeled samples in the training set) building on distributed representations from PLMs, e.g. ESM-1b, and doc2vec. As shown in Fig. 1, the proposed model is constructed by three primary modules, including: (i) Embedding Generation. For each sequence, we create its embeddings using the PLMs. (ii) Initial model training. The classifier is trained using the labeled data. (iii) Iterative self-training. The trained classifier sequentially predicts unlabeled data, reserves high-confidence samples as pseudo-labeled data, and retrains the classifier by the pseudo-labeled and original labeled data cyclically, so as to optimize iteratively. Algorithm 1 also shows the detailed process of the proposed method.


#### ESM-1b

ESM-1b processes protein sequences based on a 1280-dimensional distributed representation. Furthermore, it is essential to recognize that although ESM-1b utilizes massive external data, it serves strictly as a static feature extractor. Its immense latent potential cannot be fully realized without our self-training framework, which acts as the vital tool to dynamically constrain and refine the decision boundaries in low-data regimes.


#### Doc2vec
  This paper also uses the approach of distributed representation of sequence for mRNA/ncRNA classification, as does our previous work, CPPVec. Doc2vec [31] is a classical model in this category that has been used in a variety of natural language tasks. e.g. text classification. This model has shown the capacity to effectively find semantic-rich embeddings from language. Motivated by the similarity in structure between biological sequences and linguistic syntax, we utilize a doc2vec’s Distributed Memory Model of Paragraph Vectors (PV-DM) architecture to capture the protein sequences that are translated from the longest ORFs of RNA sequences that were selected for their functional over raw RNA sequences. Protein sequences are first tokenized into non-overlapping triplets (e.g. “MNF,” “LLS”) and utilized as corpus, while sequence IDs and their context “words” are co-embedded to maintain structural semantics. Crucially, the doc2vec model is trained exclusively on the training set partitions of our datasets, ensuring zero data leakage from the testing sets. The resulting 100-d vectors, obtained through stochastic gradient descent, have learned latent features about likely coding regions.

## Results and discussion

### Leveraging unlabeled data boosts classification accuracy
  We have investigated the role of unlabeled samples for model generalization in SSL. The experiment was initiated from a strongly supervised setup; the basic model (SVM0, a pure SL is the control group) was constructed using only 100 labeled samples (50 positive samples and 50 negative samples). Crucially, to ensure a fair comparison and isolate the effect of the learning paradigm, both this supervised control group and our semi-supervised approach were built upon the exact same high-quality features extracted from the PLMs. Then, unlabeled data were added into the training gradually through several iterations using a pseudo-label optimization method that combined self-training and co-training to dynamically adjust the model parameters.

  To monitor the training process, we divided each dataset into five kinds of subsets for multi-dimensional evaluation: a validation set to dynamically monitor overfitting risks and implement early stopping strategies, an intermediate evaluation set (Inter Set) to track the model’s overall performance during iterative training, an independent test set to reflect the model’s final predictive capability on unseen data, an SVM0 Test Set (Pure Supervised Test Set) as the baseline control group to demonstrate performance with only 100 labeled samples, and a Training Set to monitor the model’s fitting degree on training data.

  This experimental design allows us to clearly dissect the independent contributions of our framework’s two key components. Under an extreme semi-supervised scenario (1% labeled data + 99% unlabeled data), the model showed significant accuracy improvements: accuracy improved from 67.4% to 87.8% on the DBP1 test set, while it improved from 59.9% (supervised-only) to 91.3% on the DBP2 test set, demonstrating unlabeled data’s impact on decision boundaries. Here, the initial supervised-only accuracies (67.4% and 59.9%) represent the strong foundational contribution from the PLM features alone. The subsequent dramatic increase to 87.8% and 91.3% directly quantifies the substantial additional value provided by the self-training algorithm, which effectively harnesses unlabeled data to refine the decision boundaries. This phenomenon can be explained by the cluster assumption in SSL: the PLMs map sequences into a semantic space where functional classes are naturally, separated but the decision boundary is ill-defined due to sparse labels. The iterative addition of unlabeled data effectively “smooths” this boundary, propagating label information along the data manifold to cover the empty regions between sparse labeled points. Similarly, in mRNA/ncRNA classification, where it is crucial to note that the doc2vec feature extractor was trained exclusively from scratch on the local training database, ensuring zero exposure to massive external pre-training corpora, accuracy rose from 91.2% to 93.8% on the CPP1 test set, and from 91.9% to 94.4% on the CPP2 test set. This confirms that the self-training framework effectively enhances data structure utilization and drives high-quality classification independently of any external pre-training advantage. Figure 2 plots model accuracy trends across iterations for distinct datasets under the self-training process with a 100 labeled training set. These plots track how the accuracy of the Training Set, Validation Set, Inter Set, and Test Set evolves through iterative unlabeled data integration. We can observe that iterative unlabeled data utilization drives accuracy convergence.

  We further carried out a detailed comparative analysis of performance improvements and observed under which labeled data scales that do not affect the final performance. Experimental results demonstrated a consistent upward trend in model accuracy as the amount of labeled data increased. As can be seen from Fig. 3, the minimal initial labeled sample can be only 100 (about 1% of the training set). Notably, in label-scarce datasets like DBP1 and DBP2, the role of unlabeled data became particularly pronounced, the model exhibited remarkable improvements. As for the CPP1 and CPP2 datasets, unlabeled data exerts less effect, likely attributable to doc2vec’s pre-trained feature effectiveness. Ultimately, this analysis reveals a synergistic, two-stage benefit: PLMs provide a robust feature foundation that enables effective learning from few labels, while the self-training framework builds upon this foundation to unlock significant further gains by leveraging the vast pool of unlabeled data.


### Language model-based self-training beats the other two semi-supervised methods in ncRNA and DBPs prediction
  We compared our self-training approach with classic semi-supervised methods such as two semi-supervised methods (TSVMs) [32] and Seed K-means [33] to assess its efficacy. We deliberately chose these established benchmarks over more recent frameworks like MixMatch [34] for two key reasons. First, modern consistency-based methods depend heavily on data augmentation, which is a significant and unresolved challenge for biological sequences where simple transformations can alter or destroy biological function. Second, our model already incorporates a powerful form of self-supervision by using a PLM to derive features from vast unlabeled data, representing a different paradigm. Therefore, a comparison with methods that do not rely on such complex augmentations is more direct and informative.

  As shown in Fig. 4, our approach achieved the best or highly competitive results on all the benchmarks, with the exception of DBP1. Under the DBP1/non-DBP classification, for the DBP1 dataset, our method attained a slightly worse sensitivity (SN = 94.4%) than TSVM in the DBP/non-DBP decisions, but achieved a superior specificity (85.87%) and comparable performance overall as TSVM on other performance measures. On the DBP2 benchmark, our method achieved an ACC of 91.32% and an MCC of 0.828, which were substantially higher than those obtained by TSVM (ACC=83.3%, MCC=0.667) and Seed K-means (ACC=57.5%, MCC=0.156). The poor performance of Seed K-means can be attributed to its reliance on Euclidean distance metrics, which often fail to capture the complex, non-linear biological manifolds present in high-dimensional embedding spaces. Similarly, while TSVM is effective, it is notoriously sensitive to “noisy” samples near the decision boundary. By contrast, our method employs a dual-threshold confidence strategy (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\tau _{1}, \tau _{2}$\end{document}), which effectively filters out ambiguous predictions, preventing the “error propagation” that typically degrades iterative SSL. Regarding the specific mRNA/ncRNA classification, it maintained similarly high specificity (SP=97.23%), but markedly improved sensitivity (SN=90.70%) and MCC (0.880) at the CPP1 test set, compared with that of TSVM (SN=83.4%, MCC=0.759) and Seed K-means (SN=35.5%, MCC=0.455). On the CPP2 dataset, our method has clearly outperformed its competitors TSVM (ACC: 90.0%, F1: 0.898) and Seed K-means (ACC: 65.7%, F1: 0.519) with an accuracy (ACC) and F1 score of 94.47% and 0.945, respectively. These results suggest that our proposed language model-based self-training is a highly effective strategy in improving low-data conditions.


### Language model-basedself-training achieves comparable DBPs and ncRNAs performance to full supervision
  In order to verify the efficacy of our proposed approach, we compared it with the SOTA SL methods. To provide a comprehensive benchmark, we selected baseline models representing a diverse range of feature engineering strategies. For DBP prediction, these include methods based on traditional biological features (DPP-PseAAC, iDNAProt-ES), advanced multi-view feature learning (TargetDBP, TargetDBP+), and most importantly, a recent PLM-based method that operates in a fully supervised manner (DBP2Vec). This allows us to not only compare against classical approaches but also to directly assess the performance gains achieved by our semi-supervised framework over a supervised PLM-based counterpart. A similar rationale was applied to ncRNA prediction, where baselines range from k-mer and ORF-based methods (PLEK, CPAT, CPC2) to our own previous fully-supervised, PLM-based work (CPPVec). It should be mentioned that in all these approaches are based on the assumption that we have all the labels in the training set, whereas our approach uses only 1% in the training set. Also, in contrast to these multiple biological feature-based approaches, we take only features from a PLM and do not exploit any prior information about a biological sequence.

  For DBP/non-DBP prediction, we compared our method with: DBP2Vec [25], TargetDBP+ [13], TargetDBP [35], iDNAProt-ES [12], and DPP-PseAAC [8]. As shown in Table 1, our model achieved an ACC of 87.84% on DBP1, higher than DBP2Vec (85.12%) and other methods (e.g. TargetDBP+: 76.69%). This notable improvement over the fully supervised DBP2Vec highlights the core strength of our approach: the self-training cycle effectively refines the model’s decision boundary by leveraging the vast unlabeled data, capturing patterns that are missed when training on the small labeled set alone. It excelled in sensitivity (SN = 91.01%) and F1-score (0.879), outperforming DBP2Vec by 9.07% and 0.025, respectively. While DBP2Vec has slightly superior specificity (SP = 88.89%) compared to our method (85.87%), our method shows superior overall balance, with 5.4% higher MCC (0.758 and 0.704). Precision (PRE = 86.02 %) was slightly lower than DBP2Vec (87.41 %). It is worth noting that the sharp contrast between iDNAProt-ES’s sensitivity (95.95%) and specificity (41.22%) on DBP1 reveals a tendency toward excessive false positives. This indicates that while the model captures DBP motifs well, it is prone to misclassifying non-DBPs as positives due to partial structural resemblance. With respect to DBP2, we obtained 91.32% ACC shown in Table 2, which is very close to the best performing DBP2Vec (92.22%) and much higher than the other approaches (e.g. TargetDBP+: 85.83%). The performance gap between our method and the fully-supervised DBP2Vec is minimal, demonstrating the remarkable data efficiency of our framework—achieving near-SOTA performance with 99% less annotation cost. This trade-off between a minor performance decrease and a 99% reduction in labeling effort is the central value proposition of our approach in resource-constrained scenarios. This result also confirms that the PLM (ESM-1b) has already captured the fundamental “grammar” of protein function during pre-training; the labeled data is required primarily to align these features with the specific DBP task rather than to learn the representations from scratch. Importantly, even though ESM-1b provides a distinct advantage through its external pre-training corpus, it functions merely as a feature extraction instrument. The self-training mechanism acts as the indispensable tool necessary to unlock this potential; without iteratively constraining the decision boundaries using unlabeled data, the high-quality representations of ESM-1b would remain severely underutilized under extreme label scarcity. It also has higher specificity (SP = 93.96%) and precision (PRE = 93.61%) than DBP2Vec, which is an advantage to remove false positives. DBP2Vec was slightly better in terms of sensitivity (SN = 91.59%) and F1-score (F1 = 0.921) compared to us (SN = 89.21%, F1 = 0.911), though a competitive MCC valued (MCC = 0.828) of our method was very close to DBP2Vec (MCC = 0.844).

  For mRNA/ncRNA prediction, our approach was compared to SOTA methods (CPPred [3], CPAT [4], CPC2 [5], PLEK [6]). As listed in Table 3, we presented promising specificity (SP=97.23%) and precision (PRE=97.14%) on CPP1, which were slightly lower than CPPVec (SP=98.69%, PRE=98.71) but higher than these competitors (e.g. CPC2: SP=95.30%). Sensitivity (SN=90.70%) was relatively lower than CPPVec (96.67%) and PLEK (95.42%), but balanced overall accuracy (ACC=93.89% vs CPC2’s 93.07%) and discriminative ability (AUC=0.988, the second to CPPVec’s 0.997). As reported in Table 4, we obtained the accuracy (ACC) of 94.47% on dataset CPP2, which was close to CPPVec (98.05%) and overwhelming compared to others (such as CPC2: 93.40%). Our method demonstrated good specificity (SP=94.49%) and PRE (94.48%) (high sensitivity SN=94.33%; an F1-score of 0.945), which is much better than PLEK (SN=66.32%, F1=0.753). For both ncRNA datasets, the fully-supervised CPPVec sets a very high performance ceiling, suggesting the doc2vec features are inherently powerful for this task. The key finding here is not about surpassing the SOTA, but about achieving highly competitive performance (e.g. 94.47% vs 98.05% ACC on CPP2) while drastically reducing the dependency on labeled data. This demonstrates the practical viability of our method in real-world, resource-constrained scenarios.   In summary, we find that our language model-based self-training approach competes with fully supervised methods for biological sequence classification and offers an efficient, scalable alternative for transcriptome analysis, which is particularly interesting for: (i) ncRNA or DBP prediction in novel species and (ii) large-scale analyses since it keeps its high accuracy even when reducing its annotation dependency.


### Performance on experimentally annotated ncRNAs and DBPs
  To test how well our method would perform in discovering novel functional biomolecules, this work carried out thorough testing with two sets of independent confirmed in vivo experiment datasets, such as DBP3 and CPP3. Our approach achieved excellent identification performance in the DBP3 test set, that is, accurately identifying 64 DBPs, achieving an accuracy of 84.2% with a 95% Wilson score [36] confidence interval of [74.2%, 91.0%]. Of which 90.6% (58/64) of the positive samples had prediction probability exceeding 0.9, suggesting that the model had a high-confidence identification capacity with regard to DBPs. On the CPP3 test set, the model achieved very high results as well, correctly annotating 31 lncRNAs, yielding an accuracy of 91.2% with a 95% Wilson score confidence interval of [77.1%, 97.0%]. With 90% of true lncRNAs having a prediction probability of less than 0.3, this confirmed the prediction quality of ncRNAs. This result reveals that our proposed method is accurate and reliable for discovering novel biomolecules.


### Computational efficiency and runtime analysis
  We also studied the efficiency of using our proposed SSL framework. The experimental results suggest that there are many factors that interact with one another in determining the convergence time across datasets, and that sequence number, feature dimensionality, and per-round update number are particularly important. In particular, for the smaller DBP1 dataset, the convergence was fast (50–80 s) thanks to its smaller size, while for the other (DBP2) convergence took longer times, from 1500 to 6000 s. This intermediate duration is indicative of its sample size and sequence dimensionality, combined with relatively loose per-round updates. For the large CPP1 dataset, we increased the number of per-round updates to better utilize data, which led to better model performance at the cost of significantly higher computational resources. The combined need of complex sequence data with a large number of candidate features leads to a remarkable resource and convergence time (3000–4000 s). The CPP2 dataset was particularly challenging due to its cross-species nature. Even when using optimized parameter values, the biological variability between species increased the model-learning complexity drastically, resulting in long convergence times of about 4000—8000 s. This emphasizes the flexibility of the framework in different data scales and biological contexts while maintaining computational tractability.


## Conclusion
  This study presented a novel approach for biological sequence classification that integrates PLMs with SSL to help address the challenges of limited labeled data. By virtue of PLMs as strong feature extractors to learn the contextual regularity inside unannotated sequences and applying SSL to refine decision boundaries with high-confidence pseudo labeling, the proposed method reaches SOTA performance with much less annotation needed. Experimental results on two case studies for the DBP and ncRNA tasks show that it performs better than the purely supervised methods. Remarkably, with only a fraction of the labeled data, the proposed framework achieves performance that is competitive with, and in some cases surpasses, the fully supervised baselines. This work presents a scalable solution for low-resource biological sequence analysis and also lays a solid methodological foundation for biological sequence classification, where the experimental validation is expensive or infeasible.
