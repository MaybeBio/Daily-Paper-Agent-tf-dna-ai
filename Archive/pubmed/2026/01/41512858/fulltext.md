# Chromatin folding principles underlying the generation of antibody diversity


## SUMMARY
  Effective adaptive immunity requires generation of a diverse repertoire of antigen receptors via V(D)J recombination. To illuminate underlying mechanisms, we combined biophysical simulations with experimental data to model chromatin folding and dynamics of the mouse immunoglobulin heavy chain gene (Igh) locus. Simulations that best recapitulated experimental data on locus structure and recombination of Igh alleles identified three novel chromatin folding principles. First, we found that prominent structural features of the Igh locus, such as the 3’-anchored stripe, required cohesin loading throughout the locus. Second, the Eμ enhancer was best modeled as a bi-directional loop extrusion blocker though it does not bind CTCF. Third, we found that utilization of VH genes to obtain maximum diversity required both widespread cohesin loading as well as long-range associations between H3K27ac-marked regions. Our findings provide a conceptual framework to understand chromatin folding principles that enable antibody diversity and reveal mechanisms of long-range genome communication.


## INTRODUCTION
  Adaptive immunity protects against a variety of pathogens, including previously unencountered newly emergent strains. Effective immunity relies on millions of unique antigen receptors on B and T lymphocytes, the cellular mediators of adaptive immunity. This diverse repertoire of antigen receptors is generated by randomly rearranging hundreds of gene segments spread over megabases of the genome into functional genes via V(D)J recombination1,2. The process requires synapsis, the physical association between gene segments undergoing recombination1. While enzymes involved in recombination have been extensively studied3, it is less clear how recombining gene segments are brought into spatial proximity for these enzymes to function.

  The 2.8Mb mouse immunoglobulin heavy chain gene (Igh) locus comprises approximately 100 variable (VH) gene segments (2.4Mb) and a 3’ domain (260kb) that contains 8-12 diversity (DH) gene segments and 4 joining (JH) gene segments followed by exons that encode various IgH isotypes4 (Figure 1A). Two recombination events juxtapose VH, DH and JH gene segments into an exon that encodes the variable domain of antibody heavy chains. DH to JH rearrangements occur first during B cell development creating DJH junctions that are targeted by VH gene segments in the second recombination step5. Distances from DJH junctions to VH gene segments range from ~150kb (3’ most) to 2.5MB (5’ most). Despite gene-specific fluctuations of rearrangement frequencies, most VH gene segments are represented quite uniformly in the VDJ repertoire6–8. Maximally utilizing gene segments results in the most diverse repertoire of IgH variable domains for future immune responses. Here we probe mechanisms by which VH gene segments spread over a large genomic expanse recombine to DJH junctions at the 3’ end of the locus at comparable frequencies.

  Spatial proximity of distal VH genomic regions to the 3’ end of the Igh locus in pro-B cells was demonstrated by fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH)9,10. This phenomenon, referred to as locus contraction,-requires the transcription factors Pax511 and Yy112 and an intact enhancer, Eμ13, located within the 3’ Igh domain. It was reasonably inferred that locus contraction promotes distal VH recombination, a view that was substantiated by skewed utilization of 3’ VH gene segments in Pax5- or Yy1-deficient pro-B cells12,14. However, resolution of light microscopy precluded rigorous molecular understanding of the nature of locus contraction and how it drives equal access of widely dispersed VH gene segments for recombination.

  The three-dimensional structure of interphase chromosomes is established by cohesin, an ATP-driven molecular motor15–17 through a process known as loop extrusion18,19. Cohesin is loaded onto the genome by a protein complex containing Nipped-B-like protein (Nipbl)17. Thereafter, it translocates in either direction along the genome, extruding a loop of DNA as it moves. Extrusion halts at DNA-bound CTCF or upon Wapl-mediated unloading20,21. The duration of cohesin on the genome thus determines the size of loops formed. Accordingly, genetic depletion of Wapl leads to longer cohesin residence times and longer-range genomic interactions20. Busslinger and colleagues showed that Pax5 represses Wapl expression in pro-B cells to mediate Igh locus contraction22. However, molecular mechanisms by which extended cohesin residence promotes remote VH recombination remain unclear.

  Concurrent studies led to the idea that cohesin-dependent loop extrusion is used by RAG1/2 recombinase to identify pairs of gene segments to recombine23. In this RAG scanning model, cohesin tethered at the RAG1/2-rich recombination center (RC) located in the 3’ Igh domain24 reels in distal DNA thereby sequentially presenting recombination signal sequences (RSSs) of gene segments located at increasing distances from the RC to synapse with RSSs of gene segments located at the RC23. At the Igh locus, unidirectional loop extrusion from the RC towards the 5’ end (left to right in Figure 1A) would first encounter DH-associated RSSs to promote DH to JH recombination. Further extrusion would subsequently place VH-associated RSSs near the RC for the second rearrangement step. This model explains the rearrangement preference of 3’DH RSSs and is consistent with the need to reduce Wapl expression to promote long-distance loop extrusion from the RC for distal VH rearrangements. However, current models of this process are qualitative and largely inferred from end state analyses of recombination products or chromatin structure. Previous quantitative models using FISH or Hi-C data25,26 inferred static structures but omitted dynamic processes like loop extrusion, thus failing to explain locus formation or dispersed VH usage. Moreover, unidirectional loop extrusion from the RC towards the 5’ end of Igh fails to adequately explain distance independent VH utilization across the 2.5 Mb VH region.

  We combined biophysical simulations with experimental data to generate mechanistic insights into chromatin dynamics that promote a diverse Igh repertoire. Unlike prevailing dogma, we found that prominent structural features of the locus, such as stripes anchored at the 3’ end that are observed in Hi-C contact maps, required cohesin loading throughout the Igh locus. Chromatin fine structure of the 3’ Igh domain was only recapitulated by modeling Eμ enhancer, a CTCF-lacking regulatory sequence, as a bi-directional loop extrusion blocker. Unidirectional extrusion predicted only proximal VH rearrangement frequencies, whereas distal recombination required cohesin loading throughout the locus. Interactions between H3K27ac-marked regions in the VH domain and Eμ provided additional tethering of remote genomic regions to stabilize distal VH interactions with the RC. Our model correctly predicted rearrangement patterns of wild type and mutated Igh alleles, including large-scale genomic inversions. These mechanistic principles can be applied to all antigen receptor loci.


## RESULTS
  Cohesin-dependent loop extrusion stalls at oppositely oriented CTCF-bound sites18,19. Cohesin stalled at such sites may dissociate from the genome or continue to extrude after a pause regulated, for example, by the half-life of CTCF bound at that site27. The duration of cohesin extrusion also depends upon processivity of the complex. This is established in part by Wapl, a component of the cohesin off-loader20. Mirny and colleagues have developed a computational model to simulate this process of cohesin-mediated loop extrusion for specific regions of the genome18,28. Here, we applied this approach to simulate the 3D structure and dynamics of the mouse Igh locus. We modeled the 2.8Mb locus as a polymer composed of monomers representing 1kb bins. Additionally, we incorporated the unique positions and orientations of CTCF binding sites observed in the Igh locus (Figure 1A) as pause sites for cohesin. Because of variation in where cohesin loads, how many blocks it encounters and how long it remains bound to the genome, each simulation generates a contact map that represents the state of a single allele. To compare with experimental data, we performed 10,000 simulations for each condition tested and generated aggregate contact maps representing a cell population (Figure 1B).


### Cohesin loading throughout the Igh locus recapitulates long-range chromatin structure
  The Igh locus forms a topologically associated domain (TAD) visualizable by Hi-C (Figure 1C). The TAD is marked by 3 prominent stripes that originate in the 3’ region and extend until the 5’ end of the locus. These are anchored at 3’CBE (3’ CTCF binding element), Eμ and IGCR1 (Intergenic Control Region 1) (Figure 1C, left). Hi-C stripes have been interpreted as sites of cohesin loading where loop extrusion starts29. We tested this by simulating cohesin loading solely at the 3’CBE, the anchor of the strongest stripe. These conditions resulted in a sharply truncated TAD and no evidence of stripes (Figure 1C, right bottom). By contrast, cohesin loading throughout the locus yielded a Mb-sized TAD and well-defined stripes as observed experimentally (Figure 1C, right top). Our interpretation is that cohesin loaded only at the 3’ end encounters many convergently oriented CTCF sites while ‘reeling in’ the rest of the locus. These CTCF-bound sites block extrusion well before cohesin reaches the 5’ end of the locus, resulting in a truncated TAD. Instead, cohesin molecules loaded in the 5’ part of the locus extrude unimpeded until terminated by CTCF bound at 3’CBE (Figure 1D). We infer that Igh stripe anchors serve as extrusion termination sites.

  We assessed the impact of altering Wapl levels on Igh stripes. This was modeled by changing the average cohesin residence time on the genome, where lower levels of Wapl corresponded to higher cohesin residence times (Figure S1A). In the simulations, the average cohesin residence time was defined as the mean number of simulation time steps that cohesin performed loop extrusion before dissociating from the locus. We found that increasing cohesin residence times resulted in a 3’CBE stripe that extended across the entire Igh locus, whereas lower cohesin residence times reduced the 3’ CBE stripe (Figure S1B, right). Contact maps and contact frequency vs. distance plots generated from simulations with short cohesin residence times resembled Hi-C contact maps from a pro-B cell line (Figures S1B left and S1C) in which Wapl is not as effectively repressed as in primary pro-B cells23. Both the short cohesin residence time simulations and pro-B cell line Hi-C contact maps showed an absence of long-range interactions across the Igh locus that are characteristic of a contracted locus. By comparing contact maps from simulations with varying cohesin residence times, we found that a 4-fold increase in cohesin residence time was sufficient to generate both a contracted Igh locus and an extended 3’CBE stripe (Figure S1D). We infer that the length of the 3’CBE stripe is inversely related to cohesin residence time and is a measure of locus contraction. Previous work has shown that pro-B cells exhibit a 4-fold decrease in Wapl expression relative to lymphoid progenitors22, and while the precise relationship between Wapl expression and cohesin residence time in pro-B cells is unknown, this observation is consistent with our finding that a 4-fold increase in cohesin residence time can generate a contracted Igh locus.


### Eμ is a bidirectional blocker of cohesin extrusion
  The 260 kb 3’ Igh domain is configured by interactions between 3’CBE, Eμ and IGCR1, as shown in capture Hi-C experiments of Igh locus for a pro-B cell line (Figure 2A). All three sequences also contacted CTCF-bound sites near the 3’-most (proximal) VH gene segments. 3’CBE-Eμ interaction defined a 200 kb sub-TAD that contains exons of all antibody isotypes and the small (60kb) sub-TAD between Eμ and IGCR1 contains the most frequently used DH gene segments. These sub-TADs merged into one that extended from the 3’CBE to IGCR1 on Eμ-deleted Igh alleles (Figure 2B), without affecting a sub-TAD that starts at IGCR1 and extends into the proximal VH region. Deletion of IGCR1 resulted in a loop between Eμ and the 3’-most VH gene segment, VH5-2, creating a new sub-TAD between Eμ and VH5-2 (Figure 2C). The distal (5’) end of this new sub-TAD was distinct from that formed between IGCR1 and proximal VH genes on WT alleles. We also noted that the 3’CBE continued to interact with multiple proximal VH gene segments on IGCR1-deleted alleles, whereas Eμ interaction was restricted to only the most proximal VH gene segments. The greatly increased frequency of VH5-2 utilization on IGCR1-deficient30 alleles suggests that an Eμ-dependent sub-structure drives rearrangements more effectively than 3’CBE-dependent interactions. These differences may reflect mechanistically distinct ways by which the CTCF-lacking Eμ and CTCF-containing IGCR1 and 3’CBE generate chromatin sub-structure.

  Our model accurately captured chromatin fine structure of the 3’Igh domain of wild type (WT) and mutated Igh alleles only when Eμ was represented as a bi-directional extrusion blocker (Figure 2D). Modeling Eμ as a uni-directional extrusion blocker or a non-blocking element failed to recapitulate WT structure (Figure S2). We hypothesize that the loops that occur between the 3’CBE, Eμ, IGCR1 and VH5-2 are due to cohesin loading between any two of these sites and extruding until blocked (Figures 2G, 2H and 2I). In WT alleles, these loop extrusion events lead to interactions and sub-TAD formation between 3’CBE-Eμ, Eμ-IGCR1 and IGCR1-VH5-2 (Figure 2D). In Eμ-deleted alleles, cohesin no longer pauses at Eμ, resulting in the loss of loops involving Eμ and the merging of the 3’CBE-Eμ and Eμ-IGCR1 sub-TADs (Figures 2E and 2H). Similarly, loss of cohesin pausing at IGCR1 on IGCR1-deleted alleles removes IGCR1 loops and merges the Eμ-IGCR1 and IGCR1-VH5-2 sub-TADs into one (Figures 2F and 2I). We conclude that the 3’ Igh domain is configured by orientation-dependent cohesin blockade by CTCF-bound sequences and orientation-independent cohesin blockade by the enhancer.

  Contact maps remained consistent whether the number of simulations were halved or doubled, confirming that our observations regarding the 3D structure of the Igh locus were independent of the number of simulations (Figures S3A, B). We also quantified cohesin time spent in stalled, asymmetrically extruding or symmetrically extruding states. We observed that cohesin was predominately in either stalled or asymmetrically extruding states (Figure S3C), consistent with recent in vitro studies31.


### Proximal VH utilization in rearrangements
  To understand chromatin dynamics that underlie the selection of VH gene segments distributed over 2.5 Mb, we modeled VH recombination frequency. For this we calculated the average time each VH gene segment spent within a specified distance from the RC (Figure 3A), reasoning that the propensity of a VH gene segment to recombine was proportional to its proximity to the RC. We are aware that other characteristics such as RSS strength contribute to VH gene usage32. However, we factored only spatial proximity as a surrogate for recombination frequency because it is essential for synapsis and, thereby, initiation of recombination. We carried out 1000 simulations for each condition tested and calculated the fraction of time each VH gene segment contacted the RC (Figure 3A). We first tested the current model that VH rearrangements are mediated by RAG1/2 scanning from the RC into the VH region30.

  For this we determined the average time that each VH gene segment spent in proximity to the RC when cohesin was loaded only at the RC (see Methods). This model reproduced recombination frequencies of only the five closest VH gene segments, each of which has a closely positioned CTCF binding site (Figure 3B). Upon encountering the first CTCF site (near VH5-2) cohesin stalls in most simulations, thereby yielding the highest time near the RC (Figure 3C). Fewer and fewer cohesins proceed to each of the subsequent CTCF-bound sites which is reflected in the calculated average proximity time decaying over the first five proximal VH gene segments (Figure 3C). The close correspondence between the experimentally determined recombination frequencies and proximity times calculated from the simulations suggests that proximal VH utilization is directed by scanning from the RC.

  To further corroborate this mechanism, we tested the response of our computational model to mutations that affect proximal VH rearrangements. In the WT locus the closest rearrangeable VH gene segment (VH5-1) lacks a nearby CTCF binding site (Figure 3D). While VH5-1 recombines rarely in WT, de novo insertion of a nearby CTCF binding site triggers efficient recombination, a pattern captured by our model (Figure 3E). VH5-1 is followed by 5 functional VH gene segments that have CTCF binding sites located within 100bp. Previous studies showed that VH5-2 rearrangements drop precipitously when the CTCF binding site near it is mutated30; concurrently, rearrangements of the next few VH genes increase (Figure 3F, top). In simulations lacking this CTCF site, the average RC proximity times matched the experimental pattern of VH usage (Figure 3F, bottom). We also observed a close match to experimental data with removal of the CTCF binding site near VH2-2 (Figure S4A).

  Next, we investigated the effects of mutating CTCF binding sites within IGCR1. This has been shown to greatly increase use of VH5-2 and reduce the use of distal VH gene segments13. In simulations we found that loss of CTCF binding to IGCR1 increased VH5-2 use relative to WT, in agreement with experiments (Figure 3G). This was likely accounted for by increased VH5-2 residence near the RC on IGCR1-deleted alleles via increased looping between Eμ and the VH5-2-associated CTCF binding site (Figure 2C). The reason for less abrupt drop of VH2-2 recombination frequency in simulations remains to be determined. We conclude that cohesin-dependent extrusion from the RC satisfactorily explains rearrangement frequencies of only the 5-6 most proximal VH gene segments.


### Distal VH utilization
  To model a broader distribution of VH rearrangements we varied cohesin loading. Restricting cohesin loading to the VH region (Figure 3H) resulted in greater RC proximity for more distal VH segments, but the characteristic pattern of proximal VH gene segments was lost (Figure 3I, left). We found that distributing cohesin loading between the RC and VH region yielded both the proximal VH pattern as well as discernible distal VH interaction with RC (Figure 3I, right). Additionally, the percent of cohesin loaded at the RC was positively correlated with usage bias of proximal VH genes over distal VH genes (Figure S4B). We propose that proximal and distal VH gene segments recombine via 3 dimensional structures generated by cohesin loading near the RC (Figure 3C) or away from the RC (Figure 3H), respectively.

  Despite greater RC interactions of distal VH gene segments in these simulations, several gene segments in the middle of the locus and at its 5’ end were poorly represented (Figure 3I, right). Because Eμ is essential for efficient VH recombination33,34 and locus contraction13, we hypothesized that VH gene segments are activated by Eμ for rearrangements. H3K27ac Hi-ChIP in primary pro-B cells revealed Eμ contacts extending beyond the 3’ domain to form a punctate stripe throughout the VH region (Figure 4A). Some associations were obviously stronger than others, such as the cluster of sites encompassing the middle VH genes (boxed). The strongest points of association were marked by H3K27ac peaks that did not necessarily coincide with VH promoters (Figure 4B). We conclude that Eμ interacts with many H3K27ac-marked sites in the VH region to bring distal parts of the locus into proximity of the 3’Igh domain.

  We re-investigated distal VH usage after incorporating such interactions into the model. To do this, we added an additional constraint that directs any two regions with H3K27ac to associate with each other if they are brought into proximity by cohesin-mediated extrusion (Figure 4C). To ensure that these contacts were properly incorporated into the model, we compared experimental and simulated H3K27ac Hi-ChIP contact maps and found that they showed good concordance (Figure S4C and S4D). Combining cohesin/CTCF dynamics and preferential interactions between H3K27ac marked genomic sites we obtained more equitable VH rearrangement frequencies throughout the locus (Figure 4D). Specifically, middle (e.g. VH14-4) and distal (e.g. VH1-26) VH genes that were conspicuously missing in cohesin/CTCF-only simulations, were better represented in the modified model independent of simulation number (Figure S5A). We also observed that cohesin residence time played a critical role in generating these patterns. Shorter cohesin residence times restricted recombination to proximal VH genes, whereas longer cohesin residence times permitted recombination across the entire locus (Figure S5B). That the pattern was not identical to experimental observations could be due to various additional parameters that can influence locus structure (such as strength of CTCF binding) or V(D)J recombination (such as RSS strength). Nevertheless, the broad VH gene usage across the 2.8Mb locus strongly suggests that our model reveals the basic principles of chromatin dynamics that drive Igh diversity.


### Structure and rearrangements of genomic inversions
  Rearrangement phenotypes of two large genomic inversions of the VH region have supported the RAG1/2 scanning model of VH recombination22,23. The larger 2.4Mb inversion leaves only the first VH gene segment, VH5-2, in the WT orientation relative to the 3’Igh domain (Figure 5A, middle). The rearrangement profile of this configuration is dominated by VH5-2 (Figure 5B, left). The smaller 890kb inversion flips approximately a third of the distal VH region leaving intact the proximal and middle VH gene segments and 4-5 VH gene segments located at the very 5’ end of the locus (Figure 5C). This configuration suppresses rearrangement within the inversion, while preserving it in the flanking non-inverted regions (Figure 5D, left). To further test the validity of our model we simulated chromatin structure and VH recombination frequency of these altered Igh alleles.

  Simulation of the 2.4Mb inversion showed loss of the 3 stripes anchored in the 3’ Igh domain (Figure 5A). Interactions within the 3’ domain were largely unaltered (Figure S5C), except that the cluster of interactions between 3’CBE and proximal VH genes was replaced by a single interaction with the remaining gene segment. The inversion induced a large sub-TAD with a new 5’-anchored stripe, a reorganization clearly visible in the difference heatmap (Figure 5A, right). Extending our interpretation of 3’ CBE serving as a termination site for extrusion that starts throughout the locus (Figure 1D), we propose that CTCF sites at the 5’end of the locus serve as a termination site for cohesin loaded within the inverted VH region. Estimation of rearrangement frequencies on the inverted allele showed most prominent use of the VH5-2, closely mimicking experimental results (Figure 5B, right).

  Simulation of the 890kb inversion showed loss of 3’-anchored stripes only within the inverted region, with no changes within the 3’Igh domain (Figures 5C and S5C). Like the larger inversion, the smaller inverted region also formed a sub-TAD with a stripe at its 5’ end. Though the inverted region did not interact with 3’domain, the 5’-most part of the locus that was not inverted continued to do so (Figure 5C). Estimation of VH rearrangements showed substantially reduced use of gene segments located within the inverted region (Figure 5D). Importantly, the most distal VH gene segments that continued to interact with the RC were recombinationally active in these simulations, closely recapitulating experimental observations. Our interpretation of how this structure is generated is summarized in Figure 5E. In this model, cohesin loaded on the 3’ side of the inverted region will form a loop between the 3’ and 5’ ends of the locus, whereas cohesin loaded on the 5’ side of the inverted region will form a loop between the inverted region and the 5’ end of the locus. Since neither of these loops bring the inverted VH genes into contact with the RC, the usage of the inverted VH genes is substantially reduced. We conclude that parameters that undergird our model provide a mechanistic framework to understand Igh locus structure and rearrangements.


## DISCUSSION
  Using experimental data and computer simulations we propose a model to address how widely dispersed variable gene segments (VH) recombine at comparable frequencies to generate a maximally diverse IgH repertoire. We invoke three novel features of chromatin dynamics that together explain most experimental observations. First, we provide evidence that long-range chromatin structure of the Igh locus and utilization of distant VH gene segments, requires cohesin loading throughout the locus rather than at only the 3’ end. The close correspondence between structural requirements and VH recombination corroborates the starting hypothesis that locus structure underlies generation of diversity. Our model also correctly predicts structures and recombination outcomes of mutated Igh alleles.

  Second, we found that the intronic enhancer, Eμ, that does not bind CTCF, acts like a bi-directional blocker of cohesin extrusion. The biochemical basis for Eμ-dependent cohesin blocking is not clear. One possibility is that this blocking ability is due to bi-directional RNA polymerase II activity initiated at Eμ, analogous to the proposed role of Pol II activity at promoters35. Alternatively, the nucleoprotein complex generated at the enhancer, or matrix attachment regions that flank Eμ36, may impede cohesin progression to block extrusion. Yet another possibility is that the histone modification state of Eμ, which is marked by the strongest H3K27ac peak in the Igh locus, may preclude cohesin traverse. Our observations suggest that bi-directional cohesin blocking may be a feature of other genomic enhancers as well.

  Third, locus modeling by cohesin blocking alone (by CTCF and Eμ) was insufficient to accurately recapitulate VH utilization. This deficiency was circumvented by incorporating interactions between H3K27ac marked VH regions with Eμ. We hypothesize that when a VH-associated H3K27ac region is brought close to the strong H3K27ac peak at Eμ by cohesin extrusion, the two remain associated for some time, permitting nearby VH gene segments to recombine. Such associations may be mediated by co-factors that bind H3K27ac such as double bromodomain-containing proteins like Brd4, which colocalizes with H3K27ac throughout the Igh locus (unpublished observation), or by unknown epigenetic features. The notion of Eμ interacting with parts of the VH region is reminiscent of our earlier proposal that locus compaction is, in part, mediated by Eμ-dependent interactions13.

  We propose that Igh gene assembly starts with Eμ activation during B cell development, which establishes a pre-rearrangement configuration orchestrated by interactions between 3’CBE, Eμ and IGCR1. The first rearrangements occur within this 3’ Igh domain. Cohesin tracking within the 60kb Eμ-IGCR1 loop brings intervening DH gene segments close to the RC to promote their recombination. Spatial proximity of the most 5’ DH gene segment, DFL16.1, to the RC mediated by Eμ-IGCR1 interactions may drive its high-level recombination37. Being restricted to 60kb this process is likely quite efficient and proceeds simultaneously on both alleles, explaining, why it is rare to find progenitor B cells with only one DJH rearranged allele. This mechanism also precludes VH recombination to unrearranged DH gene segments. DH to JH rearrangement brings the second DH RSS into the RC, targeting it for VH recombination38,39.

  Next, a compatible VH RSS must enter the RC to promote VH to DJH recombination. Structural organization of DJH recombined alleles is likely to be similar to that of unrearranged alleles because the 3 most important cis-regulatory sequences configuring the 3’ Igh domain are still present. The only parameter that changes during DH recombination is the distance between Eμ and IGCR1. Our simulations suggest that cohesin-mediated extrusion starting in the DJH-Eμ region brings the closest VH gene segments into RC proximity. The first (3’-most) VH gene segment rearranges most frequently because cohesin loaded between the RC and VH domain pauses here in most cells. In a subset of cells, cohesin overcomes the first pause and proceeds to the next CTCF-bound site. Thus, the associated VH gene segment rearranges less frequently than the first gene segment. Applying this reasoning to the next 3-4 gene segments explains the receding pattern of proximal VH utilization.

  Rearrangement of RC-distal VH gene segments requires cohesin loading more broadly throughout the VH domain and, additionally, interactions of H3K27ac-marked sites in the VH domain with Eμ. Whether these interactions are mediated by the modification itself or other features of regions that bear these marks remain to be uncovered. It is also unclear how many VH genes are activated by interaction of one H3K27ac site with Eμ. While we simulated an H3K27ac contact activating only the nearest VH gene segment, it is quite possible that H3K27ac-mediated proximity permits rearrangement of several gene segment, albeit with different efficiencies. Our modeling studies comparing WT and large inversions within the VH domain strongly support the idea that correct orientation of CTCF sites is essential for H3K27ac-marked regions to interact. We envisage that CTCF-dependent pausing of cohesins that loaded in the VH domain bring H3K27ac-marked regions into spatial proximity that permit their interactions.

  A remarkable feature of the Igh locus is that all VH gene segments are oriented such that they only recombine by deletion to DJH junctions. This organization has promoted the view that extrusion from the 3’ end of the locus positions VH RSSs for recombination. However, we previously found that VH recombination to unrearranged DH gene segments (that have both flanking RSSs intact) proceed by deletion or inversion of intervening DNA40. Our interpretation was that once a VH gene segment is brought into proximity of DH RSSs by cohesin stalling, that gene segment can undergo diffusional interactions with either DH RSS. The model developed here, that closely recapitulates experimental VH recombination frequencies without making assumptions about VH orientation, is also consistent with this idea. We propose that extrusion stalling, that is governed by orientation of CTCF sites but not VH gene segments, determines proximity of recombinogenic RSSs. Thereafter, RSS capture by RAG proteins to form the synaptic complex occurs by diffusion that is independent of VH orientation.

  Independently generating chromosomal structure within which RSS association occurs provides a plausible mechanism to understand inversional Vκ recombination. Several of these inverted Vκ gene segments are located nearby CTCF sites that contact the Jκ-associated RC. Diffusional RSS capture within such loops readily explains their use in recombination. Structures that permit other inverted Vκs to recombine remain unclear because of the complex organization of CTCF sites in Igk that interact both towards and away from the RC41. Our model also explains similar levels of deletional or inversional recombination of an engineered Vβ gene segment in the Tcrb locus42,43.

  The folding principles that achieve a diverse antibody repertoire have broader implications, especially for understanding long-range genomic interactions that control cell-type specific gene expression. First, variation in cohesin loading or unloading alters the probability that two genomic sites interact. Biased cohesin loading at one site increases the probability of short-range interactions with that site, whereas unbiased cohesin loading favors long-range interactions. Second, enhancers can act as barriers to cohesin without binding CTCF. This ability may enable enhancers to achieve cell-specific interactions with promoters even in the absence of changes to CTCF binding. It is interesting to recall that enhancers were defined as promoter distal regulatory sequences that functioned in an orientation-independent manner. Perhaps this characteristic derives, at least in part, from their ability to block cohesin extrusion bidirectionally. This does not preclude the possibility that some distal regulatory sequences may selectively block extrusion in only one orientation, allowing greater precision in the choice of promoter s activated. Third, regions marked by H3K27ac preferentially interact with each other. The amount of H3K27ac marking enhancers and promoters may provide an additional variable to modulate the frequency of their interactions. Incorporating these features into future models of chromatin dynamics will be critical for improving their accuracy in understanding how enhancer-promoter interactions drive gene expression.


### Limitations of the Study
  The proposed model has several limitations. First, it is not known experimentally how many cohesin complexes are present on an Igh allele in living cells. Nor is it clear whether there are preferred sites for cohesin loading in the Igh TAD. Second, in the proposed model the rate limiting step for VH recombination is hypothesized to be time spent near the recombination center. Thus, additional features such as possible roles for VH promoters and RSSs, and mechanisms of RSS capture once spatial proximity has been achieved, will need to be incorporated into future models. Third, we provide evidence that contacts between H3K27ac-marked genomic regions are an essential feature of recruiting VH genes to the recombination center. However, mechanisms of these interactions remain to be elucidated.

## STAR METHODS

### EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

#### Cell lines
  The wild-type D345 pro-B cell line was provided by David G. Schatz of Yale University24. IGCR−/− and Eμ−/− cell lines were previously generated with CRISPR-Cas944. These cells were maintained in RPMI1640 (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gemini) and a 1 × penicillin-streptomycin solution (Gibco). All cells were cultured at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.


### METHOD DETAILS

#### Igh locus simulations
  Simulations of the Igh locus were performed using the software polychrom (https://github.com/open2c/polychrom). The Igh locus was modeled as a polymer composed of 3,000 monomers, where each monomer represented 1kb of DNA in the 3Mb region chr12:113,100,000-116,100,000 (mm10 coordinates). Polychrom’s loop extrusion simulation consists of two main stages: 1) a 1D simulation to determine the linear trajectories each cohesin complex along the polymer and 2) a 3D molecular dynamics simulation to determine the 3D trajectories of each monomer in the polymer. Simulations were performed for 20,000 time steps.

  In the 1D stage, six cohesins were placed on the polymer and moved bi-directionally at a rate of 1kb per time step. When a cohesin reached a monomer representing a 1kb region bound to CTCF in a convergent orientation relative to cohesin, the cohesin had a 90% probability of stalling. If stalled at CTCF, cohesin had a 0.003% probability per time step of bypassing the CTCF, resulting in an average stall time of ~333 steps. These parameters, 90% and 0.003%, were the default parameters for polychrom. Cohesin had a mean residence time of 2500 time steps for repressed Wapl simulations (used to simulate primary pro-B cells) and 250 steps for non-repressed Wapl simulations (used to simulate pro-B cell line). These values were based on the wild-type and Wapl-removal simulations described in Nuebler et al.28 At the end of its residence time, cohesin dissociates from the polymer and then loads at another position on the polymer.

  In the 3D stage, polymer simulations were performed using the molecular dynamics package OpenMM. The forces applied to the polymer were a harmonic bond potential to connect adjacent monomers, a repulsive potential to prevent monomers from occupying the same space, and an additional harmonic bond potential to connect any two monomers bound to the same cohesin. At each time step, the bonds between monomers were updated according to the positions of the cohesin from the 1D stage. To generate Hi-C-like Igh contact maps from the simulations, contacts were determined from the 3D structure of the polymer at time step 10,000. A Hi-C contact was defined as two monomers within 6 units (where 1 unit is the diameter of a monomer). Population contact maps were computed by running 10,000 simulations and summing the contact map from each simulation.

  To measure the average time that each VH gene contacted the recombination center (RC), the number of time steps where each VH gene was within 2 diameter units of the RC was recorded between time steps 10,000 and 20,000. The average contact time for each VH gene was calculated across 1,000 simulations. To incorporate H3K27ac-dependent interactions, we considered a VH gene to be in contact with the RC if both of the following were true: 1) the VH gene was marked with H3K27ac according to ChIP-seq, and 2) a cohesin was bound to both the RC and a monomer within 5kb of the VH gene. To simulate biased cohesin loading, we altered the probability that cohesin loaded at different positions on the polymer. To simulate Eμ as a uni-directional or bi-directional pause site, we modeled it as if it were bound to one CTCF or two CTCFs in a divergent orientation, respectively.


#### Capture Hi-C, Hi-ChIP and ChIP-seq
  We obtained capture Hi-C data, H3K27ac Hi-ChIP, CTCF ChIP-seq and H3K27ac ChIP-seq data for Rag2−/− primary pro-B cells from Ma et al.45 (GEO: GSE214438), and we obtained capture Hi-C data for the wild-type and Eμ−/− D345 pro-B cell lines from Ma et al.44 (GEO: GSE241661). Capture Hi-C of IGCR−/− pro-B cell lines was performed for this study using the Arima Hi-C Kit (Arima Genomics), following the manufacturer’s protocol with strict adherence to the provided instructions. To enrich the Igh locus (chr12: 113,201,001 – 116,030,000 in mm10), SureSelect Target Enrichment probes with 2× tiling density were designed and manufactured by Agilent (Agilent Technologies Inc.). Hi-C libraries were hybridized to probes as specified by the manufacturer (Agilent Technologies Inc., G9916B). Enriched libraries were sequenced using Illumina NextSeq sequencer to generate paired-end 150-bp reads.


#### VH usage
  We obtained wild-type and 2.4 Mb VH locus inverted primary pro-B VH usage from Dai et al.23 (GSE151910), and we obtained 890 kb VH locus inverted primary pro-B VH usage from Hill et al.22 (GSE140975). VH usage for wild-type and mutant pro-B cell lines was obtained from Jain et al30 (GSE113023).


### QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS
  For Capture Hi-C and Hi-ChIP data, sequencing reads were aligned to the mm10 genome and processed using Juicer to generate .hic files. Juicer was run with the flags “-g mm10 -s Arima”. Contact maps were visualized using cooltools and are shown at 5,000 bp resolution for the 3’ Igh domain and 10,000 bp resolution for the entire Igh locus. For ChIP-seq data, sequencing reads were aligned to the mm10 genome using bwa-mem2 and peaks were called using MACS2. The identification and directionality of CTCF motifs within CTCF ChIP-seq peaks was determined using HOMER. VH usage was determined using the HTGTS-rep pipeline.
