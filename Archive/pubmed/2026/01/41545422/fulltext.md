# Molecular insights on the proangiogenic effects of VEGF like growth factor derived from horseshoe crab perivitelline fluid


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32706-3.


## Introduction
  The innate immune system of horseshoe crabs and its distinct biological features are well explored, demonstrating potential advantages in biomedical research1. The significant benefits of horseshoe crab blood in the rapid identification of bacterial components are known for more than 50 years2. The hemolymph of horseshoe crabs contains a variety of active biomolecules, including lectins, antimicrobial peptides, proteinase inhibitors, and clotting factors, which have an integral role in regulating their innate immune system3. The Limulus Amebocyte Lysate (LAL) derived from hemolymph of the horseshoe crab, is a critical component in sterility testing that ensures drug and medical device safety for millions of patients every year2.

  The perivitelline fluid (PVF) of developing eggs of the horseshoe crab harbors several useful products which has great potential applications in biomedical research. PVF fills in the space between the inner egg membrane and embryo in the perivitelline space, during the developmental stages of the horseshoe crab embryo4. The pro-angiogenic, cardiomyogenic5, dendritic cell differentiation6 and β-cell proliferation7 potential of PVF is well reported. Besides, various biomolecules in PVF are reported to have a role in inducing organogenesis in chick embryo explant cultures; stimulating the development and differentiation of specific organs such as the brain and heart8. Yet another study tested in vitro proliferative effects of PVF in dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs). This study also highlighted that PVF did not exhibit genotoxicity or any chromosomal aberrations in long-term cultured cells4.

  The cardiac development-promoting properties of PVF, another interesting feature, extend from aves to mammals. The biomolecule identified to promote this effect is a 27 kDa lectin, identified in the PVF of horseshoe crabs9. The 27 kDa lectin molecule induced cardiac cell proliferation and modulated the expression of several cardiac development regulatory genes in experimental chick embryo models8. This was confirmed in vitro, using mouse embryonic stem cells. The 27 kDa lectin produced an increasing number of beating clusters, indicating an improvement in cardiomyogenesis, confirming the lectin’s cardiomyogenic effect8. Likewise, the pro-angiogenic role of the lectin was confirmed in vivo in a chick embryonic chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) model. The lectin increased the expression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and VEGF receptors (VEGFR) in a chick embryonic chorioallantoic membrane model9. Increased hematopoiesis and blood flow observed in these chick embryos, confirmed its pro-angiogenic effects. Furthermore, PVF was reported to induce a significant increase in the migration of human bone marrow endothelial cells as confirmed by wound-healing assays10. These studies indicated the potential benefits of this natural biomolecule in diverse physiological processes. However, these studies did not provide any molecular mechanisms of action of PVF.

  Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are frequently used in tissue regeneration studies due to their multipotency, that helps to explain the cellular and molecular mechanisms involved in the formation and regeneration of different tissue origins11. The clinical use of umbilical cord-derived mesenchymal stem cells (hUCMSC), the primitive MSCs derived from the human umbilical cord, has several advantages. Their isolation is ethically permitted, require less invasive procedures for isolation, less immunogenic, possess a high capacity for proliferation, and are capable of multi-lineage differentiation12. In the current study, an in-depth understanding of the pro-angiogenic role of peri-vitelline fluid (PVF) is attempted in vitro using hUCMSCs and evaluated along with known growth factors such as VEGF, EGF, FGF, and IGF. Various fractions of PVF were assessed and the proangiogenic responses of the most abundant 27 kDa fraction VII were explored by evaluating the transcript levels of endothelial markers such as von Willebrand Factor (vWF), VEGF, soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase-1 (sFlt-1) and transcription factors regulating angiogenesis such as HOXA7, HOXB5, and Notch1. Docking studies were further performed to predict the binding site of 27 kDa lectin with the receptor of the potent angiogenic factor, vascular endothelial growth factor.


## Results

### High abundance 27 kDa protein was purified from crude Perivitelline fluid (PVF)
  The pooled crude PVF isolated from day 42–44 old eggs comprised of various proteins with strikingly most abundant band at around 27 kDa (Fig. 1A, the raw image for this data is provided in supplementary Fig S1). PVF fractionation using FPLC showed 10 distinct peaks with hscPVF-VEGF being the major fraction (Fig. 1B). The purity of the collected fractions was confirmed on SDS gel (Fig. 1C, the raw image for this data is provided in supplementary Fig S2) and stored in different aliquots after passing through a 0.22µ filter. The hscPVF-VEGF was found to be enriched in the 27 kDa lectin and was used in all the experiments.

### Mesenchymal stem cell isolated from umbilical cord tissue exhibited markers of pluripotency
  On day 5 of culture, short rod-like polygonal cells started to emerge. By day 7, all cells appeared to have spindle-like morphology and a pure hUCMSC population was obtained from passage 5 onwards (Fig. 2A). Earlier passages comprised of a heterogenous population of epithelial and stromal cells, however, the epithelial cells disappeared by subsequent passages. At passage 5, the enriched stromal cell population seeded at very low densities (one to ten cells per well) formed homogenous clones that appeared like colonies (Fig. 2B). The harvested colonies that were seeded at low densities yielded a pure hUCMSC population. FACS analyses further confirmed a homogenous expression of CD90 (92 ± 2.3%), and CD44 (88 ± 2.5%) markers and a very low expression for CD34 marker (< 1%) (Fig. 2c).

### hscPVF-VEGF lacked induction of proliferative activity in treated hUCMSCs
  There was no significant increase in the proliferation of hUCMSCs observed with the use of both crude PVF and hscPVF-VEGF when compared to 10% FCS-containing medium (Fig. 3A and B). The proliferative potential of both crude PVF and hscPVF-VEGF was found to be lower than the commercially available growth factor, bFGF-containing medium suggesting that both factors did not have proliferative potential in hUCMSCs (Fig. 3A and B). The lack of proliferative potential of hscPVF-VEGF hinted us to carry out their in vitro differentiation potential assays for angiogenic and cardiogenic lineages.

### hscPVF-VEGF did not support hUCMSC differentiation to osteogenic, adipogenic and cardiomyogenic lineages
  PVF comprises of several biologically active soluble factors that play a critical role in embryogenesis and cell differentiation in the developing embryo3. This study investigated the potential of PVF and hscPVF-VEGF to induce differentiation of hUCMSCs into osteogenic and adipogenic lineages. Intriguingly, hscPVF-VEGF did not induce osteogenic/adipogenic lineage differentiation in hUCMSCs compared to the control cells grown in StemPro osteogenic and adipogenic media. Control cells exhibited positive staining for Oil Red O (for lipid droplets) and Alizarin red (for calcium deposition) respectively while hscPVF-VEGF treated cells did not show this (Fig. 4).

  We also evaluated the cardiomyogenic differentiation in the presence of hscPVF-VEGF. Cells treated with 5-azacytidine (positive control for cardiomyocyte differentiation) for 48 h showed slight elongation by day 3 and as time progressed cells started thinning out and grouped into clusters. On day 15, cells appeared very long with tube-like dense granules in the cytoplasm. No changes in the morphology were observed in 10% FBS and hscPVF-VEGF supplemented culture conditions (Fig. 5A). Molecular characterization of 10M 5-azacytidine treated hUCMSCs at 48 h revealed significant increase in expression of cardiac-specific genes cTn1 (7.23 ± 0.87; p< 0.001) and -MHC in positive control cells compared to 10% FBS grown cells (Fig. 5B).

### Increased expression of angiogenic factors was observed in hUCMSCs treated with hscPVF-VEGF
  hUCMSCs treated with hscPVF-VEGF with complete EGM2 showed a significant increase in the mRNA of endothelial markers such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and vWF. Moreover, sFlt-1, a potent anti-angiogenic factor was found to be significantly downregulated in hUCMSCs grown in hscPVF-VEGF with complete EGM2 compared to EGM2 alone (Fig. 6A). Treatment of hUCMSCs with hscPVF-VEGF resulted in a 4-fold increase in the expression of VEGF, as well as upregulation of vWF (2-fold) and downregulation of sFlt-1, indicating that hscPVF-VEGF may promote differentiation of hUCMSCs into the endothelial cell lineage (Fig. 6A).

  To understand the underlying mechanisms involved in this improved differentiation, the study further examined transcription factors critical in this transition and found a significant increase in the transcript levels of HOXA7, HOXB3, and HOXB5 genes (Fig. 6B), which supports the observed increase in endothelial markers. However, there was no alteration in the transcript levels of Notch1 with the treatment of hscPVF-VEGF.

  Given the importance of endothelial cell migration in angiogenesis, we further assessed the transcript levels of MMP2 and MMP9 with or without hscPVF-VEGF. The significant rise in the levels of MMP2 (2.5-fold) and MMP9 (4-fold) suggested enhanced functional ability of newly differentiated endothelial cells. The study also observed a 2-fold increase in CD31, a well-known marker of angiogenesis (Fig. 6C).


### Wound healing assay corroborates angiogenic potential of hscPVF-VEGF
  A visible initiation of wound closure was observed within 24 h of the assay in all the culture conditions. Cells treated with 10ng/mL FGF gradually covered the easily visualized central wound area (Fig. 7). In the FGF-free medium, the culture wounds closed rather slowly. The closure was complete by 48 h in FGF-treated hUCMSCs. In the control, over 30% of the original wound remained at 24 h and continued incubation for up to 48 h did not lead to complete wound closure. In the case of hscPVF-VEGF treated cells, cell migration was evident; however, at a slower rate compared to FGF (10ng/ml) treated cells (Fig. 7). The results further ascertain the lack of proliferative potential, instead a favorable migratory potential of fraction VII in hUCMSCs. In view of the confirmed significant angiogenic potential of PVF fraction VII enriched with 27 kDa lectin it was termed as hscPVF-VEGF (horseshoe crab PVF derived VEGF like growth factor).

### hscPVF-VEGF interacts with hVEGFR in the pocket formed by extracellular domains D5, D6 and D7
  hVEGFR is a homodimer, transmembrane protein comprising of seven extracellular domains (ECD: D1-D7), (Fig. 8A). hVEGF and hPLGF interact with D2 and D3 domains of hVEGFR (PDB IDs: 2 × 1 W & 1RV6)13,14. Molecular docking studies reported that hscPVF-VEGF interacted at the pocket formed within D5, D6 and D7 domains of hVEGFR dimer (Fig. 8D). This binding site of hscPVF-VEGF was observed to be consistent in hVEGF and hPLGF bound as well as unbound structures of hVEGFR (Fig. 8C, D and F). ZRANK score suggested that hscPVF-VEGF showed slightly higher affinity for hPLGF and hVEGF bound hVEGFR as compared to the unbound form (Table 1). hVEGF and hPLGF were found to interact between D2 and D3 domains (Fig. 8B and E) as reported in previous studies13,14, but the binding affinity of hVEGF was observed to be higher than that of hPLGF for hVEGFR (Table 1). hscPVF-VEGF demonstrated low binding affinity to hVEGFR as compared to binding affinity of hVEGF and hPLGF to hVEGFR (Table 1).

## Discussion
  The diverse biomolecule-rich horseshoe crab hemolymph has piqued the attention of biomedical researchers over the past several decades. The rich biological treasure trove is reflected in the horseshoe crab’s ability to beat the odds and survive nearly unchanged for an estimated 500 million years8. Although the massive interest in this marine species has stemmed from the discovery of hemolymph and its innate potential to ward off bacterial endotoxins, other major components like the PVF, have also gained interest among the research communities3. Hemocyanins and lectins, the two major protein components of PVF, have immense relevance in various domains of cell and tissue engineering, embryology, and immunology3. Apart from this, the biomedical community has paid close attention to the hematopoietic and angiogenic properties of PVF owing to its promising clinical significance. Despite the promising applications and diverse physiological effects explored in various studies, it is important to note that the molecular mechanisms underlying the angiogenic effects of PVF have not been explored yet. A comprehensive understanding of the molecular interactions and signaling pathways activated by PVF components is crucial for harnessing its full therapeutic potential.

  The current study has established the potential of hscPVF-VEGF in inducing angiogenesis in the hUCMSCs and deciphered its molecular mechanism of action. In agreement with previous studies8,9, crude PVF extracted from the Indian horseshoe crab embryos in the current study exhibited seven fractions by FPLC and fraction VII showed the highest amount of protein. The most abundant 27 kDa protein in fraction VII is identical to Lectin L6 from the hemocyte lysate of Japanese horseshoe crab T. tridentatus4. The current study was undertaken to unveil the multilineage differentiation potential of PVF and its fraction VII, particularly in the context of angiogenic differentiation using in vitro cultures of hUCMSCs. These multifunctional stem cells possess low immunogenicity and are ideal sources for cell proliferation and differentiation assays, raising partially differentiated progenitors for therapeutic regeneration of tissue damages caused by tissue injuries.

  VEGF is often used as a soluble factor or coupled to biomaterial scaffolds to stimulate angiogenesis or vascularization during tissue repair process15,16. However, VEGF-mediated treatment strategies are cost-intensive owing to their limited stability17. Hence scientists are trying to harness natural biomolecules that can either enhance or limit the formation of new blood vessels and this field is under constant investigation. The angiogenic growth of the vascular system is a tightly regulated process and the factors responsible for this physiological process coordinate the proliferation, migration, and differentiation of endothelial cells8.

  As a first step, we studied the potential of crude PVF along with its fraction VII, (the most abundant fraction in crude PVF), to induce proliferation of hUCMSCs. The proliferation efficiencies of both crude PVF and fraction VII were limited in the present experimental conditions employed. A previous study also reported an insignificant proliferative potential of PVF in dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs). Interestingly, in a recent study18, nano-LC MS/MS analysis revealed the presence of sesquiterpenoid signaling pathway proteins including Hsp83, Chd64, and hJHBP in the hemolymph proteins of Asian horseshoe crabs Tachypleus tridentatus (Leach) and Carcinoscorpius rotundicauda (Latreille). These studies were performed in related crab species, and we speculated that such juvenile hormone factors19 might exists in Indian horseshoe crabs as well. This could be potentially accountable for the inhibitory effects of crude and hscPVF-VEGF observed in the cell proliferative assays as these factors are mainly involved in the metamorphosis stages where cellular differentiation pathways are more pronounced11.

  The multilineage differential and cell migration potential of hUCMSCs were next explored with the supplementation of hscPVF-VEGF in culture media, and among the different lineages tested, a significant influence was observed only in the angiogenic lineage differentiation. This is in coherence with previous in vivo and in vitro studies, wherein the 27 kDa lectin fraction of PVF enhanced the differentiation of the chick embryonic heart8,9. Yet another study reported PVF-induced cardiomyogenic differentiation of bone marrow stem cells5. A similar response was expected post-exposure of hscPVF-VEGF to hUCMSCs, however, the same was not evident in the current study as confirmed by morphological and molecular assessments. It is imperative to note that all these studies were performed in different conditions and with various target cells. The hscPVF-VEGF supplementation on the other hand, significantly aided wound healing throughout 24 h of exposure in culture. As serum concentration was significantly reduced in the culture conditions, the observed cell migration in the current study might have been influenced by hscPVF-VEGF.

  Our results further provide insights into the mechanism of how hscPVF-VEGF activates the angiogenic specific pathways at a molecular level. VEGF is an endothelial specific mitogen and possess pro-angiogenic functions20. According to in-silico observations, it appears that hscPVF-VEGF can bind to the pocket formed within domains 5, 6 and 7, which is crucial for ectodomain shedding during the synthesis of sFLT-121. The binding of hscPVF-VEGF to VEGFR1 (FLT-1) not only triggers VEGFR1-mediated actions but also appears to block the synthesis of sFLT1, leading to the creation of a pro-angiogenic environment. vWF is synthesized and stored by endothelial cells that mediate platelet aggregation and adhesion22. sFlt-1, a splice variant of the VEGF receptor Flt-1 lacking the transmembrane cytoplasmic domain is an inhibitor of angiogenesis, that functions mainly by blocking the VEGF mediated action23. The concurrent increase in VEGF and vWF and decrease in sFlt-1 levels observed in the present study suggests that the hscPVF-VEGF is promoting endothelial differentiation of hUCMSCs plausibly through its interaction with VEGFR.

  CD31, also known as platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule 1, is a glycoprotein found on the surface of endothelial and certain hematopoietic cells, and is considered a reliable marker for identifying vascular differentiation24. To form new vascular branches, the underlying basement membrane must be degraded by proteinases such as MMP-2 and MMP-925. In the current study, the observed increase in the transcript levels of CD31, MMP2 and MMP9 in hUCMSCs treated with hscPVF-VEGF further corroborates their endothelial differentiation potential.

  The Hox family of transcription factors exerts master regulatory effects on multiple aspects of angiogenesis, including cell proliferation, matrix remodeling, cell migration, and vessel maturation26. HoxB3 is necessary for the subsequent capillary morphogenesis of newly formed vascular sprouts27. HOXA7 plays a crucial role in regulating gene expression, morphogenesis, and differentiation28. HoxB5 is required for differentiation of angioblasts and mature endothelial cells29. In the present study, the observed increase in the expression levels of HOXB3, HOXB5, and HOXA7 in hUCMSCs treated with hscPVF-VEGF may be the underlying molecular mechanism inducing transcription of VEGF, vWF, CD31, MMP-2, MMP-9 to aid their differentiation into endothelial cells.

  Overall, the study provides valuable information on application of PVF, particularly its hscPVF-VEGF, in promoting angiogenesis, supporting endothelial differentiation and migration using hUCMSCs. By examining the interactions with VEGFR1 and induction of key proteins like VEGF, vWF, CD31, MMP-2, MMP-9, and Hox family transcription factors, the study further provides insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying the proangiogenic effects of hscPVF-VEGF. The results obtained highlight the therapeutic potential of hscPVF-VEGF in various biomedical contexts involving impairment with angiogenesis such as preeclampsia30.

  While the present study offers valuable insights into the angiogenic potential of hscPVF-VEGF using hUCMSCs as a regenerative model system, certain limitations must be acknowledged. The absence of VEGFR inhibition assays restricts mechanistic validation, encompassing targeted inhibition assays and downstream pathway analysis. To strengthen the molecular findings of the current study, protein-level validation should be performed using western blot analysis for intracellular targets and ELISA for secreted factors. The present work further requires preclinical validation involving animal models. A comprehensive safety study involving different doses and time points in murine models is warranted. Nonetheless, our findings are supported by relevant literature and internal comparative analyses, and we plan for further comprehensive characterization of the protein fractions using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) and the presence of co-factors or synergistic proteins cannot be ruled out at this stage.


## Materials and methods

### Egg collection and PVF Preparation
  Extraction of PVF was performed as previously reported8. Briefly, 5000 fertilized eggs of the horseshoe crab (Tachypleus gigas, Müller) were collected from the wild breeding beaches of Balamgari, Odisha in India. The eggs were processed at the National Institute of Oceanography, Dona Paula, Goa, India. Eggs were incubated in artificial incubators at a constant temperature of 27 ± 2 °C in 20 ppm seawater in the laboratory and allowed to rotate continually under constant aeration until they became transparent. Eggs of the Indian horseshoe crab acquired yellow taint towards the late stage of fertilization (~ day 30, post-fertilization). These eggs were separated and kept under observation in the laboratory until they turned transparent and embryo movement was observed (approximately day 42–44 post-fertilization). The PVF was collected by puncturing into the peri-vitelline space. Approximately 150 µl PVF was collected from each egg.

  The extracted samples of PVF were further diluted to different concentrations in culture medium for subsequent experiments. Fresh PVF extracts were produced for each experiment and sterilized by passing through a 0.25µ syringe filter. The healthy larvae were incubated for an additional 75 days in a simulated environment before being released into the sea to develop into an adult crab.


### Fractionation and purification of crude PVF
  The protein concentration of the crude PVF extracts was measured using a BCA protein assay kit (Pierce BCA kit, Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. To rule out protein denaturation, crude PVF was evaluated using a 15% SDS-PAGE. 10 µl of each sample were diluted in the same volume of Lammeli buffer (company) with β-mercaptoethanol and boiled at 95˚C for 10 min. Then, 20 µl of the mix was loaded into the gel. Electrophoresis was performed for 1 h at 150 V. The gels were further stained with silver stain.

  For fractionation, an aliquot of 0.5 ml of crude PVF was subjected to gel exclusion FPLC. The peri-vitelline fluid was sub-fractionated using a Fast Pressure Liquid Chromatography (Amersham Bioscience) Superose 12 h 10/30 column at room temperature. The fractions were eluted in PBS (pH 7.4) with 1.45mM of NaCl and 0.5 ml fractions were collected and stored at −200 C until further use. AUFS and chart speed used for the chromatography was 0.1 unit each. Fractions that eluted under the same peak were pooled and labeled as pooled fractions I, II, III, IV, V, VI, and VII. All pools were collected to find out the fraction with the most abundant 27 kDa protein fraction by silver staining on an SDS-PAGE gel. The pooled PVF fractions were further subjected to dialysis using standard protocols. Briefly, a dialysis tubing of 10 kDa cut-off was rinsed in distilled boiling water and PVF fractions were added. The samples were dialyzed against cold distilled water for 2 h at continuous stirring conditions in a cold room. The samples were then collected, sterile filtered using 0.2µ filters, aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Silver staining of the pooled fractions was further performed to identify the PVF fraction with the maximum amount of protein.


### Isolation and characterization of hUCMSCs
  The human umbilical cord tissue samples were collected (N = 6) from participants undergoing normal delivery and processed for isolation of hUCMSCs. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines/regulations and all experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Clinical Ethics Committee (EC/GOVT-7/2014 dated 12/12/2015). The samples were obtained after informed consent from the participants according to the norms of the Institutional Clinical Ethics Committee. The clinical profile of the patient including age and details of any medical histories were noted. Briefly, the umbilical cord tissue was rinsed twice in PBS with antibiotics, then cut into 1–2 mm pieces and incubated in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM)/F12 containing 0.1% collagenase Type I (300units/ml) and Hyaluronidase (1 mg/ml) for 1–4 h at 37 °C in 5% CO2. The tissue digest was then passed through a 40µ filter squeezing the tissue pieces to release cells using forceps. The filtrate was then centrifuged at 1200 rpm for 7 min and the cell suspension obtained was plated in DMEM F12 complete medium supplemented with 10% FCS. The cells were cultured for a week until well-adherent colonies were formed. The colonies were further expanded for subsequent studies. The clusters were grown in monolayers and processed for hUCMSCs characterization by FACS analysis (BD FACS Aria) using anti-human CD 34-PE, CD 90-PE, and CD 44-PE antibodies (Abcam, USA) to confirm the identity of the isolated cells.


### Cell proliferation assays
  In vitro proliferative potential of crude PVF and PVF fraction VII (enriched with 27 kDa fraction, henceforth referred to as hscPVF-VEGF) was estimated by MTT assay (Sigma, USA). Both crude PVF and hscPVF-VEGF at the protein concentrations in the range of 10ng/ml to 1 µg/ml were used for the analysis. Briefly, hUCMSCs were plated in triplicates at a cell count of 2000 cells/well in 96-well culture plates. After overnight incubation, cells were washed, and serum was starved for 4 h. Following serum starvation, cells were replenished with complete media (DMEM F12 + 10% FCS) containing crude PVF and hscPVF-VEGF at various protein concentrations (ranging from 10ng − 1000ng/ml for crude PVF and 10ng − 100,000ng/ml for hscPVF-VEGF). MTT assay was performed, and the absorbance was recorded at 570 nm. The cells treated with 10 ng/ml bFGF and the untreated cells grown in normal tissue culture medium served as experimental controls. Further in vitro experiments, including wound healing and multilineage differentiation assays were conducted using only hscPVF-VEGF.


### Wound healing assay using hscPVF-VEGF
  hUCMSCs were seeded (1.5 × 105 cells/well) on 35-mm fibronectin-coated culture dishes and incubated overnight in complete DMEM-F12 media. After cell attachment, the medium was aspirated out and the cells were washed with PBS and maintained in serum-deprived (2%) conditions overnight. On the day of the wound healing assay (day 0), the culture medium was aspirated and using a sterile 200 µl pipette tip, three separate wounds were made through the cells. Immediately after wounding, the cells were rinsed very gently with PBS and cells were photographed for day 0 readings followed by the addition of complete media in the control and 2% FCS-containing media supplemented with FGF (10ng/ml) or hscPVF-VEGF (100 µg/ml) in the treatments. Cells were further incubated and photographed at 24 h and 48 h subsequently. The images acquired for each sample were analyzed by comparing the images from time 0 to the last time point 48 h. The percentage wound gap for the control and treatment groups was calculated using Image J software and six images from each study group were analyzed using Image J software. The percentage wound gap was calculated and averaged as previously reported31.


### Multilineage differentiation assays to adipogenic, osteogenic and cardiomyogenic lineages
  All the reagents required for differentiation assays were procured from Invitrogen. hUCMSCs were induced to differentiate into adipogenic, osteogenic, cardiomyogenic, and angiogenic lineages in the presence/absence of hscPVF-VEGF. For differentiation studies, 5 × 104 hUCMSCs were seeded in 12-well culture plates containing MSC growth medium (DMEM F12 supplemented with 10% FBS, 200mM Glutamax, and 10 mg/ml Gentamycin). The cells were cultured to confluence, followed by initiation of differentiation assays. hUCMSCs grown in StemPro adipogenic/StemPro osteogenic differentiation medium served as controls in the experiment. hscPVF-VEGF was used at 100 µg/ml concentration to induce adipogenic/osteogenic differentiation. Both the control and hscPVF-VEGF treated cells were incubated for 21 days with a medium change every third day. After 21 days, cells were stained with Oil-Red-O and Alizarin Red S (Sigma, USA).

  To induce cardiomyogenic differentiation, hUCMSCs were grown to confluence and treated with hscPVF-VEGF at 100 µg/ml concentration for 15 days. hUCMSCs treated with 10µM azacytidine (Cat no: 2385; Sigma, USA) for 48 h served as positive controls in the experiment. hUCMSCs were exposed to 5-azacytidine in the growth medium for a period of 48 h, followed by washing with PBS to prevent cell death from prolonged 5- azacytidine exposure. The cells were then maintained in a normal growth medium without 5-azacytidine and allowed to differentiate over 15 days. Likewise, hUCMSCs were exposed to 100 µg/ml hscPVF-VEGF for 15 days to explore their cardiomyogenic differentiation potential with regular supplementation every three days. Cells grown in a normal tissue culture medium served as experimental controls. At the end of the treatment duration, cells were harvested for molecular studies. Total RNA was extracted from treated and untreated hUCMSCs cells using Trizol reagent following the standard protocol. The yield and quality of resulting RNA was evaluated using a spectrophotometer. 1 µg of RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA using Superscript™ III first-strand synthesis kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The resulting cDNA was subjected to real-time PCR using SYBR green mastermix (Biorad) on a Bio-Rad CFX 96™ Thermal Cycler. The details of the primers (used at a concentration of 5 pM) for each gene pair are listed in Table 2. Thermal cycling conditions used for PCR reaction were: denaturation at 98 °C for 30 s, 40 cycles of (98 °C for 10 s, primer specific annealing temperature for 30 s and 72 °C for 30 s), followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. Data normalization was done using 18 s as an internal quality control. All samples were analyzed in duplicates. The specificity of each PCR reaction was validated by a melt curve analysis.

### Angiogenic differentiation of hUCMSCs with hscPVF-VEGF
  Passage 7, human hUCMSCs cells were cultured in DMEM/HAM F12 with 10% FBS till cells became 70% confluent. These cells were serum starved for 4 h in 2% FBS containing DMEM/Hams. The cells were then treated with hscPVF-VEGF (100 µg/ml) for 14 days in Endothelial cell Growth Media − 2 (EGM-2). EGM-2 medium was used to provide a pro-angiogenic environment optimal for endothelial lineage commitment. The respective media (with or without hscPVF-VEGF) was replaced every alternate day. At day 14, total RNA was isolated from the treated cells and subjected to transcript analysis by real time PCR as described in previous section for endothelial markers (Table 2) such as VEGF, vWF, sFlt-1, angiogenic transcription factors (HOXA7, HOXB3, HOXB5, Notch1) and other markers of vascularization (CD31, MMP2 and MMP9).


### Molecular docking of hscPVF-VEGF with hVEGFR in presence and absence of hVEGF and hPLGF
  Docking studies were performed to predict the binding site of hscPVF-VEGF in hVEGFR. hscPVF-VEGF was docked to unbound VEGFR as well as VEGF and PLGF bound VEGFR structures. hVEGFR is a homodimer, trans-membrane protein comprising of 7 extracellular domains (ECD: D1-D7). Crystal structure of the full-length homodimer hVEGFR is not available. Ballmer-Hofer (15) group has modelled a dimeric structure (Fig. 1A), which was considered for docking studies using BIOVIA DS 2021 (BIOVIA). hVEGF (PDB ID: 1VPF) and hPLGF (PDB ID: 1FZV) were docked to unbound hVEGFR to verify the docking algorithm based on the previously known interaction sites (Leppänen, Caporale). Subsequently, hscPVF-VEGF (AlphaFold ID: P82151 (LEC6_TACTR)) was docked with (a) unbound hVEGFR; (b) hVEGF-bound hVEGFR; and (c) hPLGF-bound hVEGFR structures. The receptor and ligands were prepared for protein-protein docking using ‘Prepare Proteins’ module with default settings of DS 2021. Docking was performed using default settings of ‘ZDOCK’ module of DS 2021, except for generating ZRank components.


### Statistical analysis
  Statistical analyses were performed using Graph Pad Prism software (Version 9) and data presented as mean ± SD. One-way ANOVA was performed followed by multiple comparison tests. Variance homogeneity was evaluated using the Brown–Forsythe and Bartlett’s tests. P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.
