# Synergistic intragenic epigenetic deregulation by IDH2 and SRSF2 mutations causes mis-splicing of key transcriptional regulators


## Abstract
  Genes affecting DNA methylation (DNAme) are frequently comutated with splicing factors in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and associate with more aggressive phenotypes. To elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms, we deeply profiled wild-type and IDH2R140Q/SRSF2P95 single- or double-mutant AMLs. We find a unique set of mis-spliced genes and differentially methylated CpGs in double mutants. Mis-spliced exons are enriched in CCNG splicing enhancers and in the corresponding DNAme changes. Using a machine learning model, we can accurately predict exon inclusion levels from proximal CpGs. These CpGs are more likely to overlap footprints of RNA binding and chromatin-modifying complexes but not transcription factors. We also report unique gene expression profiles associated with each genotype; however, the differentially expressed genes do not overlap with mis-spliced transcripts. Instead, the mis-spliced genes encode for proteins that interact with the complexes regulating these differentially expressed genes. Thus, aberrant DNAme and splicing lead to the mis-splicing of key regulatory complexes, resulting in the aberrant gene expression profiles characteristic of these AMLs.


## INTRODUCTION
  The somatic mutational landscape of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) has been extensively characterized (1–4). Among the genes recurrently mutated in AML, gain-of-function mutations in isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 or 2 (IDH1/2) result in the production of 2-hydroxyglutarate (2-HG) (5). As 2-HG accumulates intracellularly, it inhibits α-ketoglutarate–dependent enzymes, including ten-eleven translocation enzymes (TET) that catalyze the demethylation of CpG dinucleotides. Thus, mutations in IDH1/2 genes are associated with DNA methylation (DNAme) changes, primarily hypermethylation (5–7). However, the location and magnitude of DNAme changes are affected by co-occurring mutations. For instance, the interaction of DNMT3A mutations with IDH1/2 at the epigenome, transcriptome, and phenotype levels has been well established (8–10).

  A noteworthy and large group of mutations in myeloid malignancies are those affecting splicing factor genes, including SF3B1, U2AF1, and SRSF2 (3, 11–13). SRSF2 mutations are found in 10 to 15% of patients with AML and are associated with worse outcomes. Proline at position 95 (P95) of SRSF2 is a hotspot mutation that causes a conformational change affecting the protein’s binding affinity to RNA (14–16). Wild-type (WT) SRSF2 recognizes CCNG or GGNG motifs on RNA equally well, but SRSF2P95 exhibits a bias for CCNG over GGNG motifs (17, 18). As these motifs constitute exonic splicing enhancers responsible for properly recruiting the core splicing machinery for exon recognition, SRSF2 mutations lead to abnormal splicing in AML.

  SRSF2 mutations overlap significantly with IDH2 mutations in AML (19). Functional studies in human and mouse models have demonstrated that these two mutations combined promote leukemogenesis and extensive splicing abnormalities in key genes, such as INTS3 and EZH2 (17, 19). There is significant colocalization of altered splicing events with DNAme changes in SRSF2/IDH2 double mutants, suggesting a link between the epigenome and splicing (19). However, a limitation of previous studies on the interaction of IDH2 and SRSF2 mutations lies in the lack of data of sufficient resolution to correlate changes in RNA splicing with DNAme. The HumanMethylation450 array generated in the AML The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset, as well as the enhanced reduced representation bisulfite sequencing data used in subsequent studies (19), captures only 2 to 20% of all CpGs of the human genome.

  In this study, we perform a comprehensive analysis of both DNAme and splicing profiles of AML patient samples with or without SRSF2 or IDH2 mutations and provide mechanistic insight into the observed mutational cooperativity. We identify specific underlying characteristics that render certain genes and exons vulnerable to mis-splicing in SRSF2-mutant samples, e.g., CCNG density. In addition, we report a strong relationship between DNAme and splicing patterns involving multiple RNA binding and epigenetic modifier complexes. In this context, IDH2 mutations shift the SRSF2P95-driven effect toward a different set of genes, enriched in key transcription factors (TFs) and epigenetic regulator complexes that, in turn, drive downstream transcriptional differences.


## RESULTS

### IDH2/SRSF2 double-mutant patients exhibit unique expression and DNAme profiles
  To obtain a comprehensive profile of DNAme in AML with or without IDH2R140Q or SRSF2P95 mutations (here, simply referred as IDH2 and SRSF2 mutations), we assembled a cohort of diagnostic samples from 26 patients. In this cohort, IDH2 and SRSF2 were the only mutations significantly different by group [false discovery rate (FDR) < 5%; Fisher’s exact tests], although we observed a trend for enrichment of RUNX1 and depletion of NPM1 mutations with SRSF2 mutations (fig. S1A and table S1). Given this trend, we mined two large, publicly available AML cohorts, namely, TCGA (1) and BeatAML (2), for mutations co-occurring with SRSF2 and found an enrichment of IDH2 and RUNX1 mutations and depletion of FLT3 and NPM1 mutations in SRSF2-mutant patients (FDR < 5%; hypergeometric test; fig. S1, B and C). Thus, the mutation profiles of our cohort are in agreement with what is observed and expected.

  We performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) on these samples at a sequencing depth sufficient to generate accurate expression, splicing, and DNAme profiles (table S1). On average, 23.8 million CpGs were captured per sample, thereby encompassing almost 85% of all the CpGs on the human genome. This almost-exhaustive DNAme profiling allowed us to study the interplay of the epigenome with the transcriptome in greater granularity and depth than previous attempts.

  We first examined the effect of IDH2 and SRSF2 mutations on gene expression and DNAme. Compared to AMLs without either mutation (here, control AMLs), all three genotypes (IDH2 and SRSF2 single mutants as well as IDH2/SRSF2 double mutants) presented a significant number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs; SRSF2 = 200, IDH2 = 138, double mutants = 241 DEGs). Most of these changes were unique to each genotype, with 179 uniquely DEGs in double mutants (Fig. 1, A and B, and table S2). When comparing the DNAme profiles of IDH2 single mutant to that of control AMLs, we identified 63,446 differentially methylated regions (DMRs), most of which were, as expected, hypermethylated. By comparison, SRSF2 mutant cases had only 19,499 DMRs, most of which were hypomethylated, while double-mutant cases had 94,935 DMRs with respect to control samples, almost 80% of which were uniquely differentially methylated in that genotype (Fig. 1, C and D, and table S2).

  To gain insight into how these transcriptional and epigenetic differences may affect the biology of each group, we performed pathway analysis of the DEG and DMR identified. Several signaling pathways were up-regulated at the transcriptional level in all three genotypes compared to WT samples, including Hippo, vascular endothelial growth factor, transforming growth factor–β, and ErbB signaling. Only one pathway was uniquely up-regulated to each of the single mutants. By contrast, calcium, nuclear factor κB, hypoxia-inducible factor 1, and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathways as well as cell cycle were uniquely up-regulated in the expression profiles of the double-mutant samples (Fig. 1E and table S3). To link DNAme status to molecular pathways, we assigned each CpG tile to its closest gene (see Materials and Methods). We found no common pathway associated with the hypomethylated regions among single and double mutants, while the hypermethylated DMRs were annotated to genes involved in calcium signaling in all three genotypes. We note that while, at the expression level, MAPK signaling was found enriched only in DEGs from the double mutant, at the methylation level it was significantly enriched in the double mutants’ hypermethylated DMRs as well as the IDH2 single mutants’ hypomethylated ones (Fig. 1F and table S3).

  These results argue for a synergistic effect of IDH2 and SRSF2 mutations in shaping the expression and methylation profiles of AML. At the DNAme level, we observed a considerably higher number of DMRs in double mutants, but we were not able to make a connection with the observed expression differences at the pathway level.


### Subtype-specific epigenetic identity is defined by DNAme status at enhancers and coding genes
  Given the seemingly disconnected nature of the epigenome and the transcriptome and prior observations that retrotransposon methylation status may contribute to transcriptional regulation (9, 20, 21), we examined the genomic location of the identified DMRs in relationship to other known genomic elements as well as their sequence context. We first intersected the DMRs with repeat elements. Although an average of 46% of CpG tiles in our data overlapped repeat elements, we did not observe any clear enrichment of a specific family among the DMRs, indicating that retrotransposon methylation is unlikely to play a differential functional role in regulating gene expression in the context of these mutations (fig. S2).

  Next, since we had previously demonstrated that enhancer methylation status helps determine cell identity in AML (22), we looked at whether the DMRs identified in the three different subtypes were enriched at enhancer regions as identified in CD34+ hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (22). Compared to a background distribution of 0.5% of CpGs overlapping with enhancers, DMRs from all three subtypes were enriched at enhancers. When we stratified the analysis by the direction of the methylation difference, hypermethylated CpGs in both single and double mutants were significantly enriched at enhancers, even for the SRSF2-mutant subtype in which only 17% of the DMR profile consisted of hypermethylated regions (FDR < 5%; hypergeometric tests; Fig. 2A). In contrast, hypomethylated DMRs were only enriched at enhancers in the SRSF2-mutant subtype, which was the only one with a predominantly hypomethylated profile.

  When we searched for TF motif enrichment among the DMRs, we found an average of 34 and 17 motifs enriched in the hyper- and hypomethylated regions, respectively (Fig. 2B). Despite many motifs being found across all genotypes, we also identified motifs uniquely enriched in each group (Fig. 2C and table S4). Specifically, the hypomethylated CpGs of double-mutant AMLs were uniquely enriched in the motifs of MNT, MLX, and MAX; a finding that agrees with the uniquely higher expression of the MAPK signaling pathway in the double mutants. We also noted an enrichment of FOX motifs unique to the hypermethylated CpGs of the double mutants, in accordance with the lower expression of metabolic processes unique in this genotype (Fig. 1C).

  Last, given SRSF2’s function as a splicing factor, we examined the location of the DMRs with respect to coding and noncoding regions to determine whether DNAme may be playing a role in regulating gene splicing. Hypermethylated CpG regions were enriched in protein-coding genes only in AML cases that carried an IDH2 mutation, whether isolated or not (P < 0.05; chi-square test). On the other hand, hypomethylated regions were depleted from protein-coding genes in all three genotypes and enriched in intergenic regions in cases that carried an SRSF2 mutation alone or combined with an IDH2 mutation (Fig. 2D). Collectively, these results suggest that, in the context of IDH2/SRSF2 mutations in AML, the effect of DNAme on the transcriptome is more complex than one-to-one relationship and at least twofold: one component involving long-range interactions and one localized within coding genes.


### Mis-spliced exons overlap with differential methylation events and exhibit biases in their CCNG density
  We looked for splicing differences in the IDH2 and SRSF2 single- and double-mutant AMLs as compared to the control samples and found that most differential splicing events involved exons being either promoted or repressed [i.e., cassette exons (CEs)] compared to control AMLs, with the greatest number of significant events observed in double-mutant AMLs (Fig. 3A and table S2). Similar to our observations of transcriptional and epigenetic differences, the CEs were mostly unique to each genotype, with 518 events unique to double-mutant cases (Fig. 3B). Since different mutations can affect the same gene at different exons (e.g., the second exon of PREPL was repressed in IDH2 and SRSF2 single mutants, but the sixth exon of the same gene was repressed in double mutants), we next evaluated the degree of overlap at the gene level. Examining the genes that contain at least one CE, we still observed a greater number of mis-spliced genes in double-mutant AMLs, most of which were uniquely affected in this subtype (Fig. 3C). We next evaluated the effect of the CEs on transcript fate and found that promoted exons (31% of all CE) resulted in frameshift insertions in an average 29% of the transcripts; none resulted in a poison exon. Repressed exons (69% of all CE) caused frameshift deletions in 53% of the transcripts on average, 72% of which were predicted to be degraded by nonsense-mediated decay (NMD) (table S2). Pathway enrichment analysis using the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) algorithm revealed that differentially spliced genes were not enriched in any specific biological pathway (table S3). Last, to validate the genotype-specific effect of these splicing events at the protein level, we performed Western blot analysis of PAK1, CCM2, and PPOX in K562 cells expressing IDH2R140Q and/or SRSF2P95H mutations (Fig. 3D).

  Previous observations have shown that gene architecture, i.e., gene, exon and intron length, number of exons, guanine/cytosine (G/C), and retrotransposon content, has important implications in the gene’s expression and regulation (9, 23). We first assessed the positional relationship of the CE relative to the exon harboring the start codon. On average, the CE represented the seventh exon within the transcript; no CE contained the start codon. Moreover, in ~76% of mis-spliced transcripts, the CE was located downstream, rather than immediately adjacent to, the annotated transcriptional start site (TSS) or start codon–containing exon (table S2). We then tested whether differential methylation co-occurs with differential splicing events and examined the overlap of DMRs with the upstream and downstream intron-exon unions of CEs (either repressed or promoted). We observed that in all three genotypes, this overlap was significantly more than expected, being nearly twice as likely to find a DMR on or adjacent to CE than the rest of the genome (FDR < 5%; hypergeometric tests; Fig. 3E). On the basis of these observations, we hypothesized that the mis-spliced gene sets would have common underlying architectural features that enable the colocalization of DNAme and splicing events. To this end, we first examined features of the CE and the genes they affect. We found that in all three genotypes, genes with CE were longer and had a greater number of exons than expected by random chance (FDR < 5%; Kolmogorov-Smirnov and chi-square tests; fig. S3), which, in combination with the increase in DMRs, may result in an increased probability of mis-splicing. We next analyzed the specific CE that are repressed or promoted and examined their structure and sequence composition. We found no significant differences in length, G/C content, or CpG density, except in the promoted exons in IDH2 single mutants and repressed exons in double mutants, both of which were significantly sparser in CpGs than expected (FDR < 5%; Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests; fig. S4, A to C). A sparser CpG landscape suggests less biological redundancy and higher vulnerability of splicing mechanisms to deregulation of CpG methylation, as caused, for example, by IDH2 mutants. Focusing on the exonic splicing enhancers that SRSF2 recognizes, more than 90% of the exons genome-wide had at least one CCNG or GGNG motif, a proportion that was conserved at CE (fig. S4, D and E). However, we observed a direct correlation between exon promotion and CCNG density, so that CE promoted in SRSF2 single mutants or double mutants had significantly more CCNG motifs per 1000 bp than expected, while CE that were repressed had a significantly lower density of CCNGs (FDR < 5%; Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests; Fig. 3F). By contrast, there were no significant differences in the density of GGNG motifs (Fig. 3G).

  The above results argue for an architectural vulnerability for the mis-spliced exons correlating with adjacent DNAme changes, even in the absence of epigenetic modifier mutations. When SRFS2 mutations occur in the context of more extensive DNAme differences due to coexisting IDH2 mutations, the effect is shifted toward a different and greater number of CCNG-dense exons being skipped. Thus, our results suggest that it is this shared motif density that renders these genes vulnerable to the combinatorial effect of mutant epigenetic and splicing regulatory machinery.


### Exonic architecture drives mis-splicing in murine models of Idh2 and Srsf2 mutations
  To determine whether this relationship between genomic architecture of CE and the presence of IDH2-driven methylation is conserved, we resorted to genetically engineered mouse models where these mutations occur in a clean genetic background, without the presence of the confounding factors found in primary human specimens. For this purpose, we leveraged our previously generated RNA-seq data from sorted Lin− cKit+ bone marrow cells from Idh2+/+Srsf2+/+ (WT), Idh2R140Q/+, Srsf2P95H/+ conditional knock-in mice as well as Idh2R140Q/+Srsf2P95H/+ double knock-in (DKI) mice (19).

  Global analysis of gene expression levels was able to clearly distinguish the four genotypes on a principal components analysis (PCA) projection and hierarchical clustering, arguing for a strong combinatorial effect of the two mutations in the DKI setting (Fig. 4, A and B, and fig. S5, A and B). Similar to our human data, CE were the predominant splicing events and were largely unique to each genotype (Fig. 4, C and D). However, there was limited conservation on the splicing of evolutionary orthologous genes (fig. S5C). We then examined the same architectural features as in the human dataset. Once again, we observed that genes with repressed exons in the Srsf2P95H/+ and DKI genotypes were longer and had a significantly higher number of exons (FDR < 5%; Kolmogorov-Smirnov and chi-square tests; fig. S6). This observation supports the importance of the structural design of genes in the splicing abnormalities driven by these mutations.

  We then focused on the CE that are significantly promoted or repressed. Our analyses did not identify any significant differences in length but revealed that, as in humans, repressed exons were sparser in CpGs and had less G/C content than expected (FDR < 5%; Kolmogorov-Smirnov test; fig. S7). As before, promoted exons Srsf2P95H/+ and DKI mice had a significantly higher density of CCNG motifs (FDR < 5%; Kolmogorov-Smirnov test; Fig. 4E), with no significant biases in the density of GGNG splicing enhancers (Fig. 4F). The above results validate our observations from human AML and demonstrate that exonic architecture drives exon promotion or repression in the presence of Srsf2 mutations, a relationship that is evolutionarily conserved in humans and mice.


### Exon inclusion levels correlate strongly with neighboring DNAme levels
  To validate the significance of DNAme in affecting splicing, we examined whether we could use DNAme data to predict splicing patterns. Since CE were the most frequent splicing events in our analyses, we focused on exon inclusion levels expressed as a percentage, i.e., a continuous variable between zero (the exon is excluded from all quantified transcripts) and 100 (the exon is included in all quantified transcripts). We assembled a set of 28,393 exons from 7836 genes and 496,324 CpG tiles proximal to those exons. A simple correlation of exon inclusion with DNAme percentage at individual CpG tiles did not return significant relationships (fig. S8A). Thus, we hypothesized that regional methylation played a more significant role. To test this, we used a machine learning approach based on supervised principal components (superPC) (24) that integrates information from multiple adjacent CpG tiles to predict exon inclusion (Fig. 5A; see Materials and Methods for details).

  Using this approach, we were able to accurately predict exon inclusion using DNAme data as input. We found that 95% of the tested exons had more than 0.75 Pearson correlation coefficient between the actual and the predicted inclusion levels (Fig. 5, B and C). We then used this model to extract the CpGs with the highest weight score per exon prediction and asked whether this reduced number of CpGs is sufficient to predict the inclusion of the corresponding exon. For each exon, we started with the top four CpGs; if the new model performed significantly worse than the model using all proximal CpGs, then we increased the set of CpGs by one and repeated these steps until we found a model that did not yield worse predictions than the initial one. Notably, that was relatively rare as four CpGs were sufficient to perform equally or better in predicting exon inclusion from DNAme (Fig. 5D and table S5). Here, we refer to the minimum set of CpGs whose methylation can predict exon inclusion as the most informative CpGs.

  Next, we tested whether these most informative CpGs reside within areas with specific genomic features or are differentially methylated. Around 40 to 50% of these CpGs were part of CCNG motifs yet, conversely, CCNG motifs were not enriched in this set of most informative CpGs; the same held true for the GGNG motif (fig. S8B). We then took an orthogonal approach to examine the importance of CpGs overlapping CCNG in predicting exon inclusion. Specifically, we trained superPC models per exon with CpGs overlapping CCNG motifs or with CpGs not overlapping CCNG motifs. Notably, we found no significant differences between CCNG-only and non-CCNG CpGs (fig. S8C), suggesting that DNAme status is important beyond the CCNG motif itself.

  Given this observation, we hypothesized that these CpGs could play a role in recruiting regulatory proteins. To this end, we then examined whether the most informative CpGs could bind TFs or other gene regulatory complexes (GRCs). Sequence search did not yield any significant TF motif, but we were able to find a set of 49 proteins with significant binding enrichment by chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by RNA-seq (ChIP-seq) at the most informative CpGs. RNA binding proteins/complexes, like AGO1, RBM22, ribonucleoproteins or splicing regulators, as well as chromatin modifiers, like KDM1A, MBD2, and SIN3A, and TFs, like ETS1, MXI1, and TBP, were found differentially enriched in these CpGs across the three genotypes (Fig. 5, E and F, and table S5). When individual GRCs were grouped into functional categories, we found that RNA binders and chromatin modifiers were enriched, while TFs were depleted from the set of most informative CpGs. Specifically, CpGs of the IDH2-mutant mis-spliced exons were enriched in chromatin modifiers, while RNA binders were enriched in those of the SRSF2 mutant. In the case of double mutants, although RNA binders and chromatin modifiers were not enriched individually, the union of these sets was found to be significantly enriched, reflecting the synergistic effect of the combined mutations (Fig. 5G). These GRCs overlapped with less than a fifth of all most informative CpGs but more than a quarter of all the mis-spliced exons (Fig. 5H). Of those exons, most were occupied by more than one GRC (Fig. 5I), suggesting strong interrelationships and codependencies between RNA processing and epigenetic modifications.

  To validate the relationship between exon inclusion levels and DNAme in a cellular system free of mutations that might affect splicing or methylation patterns, we used a splicing reporter minigene assay containing the CE of interest, along with the upstream and downstream introns and exons, and expressed in 293T cells. For each of the CE being validated, we analyzed the ratio between the CE inclusion and exclusion transcripts resulting from transfecting the cells with the original construct. This was compared to the same ratio resulting after transfecting the cells with a construct treated with M.SssI methylase, which methylates all CpG dinucleotides (fig. S9). We analyzed the inclusion levels for exons 14 of TARS, 13 of PEX1, 13 of SH3D21, and 4 of PRC1, all of which were differentially spliced and predicted to be affected by DNAme (tables S2, S5, and S6). In all four cases, we find a significant effect of DNAme on the inclusion levels of the respective exons (Fig. 5, J and K).

  In summary, we found that exon inclusion can be robustly predicted and affected by DNAme of an exon’s proximal CpGs. The GRCs localized at these genomic regions involve RNA binding proteins/complexes and epigenetic modifiers, underlining an extensive cross-talk between the epigenome and splicing.


### Mis-splicing of key transcriptional regulators explains expression differences across the different gene mutations
  As previously reported, DEGs had virtually no overlap with the genes with CE events, either in the human or the murine cohorts (Fig. 6A and fig. S5D). With the assumption that splicing differences would precede expression differences, we hypothesized that the mis-spliced genes either are or physically interact with the TFs regulating the DEGs. For this purpose, we performed DNA motif analysis of the promoters of the DE genes to identify DNA binding proteins driving these expression differences. We found a total of 120 DNA motifs uniquely enriched in the double-mutant AML gene signature. Among these unique motifs were HOXA3, FOS, and SOX2 motifs (Fig. 6B and table S3). Notably, none of the DNA binding proteins uniquely associated with these motifs corresponded to CE events.

  Since mis-spliced transcripts are mostly predicted to be degraded through NMD and given the lack of mis-splicing of the DNA binding proteins themselves, we hypothesized that instead these mis-spliced genes were physically interacting with the identified DNA binders. To test this, we used publicly available protein-protein interaction data (25) and retrieved known protein interactors for the DNA binding proteins enriched at the DE genes. Notably, none of the known interactors for the unique DNA binders associated with the IDH2R140Q signature and only 8% of those uniquely associated with the SRSF2P95 signature were mis-spliced. In contrast, 30% of all known interactors with unique DNA binders enriched at the double-mutant signature corresponded to genes with CE events. These interactors included NCOR1, EZH2, and RUNX2 (Fig. 6, C and D, and table S3).

  Last, we sought to determine whether CE events could also affect epigenetic modifiers differentially across the three genotypes. We found that double mutants exhibited the greatest number of chromatin modifiers with CE (Fig. 6E). On the basis of this observation, we hypothesized that this might render double-mutant cells uniquely sensitive to inhibition of epigenetic pathways. Therefore, we treated K562 transduced with IDH2R140Q and/or SRSF2P95H mutations with romidepsin, tazemetostat, or iP300w that inhibit HDAC1, EZH2, and EP300, respectively. Compared to untreated control samples, we found that romidepsin treatment resulted in a significant decrease in cell viability in the double mutants, which was significantly more than either of the single-mutant cells. We observed no significant effect of tazemetostat or iP300w (Fig. 6F and fig. S10). Collectively, the above results suggest that the mis-spliced genes interact with TFs and epigenetic modifiers and that differences in their protein abundance could modulate the activity of these TFs or epigenetic modifiers who, in turn, affect the expression of their targets, including signaling genes (Fig. 6G).


## DISCUSSION
  In this study, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of the DNAme and splicing landscape in AML samples harboring IDH2 or SRSF2 mutations, addressing gaps in previous studies. We describe a strong and causal relationship between the epigenome and the splicing of proximal exons, not only as the overlap of DMRs and mis-spliced exons on the genome but also as a continuum of DNAme with exon inclusion levels. We find that double-mutant AMLs exhibit more and different DNAme changes than either of the single-mutant AMLs; these changes correlate with the splicing dynamics. The mis-spliced genes are key regulators significantly more likely to interact with TFs, epigenetic regulators, or regulatory complexes that, in turn, drive gene expression changes. Thus, we propose a mechanism through which the observed expression changes in SRSF2/IDH2 double mutants are the result of the mis-splicing of their transcriptional regulators.

  The cooperation of mutations to promote leukemogenesis is an active area of research, with examples including WT1, TET2, FLT3, or DNMT3A (26–28). Previous studies have associated SRSF2 mutants with different genes in different diseases. For instance, SRSF2 is frequently comutated with JAK2 in myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative neoplasms, while it is comutated with TET2 in the context of monocytosis (29, 30). When mutated, SRSF2 causes changes in RNA splicing that result in alteration of signaling and TF networks that display a malignant or premalignant phenotype, depending on the context (30–32). In AML, the coexistence of SRSF2 with IDH2 mutations promotes a more aggressive phenotype (19).

  Previous research has uncovered the binding motifs of SRSF2, and these motifs are enriched in mis-spliced exons (17, 19, 30). Here, we build on this line of research and show that the density of the CCNG motifs as well as the neighboring DNAme status is a determining factor for their promotion or repression. We further link these findings with the overall gene architecture and show that genes susceptible to altered splicing in SRSF2 mutant cells have more exons than expected by random chance. These findings underscore gene architecture as a central component in the expression of biological information. The assembly of the spliceosome is a complex process, and SRSF2 interacts with a series of proteins and RNAs (33); the kinetics behind splicing argue for a considerable time sensitivity of the process (34–36). As the speed of transcription is dependent on gene length (37), our data pinpoint the architectural vulnerability of the spliced exons as a determinant for mis-splicing.

  Our results highlight the rich and important information embedded at the DNAme level. We find that it is not one single CpG but rather the methylation landscape of the whole region that can explain exon inclusion. Although as low as four CpGs can mathematically predict exon inclusion, we did not find an enrichment of a specific sequence regulatory element among the most informative CpGs. However, we identified RNA binding proteins and chromatin modifiers binding to these regions, arguing for a highly tuned process. Two potential, but not mutually exclusive, mechanisms can explain this interplay. On one hand, the speed of transcription could be affected by changes in DNAme, thus not allowing for proper assembly of splicing factors. CpG methylation has a documented effect on the physical properties of double-stranded DNA (38, 39), and it affects RNA polymerase speed (40). On the other hand, changes in DNAme can change the affinity of DNA binding proteins (41), like MeCP2 (42), that in turn affect splicing. Recent evidence has also involved AGO proteins with splicing, chromatin, and the epigenome (43, 44), further extending the ways the epigenome can affect splicing. On the basis of our results, we cannot single out one of those mechanisms, but it seems likely that a combination of both can explain the impact of methylation on splicing.

  Last, analysis of genotype-specific expression signatures revealed that mis-splicing of key TF interactors may be responsible for the expression differences among the different AML genotypes. However, we note that this analysis was based entirely on in silico motif and interactome analyses. Further experimental validation will be required to analyze the different combinations and genomic footprints of interactors making up these GRCs under the different genotypes. Furthermore, our analyses focus on DNAme, and our assay does not distinguish between cytosine methylation and hydroxymethylation, nor can it capture any rapid, dynamic methylation/demethylation cycle potentially happening on these CpGs (45) that could correlate with splicing.

  In summary, by performing comprehensive RNA and DNAme profiling, we provide mechanistic insight into how mutations in IDH2 and SRSF2 cause the mis-splicing of key transcription regulators. IDH2 mutations have a profound effect on the epigenome and epigenetic modifiers on mis-spliced exons, while SRSF2 mutations affect splicing by affecting RNA-protein interactions. The affected genes are the most architecturally vulnerable, and when both mutations are combined, the effect is more potent and deregulates central regulatory complexes, resulting in the up-regulation of signaling genes.


## MATERIALS AND METHODS

### Patient sample acquisition and processing
  Studies were approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (#06-107 and #12-245) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki protocol. Clinical bone marrow or peripheral blood specimens were collected from 25 deidentified AML specimens. Mononuclear cells (MNCs) were isolated through Ficoll density centrifugation at diagnosis and frozen for later use. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients at the time of collection.


### Gene expression and splicing profiling
  For RNA-seq, polyA-selected strand-specific SureSelect (Agilent) mRNA libraries were sequenced on a HiSeq 2500 Illumina’s system. Adapters were trimmed from the acquired fastq files with cutadapt (v. 1.18) with parameter -m10, and reads were mapped on the GRCh37 human genome with STAR (v. 2.7.1a) with parameters chimSegmentMin = 10, outFilterMismatchNmax = 5, alignEndsType = EndToEnd, alignIntronMax = 1,000,000, and alignSJDBoverhangMin = 6 (46). Gene expression profiles were extracted with RSEM (v. 1.2.28) (47) using Ensembl v75 for gene annotations.

  Differential gene expression was performed with DESeq2 (v. 1.24.0) on gene counts (48). To call genes significant, we set the thresholds of adjusted P value at 0.05 and of the absolute fold change at 2. We also filtered out genes with median expression less than 1 fragment per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) and then used Significance Analysis of Microarrays (SAM; v. 3.0) on the FPKM-normalized reads to perform differential expression analysis (49). We used the list of genes ranked by the (directional) SAM score for downstream pathway and upstream regulator analyses.

  We used the STAR-generated bam files to identify significant splicing differences among mutation subtypes with rMATS (v. 4.1.2) (50), requiring an FDR threshold of 5% and an absolute mean inclusion level difference greater than 25%. Only reads spanning exon junctions were considered in our analysis. Transcript fate, e.g., poison exons, degradation through NMD (the 50-nt rule), or frameshifts, was identified using SETranslateNMD of SpliceTools (51).


### Methylation profiling
  Genomic DNA was isolated from MNCs for AML cases using the AllPrep DNA/RNA kit from QIAGEN (Valencia, CA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and used for WGBS. Libraries were constructed with the Methyl-Seq Kit (Swift Biosciences) using 75 ng of input DNA and sequenced on the NovaSeq Illumina’s system. Resulting fastq files were trimmed from adapters using cutadapt (v. 1.18) with parameters -U10 and -m25 and then mapped on GRCh37 with bismark and bismark_methylation_extractor (v. 0.22.1) (52). Mapped data were destranded, filtered for coverage (kept CpGs with coverage between 10 and 400 reads), and tiled with a tiling window of 20 nt (we use the terms CpG and CpG tile interchangeably). This set served as the background methylation set. DMRs were found with methylSig (v. 1.10.0) at an adjusted P value threshold of 0.05 and an absolute mean methylation difference of 25% (53).


### Analysis of publicly available datasets
  We accessed mutation information for the TCGA and BeatAML cohorts from the original publications (1, 2). For both cohorts, SRSF2 mutations were drawn from our previous reanalyses of these samples (19).

  We downloaded publicly available RNA-seq data for mouse sorted bone marrow Lin− cKit+ cells (PRJNA436369) (19). Adapters were cut, and mapping was performed in the same manner as we processed the human RNA-seq data but using the mouse GRCm38 genome and gene annotations from Ensembl v98 (54). PCA was run on log2-normalized FPKM matrix of genes with >1 mean FPKM expression. We evaluated splicing differences between Idh2R140Q/+, Srsf2P95H/+, or DKI mice compared to WT cases with rmats (v. 4.1.2), considering only reads spanning the respective splice junctions. We required an FDR threshold of 5% and an absolute mean methylation difference greater than 20%.


### Integration of publicly available data
  Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways were downloaded from KEGG (accessed on 06 September 2021); we kept the pathways included under the metabolism, genetic, and environmental information processing and cellular process categories (55). Coordinates of enhancers from human CD34+ cells and CpGs relevant to DNMT3A/IDH2-mutant AMLs were drawn from our previous published studies (9, 22). TF motifs were downloaded from JASPAR (JASPAR2022 CORE for humans) (56) and searched on the methylation tiles with fimo (MEME v. 5.5.1) (57). Repeat elements were downloaded from RepeatMasker (v. 4.0.5 for hg19). The Protein Interaction Knowledgebase (PICKLE; v. 3.3) was used to draw information on cross-checked gene-normalized protein-protein interactions (25). We searched for the JASPAR motifs at the promoters of the DEGs; we defined promoters as the TSS extended by 1000 bp on both sites. We examined the enrichment of motifs at the promoters of the DEGs using hypergeometric tests and correcting the resulting P values to FDR values.

  ENCODE’s TxnFactorChIPE3 table was drawn from the UCSC TableBrowser tool (accessed on 20 August 2019) and kept the data for K562 cells (58). We call the set of genes with ChIP-seq data as GRCs.

  We used DAVID (59) to examine the enrichment of KEGG pathways in differentially spliced genes. We used the SAM-ranked gene lists as an input for Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA v. 4.3.2) (60) for KEGG pathway enrichment analysis as well as for ENCODE’s ChIP-seq data using the GMT file assembled as described above. GSEA was run with parameters set_max = 500, set_min = 15, norm = meandiv, and nperm = 1000 and used an FDR threshold of 10%. We used polyEnrich of the ChIP-Enrich package (61) to examine for enrichment of the KEGG pathways based on DNAme differences. Specifically, we weighted each CpG tile based on the average methylation difference in the mutant (single or double mutant) samples with respect to the WT samples, annotating each tile to the nearest gene. We run positive (hypermethylation) and negative (hypomethylation) differences separately, each time replacing weights of the opposite sign with the minimum nonzero weight of the same sign. Naturally, for the negative methylation differences, we considered their absolute value. Polyenrich corrects for gene length, but to account for biases in the genomic distribution of the background set of CpG tiles, we run polyenrich in an unweighted manner. We considered a pathway as significantly enriched if: (i) FDRback > 20% and FDRcomp < 10% or if (ii) FDRcomp < FDRback/10, where FDRback and FDRcomp represent the respective FDR from the unweighted results (background) and from values as weighted in the comparison, respectively.

  Gene families were downloaded from HGNC’s BioMart tool (62). We grouped the genes in Argonaute RISC component family, spliceosomal A complex, spliceosomal B complex, spliceosomal E complex, major spliceosome, heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoproteins, and RNA binding motif containing as RNA binders. We grouped the genes in NSL histone acetyltransferase complex, NuRD complex subunits, histone deacetylase superfamily, SIN3 histone deacetylase complex subunits, class I HDAC complexes, and chromatin-modifying enzymes as chromatin modifiers.


### Prediction of exon inclusion from DNAme
  To predict exon inclusion from DNAme, we first filtered the rmats-generated inclusion levels to keep exons with sequencing read support in all samples and with enough variation across the dataset, i.e., less than 100% inclusion in more than half of the samples. Then, we intersected the genomic region of the exon with the CpG tiles with nonzero variation in the training and tests, as defined below. We defined the genomic region of each exon as the union of itself, its adjacent introns as well as the upstream and downstream exons. We kept exons with two or more CpG tiles in their genomic region, resulting in a set of 28,168 exons paired with a total of 507,159 CpG tiles.

  We used superPC (24) with a fourfold cross-validation (CV) strategy for continuous regression of exon inclusion for each sample based on the methylation levels of the paired, adjacent CpGs. Specifically, we split our cohort into four folds, assuring that all genotypes (WT, single, and double mutants) are represented in all four folds. For each exon, we trained four different superPC models; each model trained on the respective 75% of the samples and tested its prediction on the respective 25% samples. We used three components for the superPC model per prediction unless there were only two CpG tiles paired with the exon, in which case we used two. We assembled a vector of the predicted exon inclusion values from the respective test sets. Thus, the model that predicted the inclusion levels for each sample was trained on a different set of samples. We compared the predicted with the actual inclusion levels with Pearson correlation.

  We define feature importance (FI) as the absolute average feature scores of the trained models and then normalized them to 1. For each exon, we selected the top four most-FI CpG tiles and trained new superPC models with fourfold CV using only these tiles and evaluated the performance with Pearson correlation as above. To evaluate whether the models trained on the most-FI CpGs (let ri,m be the Pearson correlation between actual and predicted inclusion levels for exon i) performed significantly worse than the ones trained on all CpGs (ri,a), we performed a Pearson-Filon test of significance between ri,m and ri,a. If the P value of the Pearson’s and Filon’s test was less than 0.01 or if ri,m − ri,a < −0.05, then we repeated this process with the top five most-FI CpGs. We continued adding one CpG at a time, until both criteria were met. We intersected the set of most-FI CpGs with repeat elements from RepeatMasker and assigned a CpG tile to a repeat element if they overlapped, even partially. We assigned CCNG or GGNG motifs to CpG tiles if the motif’s sequence was within the tile.

  We also trained superPC models with fourfold CV but with different sets of CpG tiles. Specifically, we split CpG tiles based on whether they overlap CCNG motifs or not. In a similar manner as above, we trained models with CpGs overlapping CCNGs and CpGs not overlapping CCNGs elements that, for each exon i, we got Pearson correlations of actual and predicted inclusion levels ri,alu and ri,non, respectively. We used the Pearson’s and Filon’s test to test whether the difference between these coefficients was significant; resulting P values were corrected to FDR.


### Generation of cell lines, virus packaging, and transduction
  The K562 human myeloid leukemia cell line was purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; #CCL-243). All K562 cell lines were maintained in Iscove’s modified Dulbecco’s medium (IMDM) + 10% fetal calf serum. All cell lines were cultured at 37°C and 5% CO2 in the presence of penicillin (100 U/ml) and streptomycin (100 μg/ml). K562 SRSF2P95H transgenic cells expressing mCherry were generated as previously described (63). All cell lines were Mycoplasma-free and routinely tested by Antibody and Bioresource Core at the Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSKCC) (MycoAlert Mycoplasma Detection Kit, Lonza, LT07-701; MycoAlert Assay Control Set, Lonza, LT07-518).

  The human IDH2 gene with R140Q mutation was cloned into a yellow fluorescent protein (YFP) expressing vector pMSCV-IRES-YFP II (RRID:Addgene_52108) to generate MSCV-YFP-hIDH2-R140Q retroviral vector. For generation of the transgenic human IDH2 R140Q cell line, parental K562 cells from ATCC (#CCL-243) were transduced with the MSCV-YFP-hIDH2-R140Q vector, and mutant YFP+ cells were subject to fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) using a FACSAria III Cell Sorter (BD Biosciences, RRID:SCR_016695) to isolate the K562 IDH2 R140Q mutant expressing cell line. To create double mutant cells, K562 SRSF2P95H cells expressing mCherry, generated as previously described (63), were transduced with MSCV-YFP-hIDH2-R140Q retroviral vector, and double mutant cells were selected by FACS for mCherry+YFP+ cells. Retroviral supernatants were produced by transfecting human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293T cells purchased from ATCC (#CRL-3216, RRID:CVCL_0063) with retroviral constructs and packaging plasmids VSVG (RRID:Addgene_14888) and Gag/Pol (RRID:Addgene_14887) using polyethylenimine (Polysciences, #23966). Virus supernatants were collected at 48 and 72 hours after transfection and used for transduction. Cells were plated at 200,000 cells per well in the presence of polybrene (4 μg/ml; Sigma-Aldrich, #TR-1003-G) in 24-well plates and suspended in 1 ml of viral supernatant. Plates were spun at 2300 rpm for 90 min at 32°C.

  A pcDNA3.1–green fluorescent protein (GFP) mammalian expression vector was obtained from Addgene (RRID:Addgene_70219). SssI, New England Biolabs, #M0226S) was used according to the manufacturer’s protocol with the following modifications: reaction volume of 20 μl, 400 ng of total plasmid per reaction, and 640 μM diluted SAM.

### Statistical analyses
  Genomic arithmetic was performed with bedtools (v. 2.28.0) (64). Statistical analyses were conducted in R (v. 4.2.3) or Python (v. 2.7.16). We evaluated the enrichment or depletion of DMRs in enhancers, leukemia-relevant CpGs, TF motifs, and repeat elements with hypergeometric tests. We statistically evaluated the overlap of DMRs with coding and noncoding genes with chi-square tests, requiring a P value of less than 0.05 and an absolute percentage difference greater than 5% as compared to the background. Exonic space of a gene was defined as the union of all its exons, while intronic space was defined as the whole region of the gene minus its exonic space. Biases in the length of intronic or exonic space, the length of exons, G/C content, and the density of CpGs and CCNG and GGNG motifs in genes with skipped exons or among exons were evaluated with a one-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test with the background distribution estimated from the respective density in all exons that were included in the differential splicing analysis. Biases in the number of exons were evaluated with goodness-of-fit chi-square tests. The intersection of most-FI CpGs with repeat elements, ChIP-seq peaks of GRCs, and the enrichment of CCNG and GGNG motifs in the most-FI CpGs were evaluated with hypergeometric tests. When we mention the whole set of CpGs, we refer to the most-FI CpGs with respect to the background of all CpGs used to predict exon inclusion. When we examined the properties of the most-FI CpG tiles on the mis-spliced exons, our background was all the CpG tiles used for predicting exon inclusion of the mis-spliced exons. Unless noted, P values were corrected to FDR values.

  For our analyses, we used the numpy (v. 1.16.4), scipy (v. 1.2.1), and statsmodels (v. 0.10.1) Python packages and the R packages bsseq (v. 1.34.0) (65), chipenrich (v. 2.8.0) (61), cocor (v. 1.1-3) (66), data.table (v. 1.13.6), DESeq2 (v. 1.24.0) (48), genomation (v. 1.16.0) (67), GenomicRanges (v. 1.36.1) (68), ggplot2 (v. 3.3.3) (69), gplots (v. 3.3.1), MASS (v. 7.3-53) (70), methylSig (v. 0.5.2) (53), plotrix (v. 3.7-8), samr (v. 3.0) (49), scico (v. 1.3.0), superpc (24), and venneuler (v. 1.1-3).
