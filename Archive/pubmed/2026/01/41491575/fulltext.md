# Mitochondrial-related biomarkers as the diagnostic markers in sepsis induced acute respiratory distress syndrome


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03772-0.


## Introduction
  Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is a severe, life-threatening medical condition characterized by rapid onset in the lungs. Despite numerous advancements in intensive care, the mortality rate associated with ARDS still exceeds 40% [1, 2]. Typically, ARDS leads to increased pulmonary vascular permeability, excessive fluid accumulation in the lungs, and decreased oxygenation and gas exchange [3]. Patients with ARDS often exhibit symptoms such as severe shortness of breath, hypoxaemia, and respiratory failure, which may necessitate mechanical ventilation [4]. The pathogenesis of ARDS involves a complex interplay of inflammation and immune responses [5]. Triggered by various etiologies such as infection, trauma, or pulmonary injury, the condition manifests as an exaggerated inflammatory response leading to damage to the alveolar-capillary barrier [6]. This disruption results in leakage of fluid into the alveoli, impairing oxygen diffusion. Sepsis, a potentially life-threatening condition caused by the body's response to an infection, can lead to systemic inflammation and organ dysfunction [7, 8]. When sepsis affects the lungs, it can significantly exacerbate or directly lead to the development of ARDS. Current treatments focus on supportive care, including infection control, fluid management, and ventilatory support, but these approaches often fall short in reversing the underlying pathophysiological changes. This underscores the urgent need for novel biomarkers to enhance comprehension of mechanisms behind sepsis-induced ARDS and to identify therapeutic targets. Identifying these biomarkers may enhance diagnosis and facilitate more customized therapies, ultimately improving patient outcomes in this critically ill population.

  Growing evidence implicates mitochondrial dysfunction as a pivotal mechanism in sepsis-induced ARDS [9, 10]. Beyond their role in energy metabolism [11, 12], mitochondria are central to the disease pathology through multiple interconnected pathways. In sepsis, impaired mitochondria amplify oxidative stress and disrupt calcium homeostasis, triggering a destructive cycle of NADPH oxidase-driven reactive oxygen species (ROS) bursts, NLRP3 inflammasome activation, and pyroptosis, which ultimately progresses from inflammation to fibrosis in ARDS individuals. The nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB)-NLRP3-neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) axis appears crucial in driving alveolar-capillary barrier disruption [13]. Furthermore, oxidized mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) activates inflammatory responses via Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9) and cyclic GMP–AMP synthase–stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS–STING) pathways, creating a feed-forward loop of lung injury [14]. Conversely, protective mitophagy facilitates damaged mitochondrial clearance, with nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2 (Nrf2) activation and the protein kinase C alpha/microRNA-15a-5p/pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 4 (PRKCA/miR-15a-5p/PDK4) axis demonstrating protective effects in experimental models [15]. Therapeutically, mitochondrial-targeted strategies show considerable promise. Compounds like 4-octyl itaconate (4-OI) alleviate ARDS by preserving mitochondrial integrity, reducing mitochondrial ROS (mtROS)/mtDNA release, and suppressing NLRP3 activation via STING/interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) signaling [16]. Similarly, the thyroid hormone analog Sobetirome (GC-1) mitigates pulmonary fibrosis in epithelial cells by enhancing mitochondrial function through Nrf2-mediated antioxidant responses, thereby inhibiting ROS-activated p53 and the expression of its target gene apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD (ASC) [17]. These findings position mitochondrial pathways as viable targets for innovative ARDS treatments.

  While mitochondrial dysfunction is implicated in sepsis-induced ARDS, current evidence often stems from studies on isolated genes or pathways. A systematic, exploratory investigation into mitochondrial-associated gene networks as potential diagnostic biomarkers, particularly in the context of the immune microenvironment, remains limited. To preliminarily address this gap, we designed a bioinformatics-based discovery approach. Our study integrates cross-species transcriptome data (from human whole blood and mouse lung tissue) with immune infiltration and single-cell RNA-seq analyses, aiming to identify candidate mitochondrial-associated biomarkers and preliminarily characterize their functional and immunological associations.

  It is important to note that our approach deliberately utilized cross-species and multi-tissue data warrants explicit justification. The selection of whole blood as the primary analytical starting point was deliberate and methodologically justified. Clinically, obtaining lung tissue samples from ARDS patients is highly challenging due to invasive risks and ethical constraints, whereas whole blood represents an accessible and informative source that reflects the systemic host response in critical illness. Importantly, whole blood has been widely validated in previous sepsis-induced ARDS studies as a reliable medium for biomarker discovery [18, 19]. To further strengthen tissue relevance and biological validation, we incorporated an lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced mouse ARDS model dataset (GSE241186) and performed in vitro experiments using the mouse alveolar type II epithelial cell line MLE-12—a well-established model that closely recapitulates key alveolar injury mechanisms central to ARDS pathology [20, 21]. Thus, our multi-tiered framework—which combines clinically accessible human blood data with lung-specific animal and cellular models—aligns with recent advances in the field [22, 23] and ensures that our exploratory findings are not only translationally feasible but also biologically grounded. This integrated work aims to generate robust hypotheses and provide a foundational resource for future mechanistic and translational studies.


## Materials and methods

### Data acquisition
  The transcriptome datasets GSE32707 (GPL10558), GSE66890 (GPL6244), GSE241186 (GPL24247) and single cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset GSE264032 (platform: GPL30215) for sepsis-induced ARDS were all downloaded from gene expression omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). GSE32707 dataset included 144 whole blood samples, while 31 sepsis-induced ARDS and 58 sepsis samples were extracted for further analysis as training set. GSE66890 datatset contained 62 whole blood samples, from which 29 sepsis-induced ARDS and 28 sepsis samples were utilized for validation set. In training and validation sets, sepsis-induced ARDS samples were defined as disease group, while sepsis samples were named as control group. As for GSE241186 dataset, 5 ARDS samples, 3 control samples and 4 umbilical cord derived-mesenchymal stem cells (UC-MSCs) were screening out for subsequent analysis. GSE264032 dataset included 1 acute lung injury (ALI) and 1 control samples, using for single cell analysis. Furthermore, 2,030 mitochondrial-related genes (MRGs) were retrieved from the MitoCarta 3.0 database (https://www.broadinstitute.org/mitocarta/mitocarta30-inventory-mammalian-mitochondrial-proteins-and-pathways), along with gene set enrichment analyses (GSEA, http://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp) [24].


### Differential expression analysis
  To investigate the different expression of gene between disease and control groups in training and validation sets, “limma” package (version 3.54.2) [25] was employed to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) using the criteria of |log2 fold change (FC)|> 0.2 and p < 0.05 [16, 26]. The findings were presented using a volcanic map and heat map, generated by “ggplot2” package (version 3.5.1) [27, 28] and “pheatmap” package (version 1.0.12) [29], respectively. The heat map displayed top 20 DEGs sorted by log2FC. The DEGs that were up- and down-regulated in training and validation sets were respectively intersected, resulting in the identification of common DEGs between two sets.


### Identification and analyses of differentially expressed MRGs (DE-MRGs)
  DEGs and MRGs were intersected to obtain to acquire DE-MRGs, the venn diagram was illustrated using “ggvenn” package (version 0.1.10) [30]. Following the acquisition of DE-MRGs, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were conducted using “clusterProfiler” package (adj.p < 0.05). To investigate the correlation of DE-MRGs on protein level, a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was established using the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING) database (http://string.embl.de/) with low confidence threshold of 0.15.


### Determination of biomarkers for sepsis-induced ARDS
  Utilizing DE-MRGs, three machine learning algorithms, containing least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), Boruta, and support vector machine-recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) were employed to identify feature genes using “glmnet” (version 4.1-8) [31], “Boruta” (version 8.0.0) [32] and “e1071” package (version 1.7-14) [33], respectively. The LASSO regression was applied using the "glmnet" package with tenfold cross-validation and type.measure = "deviance" to determine the optimal penalty parameter (λ), which was selected based on the minimum binomial deviance criterion (lambda.min). The Boruta algorithm was run with default parameters, utilizing the mean decrease in accuracy (meanDecreaseAccuracy) as the importance measure and comparing features against shuffled shadow features via an internal Z-score evaluation to confirm the final set. For the SVM-RFE algorithm, a linear kernel function was adopted for its stability and interpretability in high-dimensional gene data. The recursive feature elimination process was configured to remove the bottom 10% of features ranked by importance in each iteration. The convergence of feature genes obtained from three machine learning were recognized as candidate biomarkers. Then, the expression of candidate biomarkers was evaluated, genes exhibiting substantial differences between disease and control groups in both training and validation set were ultimately identified as biomarkers for following research.


### Construction of nomogram
  A nomogram was developed in training dataset based on biomarkers utilizing “rms” package (version 6.8-0) [34] to forecast disease occurrence. Each biomarker was associated with a score, the aggregate of these scores constituted the total points. The diagnosis likelihood of the disease was projected based on the total points. A greater total of points correlated with an increased chance of sickness. The efficiency of nomogram was evaluated using calibration curve, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, and decision curve analysis (DCA). ROC and DCA analyses were both conducted in training and validation sets.


### GSEA
  Spearman correlation analysis of biomarkers and all genes was conducted using “psych” package (version 2.4.3) [35], with genes prioritized according to their correlation coefficient. Then GSEA was performed utilizing “clusterProfiler” package within c2.cp.kegg.symbols.gmt gene set, obtained from GSEA (http://www.gsea-MSigdb.org/gsea/msigdb). The pathways were evaluated based on |NES|> 1 and adj.p < 0.05, top 10 pathways of significance were visualized.


### Immune infiltration analysis
  To evaluate infiltration condition of immune cells in different samples, “CIBERSORT” package (version 0.1.0) [36, 37] was used to calculate the percentage of 22 immune cells. A bar plot illustrating the percentage of infiltration abundance in disease and control samples was created using “ggplot2” package. Furthermore, t test was employed to assess the disparity of immune cells between disease and control groups (p < 0.05). After that, spearman correlation analysis was conducted to explore the relationship between immune cells and biomarkers using “psych” package (version 2.4.3).


### Compounds prediction and molecular docking
  The treatment compounds for sepsis-induced ARDS were predicted by the Drug-Gene Interaction Database (DGIdb, http://dgidb.genome.wustl.edu/) and the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD, https://ctdbase.org/) according to biomarkers. Corresponding top-ranked compounds for each gene were extracted to perform molecular docking. The three-dimensional (3D) structure of compounds was downloaded from PubChem Compound database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) and the 3D structure of biomarkers was obtained from protein data bank (PDB) database (http://www.rcsb.org/pdb/). To further explore regulatory correlation of biomarkers, microRNAs (miRNAs)-targeting biomarkers and long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs)-targeting miRNAs were predicted by starBase database (https://rnasysu.com/encori/). A mRNA-miRNA-lncRNA regulatory network was then constructed relied on predicted miRNAs and lncRNAs.


### ScRNA-seq analysis
  Seurat objects derived from scRNA-seq data were filtered with the “Seurat” package (version 5.1.0) [38] with following criterion: (1) genes covered by fewer than 200 cells, (2) cells with more than 3,000 genes (3) nCount RNA (total RNA counts per cell) ≥ 7,000, (4) cells with more than 25% proportion of mitochondrial genes. Following cell filtering, gene expression counts were normalized using the NormalizeData function in Seurat, scaling counts per cell to 10,000 and log-transforming the results. The vst method was employed to select high-variable genes. Subsequently, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed, and the p value of each principal component (PC) was calculated and compared by JackStrawPlot function. The significance degree of PCs was quantized, those PCs which had significantly statistical discrepancy were selected for subsequent analysis (p < 0.05). Subsequently, unsupervised cluster analysis of cells was performed using the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions of Seurat package. The clustering resolution parameter was empirically optimized by testing a range of values (from 0.1 to 1.0). A resolution of 0.4 was selected as it provided an optimal balance, distinguishing biologically relevant cell populations that aligned with the expression patterns of canonical cell type marker genes, without inducing over-clustering. Cells were then visualized using the t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (tSNE) method. Furthermore, cell clusters were annotated by CellMarker 2.0 (http://117.50.127.228/CellMarker/CellMarker_annotation.jsp), and cell subpopulations were obtained and visualized by tSNE plot. Moreover, the marker genes in different cell subpopulations were identified. The cell communication of cell subpopulations was evaluated by “CellChat” package (version 1.6.1) [39]. After creating the CellChat object, importing object to ligand-receptor database (CellChatDB), and performing preprocessing, cell communication network was inferred. To further search the expression of biomarkers in cell subpopulations, the expression and distribution of biomarkers were analyzed.


### Expression of biomarkers in GSE241186 dataset
  In GSE241186 dataset, differential expression analysis was processed to screened DEGs between ARDS and control samples with the threshold of |log2FC|> 1and adj.p < 0.05. The genes were displayed by volcano map and heat map. The biomarkers and DEGs from the GSE241186 dataset were intersected to obtain key genes, and the expression of key genes was compared between ARDS, control and UC-MSCs groups.


### Quantitative reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis
  MLE-12 and MLE-12 treated with 5 µg/ml LPS were cultured in special culture medium. Total RNA was extracted from these cell samples using RNA extraction kit, in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA was subsequently reverse transcribed into cDNA. qRT-PCR was conducted to assess biomarker’s expression with SYBR PCR master mix (ABclonal-RK21205). Primers sequences used in this work were provided in Table 1. GADPH served as reference gene for all samples. ### Western blotting
  Total protein was extracted from MLE-12 and MLE-12 cells with 5 µg/ml LPS, and its concentrations were measured using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit. Proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane at 200 mA for 1 h on ice. The membranes were then blocked with 5% skim milk in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST) at room temperature for 30 min, and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated with a secondary antibody (1:5000) for 30 min the following day. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) served as loading control [41]. Protein signals were detected, and the optical density was quantified using ImageJ software.


### Statistical analysis
  All statistical analyses were conducted employing R software. The disparities were analyzed via the Wilcoxon test. A statistically significant p value was less than 0.05.

## Discussion
  The sepsis-induced ARDS, a critical condition prevalent among severe patients, results in profound respiratory distress and imposes significant economic burdens on healthcare systems. The incidence of ARDS has been rising annually, and existing treatment modalities, including mechanical ventilation and supportive care, often lack effectiveness and specificity [43]. Mitochondrial dysfunction has been linked to the pathophysiology of both sepsis and ARDS, potentially serving as a key to unlocking new diagnostic and therapeutic strategies [44, 45]. Thus, the aim of our study is to explore mitochondrial-related biomarkers in sepsis-induced ARDS, which may enhance the diagnostic accuracy and improve prognostic assessments in sepsis-induced ARDS.

  Among the identified biomarkers, RGS2 emerges as a particularly significant factor, supported by its recognition as a key mitochondria-related gene [46, 47] and its negative correlation with lung injury severity in sepsis-induced ARDS [48]. A growing body of evidence positions RGS2 at the nexus of mitochondrial and cellular functional regulation. For instance, suppression of RGS2 has been shown to inhibit cell growth by inducing ROS-mediated endoplasmic reticulum stress and autophagy in cancer models [49]. Furthermore, RGS2 overexpression has been demonstrated to alleviate PM2.5-induced apoptosis and pro-inflammatory cytokine expression by attenuating the Gq/11–phospholipase C β (PLCβ)–inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate receptor (IP3R)–calcium signaling axis, while its expression is susceptible to suppression via Toll-like receptor 2/4 (TLR2/4) activation [50]. This positions RGS2 at the nexus of inflammatory signaling, calcium dynamics, and apoptotic regulation. In bronchial epithelial BEAS-2B cells, the synergistic protective effect of Isorhapontigenin (ISOF) and dexamethasone against septic inflammation was associated with elevated RGS2 expression alongside anti-inflammatory and antioxidant responses [51], further supporting its role in maintaining cellular homeostasis under septic stress. Consistent with these findings, RGS2 underexpression has been documented in animal models of renal fibrosis and cardiac hypertrophy, whereas its upregulation conferred protection against pulmonary fibrosis in murine models, underscoring its intrinsic antifibrotic properties [52]. In macrophages, RGS2 mitigates lung injury by constraining Gαq-driven interferon-γ (IFNγ) production, and its genetic ablation impedes injury resolution. In macrophages, RGS2 mitigates lung injury by constraining Gαq-driven IFNγ production, and its genetic ablation impedes injury resolution [53]. Collectively, these findings illuminate the functional involvement of RGS2 in modulating mitochondrial stress, apoptosis, and inflammatory output, reinforcing its diagnostic and therapeutic relevance in sepsis-induced ARDS.

  In parallel, ARID4B, a transcription factor involved in regulating cellular processes such as proliferation and survival [54, 55], was significantly downregulated in our sepsis-induced ARDS models. Although its role in this specific condition is not yet fully understood, studies in other contexts (e.g., cancer) have shown that ARID4B knockdown can decrease proliferation and increase apoptosis, partly through dysregulation of the Phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/AKT (PI3K/AKT) pathway [54, 55]. Notably, the PI3K/AKT pathway is a critical regulator of inflammation, oxidative stress, and cell apoptosis in sepsis-induced lung injury. This is evidenced by studies demonstrating that both activation (e.g., by Rhodiola rosea L. extract) [56] and inhibition (e.g., by Shenfu injection) [20] of this pathway can attenuate ALI/ARDS, highlighting its central and complex role. Therefore, we hypothesize that the downregulation of ARID4B may contribute to alveolar epithelial injury in sepsis-induced ARDS, potentially through mechanisms involving the PI3K/AKT axis. However, direct experimental evidence linking ARID4B to mitochondrial function and inflammatory responses in this specific setting remains limited and warrants future investigation to validate this precise mechanistic role.

  Complementing this network, TGM2 presents a context-dependent role. While existing literature establishes TGM2 as a critical anti-inflammatory regulator in lung neutrophils [57], our finding regarding its discrepant expression between bioinformatic data (upregulated in whole blood from sepsis-induced ARDS patients) and in vitro model (downregulated in LPS-stimulated MLE-12 alveolar epithelial cells) highlights the context-dependent complexity of its role in ARDS. Previous in vivo studies (Tgm2−/− mice) have established TGM2 as a protective factor, where its deficiency exacerbates mortality and inflammation in LPS-induced sepsis by promoting mitochondria-mediated apoptosis [58], while its overexpression mitigates hyperoxia-induced alveolar injury [59]. Thus, TGM2 upregulation in whole blood may represent a systemic protective response in immune cells during sepsis. In contrast, its downregulation in alveolar epithelial cells under LPS stimulation suggests a cell-type-specific regulatory mechanism. This observation aligns with a recent scRNA-seq study [60], where decreased Tgm2 expression was noted in an alveolar type II (AT2) cell subpopulation and linked to Wnt-mediated AT2-to-AT1 differentiation. Together, these results highlight the context-dependent role of TGM2 in lung injury and repair, and future research must employ cell-type-specific models to dissect these distinct roles for precise therapeutic targeting.

  The interconnected regulatory network among ARID4B, RGS2, and TGM2 suggests a collaborative role in influencing ARDS progression. This network may be a key target for emerging therapies, such as those utilizing MSCs. Emerging evidence indicates that MSCs modulate cell death in lung diseases, including ARDS, predominantly through paracrine signaling mechanisms as well as alternative intercellular communication pathways such as gap junctions, tunneling nanotubes, and extracellular vesicles [42]. In this context, our data revealed an intermediate level of TGM2 expression in the UC-MSC treatment group. Given that TGM2 is a key mediator of transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1)-induced lung fibrosis—primarily by driving pro-fibrotic M2 macrophage polarization [61]—we propose that UC-MSCs alleviate ARDS by normalizing this pathological TGM2 overexpression via paracrine signaling, disrupting the pro-fibrotic cascade. This perspective is consistent with the dual role of TGM2 in MSC biology: it is essential for MSC migration and differentiation [62], yet its expression declines during MSC senescence [63]. Thus, the intermediate TGM2 level post-treatment may represent a therapeutic recalibration—inhibiting its detrimental signaling while preserving its reparative functions. Future research should aim to elucidate these relationships further, potentially leading to the identification of new biomarkers and therapeutic targets to improve ARDS management and patient prognosis.

  Immune infiltration and single-cell sequencing analyses further refined our understanding of this biomarker network within the lung microenvironment. Immune infiltration analysis highlighted significant alterations in naïve B cells and CD8+ T cells between disease and control groups, suggesting that these biomarkers may be linked to immune dysregulation in sepsis-induced ARDS. B cells or B lymphocytes (bursa-derived cells) are essential components of adaptive immune response, primarily responsible for humoral immunity in mammals [64]. The B-cell immune system constitutes a complex network of cells, cytokines, chemokines, and antigens that collaborate to neutralize antigens and establish long-term immunity. B cells actively direct immunity against specific proteins by sequestering antigen via the B cell receptor [65]. Recent studies have discovered that amidst chronically elevated baseline inflammation, there is a reduction in B-cell efficacy [66]. The refined classification by scRNA sequencing analysis confirmed and expanded upon our bulk infiltration findings. For instance, we could now localize Arid4b expression predominantly within the B cell compartment, providing a cellular basis for the observed bulk-level alterations in B cells. Given that ARID4B does not appear to function as a key node in the major intercellular signaling networks, and considering the current lack of direct literature evidence linking it to B-cell communication, we propose that its role is independent of direct cell–cell communication. This model suggests ARID4B operates within B cells rather than mediating their cross-talk with other populations, a premise that warrants future validation via more sensitive techniques such as single-cell RT-qPCR. Second, the strong positive correlation between RGS2 expression and neutrophil infiltration in bulk data, coupled with its highest expression in myofibroblasts at the single-cell level, paints a novel role for RGS2 in stromal-immune crosstalk. Functional studies establish RGS2 as a critical intrinsic anti-fibrotic regulator in myofibroblasts. For instance, the anti-fibrotic effect of pirfenidone is mediated through the rapid upregulation of RGS2, which inhibits pro-fibrotic responses, while RGS2 deficiency exacerbates bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis [67]. Furthermore, combination therapy with mesenchymal stem cells and pirfenidone enhances RGS2 expression in myofibroblasts and significantly reduces intracellular Ca2+ [52]. The critical role of Ca2+ flux as a pro-inflammatory signal is well-established, as evidenced by the fact that Prostaglandin D2 (PGD2) receptor-induced Ca2+ flux in macrophages directly enhances neutrophil migration and survival [68], suggesting the hypothesis that RGS2 in myofibroblasts may limit neutrophil recruitment, which is further contextualized by the documented "neutrophil-myofibroblast" vicious cycle in lung injury, where neutrophil infiltration (e.g., upon Isthmin 1 (ISM1) deficiency) promotes myofibroblast activation and fibrosis [69], and neutrophil-derived mediators, such as Gasdermin D (GSDMD)-dependent NETs, can directly drive macrophage-to-myofibroblast transition [70]. Therefore, by serving as a brake on myofibroblast activation, RGS2 likely disrupts this cycle at the stromal level, ultimately limiting neutrophil recruitment. This mechanism is consistent with prior reports that RGS2 upregulation protects against neutrophilic inflammation in murine airway models [71, 72] and aligns with its role as an immunological checkpoint in alveolar macrophages during lung injury [53, 73]), now highlighting its function within the stromal compartment, and presenting a compelling hypothesis for future experimental validation (e.g. spatial analysis, genetic causality testing). Crucially, for TGM2, which showed discrepant expression between bulk bioinformatics and our cellular validation model, single-cell resolution unveiled a cell-type-specific explanation. In disease samples, TGM2 was significantly upregulated in DCs and endothelial cells but downregulated in lipofibroblasts and fibroblasts. The opposing expression trends in different cell types necessitates future functional experiments to dissect its precise functions in different cellular niches of the sepsis-induced ARDS microenvironment.

  Based on these biomarkers, a nomogram for risk prediction of sepsis-induced ARDS was constructed, which displayed an excellent predictive performance. This approach enhances the ability to make informed clinical decisions, potentially leading to better patient management and outcomes. Yuan et al. also reveal the construction of nomogram with key genes, including ARID4B had an excellent risk prediction for fibrosis [74]. Further studies and validation in broader clinical settings are recommended to confirm these findings and optimize the use of these biomarkers in routine clinical practice.

  This study's integration of bioinformatics and molecular biology provides a robust framework for understanding and potentially mitigating sepsis-induced ARDS. It highlights the importance of a multi-disciplinary approach in tackling complex diseases like ARDS, where the interplay between various cellular processes and systemic responses dictates clinical outcomes. However, this study presents several limitations that must be acknowledged.

  First, the use of human whole-blood transcriptomics for biomarker discovery, validated in mouse MLE-12 alveolar epithelial cells and annotated with mouse lung single-cell data, introduces discrepancies due to species, tissue, and sample complexity. While this approach is pragmatically justified by data availability, it may affect the direct translatability of findings. Future studies should utilize species- and tissue-matched samples (e.g., bronchoalveolar lavage fluid or lung biopsies from ARDS patients) for definitive validation). Besides, although whole-blood transcriptomics offers practical and ethical advantages, it remains an indirect approach for studying this lung-centric pathology. Future work incorporating direct lung tissue sampling or leveraging advanced spatial transcriptomics in relevant models will be valuable for validating and extending our findings.

  Second, the study identifies correlations but does not establish causality or molecular mechanisms. Key experimental validations are lacking, including: Measurements of mitochondrial function (e.g., ATP production, ROS levels, membrane potential); Gain- or loss-of-function experiments to assess the impact of ARID4B, RGS2, or TGM2 on cellular outcomes under septic stress; Co-localization (e.g., immunofluorescence) and co-culture experiments to explore the impact of biomarker expression changes on immune cell function (e.g., proliferation, cytokine secretion), particularly between RGS2-expressing cells and neutrophils; Single-cell RT-qPCR to validate low-expression genes like ARID4B in specific cell subtypes. Future research employing cell-type-specific knockout or overexpression models, along with LPS stimulation at varying concentrations and durations, will be essential for dissecting the distinct functions of these biomarkers across different cell types.

  Third, although molecular docking predicted potential therapeutic compounds (e.g., progesterone, valproic acid), their effects on biomarker expression and functional outcomes remain unverified. Future work should test these compounds in LPS-induced models to evaluate their modulation of ARID4B, RGS2, and TGM2, while simultaneously examining their effects on cell viability, inflammatory cytokine production, and other relevant functional indicators to provide experimental basis for clinical translation.

  Finally, the nomogram model was developed using a retrospective dataset with limited demographic and comorbidity information, and the relatively small sample size may affect the generalizability of our findings. We are currently establishing collaborations with multiple medical centers to collect an independent, multi-center validation cohort. Future studies should expand cohort sizes and incorporate prospective multi-center collaborations to strengthen the clinical relevance of these biomarkers for ARDS diagnosis and management, while also validating model performance across different demographic and clinical subgroups.


## Conclusion
  In conclusion, this study highlights the potential roles of ARID4B, RGS2, and TGM2 as biomarkers in sepsis-induced ARDS, clarifying their involvement in disease mechanisms and offering new avenues for early diagnosis and targeted therapies. The insights gained from this research provide a foundation for future investigations aimed at validating these findings and exploring their implications for personalized medicine in ARDS management. By expanding our understanding of these molecular players, we can enhance patient outcomes and contribute to more effective therapeutic strategies in this critical clinical area.
