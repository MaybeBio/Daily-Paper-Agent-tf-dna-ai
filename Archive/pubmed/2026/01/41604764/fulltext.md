# Pharmacological Inhibition of APT2 by Solasonine Promotes Ferroptosis Through Palmitoylation Modulation in Gallbladder Cancer


## ABSTRACT
  Gallbladder cancer (GBC), the most prevalent and lethal biliary tract malignancy, lacks effective therapeutic strategies. Solasonine (SS), a bioactive steroidal alkaloid derived from Solanum nigrum L, exhibits antitumor activity in multiple cancers, but its role in GBC and association with ferroptosis remains unknown. The purpose of this study is to investigate the therapeutic effects of SS on GBC and its underlying mechanisms using both in vitro and in vivo models. The efficacy was tested in subcutaneous models. Then, the anti‐GBC effects of SS were assessed via CCK‐8, colony formation, and EdU assays. Biosafety was evaluated using L‐2F7 cells and murine serum biomarkers. RNA sequencing identified key pathways, validated by ChIP‐qPCR and dual‐luciferase reporter assays. Ferroptosis induction was confirmed by measuring MDA, GSH, ROS, Fe2+, qRT‐PCR/Western blot (GPX4/SLC7A11), and rescue experiments. Direct binding of SS to APT2 was demonstrated through molecular docking, CETSA, and SPR. The study employed orthotopic xenograft models, transwell assays, and wound healing assays to investigate the effects of SS on the metastasis of GBC. SS triggered ferroptosis in GBC by elevating MDA, ROS, and Fe2+ while depleting GSH and downregulating GPX4/SLC7A11. Mechanistically, SS directly bound APT2, inhibiting its enzymatic activity and blocking STAT3 depalmitoylation. This prevented p‐STAT3 nuclear translocation, suppressing transcription of ferroptosis inhibitors GPX4 and SLC7A11. In vivo, SS suppressed tumor growth by subcutaneous and orthotopic models with no systemic toxicity. Our findings reveal SS as a novel APT2‐targeted ferroptosis inducer that inhibits GBC progression via inhibiting the depalmitoylation process of STAT3, providing a safe and effective therapeutic candidate for this intractable malignancy.


## Introduction
  GBC is the most common malignancy of the biliary tract and ranks sixth among gastrointestinal malignancies (Sung et al. 2021; Vuthaluru et al. 2023). Due to its asymptomatic nature in the early stages (Roa et al. 2022), most patients have progressed to the middle and late stages by the time of diagnosis, resulting in a high mortality rate (Feo et al. 2022). GBC has a strong tendency to invade and metastasize through various routes (He et al. 2024), especially hepatic metastasis. Current clinical management relies on gemcitabine/cisplatin chemotherapy and selective resection, yet metastatic disease portends a dire prognosis with < 5% 5‐year survival (Ostwal et al. 2024). Critical challenges persist, including anatomical proximity facilitating undetectable micrometastases and the absence of targeted therapies against metastatic drivers, underscoring the need for new therapeutic strategies to improve the survival rate of GBC patients.

  Ferroptosis, an iron‐dependent cell death driven by lipid peroxidation, is mechanistically distinct from apoptosis and autophagy (Dixon et al. 2012; Henning et al. 2022). Its execution hinges on redox imbalance—notably through dysregulation of the GPX4 antioxidant system and cystine/glutamate antiporter SLC7A11, which maintain glutathione (GSH) pools to neutralize lethal lipid peroxides (Koppula et al. 2021; Li et al. 2020; Rademaker et al. 2022). Emerging evidence highlights transcriptional regulators that modulate this axis; for instance, STAT3 can upregulate GPX4 and SLC7A11 to sustain redox homeostasis, thereby promoting ferroptosis resistance (Li, Jin, et al. 2022; Ouyang et al. 2022). Conversely, suppressing such regulators destabilizes antioxidant defenses, shifting the balance toward lipid peroxidation (Ouyang et al. 2022; Zhang, Gong, et al. 2022; Zhang, Tang, et al. 2022). However, the regulatory networks governing ferroptosis in GBC—particularly, those amenable to therapeutic targeting—remain poorly defined. This gap highlights the untapped potential of natural compounds to modulate ferroptotic pathways as a novel treatment strategy for GBC.

  S‐palmitoylation—a dynamic lipid modification regulating protein membrane anchoring and signaling—serves as a metabolic checkpoint in cancer (Ko and Dixon 2018; Martin et al. 2011). This reversible modification, mediated by ZDHHC palmitoyltransferases and reversed by depalmitoylases, governs membrane anchoring of key effectors such as EGFR and RAS, thereby modulating proliferation and therapeutic resistance (Liu et al. 2022; Won et al. 2018). Notably, palmitoylation‐depalmitoylation cycles can be hijacked in cancer—for instance, SNAIL promotes tumor progression by suppressing ZDHHCs and upregulating APT2, leading to mislocalization of tumor suppressors like SCRIB (Hernandez et al. 2017). Recent studies further demonstrate that such cycles fine‐tune transcription factor dynamics (Zhang et al. 2020), highlighting palmitoylation's role in spatiotemporal control over signaling hubs. Despite these advances, the interplay between palmitoylation and ferroptosis—particularly in GBC—remains unaddressed. This gap is striking, as redox‐sensitive proteins critical to ferroptosis are plausible palmitoylation substrates, positioning this modification as a druggable axis for natural product‐based interventions.

  SS, phytochemicals from  Solanum nigrum  L., have emerged as potent anti‐cancer agents with dual pro‐apoptotic and pro‐ferroptotic effects (Li, Chen, et al. 2022; Liang et al. 2022). SS exhibits broad‐spectrum activity across malignancies—suppressing proliferation, blocking EMT‐driven metastasis, and triggering redox imbalance through GSH depletion and lipid peroxidation (Jin et al. 2020; Wang, Zhou, et al. 2023; Zeng et al. 2022). While these findings emphasize SS's capacity to target ferroptosis pathways, its potential to modulate post‐translational modifications, such as protein palmitoylation, remains unexplored. This gap is critical in GBC, where palmitoylation cycles govern oncogenic signaling. Given SS's ability to disrupt redox‐sensitive nodes upstream of ferroptosis, we hypothesize that its anti‐GBC effects may involve palmitoylation‐mediated regulation of STAT3 or related ferroptosis executors—a paradigm‐shifting mechanism bridging natural products and dynamic protein modifications.

  Here, we demonstrate that SS triggers ferroptosis in GBC by disrupting STAT3 palmitoylation, a mechanism mediated through APT2‐dependent depalmitoylation. The aim of the paper is to expand the therapeutic repertoire for GBC and pioneer a natural product‐driven strategy targeting palmitoylation‐modulated ferroptosis in gallbladder cancer.


## Materials and Methods

### Cell Lines and Culture Conditions
  HEK293T cells and human gallbladder cancer cell lines (NOZ, GBC‐SD, and ZJU‐0430) were obtained from the Cell Bank of Shanghai Institute for Biological Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Human primary gallbladder epithelial cells (L‐2F7) were kindly provided by Shanghai Biliary Diseases Research Center. All cells were maintained in DMEM (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Vazyme, Shanghai, China), 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.


### Reagents Preparation and Treatment
  SS (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) was dissolved in DMSO as a 50 mM stock solution and stored at −20°C. Working concentrations were freshly diluted in complete culture medium prior to experiments, with final DMSO concentration ≤ 0.1% to avoid cytotoxicity. Vehicle control groups received equivalent volumes of DMSO. All drug treatments, including IC50 assessments and mechanistic experiments, maintained identical 48‐h durations with media/drug refreshment at 24 h to ensure exposure stability. Requirements considered to be relevant in recent guidelines for best practice in natural products pharmacological research have been taken into account (Heinrich et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2024).


### Animal Models
  In this study, all BALB/c nude mice were purchased from Shanghai Jihui Laboratory Animal Care Co. Ltd. All research on animals was conducted in accordance with the “Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals” of the National Institutes of Health (NIH). The Animal Care and Use Committee of Xinhua Hospital of Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine approved all experimental procedures and protocols.

  For subcutaneous xenograft model, 4‐week‐old female BALB/c nude mice were randomized into three groups for subcutaneous tumor development. NOZ cells (2 × 106 cells) were injected into the right flank. Beginning 5 days postinoculation, groups received daily intraperitoneal injections of: (1) vehicle control (1% DMSO, 40% PEG300, 5% Tween‐80, 54% saline), (2) SS‐L (10 mg/kg), or (3) SS‐H (20 mg/kg) in 400 μL solution. Tumor dimensions were measured weekly using calipers, with volume calculated as (width2 × length)/2. Tumors and tissues were collected after 4 weeks after injecting cells for analysis.

  About orthotopic xenograft models (Wu et al. 2024), 6‐week‐old female BALB/c nude mice underwent surgical implantation of NOZ cells (4 × 107 cells/mL in matrigel). Following 4‐h fasting, mice were anesthetized with 0.3% pentobarbital (50–60 mg/kg, i.p.). After midline abdominal incision (1.0 cm), 25 μL tumor cell suspension was injected into the gallbladder using an insulin needle following gentle bile evacuation. The matrix was allowed to solidify for 30 s before organ repositioning and abdominal closure with 6–0 silk sutures. Beginning 7 days postprocedure, groups received daily intraperitoneal injections of: (1) vehicle control (1% DMSO, 40% PEG300, 5% Tween‐80, 54% saline), (2) Fer‐1 (2 mg/kg), (3) SS‐H, or (4) Fer‐1 + SS‐H in 400 μL solution. Mice were euthanized 4 weeks. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine Animal Ethics Committee guidelines.


### RNA Isolation and qRT‐PCR Analysis
  Total RNA was extracted from 1 × 106 cells using trizol (Invitrogen) following manufacturer protocols. First‐strand cDNA synthesis was performed using 1 μg total RNA with HiScript III All‐in‐One RT SuperMix (Vazyme). Quantitative PCR reactions contained 20 ng cDNA template and 200 nM gene‐specific primers (Table S2) in ChamQ SYBR Master Mix (Vazyme). GAPDH served as an endogenous control, with relative mRNA levels calculated via 2−ΔΔCt method.


### Cell Transfection
  Gene‐specific STAT3 siRNA (sequences provided in Table S2) and non‐targeting control siRNA were synthesized by Genomeditech (China). Transfection was performed using RFect reagent (Baidai, China) according to manufacturer protocols.

  For STAT3 overexpression, the full‐length STAT3 cDNA was cloned into the pCMVmKatePuro05 vector, with empty pCMVmKatePuro05 vector serving as control. Similarly, LYPLA2 (APT2) cDNA was subcloned into the pCMVGFPPuro04 vector to generate the LYPLA2 overexpression construct. All plasmid transfections were conducted using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) following established protocols.


### Cellular Proliferation Assays
  For CCK‐8 Assay, cells were seeded in 96‐well plates at uniform density (5 × 103 cells/well). Following treatment, 10 μL CCK‐8 reagent (Yeasen) was added per well and incubated at 37°C for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. About EdU Staining (Beyotime), cells in six‐well plates were labeled with EdU for 2 h, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), and processed using the Click‐iT reaction system. Proliferating cells were quantified via fluorescence microscopy. For colony formation, cells (8 × 102/well) were cultured in six‐well plates for 14 days. Colonies were fixed with 4% PFA and stained with 0.4% crystal violet for visualization.


### Migration and Invasion Assays
  Modified transwell chambers (8 μm pores) were used in the following steps. For migration, 2 × 104 cells in serum‐free medium were seeded in upper chambers. About invasion, matrigel‐coated (1:8 dilution) membranes were prehydrated for 1 h. Both assays used complete medium as a chemoattractant in lower chambers. After 24 h incubation, non‐migrated cells were removed by cotton swab. Transmembranous cells were fixed, stained with 0.4% crystal violet, and counted in five random microscopic fields per chamber.


### Transcriptome Sequencing and KEGG Enrichment Analysis
  Total RNA was isolated from SS or DMSO treated NOZ cells using trizol reagent. RNA integrity was verified via Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (RIN ≥ 8.0). Libraries were prepared with the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA‐seq Library Prep Kit (Vazyme) following ribosomal RNA depletion (Ribo‐Zero Gold, Illumina). Sequencing was performed by APTBIO (Shanghai) Co. Ltd. on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system with 150‐bp paired‐end reads. The threshold of differential gene screening was set as |Log2FC| ≥ 1. After obtaining differential genes, ID conversion was performed, and KEGG enrichment analysis was performed using clusterProfiler package. The R version was 4.2.1.


### Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP)
  STAT3‐DNA interactions at GPX4 and SLC7A11 promoters were analyzed using the Beyotime ChIP Kit. Briefly, NOZ cells (1 × 107/sample) were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at 37°C. Chromatin was sonicated to 200–500 bp fragments (3 × 30 s cycles). Immunoprecipitation used 5 μg STAT3 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, ab68153) or IgG control (Table S1). Purified DNA was analyzed by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis and qPCR with primers flanking putative STAT3 binding sites.


### Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay
  GPX4 and SLC7A11 promoter regions were cloned into pGL3‐Basic (Promega): Wild‐type (WT): Containing predicted STAT3 response elements. Mutant: Site‐directed mutagenesis of core STAT3 binding motifs. Luciferase activity was measured 24 h post‐transfection using the Dual Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Kit (Yeasen) according to manufacturer protocol. Relative activity was calculated as Firefly/Renilla luminescence ratio, normalized to empty vector controls.


### Western Blot Analysis
  Confluent cells were washed twice with ice‐cold PBS and lysed using RIPA buffer supplemented with 1× protease/phosphatase inhibitors. Following 30 min ice incubation, lysates were sonicated (3 × 5 s pulses, 20% amplitude) and centrifuged at 12,000g for 15 min at 4°C. Supernatants were quantified via BCA assay. Protein samples were denatured in 5× loading buffer at 95°C for 5 min, then resolved on SDS‐PAGE gels at 100 V for 120 min. Proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes using semi‐dry transfer. Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk/TBST for 1 h at RT, then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies (Table S1). After three TBST washes, membranes were incubated with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at RT. Band intensities were quantified using Image Lab 6.1 software, normalized to GAPDH loading control.


### ROS Detection
  Intracellular ROS levels were quantified using the DCFH‐DA probe (Beyotime). Cells were incubated with 10 μM DCFH‐DA in serum‐free medium at 37°C for 30 min (protected from light). After PBS washing, fluorescence intensity was measured via flow cytometry using 488 nm excitation/525 nm emission. Data were analyzed using FlowJo v10.8.1.


### Lipid Peroxidation Assay
  MDA content was determined using the Lipid Peroxidation Assay Kit (Beyotime). Cell lysates were centrifuged at 12,000g for 10 min. Supernatants reacted with working solution at 100°C for 15 min. The absorbance was measured at 532 nm using a microplate reader, and the concentration of MDA was calculated against a standard curve.


### FerroOrange Staining and GSH Assay
  For FerroOrange Staining, live cells were incubated with 1 μM FerroOrange (Dojindo) in DMEM for 1 h at 37°C. Intracellular Fe2+ was visualized using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope (E x/E m: 543/580 nm) with consistent laser power across samples. About GSH measurement, total GSH was quantified using the GSH Assay Kit (Solarbio). Deproteinized lysates were reacted with 5,5′‐dithiobis‐(2‐nitrobenzoic acid) for 5 min. Absorbance at 412 nm was measured, with GSH levels normalized to total protein concentration.


### Immunofluorescence Staining
  Cells were grown on six‐well plates to 70%–80% confluency. After PBS washing, cells were fixed with 4% PFA in PBS (pH 7.4) for 15 min at RT. Permeabilization was performed using 0.1% Triton X‐100/PBS for 10 min, followed by blocking with 5% BSA/0.1% Tween‐20/PBS for 1 h at RT. Primary antibodies (Table S1) diluted in blocking buffer were applied overnight at 4°C. After three PBS‐T washes, cells were incubated with Immunofluorescence Staining Kit (Beyotime) for 1 h at RT protected from light. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min. Imaging was performed on a fluorescence microscope.


### Co‐Immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP)
  Transfected cells expressing Flag or HA‐tagged proteins were lysed in ice‐cold RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail. Lysates were incubated on ice for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 18,000g for 15 min at 4°C. Supernatants were pre‐cleared with protein A/G magnetic beads (Beyotime) for 1 h to reduce nonspecific binding. Protein concentrations were quantified using the BCA assay, and 500 μg of total protein was incubated with 20 μL anti‐Flag M2 magnetic beads (Beyotime) or anti‐HA magnetic beads (Beyotime) overnight at 4°C with continuous rotation. After incubation, the beads were collected on a magnetic rack and washed three times with TBS‐T buffer to remove unbound proteins. Bound complexes were eluted by boiling the beads in 2× loading buffer at 95°C for 5 min. Immunoprecipitated samples were resolved on 10% SDS‐PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes for immunoblotting.


### ELISA Assay
  Cell supernatants were centrifuged (1000g, 15 min, 4°C), diluted 1:9 in assay buffer. Lyophilized standards (0–50 ng/mL) were reconstituted in 500 μL water, vortexed, aliquoted, and stored at −80°C. Anti‐LYPLA2‐precoated plates were equilibrated (30 min, RT); samples/standards (100 μL/well, duplicates) and PBS (blanks) were added. After adding 10 μL assay buffer and 50 μL LYPLA2‐HRP conjugate (37°C, 1 h), plates were washed 5× with PBS/0.05% Tween‐20. Substrates A (H2O2) and B (TMB) were added (37°C, 10–15 min), reactions stopped with 1 M H3PO4, and absorbance read at 450 nm (630 nm reference). All steps were performed according to the instructions of the ELISA kit (MyBioSource).


### Acylebiotin Exchange (ABE) Assay
  ABE assays were conducted following established protocols (Hurst et al. 2017). Samples were lysed in ice‐cold lysis buffer (100 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.2, 5 mM EDTA, 150 mM NaCl, 2.5% SDS, inhibitor cocktail) containing 25 mM N‐ethylmaleimide (NEM, Macklin) to block free thiol groups. After 2 h of rotation at RT, residual NEM was removed by three sequential chloroform–methanol–water (1:4:3) precipitation steps. The precipitated proteins were redissolved in 1 mL of lysis buffer with 5 mM biotin‐HPDP (Macklin) and incubated at 25°C for 1 h. Samples were then equally divided into two parts and incubated with 0.5 mL of 1 M hydroxylamine or negative control (1 M NaCl) respectively at RT for 3 h. Samples were precipitated again and dissolved in 200 μL of resuspension buffer (100 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.2, 2% SDS, 8 M urea, 5 mM EDTA). Biotinylated proteins were captured using streptavidin magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a ratio of 4 μg beads per μg protein overnight at 4°C. Beads were sequentially washed with PBS (1% SDS). Bound proteins were eluted in loading buffer at 95°C for 10 min. Sample proteins were analyzed by Western blot.


### Cellular Thermal Shift Assay (CETSA)
  Cells were harvested by trypsinization and resuspended in PBS supplemented with 1× protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Selleck). Aliquots (1 × 106 cells/tube) were subjected to a temperature gradient (37°C, 41°C, 45°C, 49°C, 53°C, 57°C) using a thermal cycler (Bio‐Rad, T100), with precise 3‐min heating at each temperature. Immediately after heating, samples underwent two freeze–thaw cycles: rapid freezing in liquid nitrogen followed by thawing at room temperature. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 12,000g for 20 min at 4°C. Supernatants were mixed with 5× loading buffer, denatured at 95°C for 5 min, and resolved on 10% SDS‐PAGE gels.


### Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR) Measurements
  Binding between SS and APT2 was analyzed using Biacore T200. APT2 was immobilized on CM5 chips (EDC/NHS activation, 9000 RU). SS was injected (30 μL/min, 60 s association/180 s dissociation) with reference subtraction. Data were fitted to a 1:1 Langmuir model after regeneration (10 mM glycine‐HCl, pH 2.0). Specificity was confirmed using BSA controls.


### Molecular Docking
  The APT2 crystal structure (PDB:5SYN) was preprocessed (removed water/ligands) and prepared in AutoDockTools (hydrogens added, charges assigned). Binding pockets were predicted using POCASA. Docking with AutoDock Vina utilized a 60Å3 grid, exhaustiveness = 10, and 20 independent runs per ligand. Small molecules (PubChem SDF) were docked, retaining lowest‐energy conformations. Results were visualized in PyMOL.


### Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and Hematoxylin–Eosin (H&E) Staining
  Tumor tissues were fixed in 4% PFA (24 h, 4°C), dehydrated, paraffin‐embedded, and sectioned (8 μm). After deparaffinization and rehydration, endogenous peroxidase was blocked with 3% H2O2/methanol. Antigen retrieval used microwave heating in sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Sections were blocked with 5% goat serum, incubated with primary antibodies (4°C, overnight), followed by HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies (1 h, RT). DAB staining was performed with hematoxylin counterstaining. For H&E, sections were stained with hematoxylin/eosin, dehydrated, and mounted. Imaging (Leica scanner) and quantification (ImageJ) were performed.


### Statistical Analysis
  p Values were calculated using GraphPad Prism 10 software. Student's t‐test was used for statistical analysis between the two groups involved in the study. One‐way ANOVA was used for multiple group comparisons. Data represent at least three independent biological replicates unless otherwise specified. p Values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant (*p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001).


## Results

### SS Inhibits GBC Growth In Vivo and In Vitro
  Subcutaneous xenografts were established by injecting NOZ cells into the right flank of nude mice (Figure 1A). SS administration significantly reduced tumor volume in a dose‐dependent manner (Figure 1B). Consistent with gross morphological observations, excised tumors from SS‐treated groups exhibited markedly decreased weights compared to the vehicle group, which was used to be the negative control (Figure 1C). IHC analysis demonstrated a significant reduction in Ki67‐positive cells within SS‐treated xenograft tissues, indicating suppressed tumor proliferative activity (Figure 1D, S1F). No significant body weight differences were detected between high‐dose SS‐treated mice and vehicle controls during the treatment course (Figure S1A), indicating minimal systemic toxicity. Serum biochemistry analysis and histopathological evaluation of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) revealed the favorable biosafety profile of SS in vivo (Figure S1B–E).

  IC50 assays revealed higher sensitivity of NOZ and GBC‐SD cells to SS compared to ZJU‐0430 (Figure S1G–I). Subsequent cytotoxicity assays demonstrated dose‐dependent suppression of cell viability in SS‐treated groups versus controls (Figure S1J). EdU staining, colony formation assays, and CCK‐8 assays collectively confirmed sustained growth inhibition (Figures 1E,F, and S1K), consistent with corresponding in vivo observations.


### SS Triggers Ferroptosis via GPX4 and SLC7A11 Suppression in GBC
  Transcriptome sequencing of SS‐treated NOZ cells revealed significant enrichment in ferroptosis‐related pathways and JAK–STAT signaling, with GSH metabolism and oxidative phosphorylation also implicated (Figure 2A). GO analysis showed reduced GSH peroxidase activity and antioxidant capacity (Figure 2B).

  SS treatment induced significant morphological changes and reduced cell viability in GBC cultures, suggesting activation of cell death pathways (Figure S2A). To delineate the specific death mechanism, pharmacological inhibitors targeting apoptosis, necroptosis, and ferroptosis were applied. Notably, only Fer‐1 effectively reversed SS‐induced cytotoxicity (Figure S2B). Biochemical analysis demonstrated that SS elevated MDA levels while depleting GSH, both of which were rescued by Fer‐1 co‐treatment (Figures S2C,D and 2C). Intracellular Fe2+ and ROS accumulated in SS‐treated cells (Figures 2D,E and S2E). Transmission electron microscopy revealed ferroptosis‐characteristic mitochondrial alterations, including membrane condensation, cristae loss, and organelle shrinkage, which were mitigated by Fer‐1 (Figure 2F). Immunoblotting confirmed dose‐dependent downregulation of GPX4 and SLC7A11, with Fer‐1 pretreatment restoring their expression (Figures S2F,G and 2G). Functional validation through colony formation assays showed that Fer‐1 attenuated SS‐induced growth suppression (Figure S2H). The IHC results of the subcutaneous tumor tissues in nude mice indicated that the protein expression levels of GPX4 and SLC7A11 both decreased after SS treatment, which was consistent with the results of the cell experiments (Figures 1G and S1L). Collectively, these data establish ferroptosis as the dominant mechanism through which SS exerts its antitumor effects in GBC.


### Transcriptional Repression of GPX4/SLC7A11 by SS via STAT3 Signaling Attenuation
  Considering the role of the STAT3‐mediated signaling in ferroptosis (Xie et al. 2025), we modulated STAT3 expression using siRNA knockdown (siSTAT3) and overexpression (OE‐STAT3). siSTAT3 reduced both mRNA and protein levels of GPX4 and SLC7A11, while OE‐STAT3 upregulated their expression, suggesting STAT3 transcriptionally activates these genes (Figures 3A–D and S3A,B). Using the JASPAR database, we identified one STAT3‐binding motif in the GPX4 promoter region (R) and two motifs in the SLC7A11 promoter region (R1 and R2). ChIP‐DNA and ChIP‐PCR assays confirmed STAT3 binding to these promoter regions in GBC cells, and SS treatment inhibited this binding, blocking STAT3‐mediated transcriptional activation (Figure 3E–H). Luciferase reporter assays further validated these findings: STAT3 overexpression significantly increased luciferase activity driven by WT GPX4 and SLC7A11 promoters, whereas SS treatment suppressed this activity. Mutant (MUT) promoter constructs abolished these effects, confirming the specificity of STAT3 binding (Figure 3I).

  These results demonstrate that STAT3 transcriptionally activates GPX4 and SLC7A11 by binding to their promoter regions, and SS disrupts this process. Collectively, our findings establish STAT3 as a critical mediator of SS‐induced ferroptosis in GBC cells.


### SS Blocks Nuclear Translocation of p‐STAT3
  Subsequently, we investigated the effects of SS on STAT3. Notably, SS did not alter total STAT3 or p‐STAT3 levels in whole‐cell lysates (Figure 4A). Nuclear‐cytoplasmic fractionation demonstrated SS‐mediated nuclear p‐STAT3 depletion with concurrent cytoplasmic accumulation (Figure 4B,C). Immunofluorescence analysis corroborated these findings, showing predominant cytoplasmic retention of p‐STAT3 in SS‐treated cells (Figure 4D). This nuclear exclusion mechanism explains SS‐induced GPX4/SLC7A11 downregulation and subsequent ferroptosis execution. Thus, SS disrupts STAT3‐mediated transcriptional homeostasis to drive ferroptotic vulnerability in GBC (Figure 4E).


### SS Targets APT2 to Modulates STAT3 Palmitoylation
  Palmitoylation, a reversible post‐translational modification regulating protein activity and membrane localization, is implicated in STAT3 activation and tumor progression (Jiang et al. 2023). The GO functional enrichment analysis also showed that after SS treatment of GBC cells, posttranslational modifications were enriched. To verify whether SS affects the palmitoylation modification of STAT3, we performed an ABE assay to detect the palmitoylation levels of STAT3 in GBC cells treated with SS. In this study, SS treatment significantly increased STAT3 palmitoylation levels (Figures 5A and S4A). As APT2 is the depalmitoylase for STAT3, we assessed its role in this regulation. While SS did not alter APT2 expression (Figures 5B and S4B), its enzymatic activity decreased (Figure 5C). Co‐IP demonstrated a constitutive interaction between APT2 and STAT3, which was disrupted by SS treatment, mechanistically supporting APT2‐mediated depalmitoylation of STAT3, with SS impairing their enzyme‐substrate complex formation (Figure 5D,E). Mutation of STAT3 palmitoylation site C108 abolished both basal and SS‐induced palmitoylation and suppressed STAT3 phosphorylation (Figure S4C,D), establishing palmitoylation as essential for STAT3 activation.

  CETSA demonstrated SS binding to APT2, increasing its thermal stability. The melting temperature (T m) of APT2 rose from 49.95°C to 51.47°C (GBC‐SD) and 47.68°C to 50.86°C (NOZ) with SS treatment (Figure 5F,G). The results of the molecular docking experiments also indicated that APT2 binds to STAT3 (Figure 5H,I). SPR quantified this interaction, revealing high‐affinity binding (KD = 2.982 μM; Figure 5J).

  These data mechanistically link SS to APT2 inhibition, which stabilizes STAT3 palmitoylation, disrupts its nuclear translocation, and ultimately triggers ferroptosis.


### Identification of Asp13 and Leu11 as Key Residues Governing SS‐APT2 Interaction and Functional Efficacy
  According to the results of the molecular docking, SS binds to APT2 via hydrogen bonding between its phenolic hydroxyl group and Asp13, complemented by hydrophobic interactions between the steroidal core and Leu11 (binding free energy ΔG = −8.7 kJ/mol; Figure 6A). Then, we attempted to confirm whether the two amino acid residues were essential for the interaction between APT2 and SS through the use of CETSA. Two APT2 mutants, APT2‐D13A and APT2‐L11A, were created. Then we incubated SS with cells that contained exogenously‐introduced vectors in which APT2‐D13A or APT2‐L11A was expressed. CETSA confirmed the functional necessity of these residues: in contrast to the APT2 WT, SS failed to stabilize the two mutants (Figure 6B,C). SPR assays with recombinant APT2‐D13A/L11A mutant (APT22A) showed complete loss of SS binding (Figure 6D).

  ABE assays demonstrated that STAT3 palmitoylation remained unaltered in APT22A mutant cells, even with SS treatment (Figures 6E and S5A). Nuclear‐cytoplasmic fractionation revealed SS failed to block p‐STAT3 nuclear translocation in APT22A mutants, contrasting with WT controls (Figures 6F and S5B). As visualized by FerroOrange fluorescence (Figure 6G) and quantified in Figure S5C, the accumulation of Fe2+ in GBC cells induced by SS was abrogated in APT22A mutants, demonstrating that SS fails to induce intracellular Fe2+ accumulation in these cells. Functional validation in GBC‐SD cells showed that SS‐induced ferroptotic hallmarks—MDA elevation and GSH depletion—were abolished in APT22A mutants (Figure 6H).

  These structural and functional analyses conclusively identify Asp13 and Leu11 as essential for SS‐APT2 engagement, through which SS stabilizes STAT3 palmitoylation, disrupts its nuclear trafficking, and executes ferroptosis.


### SS Limits Hepatic Metastasis in GBC‐Bearing Mice With Concordant Attenuation of EMT and Migratory Phenotypes In Vitro
  In vivo orthotopic gallbladder cancer models revealed SS's potent antimetastatic activity. Bioluminescent imaging demonstrated SS treatment reduced hepatic metastatic signal intensity compared to controls. Co‐administration of the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer‐1 partially reversed this effect, while Fer‐1 alone showed no antimetastatic activity (Figure 7A,B). Histopathological analysis of liver tissues confirmed SS treatment decreased metastatic foci count, with Fer‐1 co‐treatment restoring metastatic burden to control levels (Figure 7C). Transwell assays revealed SS dose‐dependently inhibited GBC cell migration and invasion (Figure 7D,E). Wound healing assays showed similar dynamics, with SS‐treated cells exhibiting slower gap closure (Figure 7F,G). Mechanistically, SS upregulated E‐cadherin while downregulating N‐cadherin and Vimentin, indicating suppression of epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT) (Figure S5D,E). These findings identify the anti‐metastatic activity of SS as an additional therapeutic dimension beyond its established anti‐proliferative effects, warranting further mechanistic investigation.


## Discussion
  This study elucidates the mechanism by which SS suppresses GBC progression through dual modulation of palmitoylation and ferroptosis. Our integrated approach—combining transcriptomic prediction, in vitro functional assays, and in vivo validation—identified APT2 as the critical downstream target of SS. Mechanistically, SS directly binds to and inhibits the enzymatic activity of APT2, thereby impairing STAT3 depalmitoylation. Consequently, membrane‐anchored STAT3 fails to undergo nuclear translocation. This nuclear exclusion downregulates transcription of ferroptosis regulators—notably GPX4 and SLC7A11—resulting in disruption of redox homeostasis. The resultant lipid peroxidation cascade induces ferroptosis, ultimately inhibiting GBC.

  Palmitoylation—a highly dynamic post‐translational modification—exerts spatiotemporal specificity in tumorigenesis by regulating protein membrane anchoring, subcellular localization, and functional complex assembly (Liu et al. 2022). This cyclic “on–off” process is tightly controlled by DHHC‐family palmitoyltransferases (e.g., DHHC5, DHHC17) and depalmitoylating thioesterases (e.g., APT2, ABHD17A). Dysregulated palmitoylation is strongly implicated in tumor initiation and progression, positioning palmitoyltransferase targeting as an emerging therapeutic strategy (Resh 2021). Critically, posttranslational palmitoylation serves as a pivotal regulator of STAT3 oncogenic signaling. This lipid modification, primarily mediated by DHHC palmitoyl acyltransferases, covalently attaches palmitate to conserved cysteine residues (e.g., Cys108) of STAT3. Palmitoylation enhances STAT3's membrane affinity, facilitating its spatial recruitment to receptor complexes and enabling sequential phosphorylation at Y705 by upstream kinases. Activated p‐STAT3 subsequently undergoes thioesterase‐mediated depalmitoylation (by APT2/LYPLA2), releasing it from the membrane (Zhang et al. 2020). Collectively, these events amplify STAT3 dimerization, nuclear translocation, and DNA‐binding capacity. Notably, STAT3 palmitoylation drives malignancy across diverse cancers. Sustained palmitoylation stabilizes active STAT3 dimers, transcriptionally upregulating genes governing proliferation, anti‐apoptosis and angiogenesis (Jiang et al. 2023). Our study establishes the dynamic palmitoylation‐depalmitoylation cycle of STAT3 as a key driver of GBC progression. We demonstrate that stimulus‐induced palmitoylation at Cys108—catalyzed by specific DHHC enzymes (e.g., DHHC7)—anchors STAT3 to the plasma membrane, priming it for phosphorylation by upstream kinases. Crucially, APT2‐mediated depalmitoylation liberates STAT3 for nuclear transit. SS disrupts this equilibrium by entrapping STAT3 in its palmitoylated state, leading to pathological accumulation of cytoplasmic p‐STAT3. Consequently, transcription of ferroptosis suppressors (GPX4 and SLC7A11) is suppressed, inducing redox imbalance and tumor suppression in GBC models.

  STAT3, as a pivotal effector molecule, demonstrates multifaceted regulatory complexity in malignant pathogenesis. Canonically, STAT3 oncogenicity requires Tyr705 phosphorylation‐dependent nuclear translocation, facilitating binding to γ‐activated sequence (GAS) elements in target gene promoters to drive transcription of pro‐survival and antiapoptotic regulators—exemplified by Cyclin D1 and Bcl‐xL upregulation in colorectal cancer to accelerate cell cycle progression and suppress apoptosis (Bollrath et al. 2009; Li et al. 2018). However, our study reveals that GBC cells treated with SS exhibit unaltered total STAT3 and phospho‐STAT3 levels yet demonstrate impaired nuclear translocation efficiency, indicating subcellular spatial distribution constitutes a phosphorylation‐independent functional axis. This paradigm aligns with emerging research on non‐canonical STAT3 roles: unphosphorylated STAT3 modulates inflammatory cytokine secretion via NF‐κB p65 interaction in breast cancer (Yang et al. 2007), while mitochondrial‐localized STAT3 regulates electron transport chain complex I activity in glioblastoma (Gough et al. 2009). Crucially, we identify palmitoylation as a spatial orchestrator of STAT3 dynamics—aberrant Cys108 palmitoylation accumulation traps STAT3 at the plasma membrane, abolishing nuclear translocation‐dependent transcriptional activation. This mechanism contrasts fundamentally with traditional STAT3 inhibitors (e.g., Stattic) that directly block SH2 domain‐mediated phospho‐dimerization (Schust et al. 2006). By selectively disrupting palmitoylation homeostasis rather than global STAT3 activity, our strategy offers a novel therapeutic approach to circumvent resistance associated with comprehensive STAT3 inhibition (Debnath et al. 2012).

  Ferroptosis—an iron‐dependent programmed cell death modality—holds distinct therapeutic value in oncology through its core mechanism of lipid peroxidation‐antioxidant system imbalance. The canonical ferroptosis regulatory axis centers on the System Xc−‐GSH‐GPX4 cascade: SLC7A11‐mediated cystine uptake governs GSH biosynthesis, while GPX4 utilizes GSH to reduce lipid peroxides into nontoxic alcohols, thereby preserving membrane integrity (Yang et al. 2014). Disruption of this defense system triggers iron‐accumulation‐associated phospholipid peroxidation, culminating in uncontrolled oxidative membrane damage and cell death (Conrad and Pratt 2019). Recent evidence increasingly implicates ferroptosis dysregulation in GBC progression (Liu, Li, et al. 2021; Zhao et al. 2025). Our multimodal analysis demonstrates that SS triggers ferroptosis by markedly downregulating SLC7A11 and GPX4 expression via impaired STAT3 nuclear translocation, depleting intracellular GSH reserves and elevating MDA—a lipid peroxidation marker. Mechanistically, this epigenetic modulation of ferroptosis resistance diverges fundamentally from classical inducers: whereas sorafenib inhibits System Xc− in hepatocellular carcinoma and RSL3 directly inactivates GPX4 (Louandre et al. 2013; Yang et al. 2016), SS pharmacologically lowers the threshold for ferroptosis susceptibility without direct antioxidant enzyme blockade, establishing a novel paradigm for low‐toxicity ferroptosis induction.

  The interplay between ferroptosis and tumor metastasis constitutes an emerging research frontier. Our study demonstrates that SS suppresses hepatic metastasis in orthotopic xenograft models—an effect partially rescued by the ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin‐1 (Fer‐1)—suggesting ferroptosis remodels the tumor microenvironment to impede metastatic progression. Mechanistically, SS‐induced ferroptosis: (i) triggers mitochondrial cristae disintegration and ATP depletion, inhibiting invadopodium formation essential for cellular invasion (Gao et al. 2019); and (ii) generates oxidized lipid mediators that covalently modify EMT‐related transcription factors, impairing their nuclear localization and transcriptional activity (Shin et al. 2018). These phenomena align with observed upregulation of epithelial marker E‐cadherin and downregulation of mesenchymal marker N‐cadherin (Figure S5D). Crucially, we identify GPX4 as a central metastasis gatekeeper: SS significantly reduced GPX4 levels in GBC cells and subcutaneous tumor tissue, corroborating reports that GPX4 ablation suppresses metastasis by disrupting redox homeostasis and sensitizing cells to phospholipid peroxide accumulation (Alvarez et al. 2017; Liu, Chakraborty, et al. 2021; Stockwell et al. 2020; Ubellacker et al. 2020). While SIRT3‐mediated AKT/ferroptosis signaling has been implicated in GBC progression, direct evidence linking ferroptosis to GBC metastasis remains scarce (Liu, Li, et al. 2021). Our work bridges this knowledge gap by establishing that SS inhibits STAT3 nuclear translocation, thereby downregulating ferroptosis resistance genes SLC7A11 and GPX4 to abolish metastatic competency—a strategy distinct from existing ferroptosis induction paradigms.

  From a translational perspective, ferroptosis‐targeting therapeutics must balance efficacy against toxicity. SS demonstrates superior safety by targeting the upstream APT2‐STAT3 axis: serum biomarkers (ALT, AST, Cr) in xenograft models showed no statistically significant deviation from controls (p > 0.05), with no ferroptosis‐characteristic histopathology observed in cardiac, pulmonary, or renal tissues. This selective vulnerability likely stems from differential STAT3 palmitoylation levels in malignant versus normal cells and inherent oncogenic iron addiction in gallbladder carcinoma that heightens ferroptosis sensitivity. Unlike isoliquiritigenin—a reported GBC ferroptosis inducer that provokes off‐target oxidative damage via direct GPX4 inhibition (Wang, Li, et al. 2023)—SS achieves transcriptional repression of GPX4 without instigating systemic redox imbalance. Nevertheless, compensatory resistance represents a persistent therapeutic challenge requiring further interrogation.

  A pivotal discovery of this study is the direct binding of SS to APT2—a depalmitoylase governing STAT3 membrane recycling. Molecular docking and mutagenesis identified Asp13 and Leu11 as critical residues mediating SS‐APT2 interaction, while CETSA and SPR conclusively validated target engagement. Functionally, this binding impairs APT2 enzymatic activity, trapping STAT3 in its palmitoylated membrane‐anchored state and preventing nuclear translocation—a mechanism distinct from ZDHHC‐mediated palmitoylation observed in other malignancies. This work repositions palmitoylation as a pharmacologically exploitable switch in cancer biology. Although prior studies linked STAT3 palmitoylation to inflammatory bowel disease (Li et al. 2024; Wei et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2020), we establish its oncogenic role in sustaining redox homeostasis in GBC, thereby defining a targetable vulnerability exclusive to malignant cells.

  The therapeutic implications of SS are multifaceted: it concurrently suppresses proliferation and metastasis without systemic toxicity—a critical advantage for highly metastatic GBC. Notably, preserved STAT3 phosphorylation suggests SS circumvents oncogenic rewiring risks associated with complete pathway ablation. Orthotopic models confirmed SS‐mediated reduction of hepatic metastases. Unlike existing GBC ferroptosis inducers (e.g., isoliquiritigenin or ginsenoside Rg3) that amplify oxidative stress and narrow therapeutic indices (Wang, Li, et al. 2023; Ye et al. 2024), SS minimizes off‐target toxicity through precision targeting of the APT2‐STAT3 interface. While the absence of a direct positive control for APT2 inhibition represents a limitation, this is mitigated by comprehensive validation through target engagement assays and functional rescue experiments. Future studies will utilize emerging selective APT2 inhibitors as comparators once they become available.

  Our findings demonstrate that SS exhibits differential toxicity between normal cells and cancer cells, which may be associated with the differences in APT2 levels between cancerous and normal cells. Our study further supports this conclusion. In cancer cells, ferroptosis is activated by SS, and ferroptosis‐protective genes such as GPX4 and SLC7A11 are persistently downregulated following treatment, resulting in significant cytotoxicity. As expected, SS shows no significant toxicity to normal human gallbladder epithelial cells (L‐2F7), further reflecting the stronger adaptability of normal cells. In addition, SS exhibits no apparent hepatotoxicity or nephrotoxicity in vivo, demonstrating high biosafety.


## Limitations of the Study
  While orthotopic xenografts provide proof‐of‐concept, they incompletely recapitulate the complex stromal‐immune‐metastatic niche of human GBC. Future investigations should employ Kras‐driven genetically engineered mouse models to evaluate efficacy in clinically relevant microenvironments. Furthermore, although SPR confirmed SS‐APT2 binding and downstream palmitoylation dysregulation, the precise structural mechanism—specifically how SS allosterically disrupts APT2‐STAT3 engagement—requires cryo‐EM elucidation. Although JAK–STAT pathway enrichment provided valuable initial direction, this study's scope focused on STAT3 as the terminal effector. Future work may explore potential crosstalk between palmitoylation and upstream kinases. Subsequent research must validate therapeutic universality across malignancies, characterize SS pharmacokinetics and oral bioavailability, and explore synergistic potential with gemcitabine‐based regimens. Addressing these gaps is imperative for advancing SS toward clinical translation in GBC therapy.


## Conclusion
  In conclusion, this study provides the first demonstration that the natural compound SS suppresses GBC progression by inducing ferroptosis through targeted perturbation of APT2‐mediated STAT3 palmitoylation homeostasis. Integrating in vitro and in vivo models, we established SS's dose‐responsive efficacy against GBC proliferation and metastasis, mechanistically dependent on ferroptosis activation. At the molecular level, SS directly engages catalytic residues Asp13/Leu11 within APT2's active site, inhibiting its depalmitoylase activity while allosterically disrupting APT2‐STAT3 complex formation. This dual blockade causes pathological accumulation of palmitoylated STAT3‐Cys108, trapping the transcription factor at the plasma membrane and ablating nuclear translocation. Consequently, SS abrogates transcriptional activation of GPX4 and SLC7A11—core ferroptosis defense elements (Figure 8). As the first botanically derived APT2‐specific inhibitor, SS not only expands the ferroptosis‐inducing arsenal but pioneers a novel therapeutic paradigm: pharmacologically targeting dynamic palmitoylation cycling to dismantle oncogenic adaptation mechanisms.
