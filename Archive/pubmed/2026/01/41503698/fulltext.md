# Enhancer Dynamics for Gene Regulation in the Cardiovascular System


## Abstract
  Enhancers are regulatory DNA sequences that increase the transcription of a target gene when bound by transcription factors. Decades of research endeavors have shed light on crucial roles of enhancers in cardiovascular development and disease. Besides working as cis-regulatory elements, enhancers also play critical roles in transactivating mechanisms such as enhancer RNA transcription and transcriptional condensates. Technological advances, such as single cell multi-omics, CRISPR-based enhancer perturbation screens, and machine learning technologies, provide us with unprecedented opportunities to gain novel insights into the dynamic function of cardiovascular enhancers. In this brief review, we discuss cutting-edge techniques that are being or can be leveraged to fill knowledge gaps in studying cardiovascular enhancers. Like gene expression, enhancers display functional dynamics in concordance with specific cellular stress. We summarize the findings of inducible enhancer functions in the cardiovascular system. We also discuss several recent studies that are advancing our understanding of cardiovascular enhancer functions and potentially paradigm-shifting. These insights have great potential to inform novel therapeutic strategies targeting enhancer-mediated gene dysregulation in cardiovascular disease.


## Introduction
  Enhancers are noncoding DNA elements that regulate gene expression, residing within the noncoding portion of the genome. The term “enhancer” was coined in the early 1980s, following the discovery of DNA sequences located distantly from transcription start sites that could increase reporter gene expression in an orientation-independent manner. These regulatory DNA sequences, initially identified from animal viruses and their homologous mammalian genomic sequences, immediately attracted research interests to characterize how they function1–4. Early investigations revealed that DNase I hypersensitivity sites, which mark open chromatin regions, were found to be associated with enhancers5,6. Enhancer-reporter assays, often coupled with mutagenesis, were used to identify sequence motifs critical for enhancer activity. Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) and DNA-affinity chromatography enabled the detection, purification, and cloning of transcription factors7–9. Around the same time, Escherichia coli beta-galactosidase gene (LacZ) emerged as a powerful tool for tracing the spatiotemporal activity of gene regulatory elements, laying the foundation for transgenic enhancer assays in mouse embryos10–14. In parallel, specific histone modifications were discovered to be associated with certain transcriptional states, which ultimately inspired the “histone code” hypothesis15.

  The completion of the Human Genome Project in 2003 marked the beginning of a new era, enabling the genome-wide discovery of enhancers through two complementary approaches: comparative genomics and epigenomic profiling. Comparative analysis of human genomic sequences with other species of varying evolutionary distances suggested that sequence conservation could serve as a proxy for the regulatory activity of the non-coding DNA16. This hypothesis was experimentally validated using transgenic enhancer assays in mouse embryos, leading to the establishment of the VISTA Enhancer Browser database17. Concurrently, emerging genomics technologies, including chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by microarray (ChIP-chip) or by sequencing (ChIP-seq), were developed to map the genomic landscapes of histone modifications and chromatin binding proteins18,19. Through these efforts, specific histone marks were found to be associated with various regulatory elements and their states in the mammalian genome: monomethylation of lysine 4 on histone H3 (H3K4me1) as an active/primed enhancer mark, trimethylation of H3K4 (H3K4me3) as an active/poised promoter mark, acetylation of lysine 27 on histone H3 (H3K27ac) as an active chromatin mark (at both promoters and enhancers), etc 20–22. Leveraging genomics approaches, large-scale collaborative projects such as the Encyclopedia of DNA Elements (ENCODE) and the Roadmap Epigenomics initiatives played a foundational role in identifying and cataloging enhancers in the human and mouse genomes 23,24. In addition, the development of assay for transposase-accessible chromatin with high-throughput sequencing (ATAC-seq) provided a simpler and more robust alternative to DNase-seq for profiling chromatin accessibility25. These conceptual and technical advances collectively established that enhancers can be characterized by a constellation of epigenomic features, including high chromatin accessibility, DNA hypomethylation, histone modifications such as H3K27ac and H3K4me1, binding of regulatory proteins (such as transcription factors and p300), and three-dimensional genome interactions26. However, enhancer activity is tightly regulated in a spatiotemporal manner, therefore conventional genomic approaches using bulk tissue input cannot fully resolve cell-type-specific enhancer dynamics within complex and heterogeneous tissues. This represents a major bottleneck in enhancer discovery research. In the past decade, breakthroughs in single cell and spatial genomics have enabled the construction of cell-type-resolved maps that capture a wide range of epigenomic features. In the meantime, innovative experimental approaches have been developed to characterize enhancer activity and function in vitro and in vivo, offering deeper insights into their regulatory roles.


## Technological advances to characterize cardiovascular enhancers

### Single cell RNA and chromatin accessibility co-assays.
  Chromatin accessibility reflects the ability of DNA to be contacted by regulatory proteins, with open chromatin providing permissive sites for transcription factors (TFs) and other regulatory proteins binding and gene activation, whereas closed chromatin, maintained by nucleosome compaction and repressive factors, limits access and enforces gene silencing. Single-cell and single-nucleus RNA sequencing of adult human hearts have revealed the transcriptional basis of cardiac cellular diversity, with a consensus reference provided by the Human Cell Atlas27. Single cell ATAC-seq (scATAC-seq) has also been widely applied to characterize open chromatin regions of cardiovascular cell types during development, regeneration, and disease 28–31. However, cellular annotation in scATAC-seq often relies on predicting gene activity from chromatin accessibility at gene body regions, which requires integration with single cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) data from matched or similar samples and can sometimes introduce bioinformatic artifacts. Single cell co-assay of RNA and chromatin accessibility provides a major advantage in cell type annotation, where RNA modality readout from single cells allows robust identification of cell types and cellular states, while the ATAC profile from the same cell for chromatin accessibility could sometimes be difficult to cluster or annotate unambiguously. In addition, by capturing both RNA and ATAC layers together, single cell RNA-ATAC co-assays provide rich regulatory insights for analyzing enhancer-gene correlations to directly connect regulatory elements with the genes they control within individual cells, leading to more precise mapping of cell-type-specific gene regulatory networks. Notably, using the commercial 10X Genomics Multiome technology, Ma and Conley et al. generated a single cell RNA-ATAC atlas of more than 734K cells from human hearts at various developmental stages, delineating regulatory networks across 90 cardiac cell types and states 32. Through an unbiased analysis of this comprehensive resource, the authors uncovered that both rare coding variants associated with congenital heart defects and common noncoding variants associated with valve traits converge to affect valvular interstitial cells, providing an expansive view of cell types involved in genetic risk for congenital heart defects. Besides the commercial technical platforms, various single cell RNA-ATAC co-assay methods have been developed utilizing combinatorial-indexing based barcoding strategy, such as sciCAR (Single-cell combinatorial indexing chromatin accessibility and mRNA) 33, Paired-seq (parallel analysis of individual cells for RNA expression and DNA accessibility by sequencing) 34, SNARE-seq2 (single-nucleus chromatin accessibility and mRNA expression sequencing 2) 35, SHARE-seq (simultaneous high-throughput ATAC and RNA expression with sequencing) 36, UDA-seq (universal droplet microfluidics-based combinatorial indexing for massive-scale multimodal single-cell sequencing) 37, and SUM-seq (single-cell ultra-high-throughput multiplexed sequencing) 38. These approaches substantially increase sample throughput while reducing per-cell cost, thereby facilitating the construction of large-scale single cell epigenomic atlases of the cardiovascular system. Future studies applying single cell RNA and chromatin accessibility co-assays on other pathological samples will provide more detailed regulatory landscapes of the cardiovascular system, guiding the discovery of candidate therapeutic targets and risk loci in cardiovascular disease.


### Single cell analysis of histone modifications.
  Various histone modifications are strongly associated with certain cis-regulatory elements. Mapping histone modifications reveals epigenetic mechanisms underlying disease-associated gene programs, offering opportunities for biomarker development and therapeutic modulation of maladaptive gene programs. Single cell histone modification analysis would therefore reveal chromatin states associated with cis-regulatory elements and pinpoint regulatory mechanisms involved in cell-type-specific gene expression. Several single cell methods for histone modification mapping have been developed based on chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP), cleavage under targets & release using nuclease (CUT&RUN) or cleavage under targets & tagmentation (CUT&Tag) 39,40 (reviewed in 26). Among these methods, CUT&Tag has the unique advantage to be seamlessly incorporated into single cell multiomics workflows owing to its Tn5-based design, which enables the loading of customized barcoded adapters 40,41. Because many histone modifications do not correlate directly with transcriptional output, and because single cell histone modification data are typically very sparse, these profiles alone are often insufficient for robust clustering and reliable cell type annotation. To overcome these limitations, several co-assay strategies have been developed that jointly profile histone modifications together with transcriptome in the same cell. These approaches leverage the transcriptomic readout for reliable cell type annotation and histone marks for regulatory insights, together providing an integrated view of cell identity and epigenetic regulation, including Paired-Tag (parallel analysis of individual cells for RNA expression and DNA from targeted tagmentation by sequencing) 42, CoTECH (combined assay of transcriptome and enriched chromatin binding) 43, SET-seq (same cell epigenome and transcriptome sequencing) 44, Droplet Paired-Tag 45, T-ChIC (Transcriptome + Chromatin ImmunoCleavage) 46, and uCoTargetX (ultrahigh-throughput combined tagmenting enrichment for multiple epigenetic proteins and transcriptome in the same cells) 47. Among them, Droplet Paired-Tag has been applied to both non-failing and failing human hearts to characterize the epigenomic landscapes of active chromatin mark H3K27ac and Polycomb-repressed chromatin mark H3K27me3 across diverse cardiovascular cell types at single cell resolution, providing valuable gene regulatory maps of the human heart in health and disease 48. Future mapping of additional histone modifications in normal and pathological samples will further delineate regulatory epigenetic states in a cell-type-specific manner, shedding light on mechanisms of disease progression and uncovering potential therapeutic targets.


### Single cell analysis of 3D genome interactions.
  Three-dimensional chromatin interaction maps provide critical information for linking distal enhancers to their target genes. Single cell chromatin conformation capture assays, such as single cell Hi-C 49–51, single nucleus Hi-C 52, sciHi-C (single-cell combinatorial indexed Hi-C) 53, Dip-C 54, scNanoHi-C 55, scNanoHi-C2 56, Droplet Hi-C 57, and dscHi-C 58, reveal physical chromatin contacts and higher-order chromatin structures, offering a topological basis for distal-enhancer mediated gene regulation. However, unlike scRNA-seq or scATAC-seq, single cell chromatin conformation profiles do not effectively identify cell types or cellular states. To overcome this limitation, several co-assays have been developed that combine 3D genome interactions profiling with another modality, thereby improving clustering and annotation of single cell profiles. These technologies include co-assays of 3D genome structure with DNA methylation, such as sn-m3C-seq (single-nucleus methyl-3C sequencing) 59 and single cell Methyl-HiC 60; as well as co-assays of 3D genome structure with transcriptome, such as HiRES (Hi-C and RNA-seq employed simultaneously) 61, GAGE-seq (genome architecture and gene expression by sequencing) 62, Paired Hi-C 57, LiMCA (Linking mRNA to Chromatin Architecture) 63, MUSIC (multinucleic acid interaction mapping in single cells) 64, and ChAIR (chromatin accessibility, interaction and RNA profiles ) 65. Among these technologies, Droplet Hi-C has been applied to previously mentioned non-failing and failing human hearts and revealed disease-associated chromatin reorganization in the failing human hearts 48. Looking forward, broader application of these technologies in developmental and disease contexts holds significant promise for further revealing chromatin organization mechanisms involved in context-specific gene regulation in cardiovascular homeostasis and disease.


### Spatial transcriptomics and epigenomics assay.
  Over the past few years, spatial genomics technologies have substantially deepened our understanding of the organizational principles of the mammalian heart. Using Visium, a sequencing-based spatial transcriptomics platform from 10X Genomics, Kanemaru and Cranley et al. mapped the microanatomical distribution of cardiovascular cell types in the human heart. By integrating these data with scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq data generated individually or jointly using 10X Genomics Multiome platform, the authors defined distinct cellular niches and their underlying epigenomic features 66. While Visium enables unbiased spatial detection of transcripts without relying on pre-defined probe sets, its sensitivity and resolution are constrained by the slide’s barcode array. Recent advances in sequencing-based spatial transcriptomics, such as Slide-seqV2 67, DBiT-seq (deterministic barcoding in tissue for spatial omics sequencing) 68 and Stereo-seq (spatial enhanced resolution omics-sequencing) 69, significantly improved the transcript detection efficiency while providing near-cellular or even single resolution, holding great promises for unbiased characterization of gene programs in the cardiovascular system. In addition, building on Slide-seq, Russel et al. developed Slide-tags, a platform for single cell measurements of various features, including epigenomic features, with spatial resolution 70. Using microfluidic flow barcoding strategy similar to DBiT-seq, Deng et al. developed Spatial-ATAC-seq 71 and Spatial-CUT&Tag 72, which provide spatially resolved chromatin accessibility and histone modification profiles, respectively. Zhang and Deng et al. further extended these two methods to allow simultaneous spatial co-profiling of RNA (spatial ATAC-RNA-seq and spatial CUT&Tag-RNA-seq) 73. These new technologies can be adapted to characterize the epigenome of the cardiovascular system with spatial information. Besides sequencing-based spatial methods, imaging-based spatial transcriptomics methods are also gaining popularity due to their unprecedented subcellular resolution. Combining multiplexed error-robust fluorescence in situ hybridization (MERFISH) with scRNA-seq, Farah and Hu et al. performed comprehensive spatial characterization of the developing human hearts. Using unbiased single cell transcriptomic profiling, the authors identified marker genes that can be subsequently validated and spatially resolved using MERFISH. This integrative approach allowed spatial mapping of individual cells and their organizational principles to form specific cardiac cell communities and structures, as well as cell-cell interactions within cardiac cell communities that orchestrate cardiac structure morphogenesis 74. Recently, Kern, Zhang, Lu, Eschbach, and colleagues introduced MERFISH+, an enhanced MERFISH platform that enables simultaneous, high-resolution quantification of more than 1,800 genes together with 3D chromatin organization at selected genomic loci. Applying MERFISH+ to serial sections of the developing human heart, the authors reconstructed the organ in three dimensions and uncovered intricate tissue architecture as well as the cellular composition of local cardiac neighborhoods 75. Wang et al. adopted another imaging-based platform (10X Genomics Xenium) together with single nucleus RNA-seq to characterize multiple postnatal developmental stages of the mouse heart. This integrative approach uncovered dynamic gene regulatory networks of cardiomyocyte maturation, which the authors further functionally validated in vivo using Probe-based Indel-detectable Perturb-seq 76. These studies provide high-resolution spatial mapping of single cells from the developing human hearts and postnatal mouse hearts, which can be used as references for mapping single cell epigenomic datasets for cardiovascular enhancer discovery. Future spatial genomics and epigenomic studies using healthy and disease samples could map the precise location and organization of disease-relevant cell types, define how neighboring cells interact within the tissue via cellular crosstalk, and reveal multicellular niches that link local structure to functional changes in cardiovascular pathology.


### Machine learning and cardiovascular enhancer biology.
  Rapidly advancing machine learning technologies, combined with single cell genomic methods, are providing researchers with unprecedented opportunities to conduct complex genomic analyses and extract mechanistic insights into cardiovascular functional genomics. Using a supervised machine learning approach (LASSO logistic regression), Ranade et al. predicted heart and neural crest enhancers from scATAC-seq data of developing embryonic mouse hearts. These predictions were validated with high success rates using zebrafish transgenic reporter assays, demonstrating how machine learning can guide hypothesis generation and reduce experimental workload by prioritizing high-confidence candidates with functional relevance 77. Furthermore, a variety of sequence-based models have been developed to predict gene expression and epigenetic features in large mammalian genomes, leveraging cell-type-specific epigenetic and transcriptional profiles as training data 78–82. Among these, AlphaGenome is a recently introduced unifying DNA sequence model that achieves multimodal prediction with long-range sequence context up to 1 megabase at base pair resolution 81. It was trained on an extensive collection of mouse and human datasets from the ENCODE, FANTOM5, GTEx, and 4D Nucleome consortia, including RNA-seq, PRO-cap, DNase-seq, ATAC-seq, ChIP-seq for transcription factors and histone modifications, CAGE, and genome contact maps, and it delivers state-of-the-art performance across diverse modalities, including gene expression, transcription initiation, chromatin accessibility, histone modifications, transcription factor binding, chromatin conformation, splice site usage, and splice junction properties. By integrating these modalities within a single framework, AlphaGenome provides a powerful platform for modeling the functional impact of genetic variation, including mutations associated with disease and complex traits, across multiple regulatory layers. Extending beyond the mouse and human genomes, Han et al. developed ultra-throughput, ultra-sensitive single nucleus ATAC-seq (UUATAC-seq) to map open chromatin landscapes across five vertebrate species at single cell resolution 83. Leveraging this resource, they trained a large-scale deep learning model named Nvwa cis-regulatory element (NvwaCE), which can accurately predict chromatin accessibility, infer evolutionary effects, and evaluate synthetic mutations directly from vertebrate genome sequences. The model’s name, drawn from Nvwa, the goddess in ancient Chinese mythology who created humanity and restored order to the world, echoes its mission to illuminate the hidden logic of genome regulation. Collectively, these advanced deep learning models represent a major advance in decoding the regulatory DNA sequences. Their future application holds promises to yield deeper insights into enhancer activity, transcription factor dynamics, and the regulatory consequences of noncoding variants across diverse cell types and developmental stages.


### Experimental approaches to characterize enhancer activities.
  The functional characterization of enhancers depends critically on the proper biological context, given their strong cell type specificity and dynamic temporal regulation. Transgenic enhancer assays are widely used in vivo approaches to test if a specific DNA sequence can drive gene expression in a particular tissue or cell type, as well as to evaluate its specificity. Using transgenic enhancer assays conducted in the developing mouse embryos in vivo, the VISTA Enhancer Browser (https://enhancer.lbl.gov) presents the most comprehensive database of validated mammalian enhancers 84, with recent improvements by leveraging CRISPR/Cas9-mediated site-specific enhancer-reporter insertion (enSERT) to enhance transgene integration while minimizing position effect, and a minimal promoter elements with no leakage expression 85. As part of the VISTA enhancer collection, Kosicki et al. performed systematic mutagenesis on selected human enhancers including those with heart-specific activity, and developed machine learning models that can interpret the mutagenesis results with good accuracy 86. Hollingsworth et al. further developed dual-enSERT, a dual-fluorescent reporter system that enables parallel and quantitative comparison of enhancer allele activities 87. Interestingly, Mannion et al. used an unbiased tiling approach to screen for enhancer sequences using transgenic enhancer assays, and uncovered numerous bona fide enhancer elements with no canonical enhancer marks based on existing epigenomic data, highlighting the need for a more comprehensive catalog of candidate cis-regulatory elements in the genome 88.

  Although transgenic enhancer assays can validate enhancer activity at the organismal level, providing rich, multi-tissue phenotypic information, their utility is constrained by low throughput and the limited number of timepoints that can be practically examined. On the other hand, massively parallel reporter assays (MPRAs) adopt an elegant design where candidate cis-regulatory elements can be transcribed together with their associated reporter gene for sequencing analysis, offering a promising alternative by enabling the simultaneous evaluation of numerous sequences in vitro. In a study focusing on neuronal enhancers, Kosicki et al. showed that results from MPRA and transgenic enhancer assays were strongly and specifically correlated, with transgenic enhancer assays showing pleiotropic variant effects that were not captured by MPRA 89. These findings highlight the potential of a combined strategy in which MPRA-based high-throughput screens and mutagenesis analyses are used to guide the design of transgenic enhancer assays, thereby enabling more comprehensive in vivo characterization of enhancer function and the effects of specific mutations. MPRA has been successfully applied to the cardiovascular system. In a recent study, Xiao et al. applied MPRA with lentiviral delivery in human iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes to systematically interrogate 6,590 noncoding de novo variants (ncDNVs), and identified 403 ncDNVs with significant effects on cardiac cis-regulatory element activity, offering new insights into the genetic basis of CHD. In another study, researchers used AAV9 to systematically deliver thousands of MPRA constructs in vivo and validated 229 cardiac enhancer elements with chamber-selective activities. Tiling mutagenesis of chamber-selective enhancers further identified ERRα/γ as key regulators of ventricular cardiomyocyte gene regulatory network 90. In summary, transgenic enhancer assays and MPRAs represent powerful complementary approaches for characterizing enhancer activity in vivo and in vitro. Future applications of these methods, together with emerging technologies based on similar principles, such as single cell MPRA 91 and single cell quantitative expression reporters (scQers) 92, are expected to generate critical validation data that will illuminate the functions of cis-regulatory elements across normal and pathological states of the cardiovascular system.


## Noncoding variants on enhancers impact cardiovascular disease
  The application of new technologies in cardiovascular enhancer research is advancing our understanding of how noncoding variants impact cardiovascular disease. Here, we feature several recent studies of noncoding variants from both cardiac and vascular research fields. The cardiac research field has witnessed exciting findings through innovative high-throughput methods to understand the function of enhancers. For example, Hocker et al. used scATAC-seq to define candidate enhancer elements in the four chambers of the human heart and found cardiovascular disease-associated genetic variants to be enriched within these candidate enhancer regions, including 38 candidate causal atrial fibrillation (AF) variants localized to candidate enhancer regions specific to cardiomyocytes. Functional studies using CRISPR-Cas9 gene-edited hiPSC-derived cardiomyocytes demonstrate a cardiomyocyte enhancer of KCNH2 is affected by non-coding risk variants associated with AF, leading to cardiac repolarization abnormalities 31. Gacita et al. analyzed the epigenomic landscapes of two cardiomyopathy-associated loci, MYH7 and LMNA, and demonstrated that a genetic variant within the MYH7 enhancer disrupts TBX5 binding, consequently altering MYH7 expression 93. During heart development, enhancers display dynamic activity coinciding with the temporal changes of the 3D genome architecture. Changes in the interaction between enhancer activity and 3D chromatin structure can modulate gene expression during heart development 94. Integration of transcriptomic analysis on neonatal atrial and ventricular cardiomyocytes and bioChIP-seq of a universal chromatin remodeler (P300) and several key cardiac TFs (GATA4, MEF2A, MEF2C, NKX2–5, SRF, TBX5, TEAD1) in vivo, Cao et al. generated a comprehensive dataset of potential cardiac chamber-specific enhancers (CSEs) (occupied by multiple TFs). A subset of CSEs was validated to have chamber-specific activity using an unbiased MPRA, and the authors further correlated some confirmed CSEs with human cardiovascular traits and disease-associated GWAS variants 90. Building upon this dataset, TBX5 has been identified as a key transcriptional regulator of the atrial-specific enhancer network 95. Another recent study has also validated the co-regulation of cardiac TFs on atrial-specific enhancers as implicated in this dataset 96. Importantly, cardiac enhancer studies are also advancing our understanding of the etiology of congenital heart disease (CHD). For a long time, non-coding variants have been poorly studied in CHD due to the lack of high-throughput and incisive methods to link non-coding variants to CHD directly, while the burden of potentially disruptive regulatory ncDNVs in CHD is as high as that observed for damaging coding DNVs 97. One recent study pioneered the utilization of the ncDNV information in predicting the relevant cardiac enhancer activity and the potential impact on CHD 98. Using the defined DNV information in CHD probands and parents from the Pediatric Cardiac Genomics Consortium (PCGC), Xiao et al. designed a lentivirus-based MPRA (lentiMPRA) in human induced pluripotent-stem cell derived cardiomyocytes (hiPSC-CMs) to survey the activity of candidate cardiac enhancers. Using the same methodology, they further tested the impact of ncDNVs on cardiac enhancer activity and developed a prediction model, EpiCard, that can be used for prioritizing functional ncDNVs in CHDs. This work elegantly demonstrated that how new molecular and bioinformatic tools can be leveraged to study the impact of noncoding variants on CHD in a high-throughput manner.

  Besides these high-throughput approaches in studying cardiac enhancers, GWAS locus-based noncoding variant study represents a main trend in the vascular enhancer research field. To identify noncoding variants influencing hypertension, researchers integrated human genomic methylation profiling with hypertension GWAS and identified a causal variant (rs1275988T/C) on enhancer regulating the Kcnk3 gene. By creating a mouse line carrying the rs1275988 mutant allele, they confirmed that this variant is associated with vascular remodeling and hypertension. Mechanistically, this variant reduces Kcnk3 gene expression through attenuating the binding of its TF (Nr2f2) 99. Another representative study is focused on an GWAS-identified enhancer variant rs11172113C/T relevant to various vascular diseases. Using hiPSC-derived smooth muscle cells (SMCs), researchers showed that this enhancer region regulates the LRP1 gene expression and extracellular matrix synthesis 100. One recent study focusing on the coronary artery disease-associated noncoding variants revealed that rs1320496 and rs1412444 mutations on the LIPA enhancer locus increases LIPA gene expression and the likelihood of developing atherosclerosis 101. These studies demonstrate the value of leveraging genomic information to better understand the etiology of vascular diseases. Together, these studies shed light on the impact of noncoding genomic variants on cardiovascular disease. Although vast majority of the noncoding genome is still like dark matter, integration of high throughput methods and machine learning technologies will generate new datasets and deepen our understanding of different functional modules of the genome, facilitating the candidate-based investigation of noncoding variants.


## Inducible cardiovascular enhancer activation
  Activity of enhancer elements can be highly dynamic, and many enhancers remain dormant under homeostatic conditions but become activated in response to injury or stress. Such inducible enhancers drive transcriptional programs that mediate pathological remodeling, inflammation or tissue repair, and are increasingly recognized as key regulators of disease progression and tissue regeneration102. Here, we highlight a few recent studies that identify or functionally characterize such inducible enhancers.


### Inducible enhancers in cardiac fibroblast activation.
  Fibroblast activation represents a conserved stress response program across multiple tissues. In the mammalian heart, fibroblast activation leads to fibroblast proliferation and extracellular matrix deposition, culminating in fibrosis that compromises tissue architecture and impairs cardiac function. Pharmacological inhibition of Bromodomain and extra-terminal domain (BET) proteins, using compounds such as JQ1, has shown significant therapeutic potential in mitigating cardiac fibrosis. Using scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq, Alexanian et al. profiled transcriptomes and chromatin accessibility in single cells from hearts subjected to JQ1 treatment following transverse aortic constriction (TAC)–induced heart failure and identified a dynamic enhancer required for the activation of transcriptional factor MEOX1 and fibroblast-to-myofibroblast transition. Functional studies further demonstrated that MEOX1 occupies stress-responsive enhancers and drives a broad profibrotic gene program, including Ctgf and Postn, establishing enhancer-mediated regulation of MEOX1 as a central mechanism in fibroblast activation and cardiac fibrosis 103. In a follow-up study, Alexanian et al. identified BRD4-bound enhancers that regulate macrophage-derived, stress-induced Il1b expression after TAC. Macrophage secreted IL-1β further regulates fibroblast activation through activation of MEOX1 through a specific RELA-dependent enhancer 104. Collectively, these studies underscore the pivotal role of inducible enhancers in controlling disease-associated gene expression and suggest that targeting these injury-responsive elements may offer new therapeutic avenues for cardiovascular disease.


### Enhancers dynamics in vascular diseases.
  Dramatic epigenetic changes have also been observed in vascular diseases. Vascular SMCs (VSMCs) undergo phenotypic switching from the contractile state to the synthetic state during various vascular diseases, including aortic aneurysms and atherosclerosis. One recent study comprehensively analyzed the gene expression and chromatin accessibility changes of the VSMCs using scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq, providing a valuable dataset to further explore the induced epigenetic changes governing VSMC phenotypic switching 105. Besides VSMCs, vascular endothelial cells also display dynamic activity under physiological and pathological stress. Physiologic laminar shear stress induces beneficial responses in endothelial cells by increasing enhancer accessibility of genes that promote cellular homeostasis and suppress inflammation 106. Another study revealed that disturbed blood flow can induce endothelial super-enhancers (SEs) involved in the pathological mechanotransduction responses to atherosclerosis 107. Besides the function as cis-regulatory elements, enhancers also have transactivating functions through encoding enhancer RNAs. In response to pathological stresses, a couple of enhancer-encoded long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) display obvious expression changes and have been shown to play critical regulatory roles in vascular diseases, such as CARMN 108, INKILN 109, HCG20 110, and LncRNA-ITGA2 111.


### Enhancers in heart regeneration.
  During tissue regeneration, a distinct gene program is initiated to restore lost tissue, suggesting the existence of enhancers specifically activated during this process that govern the regenerative gene program. Kang et al. identified the first tissue regeneration enhancer element (TREE) in zebrafish by transcriptional and epigenome profiling of regenerating zebrafish hearts and fins. This zebrafish lepb-linked enhancer, regulating the expression of leptin b, directs regeneration-dependent gene expression in zebrafish fins and hearts 112. Using replacement histone profiling, Goldman and Kuzu et al. uncovered additional TREEs in zebrafish cardiac regeneration 113, which Yan and Cigliola et al. further utilized to drive dCas9-epigenetic effector fusion proteins to achieve targeted modulation of endogenous gene expression, as well as YAP5SA, a constitutive active mutant form of YAP, to promote cardiomyocyte proliferation and cardiac function after myocardial infarction 114. Mechanistically, Begeman et al. assessed the compatibility of regeneration enhancers with diverse minimal promoters, offering key insights for transgenic enhancer assays in zebrafish and guiding the characterization and potential application of these enhancers 115. Not only in cardiomyocytes, injury-responsive enhancers can also mediate intercellular communications to promote cardiomyocyte proliferation, as shown by a recent study that a mmp14b nearby injury-responsive endothelial enhancer activates the extracellular matrix remodeling and promotes heart regeneration 116. These studies highlight an innovative strategy to either exogenously deliver or endogenously activate pro-regenerative factors under the control of an injury-responsive enhancer, ensuring their effects remain tightly coordinated within the regenerative gene program.

  Although adult mammalian hearts have limited capacity for regeneration after injury, the neonatal mammalian hearts have a restricted regeneration time window after birth. Do mammalian hearts have regeneration enhancers that drive the neonatal regeneration gene program? To answer this question, Wang and Cui et al. performed ChIP-seq for active chromatin mark H3K27ac and Polycomb-associated repressive chromatin mark H3K27me3 on neonatal heart tissues at different stages of the regeneration process post myocardial infarction and compared that with post-injury hearts at non-regenerative stages. Combining epigenome profiling with transcriptome profiling on the same tissue samples, the authors identified dynamic enhancer activation in regenerative and non-regenerative neonatal hearts and the gene programs that they control 117. To further reveal cardiomyocyte-specific regeneration gene program, Cui and Wang et al. applied scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq on cardiomyocytes isolated from regenerative and non-regenerative neonatal hearts, and identified a subtype of cardiomyocytes that is unique to the regenerative heart, with a gene program that features cell proliferation-related genes under the control of NFYa and antioxidant gene expression under the control of NRF1 118. In addition, regenerative gene regulatory networks in non-cardiomyocytes that facilitate heart regeneration have recently been unveiled in both mouse and zebrafish 29,119,120. Together, these studies offer valuable resources for the identification of cell-type-specific enhancers in the cardiovascular system, which can be further leveraged to investigate and potentially modulate mammalian heart regeneration for novel therapeutic strategies.


## Implications of transcriptional condensates in regulating cardiovascular cell fate
  Classical functions of cardiac enhancers in development and disease have been comprehensively reviewed. In this review, authors raised an interesting point that enhancers function as a hub through recruiting TFs which then promote polymerization of other transcriptional components towards the promoter of target genes102. It has been unclear how the speculated polymerization of transcriptional components in driving gene expression actually works. In recent years, transcriptional condensate emerges as a new model of understanding the interaction between transcriptional regulators and gene activation. Conventional wisdom depicts the transcriptional process as a multiple-component and multi-step formation of a transcriptional complex to drive gene expression, while the condensate model proposes that transcriptional condensates aggregate the different components of the transcriptional machinery via liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS). Different from the formation of transcriptional complex, which relies on direct protein-protein interactions, the formation of transcriptional condensates relies on the weak and multivalent interactions among proteins and nucleic acids. The formation of transcriptional condensates is a higher-level organization of transcriptional components, including histones, TFs, coactivators, mediators, RNA polymerase II (Pol II) and enhancers etc., which does not supersede the assembly of regular transcriptional complex 121–123. The key feature of transcriptional condensate is its dynamic and selective compartmentalization of high density of transcriptional components, facilitating rapid and robust gene activation. This is crucial for cellular processes that require substantial changes in the transcriptome, such as cell fate transition.

  Here, we focus on the emerging implications of transcriptional condensate-based gene activation in the cardiovascular system. Myocardin (MYOCD) is a highly potent transcriptional coactivator that supports the establishment of cardiac and smooth muscle cell lineages by activating the cardiac and smooth cell identity gene programs 124, while the mechanistic basis of its exceptionally high transcriptional potency has remained unclear. Our recent study demonstrated that the increase of MYOCD expression during cardiac and smooth cell differentiation leads to its condensation when reaching a critical concentration threshold 125. MYOCD condensates selectively aggregate active transcriptional components, such as RNA Pol II and P300, and preferentially interact with enhancers governing cardiac and smooth muscle cell identity gene expression. We demonstrated that the formation of MYOCD condensates is necessary for the massive cardiac and smooth muscle gene activation during cell lineage specification. Through quantitative proteomics analysis, we found that three main clusters of proteins were aggregated into the condensates for gene activation, including chromatin remodelers, RNA Pol II subunits, and mRNA processing factors 126. Integrating ChIP-seq, RNA-seq and super-resolution confocal microscopy, we demonstrated that MYOCD condensates also dedicate the specific genomic binding on CArG box-containing enhancers associated with smooth muscle cell identity genes. This study provides direct evidence showing that transcriptional condensates can compartmentalize factors essential for different steps of transcription (chromatin remodeling, transcription and mRNA processing) on specific genomic loci for cell identity gene expression. An interesting observation from this study is that MYOCD condensates not only aggregate active chromatin remodelers, such as SWI/SNF and ISWI subunits, but also several transcription repressors in NuRD complex, such as RBBP7 and CHD4 126. It is possible that transcriptional repressor can promote the function of a transcriptional activator during cell fate transition. A study focusing on atrial cardiomyocyte identity maintenance found that a transcriptional repressor, CHD4, acts as a transcriptional activator to promote the binding of TBX5 in atrial cardiomyocytes 127. Besides activating a gene program, transcriptional condensates can also play repressive roles, as shown by a recent study that HDAC4 condensates sequester transcriptional regulators to inhibit cardiac cell reprogramming. Once condensates are disrupted by a unique phosphorylation code in the 14–3-3 binding motifs, the cardiac gene program can be activated 128. Therefore, we propose that condensate-aggregated transcriptional regulators work in a highly coordinated and cooperative manner to activate a specific cell identity gene program. It will be of interest to investigate how transcriptional condensates coordinate transcriptional activators and repressors on specific enhancers to orchestrate gene regulation of a specific cell fate.


## Dosage-dependent regulation of cardiovascular cell fate by transcription factors
  CHDs are frequently caused by mutations that lead to the haploinsufficiency of TFs, such as TBX1, GATA4, TBX5, NKX2–5, HAND2 and NOTCH1 etc. 129–134. Disrupted gene regulatory networks (GRNs) and cell fate trajectories have been shown to correlate with the haploinsufficiency of these key cardiac TFs. GRN analysis also revealed critical enhancer nodes linked to key signaling pathways, which can be further leveraged for therapeutic interventions. For example, the GATA4 G296S mutation was shown to disrupt TBX5 recruitment to SEs associated with cardiomyocyte fate gene program, leading to the activation of the endothelial lineage gene program. They found that the GATA4 haploinsufficiency caused aberrant GRNs centered on PI3K signaling, which could act as a node to correct the disease-associated GRN 134. Another study focusing on the haploinsufficiency of TBX5 in CHDs demonstrated a similar mechanism. Researchers found that reduced TBX5 dosage changed the normal cardiomyocyte GRN and cell fate 133. A recent study from the same group further demonstrated that the dosage of TBX5 is essential for maintaining the 3D chromatin organization in the cardiomyocyte lineage 135. Intriguingly, many of those key cardiac TFs display haploinsufficiency in regulating cardiac GRNs, leading to altered cell fate and CHDs. Besides the cardiac lineage, a similar TF dosage-dependent regulatory mechanism has been reported in the endothelial lineage. Etv2, a master TF regulating both endothelial and erythropoietic lineages, was shown to display differential dosage requirements for both lineages. A low level of Etv2 expression is sufficient to induce and sustain the endothelial GRN, while a significantly higher threshold of Etv2 is needed for the erythropoietic development 136. One common caveat of these studies is the lack of a clear understanding of the stoichiometric relationship between the TF expression level and their activity in gene activation.

  The transcriptional regulation of a cell fate transition can be divided into three phases: coactivation, gradual biasing, and commitment 137. In multipotent cells, transcriptional regulators of competing lineages are expressed together, a process called coactivation. Gradually, the increased expression of some master TFs shifts the cell fate gene program towards one specific fate. Those master TFs reinforce their lineage gene program by increasing the expression of synergistic TFs and repressing competing cell lineage gene programs, forming positive feedback in cell fate commitment (Figure 1). Recent studies from transcriptional condensates provide a tempting model to explain the stoichiometric relationship between master TFs and cardiovascular cell fate determination 138. Many TFs and coactivators contain intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs), especially in their transcriptional activation domains (TADs) 125,139–141. IDRs drive the LLPS of TFs to form transcriptional condensates, and numerous pieces of evidence have demonstrated that these condensates control gene expression involved in cell identity and cell fate determination. Transcriptional condensates create a highly cooperative environment that concentrates RNA Pol II and cofactors at cell lineage–specific enhancers, commonly organized as SEs. The dense clustering of transcriptional regulators with numerous transcription factor binding sites within SEs enables robust, rapid activation of cell identity genes and supports switch-like transitions in cell fate. The formation and stability of transcriptional condensates largely depend on the dosage of the major LLPS driver. Our recent study supports this notion by showing that when reaching a critical concentration threshold, MYOCD forms transcriptional condensates and activates cardiac and SMC identity gene expression 125. Although MYOCD has been the only master cardiovascular transcriptional coactivator confirmed to regulate cell lineage specification through forming condensates, emerging evidence has revealed that the condensation capacity of other master TFs in cardiovascular development, such as NOTCH1 142 and FOXC1 143. Therefore, it is reasonable to speculate that many other dosage-sensitive cardiovascular master TFs function in a condensate-dependent manner to regulate the cell fate gene program.

  Although transcriptional condensate appears to be a compelling model to interpret the TF haploinsufficiency-caused cardiovascular cell fate gene program dysregulation and CHD, there are still obvious limitations: (1) Besides loss-of-function mutations that reduce TF expression level, there are numerous gain-of-function mutations that also disrupt the GRNs of cardiovascular cell fate and lead to CHD, such as TBX5 144, which cannot be readily explained by the dosage-dependent transcriptional condensate formation model. It is possible that gain-of-function mutations can influence TF condensates by changing the critical concentration threshold required for condensate formation or the composition/enhancer interaction-specificity of condensates, as reported in several other condensates 145,146. More experimental evidence is needed to test this hypothesis in the cardiovascular system. (2) Cells display heterogeneous responses to TF haploinsufficiency. For example, in the mutant TBX5 hiPSC lines, the expression level of TBX5 should be synchronized in cells carrying the same mutant alleles, while there was still a good proportion of mutant cells differentiating into ventricular cardiomyocytes. Given that the condensate-based activation of cardiomyocyte gene program is like a binary decision, it is hard to explain where the cell fate heterogeneity comes from. It could be because of the heterogeneous responses of cells to the differentiation medium, and it could also be because of the limitation of the cell fate prediction analysis used in the study. (3) Study of endogenous transcriptional condensates is technically challenging, due to the lack of highly specific antibodies targeting endogenous TFs and a system that completely recapitulates the cardiovascular differentiation process in vitro. It is nearly impossible to capture cardiovascular transcriptional condensate behaviors in live embryos due to the requirement of super-resolution confocal microscopy and the lack of probes targeting condensates. HiPSC differentiation system with the CRISPR knock-in of fluorescent epitope onto TFs of interest can be an optimal solution. In summary, transcriptional regulation of cardiovascular cell fate by condensates is an unexplored field of study, which opens up many new opportunities to understand the dynamic interactions between cardiovascular transcriptional regulators and enhancers in determining cell fate and etiology of CHDs.


## Future perspectives

### Revealing new cardiovascular disease mechanisms from enhancer biology.
  Recent advances in single cell epigenomics and multi-omics have facilitated the construction of increasingly comprehensive catalogs of enhancer elements in the cardiovascular system and yielded rich insights into the dynamics of cell-type-specific gene regulatory networks. By leveraging the cardiovascular enhancer catalog, researchers can annotate disease-associated risk variants within the non-coding genome, which accounts for ~99% of the mammalian genome and has historically been difficult to interpret. Future studies that integrate cardiovascular enhancer annotation, deep learning, human genetics, and stem cell-derived in vitro models, together with high-throughput assessment of variant effects in their native genomic context through genome editing and single cell omics, are expected to reveal novel mechanisms of cardiovascular disease rooted in enhancer biology.


### Transcriptional condensate could be a new frontier in cardiovascular enhancer study.
  In the context of cardiovascular research, elucidating the precise mechanisms or compositional intricacies of condensate formation is not the most pressing priority. Instead, it is of greater importance to critically evaluate how transcriptional condensates advance our understanding of cardiovascular biology. We propose that transcriptional condensates are ubiquitous in the cardiovascular system and play critical roles in various cellular processes that demand rapid and robust gene activation, such as cell fate transitions, inflammatory responses, and injury responses. Investigating these condensates will not only provide new insights into cardiovascular biology but may also uncover novel therapeutic opportunities through targeted modulation of condensate dynamics.
