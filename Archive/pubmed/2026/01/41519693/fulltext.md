# PfTALE5 Acts as a hub for multi-stress resilience: a genome-wide study of the TALE family in Paulownia fortunei


## Background
  The Three-Amino-Acid-Loop-Extension (TALE) transcription factors are key regulators of plant development and stress responses. However, a systematic study of the TALE gene family in Paulownia fortunei has not been reported to date.


## Results
  Here, we conducted the first genome-wide analysis and identified 38 PfTALE genes, which were phylogenetically classified into the KNOXI, KNOXII, and BELL subfamilies. These genes were unevenly distributed across 15 chromosomes. Promoter analysis revealed abundant cis-elements associated with light, hormones, and stress. Transcriptome profiling and qRT-PCR analyses showed that PfTALEs were differentially induced by drought, salt, and phytoplasma infection. Among them, PfTALE5, PfTALE12, PfTALE16, and PfTALE18 were significantly responsive to all three stresses, suggesting pivotal roles in stress adaptation. Notably, PfTALE5 exhibited consistent and strong upregulation under all stress conditions and was localized to both the nucleus and cytomembrane. Furthermore, yeast two-hybrid assays and molecular docking confirmed that PfTALE5 physically interacts with PfKNAT7 via hydrogen bonds, implying their cooperative function in stress response regulation.


## Conclusions
  Our study provides the first comprehensive genomic and functional insights into the TALE gene family in P. fortunei, highlighting PfTALE5 as a key regulator conferring multi-stress resilience. These findings offer valuable genetic targets and strategic guidance for breeding stress-resistant woody cultivars.

## Introduction
  Plants are consistently exposed to a variety of environmental stresses during their growth and development, including drought [1, 2], extreme temperatures [3, 4], salinity [5, 6] and pathogen [7, 8] attacks. These adversities disrupt normal physiological processes and significantly compromise crop yield and quality. To cope with these challenges, plants have evolved sophisticated mechanisms that balance growth with defense responses [9, 10]. Transcription factors (TFs) play central roles in these adaptive processes, orchestrating the expression of genes related to development, stress signaling, and hormonal regulation [11, 13]. Among them, the Three-Amino-Acid-Loop-Extension (TALE) superfamily of homeodomain-containing TFs has been recognized as a key regulator integrating developmental programs with environmental adaptation in diverse plant species [5, 14, 15].

  The TALE superfamily constitutes an evolutionarily conserved group of TFs characterized by a distinctive homeodomain structure featuring a three-amino-acid loop extension between the first and second helices [16]. TALE proteins are typically classified into two subfamilies—KNOX and BELL, which often form heterodimers to precisely modulate gene expression involved in development and stress responses [17, 18]. KNOX proteins usually harbor specialized domains, such as KNOX1, KNOX2, ELK, and Homeobox KN, that facilitate DNA binding and protein–protein interactions [19, 21]. Compared with the KNOX protein, there is relatively less research on the BELL protein. Bell proteins typically contain two domains, POX and Homeobox_KN [22]. It is reported that BELL protein plays an important role in the development of ovules, petioles and fruit ripening [23]. Beyond their well-established roles in meristem maintenance [24], organ development [25, 30], and hormone signaling [31], TALE genes are increasingly implicated in responses to both abiotic and biotic stresses. For instance, TaKNOX11-A in wheat enhances tolerance to drought and salt [32], while GmSBH1 in soybean contributes to thermotolerance and influences leaf morphology [33]. In Populus, PagKNAT2/6b promotes drought resistance by suppressing gibberellin biosynthesis [34], and in cotton, the GhBLH7-D06/GhOVP3-D13 complex modulates resistance to Verticillium wilt [35]. Additionally, multiple TALE genes in Citrus sinensis are upregulated in response to fungal and bacterial infections [36], further underscoring their functional versatility in stress adaptation.

  Paulownia is a fast-growing deciduous tree native to China, valued for its lightweight yet durable wood, high biomass production, and vigorous regenerative ability. These traits support its extensive use in afforestation, furniture manufacturing, traditional instrument crafting, biofuel synthesis, biomedical applications, aerospace and aviation [37, 40]. However, Paulownia is susceptible to various biotic and abiotic stresses that severely compromise its growth and productivity. A major biotic threat is Paulownia witches' broom disease (commonly known as “Paulownia cancer”), caused by phytoplasma infection [41]. This disease leads to leaf yellowing, reduced photosynthetic efficiency, stunted branch development, and in severe cases, tree death [42, 44]. Additionally, abiotic stresses including drought [45, 46], soil salinization [47, 49], and extreme temperatures [50, 52] further impair growth and reduce wood quality, threatening the sustainable development of the Paulownia industry. Despite the economic and ecological importance of Paulownia and the well-established roles of TALE genes in stress responses in various plant species, their functions in Paulownia have not yet been investigated. The recent availability of the P. fortunei genome sequence [53] has enabled genomic investigations into TALE transcription factors, providing an opportunity to address this knowledge gap.

  In this study, we conducted a genome-wide identification and characterization of the TALE gene family in P. fortunei. We analyzed their physicochemical properties, phylogenetic relationships, gene structures, collinearity, and expression patterns under various stress conditions. Furthermore, we investigated the transcriptional self-activation activity and protein interactions of selected members. This comprehensive analysis provides a molecular foundation for understanding stress adaptation mechanisms in Paulownia and supports the development of stress-resistant germplasm. Our findings not only elucidate the functional roles of PfTALE genes in stress responses but also offer valuable genetic resources and practical strategies for breeding stress-tolerant Paulownia varieties, thereby promoting sustainable forestry and industrial production.


## Materials and methods

### Identification of the PfTALE gene family in Paulownia fortunei
  The genome and protein sequences of Arabidopsis thaliana were obtained from the TAIR database (https://www.arabidopsis.org/, accessed on 15 March 2025). The genome data of P. fortunei (v1.0) were obtained from NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/, accessed on 9 March 2024). The Hidden Markov Model (HMM) profile of TALE structural domain (PF05920) was retrieved from the Pfam database (https://pfam.xfam.org, accessed on 22 May 2025). Putative PfTALE proteins were identified by HMMER searches against the P. fortunei protein dataset, followed by removal of redundant sequences. The physicochemical characteristics of PfTALE proteins, including amino acid length, molecular weight (kDa), isoelectric point (pI), and grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY), were calculated using ProtParam (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/, accessed on 23 May 2025). Subcellular localization was predicted with WoLF PSORT (https://www.genscript.com/wolf-psort.html, accessed on 23 May 2025).


### Analysis of conserved motifs and gene structure of PfTALEs
  Conserved domains of PfTALE proteins were identified using the NCBI Batch CD-Search tool (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, accessed on 22 May 2025). Conserved motifs were predicted with MEME Suite (https://meme-suite.org/meme/, accessed on 22 May 2025) using a maximum of 10 motifs to characterize motif types and distributions among PfTALE subgroups. Protein domain annotations were retrieved from the NCBI Conserved Domain Database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/bwrpsb/bwrpsb.cgi, accessed on 22 May 2025). Gene structures, functional domains, and conserved motifs was visualized with TBtools-ll v2.340 [54].


### Phylogenetic analysis of PfTALE proteins
  Multiple sequence alignment of PfTALE and Arabidopsis TALE proteins was conducted using MEGA11. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the neighbor-joining (NJ) method with 1,000 bootstrap replicates. The tree was visualized and annotated with the Evolview (https://www.evolgenius.info/evolview-v2/, accessed on 19 August 2025).


### Chromosomal localisation and cis-acting element analysis of PfTALEs
  Chromosomal distribution of PfTALE genes was mapped with TBtools-ll v2.340 based on the P. fortunei genome. The 2.0 kb promoter regions upstream of the transcription start sites were extracted from the genome annotation (GFF3 file) using TBtools-ll v2.340. Putative cis-acting elements in these promoter sequences were predicted with the PlantCARE (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/, accessed on 19 August 2025).


### Expression pattern analysis of PfTALEs under biotic and abiotic stress conditions
  The coding sequences (CDS) of the PfTALE genes were obtained from the P. fortunei genome for expression analysis under abiotic stresses (drought and salt) [55, 56] and biotic stress (phytoplasma infection) [57, 58]. Transcriptome datasets from samples collected before and after stress treatments were used to identify differentially expressed PfTALE genes. Gene expression levels were quantified based on FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads) values and visualised as heatmaps with TBtools-ll v2.340, providing a comparative overview of PfTALEs expression dynamics under various stress conditions. The transcriptome datasets were retrieved from the NCBI (SRR11787883, SRR11787894, SRR11787905, SRR11787916, SRR11787922–SRR11787928, SRR11787938, SRR11787942–SRR11787944, SRR11787949, SRR11787960, SRR11787971, PRJNA221355, and PRJNA289582).


### Plant materials and stress treatments
  Healthy and phytoplasma-infected seedlings of P. fortunei were obtained from the P. fortunei Biology Laboratory, Henan Agricultural University. Plants were maintained under controlled conditions (25 ± 2 °C, 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod, 70% relative humidity). Shoot apices from one-month-old seedlings were collected, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at –80 °C.

  For stress assays, healthy seeds of diploid P. fortunei were surface-sterilized with 30% (v/v) H₂O₂ for approximately 12 min, rinsed three times with sterile ddH₂O, and germinated on 1/2 MS medium (Murashige and Skoog, Phytotech, USA) under the above growth conditions. After 7–10 days, seedlings were first cultured on 1/2 MS medium for 4 weeks, and then transferred to fresh 1/2 MS medium for further culture, followed by 5–6 days of acclimation. Uniform seedlings (4–5 cm in height) were transplanted into plastic pots containing a 3:1 (v/v) mixture of sterilized soil and vermiculite and grown for 7–10 days prior to stress treatments.

  For stress treatments, seedlings were irrigated with 0.4% NaCl solution (salt stress) or 15% PEG 6000 solution (drought stress) for 24 h, while untreated or normally watered seedlings served as respective controls. Leaf, stem, and root tissues (for salt stress) or leaf tissues (for drought stress) were harvested, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at –80 °C for subsequent RNA extraction.

  Each treatment included three biological replicates, with five seedlings per replicate.


### RNA extraction and quantitative PCR analysis
  Total RNA was extracted from P. fortunei seedlings using the FastPure Universal Plant Total RNA Extraction Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), and cDNA synthesis was performed with the 2 × RealStar Fast SYBR qPCR Mix Kit (GeneStar, Beijing, China). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was conducted on an Applied Biosystems™ 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with the Actin gene as the internal reference. Relative expression levels were calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method. Each experiment was performed in triplicate. Statistical analysis and data visualization were performed with GraphPad Prism v10.1.2. Gene-specific primers are listed in Supplementary Table S1.


### Subcellular localisation
  The coding sequence was cloned and inserted into a 35S:GFP vector to generate the 35S:PfTALE5-GFP construct (Table S2). The recombinant plasmid pSAK277-PfTALE5 was then transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 and introduced into leaves of four-week-old Nicotiana benthamiana. After infiltration, the plants were kept in darkness for 24 h, followed by a 10 h light/14 h dark photoperiod for an additional 2 days. GFP fluorescence signals were observed using a laser scanning confocal microscope (A1, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).


### Yeast Two-Hybrid (Y2H)
  To assess the yeast self-activation activity of PfTALE5, the recombinant vectors pGBKT7-PfTALE5 and pGADT7 were co-transformed into yeast strain AH109 for self-activation assay. The combinations pGBKT7 + pGADT7 and pGBKT7-53 were used as negative and positive controls, respectively. For protein interaction analysis, pGBKT7-PfTALE5 and pGADT7-PfKNAT7 were co-transformed into AH109 cells. Transformed yeast was first cultured on SD/-Trp/-Leu medium at 30 °C for 3 days, and colonies were subsequently screened on SD/-Trp/-Leu/-Ade/-His medium. Protein interactions were determined based on reporter gene activation.


### Prediction of the tertiary structure and molecular docking of PfTALE proteins
  The tertiary structures of 38 PfTALE proteins were predicted with the SWISS-MODEL (https://www.swissmodel.expasy.org/). The potential interaction between PfTALE5 and PfKNAT7 was investigated by molecular docking. The complex structure of the two proteins was predicted using the AlphaFold Server (https://deepmind.google/science/alphafold/alphafold-server/), Structural optimization was performed with Maestro v2.5.0 [59], and the results were visualized with PyMOL v4.6 [60].


## Results

### Identification and characterisation of the PfTALE gene family in P. fortunei
  We identified 38 non-redundant PfTALE genes in the P. fortunei genome through a combined approach using BLAST against Arabidopsis TALE proteins and validation with the TALE domain HMM profile (PF05920). These genes were systematically named PfTALE1 to PfTALE38 based on their chromosomal order (Table 1). Phylogenetic analysis classified the PfTALE proteins into three subfamilies—KNOXI, KNOXII, and BELL, following the established classification framework for Arabidopsis TALE proteins (Fig. 1). The KNOXI subfamily comprised eight PfTALE members, while the KNOXII subfamily included seven members. Notably, no PfTALE members were assigned to the KNOXIII subfamily. In contrast, the BELL subfamily contained a larger number of PfTALE members (23), all of which possessed the characteristic BELL domain (Fig. 2C). Physicochemical analysis revealed that PfTALE proteins vary in length from 293 (PfTALE13, PfTALE33) to 830 amino acids (PfTALE9), with molecular weights ranging from 33.21 to 91.22 kDa (Table 1). All PfTALE proteins except PfTALE10, PfTALE22, PfTALE34, PfTALE36, and PfTALE38 had isoelectric points below 7 and GRAVY values below 0, indicating overall acidic and hydrophilic properties. Subcellular localisation predictions further suggested that all proteins were localized to the nucleus. ### Conserved motifs and gene structure analysis of P. fortunei TALE proteins
  To investigate the conserved motifs and gene structures of the PfTALE family, we identified 10 conserved motifs using the MEME suite (Fig. 2A, B). Proteins within the same subfamily shared similar motif compositions and arrangements. KNOXI members (e.g., PfTALE1, 2, 5, 13, 16, 30, 33) contained motifs 1, 4, 5, and 7, whereas KNOXII members (e.g., PfTALE3, 8, 12, 17, 18, 27–29) carried motifs 1, 4, 5, and 8. BELL members showed greater variability: PfTALE34 contained only motifs 1, 3, and 9; a subset (PfTALE7, 15, 20, 25, 31, 35, 37) contained motifs 1, 2, 3, 6, and 9; and the remaining BELL members contained six motifs (1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 10). Domain analysis further indicated that all KNOXI members except PfTALE13 and PfTALE33 contained both KNOX2 and ELK domains, suggesting subfunctionalization within this group (Fig. 2C).

  Gene structure analysis revealed that most PfTALE genes contain 3–5 introns and 4–6 exons (Fig. 2D). KNOX subfamily genes generally possessed more introns and longer exons compared to BELL members, implying structural and potentially functional divergence between subfamilies.


### Analysis of cis-acting elements in the promoter regions of the PfTALEs
  To infer the regulatory mechanisms of PfTALE genes, we analyzed cis-elements within 2.0 kb promoter regions upstream of the transcription start sites. A wide array of elements associated with light responsiveness, hormone signaling (e.g., auxin, GA, ABA, MeJA, and SA), and stress responses (e.g., drought, low temperature, and defense) were identified (Fig. 3). Light-responsive elements were the most abundant, suggesting a potential role for PfTALEs in light-mediated development. ### Interaction between PfTALE5 and PfKNAT7
  Potential protein interaction partners of PfTALE5 were predicted using the STRING database, which identified several candidates, including PfKNAT7 (Fig. 7B). Verified via yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assay, the yeast self-activation activity of PfTALE5 was first detected. Transformants containing pGBKT7-p53 + pGADT7-T (positive control), pGBKT7 + pGADT7 (negative control), or pGBKT7-PfTALE5 + pGADT7 exhibited normal growth on SD/-Trp/-Leu medium. However, only the positive control grew on SD/-Trp/-Leu/-His/-Ade medium, confirming that PfTALE5 had no transcriptional activation activity, and demonstrating the suitability of the pGBKT7-PfTALE5 construct for interaction studies (Fig. 7C). For the interaction assay, pGBKT7-PfTALE5 and pGADT7-PfKNAT7 constructs were co-transformed into yeast AH109 cells and grew normally on both SD/-Trp/-Leu and SD/-Trp/-Leu/-Ade/-His media, demonstrating the interaction between the PfTALE5 and PfKNAT7 proteins (Fig. 7C). These findings indicate that PfTALE5 may regulate stress responses through complex formation with partners such as PfKNAT7, rather than functioning as a transcriptional activator itself (Fig. 7C).


### Tertiary structure analysis of PfTALE proteins
  To gain structural insights into the PfTALE proteins, homology modeling of their tertiary structures was performed. All 38 PfTALE proteins exhibited a high degree of structural conservation, sharing a characteristic fold typical of TALE homeodomain proteins (Fig. 8A). Despite this overall similarity, subtle variations were observed among individual members, primarily arising from differences in secondary structure composition, including the relative proportion and spatial arrangement of α-helices, β-sheets, turns, and random coils. This structural diversity may underlie the functional specialization of PfTALE proteins.Fig. 8Prediction of the tertiary structure of the P. fortunei PfTALE protein and molecular docking analysis of PfTALE5 with PfKNAT7


### Molecular docking of PfTALE5
  To investigate the molecular basis of the interaction between PfTALE5 and PfKNAT7, molecular docking simulations were performed to predict the structure of the protein complex. The results revealed that PfTALE5 and PfKNAT7 formed 15 stable hydrogen bonds at the binding interface. with PfTALE5 contributing 10 bonds as a donor and PfKNAT7 forming 5 bonds. Key residues involved in these interactions included ARG243, ASN294, LYS221, and ARG298. These hydrogen bonds were distributed across the binding interface, forming a compact and stable network that likely facilitates strong molecular recognition between the two proteins (Fig. 8B). These findings provide structural insights into the specificity and stability of the PfTALE5–PfKNAT7 complex, supporting the experimental observation of their functional interaction. The detailed hydrogen-bond parameters are provided in Supplementary Table S5.


## Discussion
  The TALE gene family, encoding homeodomain transcription factors with conserved KNOX and BELL motifs, plays essential roles in plant growth regulation and stress responses. Our study provides the first comprehensive genome-wide analysis of the TALE family in P. fortunei, identifying 38 PfTALE genes and characterizing their structural features, evolutionary relationships, and expression patterns under multiple stress conditions.

  Gene duplication serves as a primary mechanism for gene family expansion and functional diversification [61, 62]. We identified 21 segmentally duplicated gene pairs within the PfTALE family, indicating that segmental duplication has been the main driver for its expansion. This pattern aligns with observations in cotton (Gossypium hirsutum) [62] and soybean (Glycine max) [63], and likely contributed to functional innovation and stress adaptation in P. fortunei. Domain analysis revealed that while all PfTALE proteins contain the characteristic Homeobox_KN domains essential for DNA binding and transcriptional regulation [64, 65], structural variations exist. For instance, PfTALE13 and PfTALE33 lack the ELK domain, suggesting potential functional differentiation within the KNOX subfamily. Similar structural modifications have been reported in Cucumis sativus [64] and Lotus japonicus [66], where domain loss often associates with neofunctionalization, particularly under stress conditions [67].

  Promoter cis-element analysis revealed an abundance of light-responsive, hormone-related, and stress-inducible elements, indicating that PfTALE genes may serve as hubs integrating multiple signaling pathways into stress response networks. The prevalence of light responsive motifs, consistent with findings in L. japonicus [66] and C. sativus [64], suggests a conserved role in photomorphogenesis. Likewise, the enrichment of drought, low temperature, and hormone responsive elements supports their involvement in abiotic and biotic stress responses, similar to reports in Triticum aestivum [32] and Brassica napus [68].

  Expression profiling under various stresses demonstrated that PfTALE genes exhibit both stress-specific and tissue-specific expression patterns. Several members (including PfTALE1, PfTALE2, PfTALE6, PfTALE7, PfTALE9, PfTALE10, PfTALE13, PfTALE16, PfTALE19, PfTALE22, PfTALE30, PfTALE31, PfTALE34, PfTALE35, PfTALE36 PfTALE37, and PfTALE38) displayed antagonistic expression trends under drought versus salt stress, indicating functional divergence and possible subfunctionalization after gene duplication. Such expression plasticity likely enables P. fortunei to fine-tune adaptive responses, a phenomenon also observed in wheat (TaKNOX11-A) [32]. Under salt stress, the root-specific upregulation of PfTALE5 and PfTALE16 is consistent with the role of roots as the primary salt-sensing organs and with the conserved functions of TALE proteins in abiotic stress adaptation. Similar to ZmKNOX05 in maize, which is highly expressed in roots and responds to salt stress by regulating stress-related pathways [69], PfTALE5 may enhance root osmotic adjustment and ion homeostasis.

  The leaf-specific upregulation of PfTALE18 under phytoplasma stress corresponds to the role of leaves as the primary sites of pathogen colonization. PfTALE18 may similarly regulate leaf reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging or pathogenesis-related (PR) protein genes, as observed in Citrus TALE-mediated pathogen resistance [36], thereby limiting phytoplasma proliferation and protecting photosynthetic activity. This tissue-specific expression pattern reflects functional specialization of PfTALE genes, optimizing stress adaptation efficiency.

  Notably, PfTALE5 emerged as a central regulator, showing consistent and strong upregulation under drought, salinity, and phytoplasma infection stresses, highlighting its role in broad-spectrum stress defense. Similar multifunctional induction has been reported in cucumber TALEs under hormonal and waterlogging treatments [65] and in Citrus responding to both drought and Candidatus Liberibacter asiaticus infection [36]. Subcellular localization analysis showed that PfTALE5 is dual-targeted to the nucleus and cytomembrane, similar dual-localization patterns have been reported in other transcription factor families; for example, PfGRAS12 localizes to the nucleus to regulate gene expression while also participating in signal perception or protein interactions at the cell membrane [70], and alfalfa MsMYB35 exhibits nuclear–membrane dual localization and performs multiple regulatory functions by modulating downstream target genes [71]. These studies collectively suggest that dual localization is often associated with functional diversification of transcription factors. Therefore, it is plausible that PfTALE5 may possess both transcriptional regulatory activity and membrane-associated signaling functions, mediating gene regulation in the nucleus while also participating in signal transduction or regulatory network formation through membrane-related pathways.

  Based on Y2H and molecular docking techniques, this study is the first to confirm a direct interaction between PfTALE5 and PfKNAT7. In terms of protein structural and functional characteristics, PfTALE5 belongs to the TALE transcription factor family, and its typical Homeodomain is a core element for DNA binding and transcriptional regulation [31]; As a member of the KNOX family, the Arabidopsis homolog AtKNAT7 has been demonstrated to play a key role in secondary cell wall biosynthesis, functioning as a negative regulator of secondary wall formation. AtKNAT7 regulates downstream cell wall–related genes by forming transcriptional complexes with other TFs, such as MYB family members, thereby coordinating secondary wall deposition [72]. It is speculated that the interaction between PfTALE5 and PfKNAT7 may play a key role in the regulation of target gene expression through the formation of a transcriptional regulatory complex.

  Collectively, our findings indicate that while different PfTALE members contribute to stress-specific signaling pathways, PfTALE5 may act as a pivotal hub integrating diverse environmental cues, thereby strengthening Paulownia's resilience to multiple stresses. The broad and consistent induction of PfTALE5 under multiple stresses, its dual subcellular localization, and its interaction with PfKNAT7 suggest that it serves as a regulatory hub integrating biotic and abiotic stress responses.

  The limitations of this study include a relatively small sample size and the need for further functional validation of PfTALE5. In this study, both qRT-PCR and yeast two-hybrid experiments were conducted with three biological replicates. In future studies, the number of replicates will be increased to further enhance the reliability of the conclusions. In addition, the function of PfTALE5 still requires in-depth validation: subsequent research could involve generating PfTALE5 overexpression lines or CRISPR-Cas9-mediated knockout lines in P. fortunei, or conducting heterologous overexpression analyses in model plants such as Arabidopsis or N. benthamiana, combined with systematic phenotypic assessments to comprehensively elucidate its biological functions. Additionally, investigating its involvement in hormone signaling crosstalk, particularly with ABA, SA, and cytokinin, could further clarify how TALE transcription factors fine-tune environmental adaptation in P. fortunei. Studies exploring the downstream target genes of PfTALE5 and their regulatory mechanisms would also provide deeper insights into its molecular functions.


## Conclusion
  This study provides the first comprehensive genome-wide characterization of the TALE gene family in P. fortunei, identifying 38 PfTALE genes and elucidating their phylogenetic relationships, gene structures, chromosomal distribution, and stress-responsive expression patterns. Our results indicate that segmental duplication has been the primary driver of TALE family expansion in P. fortunei. Expression profiling revealed that PfTALE genes exhibit distinct tissue- and stress-specific expression patterns, with PfTALE5 showing consistent upregulation under drought, salt, and phytoplasma infection stresses. Its dual subcellular localization, together with the experimentally verified interaction with PfKNAT7, suggests that PfTALE5 may function as a key regulator integrating multiple stress-response pathways. Collectively, these findings deepen our understanding of TALE gene evolution and function in P. fortunei and offer valuable genetic resources for developing germplasm with enhanced resistance to multiple stresses.
