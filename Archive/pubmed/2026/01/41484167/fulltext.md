# α-terpineol induces apoptosis in melanoma cells and its underlying mechanism


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-34538-7.


## Introduction
  Malignancy of the skin’s pigment lineage often ignites when dormant, mole-born cell clusters—legacy or late-appearing—flip their fate toward unchecked cancerous growth. It primarily affects adults, with approximately 90% of cases associated with inadequate ultraviolet (UV) radiation protection1. In the last ten years, this pigment-born skin cancer has quietly accelerated; left unflagged, it readily slips into the bloodstream and seeds far-flung tissues. Epidemiological data indicate that hepatic metastases occur in 50%–80% of advanced-stage patients, while cerebral metastases develop in 8%–46% of cases2. Notably, certain melanoma subtypes can also infiltrate uveal, meningeal, and mucosal tissues. Although the advent of targeted therapy, immunotherapy, and oncolytic virotherapy has improved survival rates, these treatments are often limited by low efficacy, exorbitant costs, and significant adverse effects3. Consequently, researchers are actively exploring novel therapeutic strategies to enhance outcomes in advanced-stage melanoma while minimizing treatment-related toxicity. Given these challenges, the development of more effective anticancer agents is imperative, offering critical implications for the clinical management of melanoma.

  Natural products are highly regarded in anticancer drug development for their accessibility and strong bioactivities. Pine needle oil, a volatile extract from pine needles known for its unique aroma, has been used in traditional Chinese medicine for its antitumor properties4. The crude extract and its primary monomeric components have shown inhibitory effects on the growth of multiple cancer cell lines. Research indicates that pine needle oil causes G2/M phase arrest and DNA damage in HepG2 hepatocellular carcinoma cells in a dose-dependent manner5,6, and significantly suppresses the in vitro growth of BEL-7402 liver cancer cells7. Research by Wei Fengxiang et al.8 confirmed its pro-apoptotic effect on hepatoma cells, with apoptotic rates increasing proportionally with concentration and exposure duration, establishing apoptosis induction as a key antitumor mechanism. Wu Dan’s team9 observed concentration-dependent inhibition of SGC-7901 gastric cancer cell proliferation, while Chen Yuqiang et al.10 documented remarkable antitumor activity of Pinus massoniana needle ether extracts against transplanted tumors. Pine needle oil induces apoptosis and autophagy in human breast cancer MCF cells11. α-terpineol, a key component of this oil, exhibits diverse pharmacological effects, including antimicrobial, antifungal, anti-inflammatory, antiviral, and antioxidant activities, making it valuable in cosmetics12. Emerging research suggests its potential as an anticancer agent by influencing tumor cell proliferation and apoptosis13. For instance, in glioblastoma, α-terpineol specifically targets KDELC2 to suppress tumor growth, migration, invasion, and angiogenesis via downregulation of the Notch and PI3K/mTOR/MAPK pathways, while inducing early/late apoptosis and necrosis, and reducing chemoresistance14. It also inhibits small cell lung cancer growth through NF-κB pathway suppression15. Toxicological evaluations in non-clinical assays revealed its selective cytotoxicity against B16-F10 melanoma cells over macrophages, with minimal organ toxicity16. Given the multicomponent nature of pine needle volatile oil, this study focuses on α-terpineol to elucidate its role as the potential pharmacodynamic basis for the oil’s antitumor effects.

  The JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway is crucial in eukaryotes for regulating key cellular processes such as proliferation, differentiation, repair, inflammation, oxidative stress, and cytokine expression17. During tumorigenesis and progression, abnormal activation and overexpression of the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway are frequently observed. This dysregulation fosters a tumor-associated inflammatory microenvironment and is linked to the development and progression of several human malignancies, including lung, liver, gastric, breast, colorectal, pancreatic, and ovarian cancers18,19. Studies have demonstrated that inhibiting JAK2/STAT3 signaling can effectively suppress cancer cell growth and induce apoptosis. Notably, this pathway is also crucial in melanoma. Multiple studies have shown that reducing STAT3 phosphorylation can inhibit the malignant behavior of cutaneous melanoma, confirming the impact of blocking the STAT3 signaling pathway on the proliferation of cutaneous malignant melanoma cells and the alteration of the G1-S cell cycle transition20,21. Similarly, one study22 revealed that Janus kinase 2 (JAK2) inhibitors and STAT3 inhibitors could impede melanoma growth and modulate the immune system by blocking certain enzymes essential for cell proliferation. Furthermore, Zhou Xiaocheng et al.23 reported that exosomal miR-155-5p derived from melanoma could activate the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway, thereby promoting the pro-angiogenic phenotypic transformation of tumor-associated fibroblasts. However, to date, the potential anticancer mechanisms of α-terpineol in human melanoma have not been thoroughly investigated. Therefore, based on the aforementioned background, this study explores the potential of α-terpineol to inhibit human melanoma, demonstrating that α-terpineol can suppress the proliferative activity of A375 cells and inhibit the activation of the JAK2/STAT3 pathway, thereby inducing tumor cell apoptosis and ultimately exerting antitumor effects.


## Materials and methods

### Storage of α-terpineol
  The α-terpineol standard was purchased from MedChemExpress and stored appropriately at -20 °C. α-Terpineol was dissolved in DMSO to create a 1540 mg·L⁻¹ stock solution, aliquoted, and stored at -20 °C for future experiments. Vemurafenib was purchased from Selleck Biotech Co., Ltd. in the United States.


### Cell culture
  Human melanoma A375 and MV3 cell lines were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) and cultured in high-glucose DMEM medium (Invitrogen Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Solarbio Life Science, Beijing, China). Cell cultures were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ in a humidified environment using a Thermo Fisher Scientific incubator. Subculturing was routinely performed at a 1:3 split ratio upon reaching the appropriate confluence.


### Assessment of α-terpineol’s effects on cell proliferation using CCK-8 assay
  Single-cell suspensions of A375 and MV3 cells were prepared by trypsinizing with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were counted using an Oxbow chamber and seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per 100 µL well. Following a 24-hour incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2 to facilitate cell attachment, the culture medium was refreshed with new medium containing α-terpineol at concentrations of 0.385, 0.77, 1.54, 3.08, 6.16, 12.32, 24.64, and 49.28 mg·L− 1. A DMSO-treated group served as the negative control. After 48 h of treatment, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (Solarbio Life Science) was added to each well, followed by a one-hour incubation. The optical density (OD) at 450 nm was assessed with a multifunctional microplate reader (Molecular Devices, Shanghai, China). The proliferation inhibition rate was calculated for both cell lines at each concentration, with six replicate wells per concentration and three independent experimental repeats. IC50 values were assessed for both cell lines following 48 h of treatment. The IC50 value of Vemurafenib was determined using the same method.


### Scratch wound healing assay for evaluation of cell migration capacity
  A375 and MV3 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded at 4 × 10⁵ cells per well in 6-well plates and cultured to form a confluent monolayer. A sterile 200 µL pipette tip was employed to create uniform linear scratches, and PBS was subsequently used to remove dislodged cells. The control group was treated with a medium containing 2% FBS, while the experimental groups received media supplemented with 2% FBS and either 6.16 mg·L⁻¹ α-terpineol, 12.32 mg·L⁻¹ α-terpineol, or 0.16 mg·L⁻¹ Vemurafenib, respectively. Initial microscopic images were captured immediately after scratch formation (0 h), followed by subsequent imaging at 24 h and 48 h intervals using an inverted phase-contrast microscope (model and manufacturer details if available). The experiment was independently repeated three times. Wound closure was quantified using ImageJ software (version 2.1.0; Bethesda Softworks, Bethesda, MD, USA) by comparing the remaining scratch area to the initial wound area at each time point.


### Transwell migration assay for the evaluation of cell invasive capacity
  The Matrigel matrix (BD Biosciences, New York, NY, USA) was thawed at 4 °C twelve hours before the experiment. A uniform layer of 50 µL Matrigel was applied to the upper chambers of Transwell inserts (8 μm pore size; Corning Life Science, New York, NY, USA) using pre-chilled pipette tips, and allowed to polymerize overnight at 37 °C. A375 and MV3 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were serum-starved in serum-free medium for 24 h, followed by digestion and resuspension. The cells were then adjusted to a density of 4 × 10⁴ cells per 200 µL in a 2% FBS medium containing one of the following: no drug (control), a low or high concentration of α-terpineol, or vemurafenib. Cell suspensions were added to the polymerized Matrigel-coated upper chambers, and the lower chambers were filled with 800 µL of medium containing 15% FBS as a chemoattractant. After 48 h of incubation, the non-invaded cells on the upper membrane surface were mechanically removed. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution (Solarbio Life Science) for 10 min. Cells were quantitatively analyzed by counting in five randomly selected fields per insert using an inverted microscope at 200× magnification. The invasive potential was expressed as the mean number of transmigrated cells, with the entire experiment repeated in triplicate.


### Cell colony formation assay
  A375 and MV3 cells, during their logarithmic growth phase, were seeded at a density of 800 cells per well in 6-well plates. Following cell attachment, the cultures were treated with 6.16 mg·L⁻¹ α-terpineol, 12.32 mg·L⁻¹ α-terpineol, or 0.16 mg·L⁻¹ Vemurafenib, along with a parallel DMSO control group. After 48 h of treatment, the culture medium was replaced with a complete medium, which was renewed every two days. After 14 days of culture, cell colonies (defined as aggregates of > 50 cells) were observed. The culture medium was removed, and the cells were rinsed three times with PBS. Each well was treated with 1.5 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, stained using 0.1% crystal violet for 15 min, rinsed with PBS, and air-dried before imaging. Colony counts were averaged, and the experiment was conducted independently three times.


### EDU assay
  The EDU assay kit was purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (#C0071S, Shanghai, China). Following the manufacturer’s protocol, A375 and MV3 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded in 6-well plates at 5 × 10⁴ cells/mL. After cell attachment, the cells were treated with 6.16 mg·L⁻¹ α-terpineol, 12.32 mg·L⁻¹ α-terpineol, or 0.16 mg·L⁻¹ Vemurafenib for 48 h. Pre-warmed EDU solution (10 µM) was then added and incubated for 2 h in a CO2 incubator to label proliferating cells. After EDU incorporation, the culture medium was discarded, and cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Leagene Biotechnology, Beijing, China) for 15 min at room temperature. Cells were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 min, then incubated with a freshly prepared Click reaction mixture for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. The cells underwent three PBS washes, followed by a 5-minute DAPI counterstaining of the nuclei. EDU-positive cells were visualized and imaged under a fluorescence microscope.


### AO/EB staining assay for apoptosis detection
  A375 and MV3 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were collected, resuspended in PBS, and counted. Cells were seeded in confocal culture dishes at 1 × 104 cells/mL and cultured for 24 h. The cells were treated for 48 h with DMSO (control), 6.16 mg·L⁻¹ α-terpineol, 12.32 mg·L⁻¹ α-terpineol, or 0.16 mg·L⁻¹ Vemurafenib. Post-treatment, the AO/EB working solution (Yuanye Bio, Shanghai, China) was added to the cell suspension and mixed thoroughly. The mixture was incubated for 15 min at room temperature, ensuring all steps were conducted in light-protected conditions. Cell morphology was immediately observed and photographed using fluorescence microscopy. Finally, the images were merged using ImageJ software.


### Analysis of cell cycle distribution using flow cytometry
  A375 and MV3 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were trypsinized to create single-cell suspensions and uniformly seeded into 6-well plates. After 24 h of incubation, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing 6.16 mg·L⁻¹ α-terpineol, 12.32 mg·L⁻¹ α-terpineol, or 0.16 mg·L⁻¹ Vemurafenib. A control group treated with DMSO was also included. After 48 h of treatment, cells were trypsinized, resuspended at 5 × 10⁵ cells/mL, and washed twice with ice-cold PBS. Cells were fixed overnight at 4 °C using pre-chilled 75% ethanol. Subsequently, the fixed cells were centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min, and the supernatant was removed. Cell pellets were resuspended in 500 µL of a staining solution comprising 25 µL of propidium iodide (PI) and 10 µL of RNase A (Meilunbio, Dalian, China), mixed thoroughly, and incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 30 min. The cell suspension was filtered through a 40-µm nylon mesh to eliminate aggregates before analysis. Cell cycle distribution was then assessed using a flow cytometer, with data processed via FlowJo software (version 10.10.0). The cell cycle distribution percentages in G0/G1, S, and G2/M phases were measured, with the experiment independently repeated three times.


### Flow cytometric analysis of apoptosis rate
  A375 and MV3 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were trypsinized to create single-cell suspensions and evenly distributed in 6-well plates. After 24 h of incubation, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing 6.16 mg·L⁻¹ α-terpineol, 12.32 mg·L⁻¹ α-terpineol, and 0.16 mg·L⁻¹ Vemurafenib, with a DMSO vehicle control group set up in parallel. Cells were collected after 48 h of treatment and adjusted to a concentration of 5 × 105 cells/mL. The cell suspensions were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and then centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 minutes. Cell pellets were resuspended in 500 µL of binding buffer from the Meilunbio apoptosis detection kit (Dalian, China), then 5 µL each of Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) was added. Following a 30-minute dark incubation, the stained cells were filtered through a 40-µm nylon mesh and analyzed with a BD Biosciences flow cytometer. FlowJo software (version 10.8.1) was used to quantify apoptotic populations, with three independent replicates conducted for each experimental condition.


### Screening of potential target proteins for α-terpineol
  The 3D structural model of α-terpineol was submitted to two online analysis platforms: PharmMapper (http://www.lilab-ecust.cn/pharmmapper/) and the Swiss Target Prediction database (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/). In PharmMapper, we specifically utilized the pharmacophore model library for in-depth screening. A list of genes closely linked to melanoma was obtained from the GeneCards, OMIM (https://omim.org/), and TTD (https://db.idrblab.net/ttd/) databases. Subsequently, the screening results from PharmMapper and Swiss Target Prediction were subsequently cross-referenced with the melanoma-related genes obtained from GeneCards to identify overlapping target genes. Finally, these intersecting results were visually presented in the form of a Venn diagram.


### Functional annotation analysis of key signaling pathways
  The overlapping target genes of α-terpineol were analyzed using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/kegg1.html)24–26 database to explore their links to key signaling pathways. Utilizing KEGG’s mapping function, subsequent analysis employed the enrichment factor (Rich factor) and P-value to quantify the degree of enrichment. The enrichment factor indicates gene clustering within specific pathways, and the P-value evaluates the statistical significance of this clustering.


### Molecular docking prediction of α-terpineol with key target proteins
  KEGG enrichment analysis was used to screen signaling pathways linked to melanoma pathogenesis, identifying key target protein nodes within these pathways. The structural data for the relevant targets were obtained from the AlphaFold database (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/). Dimeric proteins underwent structural optimization to ultimately obtain the three-dimensional conformations of the target proteins. Molecular docking analysis was then performed between α-terpineol and these targets using the MOE software package. The binding free energy of receptor-ligand complexes was calculated during docking, with lower values signifying stronger binding affinity. This systematic approach facilitates a comprehensive understanding of the interactions between α-terpineol and melanoma-related targets, providing valuable reference information for drug development.


### Analysis of protein expression via Western blot
  A375 and MV3 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well. The next day, cells were grouped and exposed to either 6.16 mg·L− 1 or 12.32 mg·L− 1 α-terpineol, or a DMSO-containing culture medium as a vehicle control for 48 h. All subsequent procedures were performed on ice unless otherwise specified. Cells were lysed using RIPA buffer (Solarbio Life Science) containing 1% (v/v) PMSF and phosphatase inhibitors, followed by intermittent ultrasonication. The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. Protein concentrations in the supernatants were measured following the BCA Protein Assay Kit instructions from Solarbio Life Science. Protein samples were combined with 5× loading buffer (Meilunbio), heated at 100 °C for 10 min, and preserved at -20 °C for later analysis. SDS-PAGE gels (10% or 7.5%, Epizyme, Shanghai, China) were prepared based on the molecular weights of the target proteins. Proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) after electrophoresis. Membranes were incubated in 5% skim milk (Solarbio Life Science) at room temperature for 2 h to block non-specific binding. Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C. The following day, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Protein bands were detected with ECL chemiluminescence reagent (Meilunbio). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software, with GAPDH as the internal control. The study utilized antibodies from Proteintech Group, including GAPDH (#10494-1-AP, 1:10000), Bax (#60267-1-Ig, 1:5000), Bcl-2 (#60178-1-Ig, 1:6000), Caspase-8 (#66093-1-Ig, 1:6000), Caspase-3 (#66470-2-Ig, 1:2000), and HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (#SA00001-2, 1:6000) and Anti-Mouse IgG (#SA00001-1, 1:6000). Additionally, antibodies from ABclonal Technology were used, including JAK2 (#A19629, 1:1000), Phospho JAK2 (#AP0531, 1:800), STAT3 (#A1192, 1:1000), and Phospho STAT3 (#AP0715, 1:1000).


### In vivo investigation of α-terpineol’s anti-tumor properties
  Ten 4-week-old male BALB/c nude mice (nu/nu) were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. The experimental group and control group each consisted of 5 nude mice, with randomization performed using a random number table. The study protocol was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Guizhou Medical University (Approval No.: 2403610), and all procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines. Mice were kept in plastic cages under standard conditions (25 ± 2°C, 70% humidity, 12-hour light/dark cycle) with unrestricted access to food and water. B16-F10 murine melanoma cells were prepared at 3 × 107 cells/200 µL in PBS, and 200 µL was subcutaneously injected into the right flank of each mouse using a 1 mL syringe. Daily monitoring included assessment of mental status, food/water intake, and tumor formation. Upon palpation of tumors, their dimensions were measured with digital calipers, and the volume (V) was calculated using the formula V = 1/2 × (maximum length × maximum width2). Mice with tumors around 80 mm3 were randomly assigned to two groups (n = 5). The α-terpineol dosage of 200 mg/kg was chosen based on prior research indicating its in vivo anti-inflammatory and anti-cancer properties (Souza R, et al.27–29. The treatment group received α-terpineol through oral gavage, while the control group was given 5% DMSO in physiological saline. Body weight and tumor measurements were taken every 48 h. Following 10 gavage administrations, all mice were euthanized for tumor excision and normal organ collection for subsequent analyses. Tumor volume measurement and data analysis were both performed using blinded evaluation methods. Mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 50 mg/kg sodium pentobarbital. Animals were euthanized in the study. A fatal dose of sodium pentobarbital (150 mg/kg, IP) was given, with death confirmed by the lack of heartbeat and corneal reflex.


### Immunohistochemical analysis and hematoxylin-eosin staining
  The excised tumor and normal organ tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and processed using an automated tissue dehydrator (Wuhan Junjie Electronics Co., Ltd. ) following a standardized protocol. This involved sequential incubations in 75% ethanol (3 h), 85% ethanol (1 h), 95% ethanol (1 h 20 min), three stages of absolute ethanol (25 min, 20 min, 20 min), a 1:1 ethanol-xylene mixture (20 min), two stages of xylene (15 min each), and paraffin infiltration in three stages (1 h, 2 h, 3 h). The tissues were then embedded in paraffin using a tissue embedding machine (Changzhou Jiaoqu Zhongwei Electronic Instrument Factory) and sectioned at 4 μm thickness. Immunohistochemical analysis involved staining tumor sections with anti-p-JAK2 and anti-p-STAT3 antibodies to assess cell proliferation, and nuclear proliferative activity was evaluated using rabbit anti-human Ki-67 monoclonal antibodies (1:400, Cell Signaling Technology, Boston, USA). Apoptosis was assessed using a TUNEL reaction mixture composed of 50 µL Solution 1 and 450 µL Solution 2 from the In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit POD (Roche). The mixture was applied to dried slides, covered with coverslips, and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified dark chamber for 1 h, followed by the manufacturer’s protocol. For histological examination, sections were deparaffinized and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (Beyotime), and all stained sections from both tumor and organ tissues were ultimately examined under an Olympus IX83 light microscope for comprehensive pathological evaluation.


### Reporting guidelines
  This study was reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.


### Statistical analysis
  All data are representative of at least three independent experiments and are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. The sample size (n) refers to the number of biological replicates, with no fewer than three technical replicates included in each experiment. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.1.2). Comparisons between two groups were analyzed by an unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-test. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed, followed by an appropriate post-hoc test. Specific p-values are indicated in the figures or figure legends, with values less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.


## Results

### α-terpineol inhibits cell proliferation and colony formation in melanoma A375 and MV3 cell lines
  α-Terpineol is a cyclic monoterpenoid compound (Fig. 1A). Exposure to varying concentrations of α-terpineol (0.385 to 49.28 mg·L− 1) for 48 h resulted in dose-dependent inhibition of A375 and MV3 cell proliferation (Fig. 1B, C). The IC50 values from the CCK-8 assay indicated that α-terpineol achieved 50% inhibition at concentrations of 34.01 mg·L− 1 for A375 cells and 22.91 mg·L− 1 for MV3 cells after 48 h of treatment. The IC₅₀ of Vemurafenib was determined to be 0.46 mg·L⁻¹ for A375 cells and 0.32 mg·L⁻¹ for MV3 cells, respectively. Following the determination of IC50 values for both cell lines after 48-hour exposure to α-terpineol, subsequent experiments utilized 6.16 mg·L− 1 and 12.32 mg·L− 1 as the low-dose and high-dose groups, respectively. Vemurafenib was used as a positive control at a concentration of 0.16 mg·L⁻¹. These results indicate that α-terpineol suppresses the proliferation of both A375 and MV3 cells cultured in vitro for 48 h at these two concentrations.

  The EDU assay demonstrated that α-terpineol, at both low and high concentrations, significantly and dose-dependently inhibited the proliferation of A375 and MV3 cells after 48 h of treatment. High-concentration α-terpineol exerted a stronger inhibitory effect on cell proliferation than Vemurafenib (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1D). Furthermore, compared to the DMSO control group, the colony formation rates of both the low- and high-dose groups were markedly reduced after 48 h of α-terpineol treatment, indicating that α-terpineol exerts a concentration-dependent inhibitory effect on the clonogenic ability of A375 and MV3 cells. Both high-concentration α-terpineol and Vemurafenib demonstrated a strong ability to inhibit colony formation (Fig. 1E).

### α-terpineol significantly attenuates the migration and invasion potential of human melanoma A375 and MV3 cell lines in vitro
  The invasion and migration of malignant tumors are key determinants of prognosis and major contributors to disease recurrence30. In the scratch wound healing assay, α-terpineol treatment notably inhibited the migration of A375 and MV3 cells compared to the control group. The inhibitory effect intensified with higher concentrations of α-terpineol and extended incubation periods. No significant difference was observed in the inhibition of cell migration between high-concentration α-terpineol and Vemurafenib (P > 0.05) (Fig. 2A). The Transwell assay was utilized to assess the effect of α-terpineol on melanoma cell invasion. The study results showed that increasing concentrations of α-terpineol led to a progressive decrease in the number of invading cells in both low- and high-dose groups, suggesting a concentration-dependent reduction in the invasive capacity of A375 and MV3 cells. No significant difference was observed in the inhibition of cell invasion between high-concentration α-terpineol and Vemurafenib (P > 0.05) (Fig. 2B).

### The effects of α-terpineol on cell cycle and apoptosis in melanoma A375 and MV3 cells
  Quantitative analysis of EB-stained cells (red fluorescence) demonstrated a concentration-dependent increase in apoptotic A375 and MV3 cells following 48-hour α-terpineol treatment, confirming its pro-apoptotic effect on melanoma cells. Both high-concentration α-terpineol and Vemurafenib demonstrated a strong ability to promote cell apoptosis (Fig. 3A). Cell cycle profiling revealed significantly elevated G1 phase populations in both cell lines after α-terpineol exposure compared to DMSO controls, with the percentage of G1-arrested cells showing progressive enhancement at higher concentrations, indicating G1 phase blockade as the primary cell cycle arrest mechanism. Vemurafenib also induced G1 phase arrest in the cells (Fig. 3B). Flow cytometric analysis showed substantial increases in both late-stage and total apoptosis rates, with the apoptotic induction exhibiting clear dose-response relationships in both cell lines. No significant difference was observed in the promotion of apoptosis between high-concentration α-terpineol and Vemurafenib (Fig. 3C). Western blot analysis revealed that α-terpineol treatment modulated apoptotic pathways by upregulating cleaved caspase-3, cleaved caspase-8, and Bax, while downregulating Bcl-2, in a concentration-dependent manner. Normalized protein loading was confirmed by consistent GAPDH band intensities across all experimental groups, ensuring data reliability (Fig. 3D).

### Target screening profile of α-terpineol
  The planar and three-dimensional chemical structures of α-terpineol were successfully retrieved from PubChem, a comprehensive database of bioactive small molecules. These structural data were subsequently submitted to both PharmMapper and Swiss Target Prediction databases for systematic target screening, yielding 154 potential pharmacological targets. Concurrently, melanoma-associated genes were extracted from three authoritative databases (GeneCards, TTD, and OMIM), comprising a total of 9,924 genes. To identify molecular targets potentially relevant to α-terpineol’s anti-melanoma activity, an intersection analysis was performed between the predicted drug targets and melanoma-related genes. This integrative approach ultimately identified 97 putative molecular targets (Fig. 4).

### Systems-level pathway mapping of key genes using KEGG database
  To elucidate the therapeutic relevance and signaling mechanisms of these targets, we systematically mapped the identified targets to disease treatment categories and canonical pathways in the KEGG database. Bioinformatics analysis revealed that the core target genes are extensively involved in multiple crucial signaling networks, including the JAK2/STAT3, cAMP, MAPK, AMPK, and RAP1 signaling pathways (Fig. 5). These pathways collectively oversee essential cellular functions, including growth, proliferation, apoptosis, and metabolic regulation. Notably, the JAK2/STAT3 pathway demonstrated the most significant enrichment among all identified pathways, suggesting its potential central role in the therapeutic mechanism.

### Molecular docking analysis of α-terpineol with key target proteins and subsequent expression modulation
  “At the initial stage of the study, the predicted three-dimensional structures of the key proteins JAK2 and STAT3 in the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway were retrieved from the AlphaFold database (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/). The corresponding model identifiers used were JAK2 (AF-060674-F1-v6) and STAT3 (AF-P40763-F1-v6).” Molecular docking simulations using MOE software were conducted to explore the binding interactions between α-terpineol and these proteins, with results depicted in the figures. Detailed analysis of the docking conformations revealed that α-terpineol primarily interacts with Thr526 and Thr527 residues on the STAT3 protein while also exhibiting significant binding affinity toward Asn678 on the JAK2 protein (Fig. 6A).

  Further computational analysis quantified the binding free energies between α-terpineol and these target proteins using MOE, where binding free energy serves as a critical thermodynamic parameter for evaluating ligand-protein interaction strength, with more negative values indicating stronger binding affinity. Specifically, binding free energy values below − 5 kcal/mol typically suggest stable molecular interactions. The calculated binding free energies of -5.44 kcal/mol (α-terpineol-STAT3) and − 5.19 kcal/mol (α-terpineol-JAK2), both exceeding this stability threshold, confirmed the formation of stable complexes with substantial binding potential.

  Considering the established role of JAK2/STAT3 signaling in immune regulation and tumorigenesis31, validating α-terpineol’s inhibitory effects on this pathway was essential. Western blot analysis revealed that α-terpineol treatment, regardless of concentration, did not affect total JAK2 and STAT3 protein levels compared to DMSO controls. However, it significantly reduced the phosphorylated forms (p-JAK2 and p-STAT3) in a concentration-dependent manner, decreasing the p-JAK2/JAK2 and p-STAT3/STAT3 ratios. This suggests that α-terpineol may induce apoptosis in A375 and MV3 cells by selectively inhibiting JAK2/STAT3 pathway activation (Fig. 6B).

### α-terpineol inhibits melanoma growth in vivo
  To investigate the antitumor effects of α-terpineol on the in vivo growth of melanoma B16-F10 cells, mice were administered 200 mg/kg α-terpineol via oral gavage for 10 treatments. Afterward, tumors were removed, and the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, and gastrointestinal tract were gathered for additional examination (Fig. 7A). Compared with the control group, the administration group exhibited slower tumor growth by day 10 of treatment. Ultimately, the average tumor dimensions in the control group measured 1.33 cm in length and 1.03 cm in width, with a volume of approximately 711.70 mm³, while those in the treatment group measured 0.98 cm in length and 0.89 cm in width, with a volume of about 410.99 mm³. These results indicate that α-terpineol treatment significantly reduced both tumor weight and volume relative to the control group (Fig. 7B, D and E). Interestingly, despite drug administration, no significant changes in body weight were observed in nude mice (Fig. 7C). Immunohistochemical analysis showed decreased Ki67 expression in tumor tissues treated with α-terpineol, suggesting reduced tumor cell proliferation. Additionally, the treatment group exhibited a significant reduction in positive staining for p-JAK2 and p-STAT3 (Fig. 7F). TUNEL staining indicated higher apoptosis rates in the treatment group compared to the controls (Fig. 7G). These findings demonstrate that α-terpineol significantly inhibits melanoma growth in vivo and suppresses the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway. Systemic toxicity was assessed by performing hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining on major organs of treated mice, such as the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, and gastrointestinal tract. The results showed no observable inflammatory cell infiltration or fibrotic formation in these tissues (Fig. 7H). Collectively, these data indicate that α-terpineol does not exhibit significant toxicity in vivo.

## Discussion
  Melanoma, a malignant tumor arising from aberrant proliferation of melanocytes, primarily develops in the skin, acral regions, and mucous membranes, though other sites may also be affected. This aggressive cancer, known for its high metastatic potential and rapid progression, is a leading cause of death among skin cancer patients. While surgical resection remains the first-line treatment for early-stage localized melanoma, the management of advanced metastatic melanoma continues to pose significant clinical challenges. Current therapeutic strategies predominantly involve targeted therapy, immunotherapy, and chemoradiotherapy; however, these approaches are often limited by substantial toxicity and the frequent development of drug resistance. Due to these limitations, it is crucial to investigate new therapeutic approaches that offer enhanced safety and effectiveness. Natural compounds have emerged as pivotal resources for drug discovery32, and the identification of bioactive natural products with antitumor properties constitutes a critical pathway for developing modern anticancer agents33. Although previous studies have shown α-terpineol’s antitumor effects in various cancer cell lines, its role and regulatory mechanisms in melanoma are not well understood. In this study, we employed a network pharmacology-based approach to systematically elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying α-terpineol’s therapeutic effects against melanoma. Our integrated in vitro and in vivo studies confirmed that α-terpineol suppresses melanoma cell proliferation and induces apoptosis by inhibiting the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway.

  Traditional Chinese herbal medicine has served as a valuable resource for modern drug discovery, providing numerous bioactive compounds with therapeutic potential. α-Terpineol, a promising monoterpenoid, exhibits diverse biological activities and has been widely utilized in the flavor, fragrance, and pharmaceutical industries. Extensive studies have documented its anti-inflammatory34, antibacterial35,36, antifungal37, antioxidant38, antiviral29, and analgesic39 properties. Notably, emerging evidence highlights its significant antitumor effects. In a study by Wen-Wan Chao et al. According to40, α-terpineol increased cellular glutathione levels in a dose-dependent manner, mitigating α-MSH-induced oxidative stress in melanoma B16 cells. Additionally, it suppressed melanin production by inhibiting tyrosinase activity, ultimately impeding B16 cell proliferation. Similarly, α-terpineol demonstrates broad-spectrum antitumor activity against other cancer cell lines, including cervical, lung, breast, colorectal cancers, and leukemia41. Motivated by these promising findings, we designed this study to investigate α-terpineol’s antiproliferative effects on melanoma cells and elucidate its underlying mechanisms. Treatment of human melanoma A375 and MV3 cells with different concentrations of α-terpineol decreased cell viability, demonstrating its strong inhibitory effect. Further functional assays—scratch wound healing, Transwell migration/invasion, and colony formation—revealed that α-terpineol concentration-dependently suppressed migration, invasion, and clonogenic capacity in both cell lines. The eukaryotic cell cycle includes five phases: G0, G1, S, G2, and M. Human cells spend approximately 90% of their time in the initial three phases, known collectively as interphase. Cell cycle arrest is a validated strategy for eliminating cancer cells42,43. Our data indicate that α-terpineol inhibits proliferation by inducing G1-phase arrest in A375 and MV3 cells in a dose-dependent manner. Apoptosis, a regulated programmed cell death process, involves specific morphological changes and the activation of caspase- and mitochondria-dependent pathways. A 48-hour α-terpineol treatment significantly increased late and total apoptosis rates in A375 and MV3 cells in a dose-dependent manner.

  To clarify the signaling pathways associated with α-terpineol’s effects on melanoma growth inhibition and cell death, data on 97 primary protein targets of α-terpineol were submitted to the KEGG database. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway as a potential therapeutic target. Numerous studies have shown that the JAK2/STAT3 pathway is abnormally activated in various cancers, facilitating tumor growth, metastasis, drug resistance, and immune evasion44–46. The JAK2/STAT3 pathway, recognized for its oncogenic role, is a validated molecular target for cancer therapy. The JAK2/STAT3 pathway is crucial in melanoma pathogenesis, aligning with previous findings. Xiu-Qiong Fu et al.47 demonstrated that atractylenolide I exhibits anti-melanoma properties by suppressing the JAK2/STAT3 pathway and decreasing the expression levels of p-JAK2 and p-STAT3 proteins. Huijuan Meng et al.48 showed that podocarpusflavone A inhibits JAK2 phosphorylation, leading to suppressed STAT3 activation, which induces cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in melanoma cells. Research indicates that amentoflavone analogues and quercetin exhibit anti-melanoma properties by targeting the JAK2/STAT3 pathway49,50. These findings indicate that the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway represents a potential therapeutic target for melanoma. Experimental studies demonstrated that α-terpineol is associated with reduced phosphorylation levels of both JAK2 and STAT3. In vivo experiments validated that α-terpineol significantly inhibits melanoma growth by downregulating p-JAK2 and p-STAT3 expression, thereby reducing tumor cell proliferation and inducing apoptosis, supporting our in vitro results.


## Conclusions
  In conclusion, this study shows that α-terpineol, a natural compound from essential oils, effectively suppresses melanoma cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, while inducing apoptosis. The underlying mechanism appears to be associated with modulation of the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway, providing experimental evidence supporting the potential development of α-terpineol as a novel herbal therapeutic agent against melanoma. However, several limitations should be acknowledged in the current study. Despite the compelling evidence from molecular docking and the dose-dependent suppression of JAK2 and STAT3 phosphorylation, this study has a limitation that should be considered. The causal relationship between JAK2/STAT3 inhibition and the biological effects of α-terpineol was not fully validated through functional rescue experiments, such as using JAK2 agonists (e.g., IL-6) or specific inhibitors. Therefore, while our data strongly suggest the involvement of the JAK2/STAT3 pathway, the precise mechanistic link warrants further investigation using genetic or pharmacological approaches in future studies. It is uncertain if other signaling pathways, aside from JAK2/STAT3, play a role in α-terpineol’s suppression of melanoma cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, or if these pathways interact with JAK2/STAT3. Future investigations will focus on addressing these two critical aspects.
