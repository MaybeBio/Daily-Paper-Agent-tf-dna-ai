# SCHEMBL4796824: a multifaceted antitumor agent targeting microtubule dynamics, DNA damage, and Wnt/β-catenin signaling in ovarian cancer cells


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13048-025-01951-5.


## Introduction
  Ovarian cancer accounted for 32,600 deaths among gynecological cancers in 2022, primarily due to the absence of clinical symptoms and undetected early stages [1]. Multidrug resistance (MDR) to chemotherapy further complicates treatment outcomes and disease control in ovarian cancer [2].

  Microtubule-targeting agents (MTAs) bind to tubulin, modulating microtubule dynamics [3] by stabilizing or destabilizing them, disrupting the balance between tubulin monomers and polymers. By perturbing the dynamic stability of spindle microtubules, MTAs arrest cell division and inhibit proliferation. Colchicine binding site inhibitors (CBSIs) destabilize microtubules, leading to cell cycle arrest, reduced motility, hindered protein assembly, and strong anti-angiogenic effects [4]. MTAs binding to vinca and taxane sites are administered intravenously due to poor solubility, while CBSIs, with better solubility, can be administered orally. Long-term MTA treatment may lead to drug resistance due to transporter protein overexpression, but CBSIs are less susceptible to MDR [4, 5].

  Recent research has shown that selenylated imidazo[1,2-a]pyridines inhibit breast cancer cell proliferation by inducing the DNA damage response (DDR). These compounds also elaborates on other anticancer properties, such as inhibiting nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase (NAMPT), cell-cycle-dependent protein kinases, and insulin-like growth factor-I receptor tyrosine kinase [6–10]. An imidazopyridine analogue, IAZP, can induce DDR by inhibiting DNA repair. DDR activation occurs through ATM kinase in response to DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) and ATR kinase in response to DNA single-strand breaks (SSBs). ATM and ATR are checkpoint kinases that sense DNA damage and initiate cis-phosphorylation of histone H2A.X at serine 139, forming phosphorylated histone H2A.X (γH2A.X). This activation leads to Chk2 phosphorylation by ATM, activating the p53 pathway and leading to p21 (CDKN1A) expression and cell cycle arrest. ATR phosphorylates Chk1, which regulates the cell cycle through mechanisms like inhibiting Wee1 kinase [11]. DDR signaling induces cellular senescence, characterized by DNA damage accumulation and stem cell function decline, reducing tissue regenerative potential, leading to dysfunction, and blocking tumorigenesis [12–14]. Telomere capping protects chromosome ends from DDR, while telomere shortening may involve DDR-related signaling pathways, specifically ATM and ATR kinase cascades. Therefore, cellular senescence is regarded as a persistent DDR state [15].

  The combination of MTAs and DNA damaging agents (DDAs) is a first-line therapy for various cancers, including breast, ovarian, lung, head and neck, and lymphomas, augmenting tumor cell killing and therapeutic efficacy [16]. Combining MTA with DDA synergistically targets cancer cells through microtubule depolymerization and DNA damage, potentially enhance both targeting and efficacy, paving the way for a promising avenue in the development of novel antitumor drugs [17]. SCHEMBL4796824 [18], a derivative of BPR0L075 with a 5,6-fused bicyclic heteroaromatic structure, exhibits anti-cancer activity with IC50 values of 770 nM and 730 nM against cervical and gastric cancer cells, respectively [19]. SCHEMBL4796824 was rationally designed as a structural hybrid, incorporating an imidazo[1,2-a]pyridine moiety into the BPR0L075 scaffold. This design was predicated on reports that the imidazopyridine structure is implicated in inducing DDR [20, 21], and potentially inhibiting Wnt/β-catenin signaling, offering the possibility of a single agent with multi-mechanistic capabilities beyond microtubule destabilization. The anti-tumor mechanism of SCHEMBL4796824 remains unclear, but targeting microtubules and DNA damage and oncogenic signaling pathways provides a potential new therapy for ovarian cancer.

### DNA damage of SCHEMBL4796824 on ovarian cancer SK-OV-3 and A2780 cells
  Microtubule dysfunction indirectly compromises DNA stability and integrity by affecting cell division, cycle regulation, apoptosis, stress response, and material transport, elevating DNA damage risk. Nine DNA repair proteins associated with pterins and microtubules are disrupted by MTAs, prolonging DNA damage. Some of these proteins are known to interact with microtubules or their trafficking [22–24]. A critical cellular defense against DNA damage is cell cycle arrest, triggered by rapid activation of signaling pathways that sense DNA insult, engage checkpoints to halt cell progression, and inhibit mitosis of damaged cells, allowing time for repair.

### Cellular senescence of SCHEMBL4796824 on ovarian cancer SK-OV-3 and A2780 cells
  The unmitigated accumulation of DNA damage triggers a sequence of biological responses, ultimately leading to cellular aging, a process marked by hallmarks such as cellular senescence [12]. Senescent cells, as a result of this irreversible growth arrest, exhibit a distinctive phenotype marked by cellular hypertrophy, upregulated senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) expression, and metabolic alterations [25]. This arrest in cell proliferation leads to the accumulation of biosynthetic intermediates and products, enlarging and flattening the cells, thereby increasing their projected surface area [26]. As shown in Fig. 6A, the unchecked DNA damage in cells manifests as morphological changes under the microscope, indicative of cellular senescence through pronounced enlargement and flattening, corroborating the characteristic hallmarks of senescence. Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 6A-B, the presence of senescence in ovarian cancer SK-OV-3 cells treated with SCHEMBL4796824 was confirmed by SA-β-gal staining. The notable increase in the proportion of positively stained cells underscores the potent ability of SCHEMBL4796824 to induce cellular senescence in SK-OV-3 cells. As shown in Fig. 6C, Real-time qPCR analysis showed upregulated IL-6, TNF-α, and CXCL2 in SCHEMBL4796824-treated SK-OV-3 and A2780 cells. Conversely, LAMIN B1 and HMGB1 were downregulated, suggesting nuclear structural alterations associated with senescence. ### Inhibition of Wnt/β-catenin pathway by SCHEMBL4796824 on ovarian cancer SK-OV-3 and A2780 cells
  Enhanced DNA damage response induced cell cycle arrest, senescence markers, and altered Wnt/β-catenin pathway components. Reduced β-catenin expression correlates with increased γ-H2A.X levels, indicating increased DNA damage and repair impairment [27]. β-catenin depletion disrupts hematopoiesis, enhances reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and hinders DNA repair [28]. Thus, the Wnt/β-catenin pathway is a crucial regulator linking DNA damage, cellular senescence, and aging processes.

### Inhibition of SK-OV-3 Xenograft Growth by SCHEMBL4796824 in nude mice
  To assess the in vivo antitumor efficacy of SCHEMBL4796824 in a SK-OV-3 ovarian cancer xenograft mouse model, a daily dose of 100 mg/kg was administered, guided by preclinical data of its lead compound BPR0L075 [19] and its in vitro antitumor performance. A vehicle control group, which received only the solvent without the active compound, and a vincristine-treated group were served as comparisons. As shown in Fig. 8 A-B, in a BALB/c nude mouse model of SK-OV-3 ovarian cancer xenografts, daily administration of 100 mg/kg SCHEMBL4796824 significantly inhibited tumor growth after 16 days (p < 0.05), as compared to vehicle control and vincristine-treated groups. As shown in Fig. 8 C, both SCHEMBL4796824 and vincristine significantly decreased final tumor volumes and weights (p < 0.01), with vincristine exhibiting slightly higher efficacy. As shown in Fig. 8 D, body weight and survival rates indicated no significant differences between SCHEMBL4796824, vincristine, and control groups, indicating SCHEMBL4796824's low systemic toxicity and favorable safety profile. ## Discussion
  This study positions SCHEMBL4796824 as a multi-mechanistic agent whose anti-tumor effects extend beyond simple microtubule destabilization. While it shares this property with its parent compound BPR0L075 and other colchicine-site binders, its novelty stems from its concurrent and functionally interconnected targeting of the DDR and the Wnt/β-catenin/c-MYC axis. The reversal of SCHEMBL4796824-induced senescence and DNA damage by the β-catenin agonist SKL2001 (Fig. 7C, D) provides compelling functional evidence that the observed anti-tumor effects are mechanistically linked to the inhibition of this pivotal signaling pathway.

  A pivotal finding of our work is the differential cellular response observed between SK-OV-3 (c-MYC amplified) and A2780 (non-amplified) cells. The heightened sensitivity of SK-OV-3 cells to SCHEMBL4796824, manifested as more substantial suppression of β-catenin/c-MYC and a stronger senescence response, suggests that the compound's efficacy may be particularly relevant in tumors with hyperactive Wnt/β-catenin signaling and c-MYC amplification. This cell line-specific effect unveils a potential biomarker for patient stratification and highlights a novel context for the application of this agent. The differential effects on EMT markers and DNA repair protein dynamics further underscore how the genetic background of a tumor can shape its response to this multi-targeted therapy.

  β-catenin plays a pivotal role in modulating microtubule dynamics by enhancing centrosomal microtubule regeneration upon phosphorylation [29]. MTAs like vincristine, paclitaxel, and C12 suppress Wnt/β-catenin signaling in oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), with C12 effectively inhibiting OSCC growth and Wnt/β-catenin signaling in murine models [30]. Imidazo[1,2-a]pyridine compounds selectively inhibit Wnt/β-catenin in vitro, reducing β-catenin levels and suppressing proliferation of β-catenin-mutated cancer cells [21]. While the established MTA vincristine showed greater potency in standard cytotoxicity assays, SCHEMBL4796824 presents a distinct mechanistic profile. Its ability to simultaneously disrupt microtubule integrity, inflict DNA damage, and suppress the Wnt/β-catenin/c-MYC oncogenic network may translate into efficacy against tumors that rely on this pathway for survival or are resistant to conventional MTAs. The induction of senescence, a durable form of cell cycle arrest, in a c-MYC-driven context is a particularly promising outcome, as it represents a alternative approach to eliminating tumor cells.


## Conclusion
  This study reveals SCHEMBL4796824, a BPR0L075 derivative, as a potential antitumor agent in ovarian cancer cells. It exerts anticancer effects via DNA damage induction, cell cycle arrest, and cellular senescence. By targeting tubulin's colchicine binding site, SCHEMBL4796824 destabilizes microtubules, causing G2 phase arrest and inhibiting cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. Additionally, it functionally inhibits the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway and its key effector c-MYC, leading to a senescence program that is particularly effective in c-MYC amplified cells. These results indicate SCHEMBL4796824 as a promising therapeutic for ovarian cancer, providing a multifaceted treatment strategy whose efficacy may be modulated by the tumor's oncogenic signaling landscape.


## Material and methods

### Cell culture
  SK-OV-3 and A2780 ovarian cancer cells (Shanghai Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences) were cultured in RPMI medium (Gibco, 61,870,036) supplemented with 10% FBS (Jiangsu Yikesai, FSP500) and 100X pen-strep (Sangon Biotech, E607011). Cells were maintained at 37℃, 5% CO2 in a humidified Cytomat 5 C incubator (ThermoFisher).


### CCK-8 assay
  After cell adherence, add SCHEMBL4796824 to triplicate wells (2 × 103 cells/well) in 96-well plates across a predetermined concentration range. Incubate for 72 h. Before assessing viability, add 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent (Beyotime Biotechnology, C0043) to each well in the dark and incubate for 2 h. Measure OD at 450 nm to estimate cell viability.


### Colony-formation assay
  After 48 h of adherence, 1000 cells were exposed to SCHEMBL4796824 at concentrations ranging from 0.5 to 2 µM for 48 h. After drug treatment, cells were washed to remove residual compound and refreshed with new medium every 3 days. Colony growth was monitored microscopically for 10 days until monoclones exceeding 50 cells. Colonies were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Shanghai Nova Medical & Technology Co., Ltd., 082755) and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Beyotime Biotechnology, C0121), followed by washing to remove excess stain.


### Transwell assay
  A 1:8 dilution of serum-free medium and Matrigel (ABW, 082724) was coated onto the bottom of 24-well Transwell® inserts and incubated at 37℃ for 1 h. After aspirating excess liquid, the inserts were further incubated with serum-free medium at 37℃ for 30 min. Successful coating was confirmed by absence of leakage. After cell adherence, various concentrations of the test compound were added. After 48 h, cells were trypsinized, centrifuged, and resuspended in fresh medium for plating in Transwell® chambers (8.0 μm pore size) with complete medium in the lower chamber and serum-free medium containing cells in the upper chamber. After 24 h, cells were fixed with 4% PFA, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and non-migrated cells were removed.


### Cell cycle analysis
  Cells at logarithmic growth were plated at 1 × 105 cells/well in 6-well plates and maintained at 37℃ with 5% CO2. After adherence, SCHEMBL4796824 was added at varying concentrations. After 48 h, cells were harvested, centrifuged, and rinsed with PBS. They were then fixed in 70% ethanol at 4℃ overnight. Prior to analysis, cells were incubated with RNase (50 μg/mL) and PI (65 μg/mL in 0.1% Triton X-100/PBS) on ice in the dark for 60 min. After filtration, 5 × 104 cells were analyzed by flow cytometry (cytoFlex S, Beckman Coulter) using PI (Beyotime Biotech, ST512), and cell cycle distribution was determined with FlowJo software.


### Immunofluorescence assay
  SCHEMBL4796824 was applied at varying concentrations to 24-well plates containing 5 × 102 cells/well. After 48 h, cells were fixed with 4% PFA, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS, and blocked with 5% BSA in PBS. Cells were then incubated overnight at 4℃ with the primary antibody in a humidified chamber. Following washes, cells were stained with an FITC-conjugated secondary antibody for 2 h at RT and nuclei with DAPI (Solarbio, C0065). After removing excess stain, anti-fade medium was applied. Images were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Olympus IX83-FV3000) with FV31S-SW software. Anti-fade mounting medium (S2100) and 5% BSA blocking solution (SW3015) were sourced from Solarbio, Beijing, China.


### β-galactosidase staining
  Varying concentrations of SCHEMBL4796824 were applied to 6-well plates with 8 × 104 cells/well for 48 h. Post-treatment, cells were rinsed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, and rinsed again with PBS. Cells were then stained with crystal violet staining solution for 20 min, rinsed with water, and allowed to air-dry.


### Real-time qPCR

### Animals
  Female Balb/c nude mice (4–5 weeks old) from Changzhou Kavinsi Experimental Animal Co., Ltd. (Qualification No.: NO. 20230906Abzz0619999832) were housed in an SPF facility approved by the Ethics Committee (Approval No.: TZXY-2024–20241127). Mice were maintained under controlled conditions (20–25 °C, 40–60% humidity) with sterile food and water. After a week of acclimation, a tumor-bearing model was established by subcutaneous injection of 5 × 106 SK-OV-3 cells/mouse (resuspended in a 1:1 PBS:matrix gel mixture) into the right axillary region. Post-inoculation, mice were monitored for body weight and tumor size, with drug treatment initiated when tumors reached ~ 100 mm3 and continued daily for 2 weeks.


### Statistical analysis
