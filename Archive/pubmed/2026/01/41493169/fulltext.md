# Identification of novel HIF2α inhibitors: a structure‐based virtual screening approach


## Abstract
  HIF2α is aberrantly upregulated in some renal cell carcinomas due to VHL mutations, supporting HIF2α inhibition as a compelling therapeutic approach for such cases. Therefore, the six compounds (designated as Compounds 1–6) were screened from the Maybridge database based on the constructed pharmacophore model and molecular docking. Subsequently, the docking models of Compounds 1–6 with HIF2α were analysed. Affinity assays revealed that both Compound-4 and Compound-5 exhibited robust affinity towards human recombinant HIF2α. MD simulations displayed that Compound-4 and Compound-5 stably bound to the active pocket of HIF2α. Cell experiments demonstrated that Compound-4 effectively inhibited the growth of the 786-O human renal cell carcinomas line (IC50 = 1.35 ± 0.06 μM). This study demonstrates that Compound-4 may serve as a potential candidate compound for renal cell carcinomas therapy.


## Introduction
  Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is among the three major malignant tumours of the urinary system and stands as the 14th most common malignant tumour worldwide, contributing substantially to global cancer-related morbidity and mortality1. In 2020, an estimated 431,288 new cases of renal cancer were diagnosed globally, with approximately 179,368 associated deaths reported2. RCC is the predominant pathological subtype of renal cancer, accounting for over 90% of all malignant renal tumours3. Surgical resection, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapies collectively constitute the foundational pillars of oncological management, representing the core modalities in contemporary cancer treatment4,5. Despite advances, tumour complexity, treatment toxicities, recurrence, and drug resistance remain major challenges in oncology6,7. Therefore, the identification of novel alternative therapeutic strategies for RCC remains of paramount importance.

  The oxygen homeostasis is regulated by HIF family members in biological systems8. Under normoxic conditions, HIFs undergoes proteasomal degradation. Whereas in cancer-induced hypoxic environments, HIFs becomes stabilised and activates the transcription of target gene like erythropoietin (EPO) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)9. HIFs are classified within the basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) transcription factor superfamily. All HIFs exist as heterodimers, each composed of an α subunit and the β subunit (789 amino acids (aa)), known as aryl hydrocarbon receptor nuclear translocator (ARNT, HIF1β)10. The α subunit functions as the oxygen-responsive core including HIF1α (826 aa), HIF2α (870 aa), and HIF3α (669 aa)11. Owing to limited studies on HIF3α, this work emphasises the roles of HIF1α and HIF2α encoded by endothelial PAS (Per-Arnt-Sim) domain-containing protein 1 (EPAS1)12. In contrast to HIF1α, HIF2α contains a larger hydrophobic cavity in its PAS-B domain13. Numerous studies report HIF2α upregulation in malignant cells, including clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) and breast cancer, where it correlates with dismal clinical outcomes and shortened patient survival11,14,15. Consequently, our research centres on the development of HIF2α inhibitors.

  Under normoxic conditions, the proline residues Pro405 and Pro531 located at the N-terminal region of the PAS-B domain in HIF2α are enzymatically hydroxylated by prolyl hydroxylase domain (PHD) enzymes using iron ions as cofactors16. Hydroxylated HIF2α functions as a direct substrate for von Hippel-Lindau protein (pVHL), engaging its β-domain residues Trp88, Trp98, His115, and Ser111 through specific binding interactions17. The pVHL recruits the Elongin B/Elongin C (EloB/C) heterodimer, which binds to the N-terminal domain of Cullin 2 (CUL2), while CUL2 serves as a molecular bridge linking its C-terminal domain to RING-box protein 1 (RBX1)18. The RING domain of RBX1 directly interacts with E2 ubiquitin conjugating enzymes, thereby catalysing the ubiquitin-proteasome degradation pathway through its E3 ligase activity19. In summary, HIF2α is degraded under normoxic conditions. However, the majority of RCC cases are driven by von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) mutations, which abrogate the ubiquitin-proteasome-mediated degradation of HIF2α protein20,21. Consequently, HIF2α inhibitors directly block the dimerisation of HIF2α with the ARNT, thereby reactivating VHL-mediated ubiquitin-proteasomal turnover of HIF2α.

  Currently, multiple candidate HIF2α inhibitors have entered clinical development stages. Casdatifan selectively inhibits HIF2α, effectively suppresses xenografted ccRCC tumour growth in vivo, and has entered Phase I clinical trials for ccRCC treatment22. PT2399 directly binds to the PAS-B domain of HIF-2α, abrogating its dimerisation with ARNT, thereby inhibiting HIF2α target gene expression in VHL-deficient ccRCC cells in vitro and suppressing tumour growth in mouse xenograft models23. As an optimised derivative of PT2399, PT2385, a small-molecule HIF2α inhibitor and the first drug to enter clinical trials, served as the control in this study24. The development of HIF2α inhibitors is primarily constrained by off-target effects, and low efficacy25. Therefore, it is necessary to find HIF2α inhibitors with high affinity for cancer treatment.

  CADD (computer-aided drug design) integrates computational methods to accelerate compound discovery, design, and optimisation across all drug development phases26. Unlike traditional laboratory-based high-throughput screening (HTS), CADD demonstrates rapid speed, excellent efficiency, and cost-effectiveness27. In recent years, we have identified a series of new, highly selective and efficient compounds for cancer treatment through pharmacophore modelling, virtual screening, molecular docking, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, in vitro and in vivo evaluation28–33. In this study, we identified a series of new and potent HIF2α inhibitors (termed as Compounds 1–6) through a virtual screening workflow. Among Compounds 1–6, Compound-4 showed the best performance in vitro evaluation.


## Materials and methods

### General
  The PT2385 (CAS number: 1672665-49-4) was purchased from MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). Compounds 1–6 were obtained from WuXi AppTec (Shanghai, China). The human recombinant HIF2α was purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, MA, USA). Human renal carcinoma cell line 786-O (ATCC CRL-1932) was purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) was supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 units/mL of penicillin, and 100 µg/mL of streptomycin. The FBS, penicillin, streptomycin, and DMEM were purchased from Gibco (Grand Island, New York, USA). The cells were cultured in an incubator humidified with 5% CO2 and 95% air at 37 °C.


### Construction of pharmacophore model for HIF2α inhibitors
  The crystal structure of PT2385 in complex with the HIF2α PAS-B domain (HIF2α-B*) and the ARNT PAS-B domain (ARNT-B*) (PDB ID: 5TBM), with a resolution of 1.85 Å, was downloaded from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) database and analysed using the Molecular Operating Environment (MOE, Chemical Computing Group Inc, Montreal, Quebec, Canada) software34. Using the Amber10:EHT force field, the complex was preprocessed with the QuickPrep tool, including processes of hydrogen addition, charge calculation, water molecules removal, and energy minimisation. Under the System option, protein refinement was performed to obtain simplified protein-ligand interaction relationships. The HIF2α-B* was rendered grey and PT2385 was shown in yellow. The key amino acid residues in the active site of HIF2α-B* were coloured green. The docking active site of the complex between HIF2α-B* and PT2385 was visualised by clicking Site View. The Ligand Interactions tool was used to analyse interaction relationships. Based on interaction analysis, pharmacophore features of HIF2α-B* were constructed using the pharmacophore editor. These features primarily included hydrogen bond donors, hydrogen bond acceptors, aromatic centres, and hydrophobic centres, which together formed the pharmacophore model of HIF2α-B*. This model was applied in subsequent screening processes for potential active compounds targeting HIF2α.


### Virtual screening
  To identify effective HIF2α inhibitors, the database comprising 53,358 compounds was screened. The database employed the Minimise algorithm to facilitate the conversion of compound structures from two-dimensional (2D) to three-dimensional (3D) representations, thereby incorporating spatial characteristics into the dataset. The HIF2α-B* pharmacophore model, previously established, served as a molecular sieve for the initial screening of the compounds. The screened compounds were then docked into the active site of HIF2α-B* for further screening. The Placement process was implemented using the Triangle Matcher method to achieve the initial docking of the ligand with the receptor, and the docking results were evaluated using the London dG function. Each screened compound had 30 conformations. The Refinement process was carried out using the Rigid Receptor method to achieve further docking of the ligand with the receptor, and the docking results were evaluated using the GBVI/WSA dG function. Each screened compound had five best conformations. Based on the docking scores, the top six compounds were selected for in vitro evaluation experiments. Through a combination of computational docking, 50 ns MD simulations, and cytotoxicity assays, Compound-4 was selected as a promising lead compound for oncology development.


### MST assay
  The microscale thermophoresis (MST) experiment was performed to determine the affinity between ligands and proteins, expressed as the Kd value35. The experiment was conducted using a Monolith NT.115 instrument (NanoTemper Technologies). The human recombinant HIF2α was labelled using the MonolithTM RED-NHS second-generation protein labelling kit. A preliminary experiment was carried out to evaluate the labelling efficiency of HIF2α. Compounds 1–6 and PT2385 were solubilised in 1 × PBST (Phosphate-Buffered Saline with Tween) buffer supplemented with 0.05% Tween 20. The ligands were thoroughly mixed with fluorescently labelled HIF2α. And the ligands at half-log dilution concentrations and incubated in the dark for 5 min. Samples were loaded into Monolith NT.115 Capillaries. MST data were collected for the samples. The data were analysed using MO Affinity Analysis software with a 1:1 binding model. Finally, the Kd values for the samples were calculated.


### MD simulations
  The optimal binding modes of PT2385 and Compounds 1–6 to the active site of HIF2α-B* were obtained during the molecular docking. The complexes were imported into MOE, where ligand and protein moieties were isolated and exported as separate PDB files. HIF2α-B* was preprocessed using the Amber: 10EHT force field in the QuickPrep module of MOE. The topology of HIF2α-B* was generated within the AMBER99SB-ILDN force field. The PT2385 and Compounds 1–6 were also preprocessed using the MMFF94 force field. The topology files for PT2385 and compounds 1–6 were added to the file of HIF2α-B* to obtain the topology files for the complexes. The complex systems underwent the 50 ns MD simulations in a cubic with an edge length of 1.0 nm. The solvent molecules and counterions were added to the complex systems to balance the system charge and improve the accuracy of MD simulations. An NVT equilibration (300 K) and an NPT equilibration (0.1 bar) were conducted for pre-equilibration of the complex systems. Root-mean square deviation (RMSD), root-mean square fluctuation (RMSF), secondary-structure contents, and radius of gyration (Rg) served as the four primary indicators for evaluating the stability of the complex systems. The trajectory data were recorded every 10 ps.

  The RMSD and Rg values of the HIF2α-Compound-4 and HIF2α-Compound-5 complexes at corresponding time points were integrated. The free energy landscapes were generated via GROMACS, followed by conversion of the landscape data into numerical files using Python and subsequent rendering of the 3D landscape plot with the Matplotlib library.


### MTT assay
  The cell proliferation assay was performed as described in previous research34. Briefly, 1 × 104 786-O cells were seeded into 96-well plates. Twenty-four hours after seeding, PT2385 (positive control) and the test compounds were added to the respective wells. Following 72 h of incubation, thiazolyl blue tetrazolium bromide (MTT, Sigma-Aldrich) was added to a final concentration of 0.5 mg/mL. After an additional 4-h incubation, the cell culture medium was removed by aspiration, and 100 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added to each well. After shaking for 10 min, the optical density (OD value) was measured at a test wavelength of 570 nm and a reference wavelength of 650 nm using a microplate reader (Synergy-HT, BioTek).


### Statistics analysis
  The data processing and graphing were both done with GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software Inc, San Diego, CA, USA) (version 9.5). The One-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was applied for analyses involving multiple groups. The p values lower than 0.05 were deemed to be statistically significant. Each experiment was repeated three times. The data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD).


## Results

### Pharmacophore model of HIF2α inhibitors
  The crystal structure of PT2385 bound to the HIF2α-B*: ARNT-B* complex (PDB ID: 5TBM) was downloaded. The interaction between PT2385 and the active site of HIF2α was analysed using the Ligand Interactions tool. Studies indicated that the amino acid residues Met252 and His293 in the active site of HIF2α were critical for the efficacy of HIF2α inhibitors36. PT2385 was a bis-aromatic ring-containing compound, which was widely studied for its HIF2α inhibitory activity37. Therefore, based on the two benzene ring features of PT2385, pharmacophore features with two aromatic centres (F1:Aro, F2:Aro) were created using the pharmacophore editor. The amino acid residues Phe244, Phe254, Tyr307, Met309, Leu319, Met289, Val302, His293, Asn341, and Met252 in the active pocket of HIF2α formed a hydrophobic cavity. The methyl group attached to the thiophene moiety of PT2385 penetrated deep into the interior of this hydrophobic cavity. Consequently, a pharmacophore feature centred on this hydrophobic core (F3:Hyd) was established. As described above, a pharmacophore model for HIF2α inhibitors (Figure 1) was constructed for subsequent virtual screening processes.


### Virtual screening
  A Maybridge database consisting of 53,358 compounds was employed in the virtual screening process (Figure 2). Based on the constructed pharmacophore model for HIF2α inhibitors, 1,070 compounds were initially screened out, all of which satisfied the three pharmacophore features of HIF2α. These compounds were then docked into the active pocket of HIF2α, and the top six compounds were selected based on docking scores. The chemical structures of compounds 1–6 were presented in Figure 3. Furthermore, interaction analysis of their binding modes with HIF2α was performed using the Ligand Interactions tool in MOE. Two compounds were filtered out through MST assays and MD simulations, based on their binding potency and structural stability with HIF2α. Ultimately, one optimal compound was prioritised via in vitro cellular experiments, qualifying it as a potential HIF2α-targeted inhibitor for the RCC therapy.


### Binding free energy
  PT2385 and Compounds 1–6 were docked into the active site of HIF2α using the Rigid Receptor docking method, with poses ranked by GBVI/WSA dG scores. The calculated binding free energies of PT2385 and Compounds 1–6 were concisely displayed in Figure 4. The values of the binding free energy was an assessment of whether the ligands were a perfect fit to the active pocket of the protein. The smaller the binding free energy, the more stable the binding. PT2385 exhibited a binding free energy of −9.11 ± 0.19 kcal/mol. Compared with PT2385, Compounds 1–6 showed the lower binding free energies and more stable binding to the active site of HIF2α. Among Compounds 1–6, Compound-4 (−12.77 ± 0.64 kcal/mol) and Compound-5 (−12.56 ± 0.49 kcal/mol) exhibited the best performance, with stronger interactions with key amino acid residues in the active pocket of HIF2α.


### Interaction analysis
  The binding modes of Compounds 1–6 with the active site of HIF2α were obtained through molecular docking (Figure 5). The complexes were introduced into the MOE, and their interactions with the HIF2α active site were analysed using the Ligand Interactions tool. In Compound-2 and Compounds 4–6, a hydroxyl group formed hydrogen bonds with the imine nitrogen atom of residue His293 in the HIF2α domain, enhancing complexes stability. All Compounds 1–6 engaged in hydrophobic interactions with the HIF2α active site residues: Phe244, Phe254, Tyr307, Met309, Leu319, Met289, Val302, His293, Asn341, and Met252. Overall, the Compounds 1–6 maintained stable binding to the active site of HIF2α.


### MST analysis
  Compounds 1–6 exhibited high complementarity to the HIF2α PAS-B domain’s active pocket with low binding free energies. Subsequently, MST analysis determined the binding affinities of PT2385 and Compounds 1–6 to human recombinant HIF2α. The lower Kd values inversely correlated with the higher binding affinity. As shown in Table 1, the Kd values of Compounds 1–6 were lower than that of PT2385 (Kd = 17.21 ± 1.19 nM). Within the Compounds 1–6, Compound-4 and Compound-5 emerged as top performers with the highest binding affinity values. Compound-4 showed a Kd value of 5.42 ± 0.13 nM, and Compound-5 exhibited a Kd value of 7.26 ± 0.17 nM. Therefore, Compound-4 and Compound-5 demonstrated the lowest Kd values and the strongest affinity for human recombinant HIF2α. The binding free energy results of Compounds 1–6 were consistent with the affinity test results. Notably, both Compound-4 and Compound-5 achieved the low binding free energies and exhibited the extremely high affinity for the active site of HIF2α.


### MD simulations
  Compound-4 and Compound-5 underwent the 50 ns MD simulations using the GROMACS (GROningen Machine for Chemical Simulation) platform due to their low binding free energies and high affinity for the HIF2α. RMSD was employed to evaluate the structural stability of the complexes. The lower RMSD values indicated stronger conformational stability of ligands at the binding site38. For Compound-4, the RMSD value (Figure 6(A)) remained stable after 10 ns, demonstrating strong conformational stability at the HIF2α binding site. In contrast, the RMSD value of Compound-5 (Figure 6(B)) increased after 20 ns and stabilised near 0.17 nm, indicating unstable binding to the HIF2α active site. RMSF was used to detect amino acid residue flexibility and identified functionally important regions of the protein39. The key amino acids in the HIF2α active site, including Phe244, Phe254, Tyr307, Met309, Leu319, Met289, Val302, His293, Asn341, and Met252, exhibited RMSF values all below 0.13 nm in both HIF2α-Compound-4 (Figure 6(C)) and HIF2α-Compound-5 complexes (Figure 6(D)). The higher RMSF values of the N-terminal and C-terminal residues suggested that the N-terminus and C-terminus of the protein possessed greater flexibility. The changes in the protein’s secondary structure could be used to predict how ligands affected the protein’s structure, function, and stability40. The secondary structure of the HIF2α mainly consisted of Structure, Coil, and β-sheet structures in between the HIF2α-Compound-4 (Figure 6(E)) and HIF2α-Compound-5 complexes (Figure 6(F)). The Rg served as an indicator to evaluate the compactness of HIF2α structure. The smaller Rg value indicated that the HIF2α structure was more compact and stable41. The Rg values of Compound-4 (Figure 6(G)) fluctuated steadily during the 50 ns simulation. Compared with Compound-4, the Rg values of Compound-5 (Figure 6(H)) showed greater fluctuations during the 50 ns simulation. In conclusion, Compound-5 could bind stably to HIF2α.

  The free energies of the HIF2α-Compound-4 and HIF2α-Compound-5 complexes were calculated based on the RMSD and Rg values at corresponding time points. The free energy landscapes from the 50 ns MD simulation are presented in Figure 7. In the figure, the blue region corresponds to the most stable conformational state of the system: lower energy and a more concentrated distribution indicate higher conformational stability. Conversely, the red region represents the highest energy and least stable conformation of the system. Two distinct “energy basins” (blue core regions) were observed for the HIF2α-Compound-4 complex, indicating the formation of a stable conformation following their binding (Figure 7(A)). In contrast to Compound-4, only one energy minimum was detected in the HIF2α-Compound-5 complex, suggesting a moderately stable conformation upon their interaction (Figure 7(B)). Collectively, these data demonstrate that Compound-4 could be a potential potent inhibitor.


### MTT analysis
  In the MTT proliferation inhibition assay using human 786-O renal cancer cells (Table 1), Compound-4 exhibited significantly superior anti-tumour proliferative activity compared to other tested compounds: its IC50 value against 786-O cells was as low as 1.35 ± 0.06 μM, ranking the best among all compounds (Compound 1–6). In contrast, the IC50 of Compound-5 was 3.91 ± 0.25 μM, the IC50 values of Compound 1–3 and Compound-6 were in the range of 4.27–9.42 μM, and the IC50 of the positive control PT2385 was even greater than 10 μM. The above results fully demonstrate that Compound-4 has a potent inhibitory effect on the proliferation of 786-O renal cancer cells and possesses significant advantages in terms of anti-tumour activity.


## Discussion
  The structural features of compounds bear a close relationship to their activities42. Among Compounds 1–6, neither Compound-1 nor Compound-3 was capable of forming hydrogen bonds with the residue His293 located in the active pocket of HIF2α. Consequently, Compound-1 and Compound-3 exhibited high docking scores (docking scores = −11.19 ± 0.41 kcal/mol and −10.67 ± 0.37 kcal/mol respectively) and low binding affinities (Kd = 13.37 ± 0.52 nM; Kd = 15.63 ± 0.78 nM). The hydroxyl group connected to the benzene ring was in conjugation with the benzene ring, making it much more likely to serve as a hydrogen bond donor43. In Compound-2 and Compounds 4–6, the hydroxyl group of Compound-2 weakly interacted with the nitrogen atom of His293 residue in the active pocket of HIF2α via hydrogen bonding. Moreover, the chlorine atom associated with the hydrophobic pocket of the active residues within HIF2α, thereby further strengthening the hydrophobic interactions44. Among Compounds 4–6, the structures of Compound-4 and Compound-5 incorporated the chlorine atoms, enabling stronger binding to the hydrophobic pocket of HIF2α. Compound-4 established hydrogen bond with the residue His293 of HIF2α at a distance of 3.16 Å, while Compound-5 formed hydrogen bond with the same residue at a distance of 2.23 Å. MD simulations indicated that Compound-4 more stably bound to the HIF2α-PAS-B compared to Compound-5. Consequently, among Compounds 1–6, Compound-4 demonstrated the optimal performance during the entire screening process. Specifically, Compound-4 had the docking score of −12.77 ± 0.64 kcal/mol and the Kd value of 5.42 ± 0.13 nM. Compound-4 potently inhibited the growth of human 786-O renal cancer cells with the IC50 value of 1.35 ± 0.06 μM. Moreover, the consistency witnessed among the docking scores, binding affinity estimations, MD simulation results, and cellular experimental results lent significant credence to the conclusions drawn. In summary, we successfully discovered a potent and high-affinity HIF2α inhibitor for cancer therapy through pharmacophore model, virtual screening, MD simulations, and in vitro evaluation.


## Conclusion
  The pharmacophore model of HIF2α was meticulously constructed based on the comprehensive analysis of the intricate interactions between PT2385 and the active pocket of the HIF2α-PAS-B domain. The six compounds, designated as Compounds 1–6, were screened from the Maybridge database based on the constructed pharmacophore model and molecular docking. Subsequently, the docking models of Compounds 1–6 with HIF2α were analysed using the Ligand Interactions module. Affinity assays revealed that both Compound-4 and Compound-5 manifested robust affinity towards human recombinant HIF2α. MD simulations displayed that Compound-4 and Compound-5 stably bound to the active pocket of HIF2α. Cell experiments demonstrated that Compound-4 could inhibit the proliferation of human 786-O renal cancer cells. Consequently, Compound-4 may emerge as a candidate compound targeting HIF2α for cancer treatment.

  Compound-4 demonstrated good inhibitory efficacy against the proliferation of the tumour cell line in vitro. It holds potential for the in vivo evaluation of both its efficacy and safety. In the future, experiments will be conducted to assess the tumour volume inhibition and monitor body weight changes in mice. Additionally, pharmacokinetic (PK) studies will be carried out to evaluate the absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) of Compound-4 in mice. Furthermore, its potential for augmenting therapeutic outcomes in combination with other drugs will be investigated. In summary, we identified a high-affinity HIF2α inhibitor, which represented a candidate compound for RCC therapy.
