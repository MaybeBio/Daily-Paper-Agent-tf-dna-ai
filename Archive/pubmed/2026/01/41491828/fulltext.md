# Intrinsically disordered regions in the yeast transcriptional regulator Ixr1 support prion-like behavior


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33972-x.


## Introduction
  The IXR1 gene of Saccharomyces cerevisiae encodes the protein Ixr1 (67 kDa) that was first characterized by its ability to bind to cisplatin-DNA adducts1. This interaction masks the DNA adduct to the action of the Nucleotide Excision Repair (NER) system, thus conferring cellular resistance to the drug1. The combined activity of Ixr1 and Hmo1 proteins provides efficient correction of both DNA repair and replication errors2. Ixr1 also regulates the expression of RNR1, encoding the catalytic subunit of the ribonucleotide reductase enzyme that synthetizes dNTPs, which are required in DNA synthesis and DNA repair processes3. In response to iron limitation, Ixr1 and Aft1, a transcription factor activating the iron regulon in S. cerevisiae, enhance the expression of RNR1, increasing both mRNA and protein levels4. Ixr1 controls the yeast response to other stress conditions, such as oxidative stress or hypoxia5,6 and binds to Ssn8, other factor involved in the yeast hypoxic response7. Besides, Ixr1 also regulates transcriptional expression of other transcriptional factors that respond to nutrient availability or extracellular and intracellular stress stimuli, some controlled by the TOR pathway and PKA signaling8. It has been proposed that, during periods of nutrient stress and reduced TORC1 signaling, chromatin anchors the HMG proteins Ixr1 and Hmo1 to suppress necrotic cell death9. Ixr1 also regulates yeast growth by controlling ribosome biogenesis8 and cell cycle through the repression of the CLB1 and CBL2 genes, encoding B-type cyclins10, and it is negatively regulated by Puf511. Ixr1 is also involved in the production of volatile metabolites in S. cerevisiae during alcoholic fermentation, which enhances its biotechnological interest for the industry of beverages and fermented products12.

  Ixr1 contains two HMG-box domains that bind to DNA13, and differential binding to each has been proposed as a mechanistic cause of the dual function of Ixr1 in transcriptional regulation and recognition of cisplatin-DNA adducts14. Ixr1 contains several poly-glutamine regions flanking the DNA binding domains, but its functional significance has not been yet studied. Glutamine/asparagine-rich (Q/N-rich) sequences are found in almost all known yeast prions as part of the “prion-forming-region” (PFR) required for prion formation and propagation15–17, with the exception of Mod518. Frequently, priogenic proteins are transcription factors or RNA binding proteins that control gene expression, cell signaling and the response to external stimuli such as stress15–17. It has been recently shown that the terminal location of a PFR strongly increases its propensity to form amyloids19. Considering the role of Ixr1 in the regulation of gene expression in response to stress conditions, such as DNA-damage, iron limitation, oxidative stress or hypoxia, we have analyzed the functional significance of the N-terminal poly-glutamine regions present in Ixr1 in relation to its capacity to form amyloids or to have priogenic characteristics. We show that Ixr1 is an intrinsically disordered protein with high aggregation propensity. Ixr1 aggregation is highly ordered and the protein is able to form amyloids, showing also characteristic of a prion-like protein.


## Results

### Intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) flank the HMGB box domains of Ixr1
  Under normal physiological conditions, some proteins, the so-called Intrinsically Disordered Proteins (IDPs), do not fold into globular conformations or they have Intrinsically Disordered Regions (IDRs). Structural disorder prediction uses methods based on theoretical approximations and physicochemical parameters. Therefore, Ixr1 was analyzed using different predictor tools suitable for short disordered regions (Fig. 1). PONDR-FIT20 and DISOPRED221 are machine-learning algorithms trained on data sets of disordered regions deposited in different databases, such as the Database of Protein Disorder (DisProt)22–24, IDEAL database25 and MobiDB26. Other disorder predictors based on amino acid characteristics were also applied (Figure S1), as the local server ‘MeDor’ (MEtaserver of DisORder)27, which shows a high content of IDRs in Ixr1; the HCA plot28 shows that Ixr1 protein has extended regions depleted of hydrophobic clusters and containing charged amino acids, mainly in the N-terminal end; IUPred29 evaluates the energy resulting from inter-residue contacts in globular proteins; GlobPlot30 is based on a running sum of the propensity of the amino acids to be in an ordered or disordered state; FoldIndex31, is based on the boundary equation; finally DisEMBL REM46532 was included to perform a better prediction of long disordered segments. Altogether, results showed that Ixr1 protein tends to have disordered structure in approximately 50% of its amino acid sequence.

### Ixr1 is non-globular and aggregation-prone in solution
  IDPs may exist in at least three separate functional conformations: molten globule (MG), premolten globule (PMG), and random-coil-like (RC-like)33. In the MG state, IDRs can collapse to native secondary structures, reaching a compact state close to that of ordered globular proteins. On the contrary, the RC-like state has hydrodynamic dimensions typical of proteins unfolded, without any ordered secondary structure. The PMG state is an intermediate state between both. To investigate the conformational properties of Ixr1 and its oligomeric state in solution, we expressed and purified the full-length Ixr1 protein (1-597) and truncated variants containing either its N-terminal region (1-336), or the HMG box domains in tandem (338–510) (Fig. 2a). Both full-length protein and the N-terminal region showed low solubility and high tendency to aggregate at final steps of purification, when protein concentration increased (maximum up to ≈ 75 µM in case of full-length protein). Gel filtration analysis34 using size exclusion chromatography also showed that the hydrodynamic volume of the full-length Ixr1 protein was 1.5 fold higher than the expected for the monomeric form, taking into account the Stoke’s radio (Fig. 2b). This result is consistent with a more extended shape, which hydrodynamic volume increment corresponds to the expected for a MG state35,36.

  Sedimentation equilibrium experiments by analytical centrifugation clearly indicated that the monomer is the predominant form of Ixr1 in solution at a concentration of 0.8 mg/mL (corresponding to ≈ 15 µM) (Fig. 2c). The frictional coefficient ratio (ƒ/ƒ0), calculated from the molecular mass and the sedimentation coefficient (2.9 S), is 1.97 (Fig. 2d), which is compatible with a rod-like structure having a high axial ratio or with the coil-like structure of IDPs37.

  Many proteins maintain a dynamic equilibrium between monomers and oligomers, which is dependent on protein concentration38,39. Ixr1 switched from a monomeric, at ≈ 75 µM, to a homodimeric state, at 290 µM (Fig. 2e). Furthermore, protein treatment with 150 µM glutaraldehyde, to stabilize and fix weak protein-protein interactions, showed that Ixr1 has high tendency to aggregate and form larger oligomeric states (Fig. 2f).


### The N-terminal region of Ixr1 is intrinsically disordered (IDR), while the region containing the HMG-box domains is folded
  In general, far-UV circular dichroism spectra of unfolded polypeptide chains are characterized by a prominent minimum ellipticity in the vicinity of 190 to 200 nm and values close to zero in the vicinity of 222 nm40. The far-UV CD spectrum of full-length Ixr1 protein (Fig. 3a, black line), as well as its N-terminal region containing IDRs (Fig. 3a, blue dotted line), revealed a typical profile for disordered proteins. On the contrary, the HMG-box tandem region showed predominately α-helical structure (Fig. 3a, green dashed line). Displaying the Mean Residue Ellipticity (MRE) at 200 and 222 nm in a double wavelength plot33 allowed to discriminate between full-length Ixr1 protein, having an extended MG or PMG state, and its N-terminal region showing a RC-like state.

(b) TFE titration; the concentration range from 0 (black) to 60% (lighter gray). (c) CD melting curve (5–75 °C) of full-length Ixr1 protein (black) and their HMG-box domains (green). (d) Differential scanning fluorimetry of full-length Ixr1 (black) and their HMG-box domains (green); dF/dT, first derivative of SyproOrange fluorescence as a function of time.

  Trifluoroethanol (TFE) is widely used in order to stabilize regions with pre-existent alpha helical structure by lowering the dielectric constant of the solution41. When full-length Ixr1 protein was titrated with increasing TFE concentrations, it folded into a helical conformation (Fig. 3b), diminishing negative ellipticity at 200 nm and increasing negative values at 222 nm (Fig. 3b inset). This result opens the possibility that in natural occurring conditions, several disordered tracts may fold, for example upon binding to a ligand or an interaction partner.

  CD melting curve comparison of full-length Ixr1 protein with their HMG-box domains was also performed (Fig. 3c) as well as differential scanning fluorimetry, and the plot was converted into first derivative of SyproOrange fluorescence as a function of time (Fig. 3d). The results indicated that folding was directed by the HMG-box domains.

  Proteinase K accessibility is a sensitive probe for determining the extension of global tertiary structure in a protein. There are 208 consensus proteinase K cleavage sites throughout Ixr1, and 75 of them are inside the HMG-box domains (Fig. 4b). Purified full-length Ixr1 protein was digested with increasing amounts of proteinase K, and the proteolytic products were resolved by SDS-PAGE (Fig. 4a). After 30 and 60 min of proteinase K digestion (1:500 molar ratio) high proteolysis was observed, which indicates the existence of large, unfolded regions (Fig. 4a, lanes 9 and 10). However, even after 60 min of digestion, three protease resistant bands were observed with apparent sizes of 25.6, 23.9 and 15.6 kDa (Fig. 4a, lanes 6 and 10). These bands were excised from the gel and subjected to peptide mass fingerprinting to identify their composition. Results showed that peptides correspond to the HMG-box DNA binding domains of the Ixr1 protein (Fig. 4b). The HMG-box domains are predicted to adopt characteristic L-shaped fold, containing three α-helices with an angle of ≈ 80o between the arms. The long arm or minor wing is composed by the extended terminal strand and helix III, while helix I and II form the short arm or major wing42. This native structure is rigid and avoids proteinase K digestion as confirmed by our results.

### Ixr1 forms amyloids fibrils in vitro and aggregates into foci in vivo
  To assess whether IDRs detected in Ixr1 contain priogenic domains (PrD) and produce self-assembly into amyloids, four different constructs, corresponding to the full length Ixr1 protein (PrD1) and three different regions (PrD2/3/4) of the N-terminal region (Fig. 5a) were cloned in the pETNKI-his3C-LIC-Amp plasmid, expressed in bacteria and purified under denaturing conditions. Sup35 prion domain15 was also purified as a positive control (Sup35-PrD). After purification, amyloid formation along time was monitored in the presence of Thioflavin T (ThT), a benzothiazole dye that emits fluorescence upon amyloid binding without interfering amyloid assembly43. All constructs showed positive amyloidogenic properties; PrD2 (Fig. 5d) and PrD4 (Fig. 5f) showed the highest aggregation rates, completing amyloid formation within the first 10 h of the experiment time-course, similarly to Sup35 PrD (Fig. 5b). PrD1 (Fig. 5c) and PrD3 (Fig. 5e) showed large initial lag phases, but if they were seeded with initial amyloids of each PrD, their kinetic profile quickly reached the plateau phase (Fig. 5c and d). It has been reported that some non-amyloid β-sheet structures can also alter ThT fluorescence43; therefore, in order to differentiate true amyloids from non-amyloid aggregates, samples were treated with 2% SDS or 0.1% Tween and passed through non-binding 300 kDa membrane spin column. In these denaturing conditions, non-amyloid aggregates become solubilized and flow through, but true amyloids are resistant, as we observed for Ixr1 (Supplementary Figure S2). Finally, the fibrillar nature of Ixr1-PrD aggregates was confirmed by Transmission electron microscopy (Fig. 6). Different fibril morphologies are often observed in the same preparation, with a variable number and arrangement of protofilaments.

  Amyloids from priogenic proteins form foci in yeast cells44. To investigate the propensity of the Ixr1 protein to form foci in living yeast cells, the different Ixr1 PrDs were cloned into the pAG426GAL-ccdB-EGFP plasmid to overexpress them as chimeras with green fluorescent protein (PrD-EGFP). Sup35 PrD was included as positive control (Sup35N-EGFP). YJW509 [pin-] and YJW584 [PIN+] transformed cells were incubated in SRafGal medium and samples were taken at 24, 48 and 72 h, since amyloid formation is time and concentration dependent. In general, all the PrDs under study were capable to form foci with a punctate form and close to the vacuole (Fig. 7). However, PrD2 and PrD3 showed higher frequencies to form aggregates into the cell. A remarkable feature observed was the capability of PrD1 and PrD2 to induce cell flocculation when the protein construction was overexpressed, meanwhile PrD3 and PrD4 did not show this characteristic. Since foci formation can also be produced by overexpression of many non-prion proteins, cells were lysed and analyzed by semi-denaturing detergent-agarose gel electrophoresis (SDD-AGE) to determine if Ixr1 PrDs aggregates formed in vivo correspond to highly ordered amyloid fibrils or to amorphous superstructures45. Despite off differences in amyloid formation rates and flocculation phenotypes, all Ixr1 PrDs aggregates show resistance to the presence of 1% SDS detergent (Supplementary materials Figure S3).

### A Sup35-based prion assay identifies phenotypic switching induced by Ixr1
  In order to test the ability of each domain to form prions, we used the method previously described by taking advantage of the fact that [PSI+] increases read-through of stop codons46, and therefore [PSI+] detection is possible by nonsense suppression of the ade1-14 allele. Non-prion ade1-14 mutants do not grow in medium deprived of adenine or turn red when adenine is limiting; but when these cells express the prion [PSI+] they can grow in the absence of adenine and remain white or light pink on limiting adenine. Sup35, the protein determining [PSI+], is modular and consists of an N-terminal PrD (N), a highly charged middle domain (M) and a C-terminal domain (C), which provides the translation termination function. A PrD domain from a prion might be transferred to other proteins conferring the elements of inheritance47–49. In these assays, the phenotypic strength of the prion state correlates with the intracellular number of prion particles. Since prion particles are thought to be distributed passively and asymmetrically to daughter cells during cell division, progeny that inherit more particles will have faster total prion polymerization rates and will accumulate more prion particles, which will, in turn, strengthen the prion phenotype (light pink and white cells) in subsequent generations. Conversely, cells that inherit fewer particles will have slower polymerization rates and weaker phenotypes producing red and pink cells16. We take advantage of this property and fusioned PrD selected regions of Ixr1 with the C terminal domain of Sup35 to create PrD-Sup35C chimeras under the control of the constitutive ADH1 promoter. We test them for their ability to suppress the ade1-14 allele as previously assayed with other prions15 and we obtain positive results (data not shown). In the ade1-14 cells transformed with the chimera, Ade+ colonies could result from either DNA mutation or [PSI+] formation. To distinguish between these two possibilities, we tested the effect of PrD(s) overexpression directed by a promoter inducible by galactose, or by PrD(s) amyloid cytoduction in cells containing PrD1-Sup35C chimera instead of endogenous full-length Sup35 protein, since the frequency of events necessary for [PSI+] formation is dependent on Sup35 concentration, whereas the frequency of chromosomal mutations is not. Overexpression (SGal medium) or cytoduction of the different PrD(s) increased the Ade+ phenotype (Fig. 8a), suggesting that the Ade+ phenotype was a result of prion formation. All known fungal prions are dependent on chaperones to induce and maintain a prion state, although they differ in the chaperon nature17. In most of the cases, amyloid-based fungal prions are critically dependent on the protein remodeling factor Hsp104p, whose activity is inhibited by guanidine hydrochloride (GdmHCl)48,50. However, not all fungal prions rely on Hsp104p for propagation, and some prions are resistant to GdmHCl inhibition or require alternative chaperone systems51. Thus, we tested yeast cells for prion cure by culturing them in media containing (or not) 5mM GdmHCl for 40 generations. No prion curation was obtained for PrD1-PrD3, since Ade+ phenotype was maintained, meanwhile PrD4 turned to Ade− at longer cultures (Fig. 8b).

## Discussion
  Until the earliest 1990s, the unique accepted concept of protein function was the “sequence-structure-function paradigm” in which loss of specific three dimensional fold was known as a “denatured” state, in which the proteins lost function. However, the discovery of intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) have significantly broadened the view of the scientific community, increasing the interest to study these intriguing class of proteins52. In general, eukaryotes exhibit higher proteome content in IDPs than either prokaryotes or archaea, and prediction of long intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) in Saccharomyces cerevisiae proteins reaches 52–67%53. It was hypothesized that the higher abundance of intrinsic disorder in eukaryotes could be a consequence of the increased need for cell signaling and regulation in higher organisms54. Ixr1 is involved in diverse regulatory mechanism in yeast cells1,3,5,8,10 .Besides, bioinformatic analyses, some of them based on machine-learning algorithms trained on data sets of disordered regions deposited in different public databases, reveal several stretches of IDRs in Ixr1 that we have studied in this work.

  We show that purified Ixr1 protein has a high aggregation propensity, which is frequently observed in IDRs with Q/N-rich sequences, and suggested to be a potential prion-like protein55. Two different structural forms for IDRs: molten globule-like (collapsed) and random coil-like (extended) have been proposed56. Later, the pre-molten globule form distinguishable from the other two by the presence of unstable secondary structure, was also added57. Several experimental evidences in the present work support that Ixr1 IDRs switch between random coil-like to pre-molten and molten globule forms. First, according to equilibrium sedimentation experiments, at low protein concentration Ixr1 predominates as monomer. However, analyses of Ixr1 protein size and Stoke’s radius by size-exclusion chromatography showed a 1.5 fold increment in its hydrodynamic volume with respect of the theoretically expected. Second, the frictional ratio (ƒ/ƒ0 = 1.97) of full-length Ixr1 obtained from sedimentation velocity experiments is compatible with a rod-like structure of high axial ratio or with a coil-like structure37. Moreover, far-UV CD data obtained from full-length Ixr1 protein and their N- terminal region showed a typical non-folded profile, with a characteristic minimum ellipticity in the vicinity of 190–200 nm and values close to zero in the vicinity of 222 nm40,58. Third, limited proteolysis with proteinase K digestion, followed by identification of resistant peptides by MALDITOF fingerprint, reveals that only HMG-box domains are folded in compact proteolytic resistant regions.

  Glutamine/asparagine-rich (Q/N-rich) domains are found in known yeast prions as part of the “prion-forming-region” (PFR) required for prion formation and propagation. Alberti and co-workers were the first to systematically test whether compositional similarity to known PFRs is sufficient to distinguish between Q/N-rich proteins that form prions and those that do not15. More recently it has been reported that the prion-forming regions of yeast prion proteins are usually located at either the N- or C-terminus of a protein in IDRs containing QN-rich sequences19,59. We have demonstrated that the N-terminal IDRs of Ixr1 direct the formation of amyloids in vitro and in vivo. Full-length Ixr1 protein or different stretches of the prion-like domain expressed and purified in E. coli cells, aggregate into amyloid fibrils in adequate solution conditions, showing resistance to 2% SDS and ThT fluorescence signal. We also show that the different Ixr1 PFR studied are able to coalesce into foci in vivo when they were overexpressed in yeast cells. We confirmed that these foci aggregates correspond to highly stable structures, showing SDS resistance by SDD-PAGE assay.

  Several self-propagating protein conformations have been related to prion formation60,61, and amyloids are the most common self-templating replicative state found in previously characterized prions15,62. We have confirmed that Ixr1 can replace the PrD of Sup35 in the restoration of de Ade+ phenotype. Presented data allow to consider Ixr1 as a prion-like protein. Although Ixr1 appeared as a good candidate of priogenic protein with a high score (13th position of one hundred candidates) in a previous predicting model15, these are the first experimental evidences reported to date about the Ixr1 capacity to form amyloids or to have some prion characteristics. The question remains about whether Ixr1 behaves as a real S. cerevisiae prion in vivo. A wide-genome analysis, designed to find protein based inheritance in S. cerevisiae, overexpressed 5300 ORFs from an inducible promoter and recorded alterations of growth in ten different stress conditions, using stringent cutoffs17. Although Ixr1 was not discovered in this screening as a protein whose inheritance could confer adaptive advantages. It is worth noting that the stress conditions tested in the work from Chakrabortee and col17. do not coincide with the stress conditions, such as addition of cisplatin, hypoxia, oxidative stress or iron limitation, in which Ixr1 plays a regulatory role1,4–6. It is possible to find environmental scenarios in which the prion form of Ixr1 may represent an adaptive advantage; indead, it has been reported that elevated intracellular levels of Ixr1 induce a necrosis cell-death response9 and we have also experienced that high induced Ixr1 overexpression decrease dramatically cell viability. It is possible that stratification in Ixr1 protein among different monomer-oligomer-amyloid states during adaptation to stress signals that increase Ixr1 expression, could allow the population to survive in adverse conditions. Transcription factors and other proteins that control gene expression are usually among priogenic proteins15–17. We have previously reported that Ixr1 interacts with Ssn8 (Srb11) and Med137, both components of the Cdk8 kinase module that is part of the yeast mediator complex, which transduce signals from activators or repressors to the RNA polymerase II63. Other proteins related to stress response which are components of the mediator tail also have priogenic characteristics, for instance Med3 and Med1564. On the contrary, it has been reported that high levels of Ssn8 expression have prion-curing effects65, therefore it is possible to consider that Ssn8 levels might control the formation of Ixr1 amyloids during stress conditions. High levels of Ssn8 affect promoters of the genes encoding Ssa4 and Hsp104 chaperones, and also to modified promoters in with either the heat shock or stress response elements were included65. This mechanism is plausible for the regulation of structural Ixr1 features, considering that we have found that inhibition of chaperone Hsp104 affects the priogenic characteristics of PrD4 region of Ixr1. Nevertheless, considering the physical interaction described between Ixr1 and Ssn87 and that both factors are up regulated in stress conditions5,65,66 we used IA-assisted molecular modeling to test whether interaction with Ssn8 modifies the folding/docking of Ixr1 IDRs and we found that the interaction of both proteins, with a predicted template modeling (pTM) score of 0.42 and an interface predicted template modeling (ipTM) score of 0.28, (Supplementary materials Figure S4a) produces an increase in the secondary structure content of Ixr1 from 252 amino acids forming part of 14 alpha helices, to 289 amino acids forming 21 alpha helices in the complex with the Ssn8 protein (Figure S4b). The prediction of a higher foldin3g order in the Ixr1 protein is the result of greater stabilization in the complex, where up to 41 residues participate in the 245 contacts that occur between both proteins (Figure S4c). It is worth highlighting the L234-L255 region of the Ixr1 protein, forming a stable and reliable interaction with Ssn8, with PAE (Predicted Aligned Error) values below 15, and in which up to 4 hydrogen bonds occur between both proteins (Figure S4d). Future experiments to elucidate the functional significance of a prion state for the Ixr1 protein should contemplate this issues.


## Methods

### Strains and culture media
  The Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Escherichia coli strains are summarized in Suplemmentary Materials (Table S1). Yeast cells were grown at 30 °C in rich media, YPD (2% glucose, 2% Bacto peptone, 1% yeast extract) or complete media (CM) lacking particular amino acids and containing either 2% D-glucose (SD), 2% D-Rafinose (SRaf), or a mix of raffinose and galactose (SRafGal, 1% each. Plates used for prion curing contained 5 mM GdmHCl). Bacterial cells were grown at 37 °C in Luria-Bertani (LB) or 2xYT (1,6% Bacto tryptone, 0,8% Yeast Extract, 0,5% sodium chloride) supplemented with suitable antibiotics for selection.


## Molecular cloning
  PCR reactions were performed using genomic DNA from S. cerevisiae BJ3505 strain as template. Primers (Table S2) and details of constructs (Table S3) are available as Suplementary Materials. Constructs obtained from pKLSL150 and pGBD-C2 plasmids were cloned by ligation between SacI and XhoI or BamHI and SalI restriction sites respectively. Those obtained from pETNKI-his3C-LIC-Amp were obtained by Ligation-Independent Cloning (LIC); in brief, the plasmid was linearized by KpnI digestion, compatible ends between plasmid and inserts were generated by T4 polymerase treatment in presence of dTTP or dATP, respectively, and finally transformed in E. coli competent cells to repair the nicks. Constructs obtained from pAG426GAL-ccdB-EGFP15 were cloned by homologous recombination in S. cerevisiae BJ3505 competent cells, using primers for PCR amplification carrying 30–35 nucleotide tails with the specific sequence required for homologous recombination with the corresponding plasmid. Constructs obtained from pAG415ADH-ccdB-Sup35C were cloned by using the GATEWAY ® system following manufacturer’s instructions (Invitrogen, United States), using pDONR211 vector (Invitrogen, United States) as donor plasmid.


## Protein expression and purification
  The proteins obtained from pKLS150 constructions were expressed in BL-21(DE3) or Rosetta™ 2(DE3)pLysS cells (Novagen, Argentina). Cultures growing in 2xYT medium were treated with 1 mM IPTG to induce the expression during 3 h at 37 °C and 200 rpm of shaking. After expression, cell pellets were collected and lysed by sonication in high salt lysis buffer (50 mM sodium phosphate buffer pH 6.9, 1 M NaCl, 2 mM dithiothreitol and 2x protease inhibitor cocktail from Roche, Switzerland). After clarification by centrifugation 30 min at 23000 x g, lysates were passed through Sepharose CL-6b resin (Sigma-Aldrich, United States) packed into a Tricorn column (GE Healthcare, United States) equilibrated in wash buffer A (50 mM sodium phosphate buffer pH 6.9, 200 mM NaCl, 2 mM dithiothreitol and 1 mM EDTA) with the aid of a peristaltic pump. Proteins were eluted in an AKTA-prime plus equipment (GE Healthcare, United States) by linear gradient from 0% to 100% of buffer elution A (50 mM potassium phosphate buffer, pH 6.9, 1 M NaCl, 2 mM dithiothreitol and 300 mM lactose) and loaded on a HisTrap HP 5 mL column (GE Healthcare, United States) equilibrated in wash buffer B (50 mM sodium phosphate buffer, pH 6.9, 200 mM NaCl, 2 mM dithiothreitol). After elution by linear gradient from 0% to 100% of buffer elution B (50 mM potassium phosphate buffer pH 6.9, 1 M NaCl, 2 mM dithiothreitol and 300 mM imidazole), the different polypeptides were dialyzed (50 mM sodium phosphate buffer, pH 6.9), 200 mM NaCl, 2 mM dithiothreitol and 1 mM EDTA) and simultaneously digested with TEV protease (Sigma-Aldrich, United States) for 16 h and 8 °C. Next day, proteins digested were further purified using gel filtration chromatography using a Hi-load Superdex 200 16/60 column (GE Healthcare, United States) pre-equilibrated with running buffer (50 mM potassium phosphate buffer, pH 6.9, 100 mM KCl, 2 mM dithiothreitol and 1 mM EDTA). The proteins were concentrated by ultrafiltration using Amicon® Ultra-15 Centrifugal Filters, 10 kDa (Merk-Millipore, United States). The homogeneity of the purified protein sample was checked by electrophoresis in denaturing conditions and protein concentrations were determined by absorbance at 280 nm using extinction coefficients calculated with Protparam (http://web.expasy.org/protparam/). The proteins obtained from pETNKI-his3C-LIC-Amp constructions were expressed in Rosetta™ 2(DE3)pLysS cells (Novagen, Argentina)) and purified as previously described15. Cultures growing in 2xYT medium were treated with 1 mM IPTG to induce the expression during 3 h at 37 °C and 200 rpm of shaking. After expression, cell pellets were collected and re-suspended in lysis buffer (7 M guanidine hydrochloride, 100 mM K2HPO4 pH 8.0, 5 mM imidazole, 300 mM NaCl, 5 mM 2-mercaptoethanol) for 1 h at room temperature and constant circular rotation. Lysates were then cleared for 30 min at 23000 x g and loaded onto Econo-Pac® Chromatography columns (Bio-Rad, United States) for purification using HisPurTM Ni-NTA resin (Thermo Scientific, United States), according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Proteins were eluted (8 M urea, 100 mM NaOAc/HOAc pH 4, 5 mM β-mecaptoethanol), precipitated by methanol-chloroform and resuspended finally in resuspension buffer (7 M GndHCl, 100 mM K2HPO4 pH 5.0, 300 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 5 mM TCEP). Protein concentrations were determined by measuring absorption at 280 nm using calculated extinction coefficients.


### Size-exclusion chromatography
  The molecular weight and Stokes radius of Ixr1 protein were determined by size-exclusion chromatography. The chromatographic separation was done on a Superdex 200 increase 3.2/300 column (GE Healthcare, United States) connected to a ÄKTA-prime purifier system (GE Healthcare, United States), pre-equilibrated with running buffer (50 mM potassium phosphate buffer pH 6.9, 100 mM KCl, 2 mM dithiothreitol and 1 mM EDTA) and calibrated using the protein standards from Bio-Rad, United States (bovine thyroglobulin bovine (670 kDa; Stokes radius: 7.8 Å); bovine γ-globulin (158 kDa; 51 Å); chicken ovalbumin (44 kDa; Stokes radius: 31.2 Å); equine myoglobin (17 kDa; Stokes radius: 19 Å); Vitamin B12 (1.35 kDa; Stokes radius: 8.5 Å). Blue dextran (≈ 2 MDa) was used to determine the void volume (V0) of the column. Flow rate was 100 µl/min and elution of proteins was monitored by absorbance at 280 nm. The relative molecular weight of the elution peak of Ixr1 protein was then estimated using a standard curve obtained by plotting the logarithm of the molecular weight of the known proteins against Kav (Kav = (Ve-V0)/(Vt-V0)), being Ve the volume value of elution for each protein and Vt the total volume. Estimation of the Stokes radius (RS) of Ixr1 protein was obtained plotting the linear dependency of log Stokes radius versus Kav67.


### Analytical ultracentrifugation
  Sedimentation velocity experiments were performed using a analytical ultracentrifuge (Optima XL-A, Beckman Coulter, United States) equipped with absorbance and interference optics. 400 µl of a protein solution at 0.8 mg/mL extensively dialyzed was placed in the sample compartment of an epon double-sector centerpiece and 400 µl of the same solution without protein was placed in the reference compartment. The samples were centrifuged at 293 K and 13,100 rpm using an An60-Ti rotor. Scans were acquired using both interference and absorbance optical systems without any significant systematic variation, indicating the absence of signal contributions from residual unmatched buffer components. Sedimentation coefficients were corrected using the software package SEDNTERP68. Sedimentation coefficient distributions c(s) and molar mass distribution c(M) were calculated using the program SEDFIT69 .


### Blue native PAGE
  Blue Native PAGE (BN-PAGE) was performed running linear 4%- 13% (w/v) polyacrylamide gradient. Protein samples were supplemented with a tenfold concentrated loading dye (75 mM Imidazole, 0,1% Ponceau S, 50% glycerol, (w/v)). The electrophoresis was started at 120 V for thirty minutes and continued for four hours at 6 mA at room temperature. Gels were stained with Coomassie brilliant blue. Additional supporting SDS-PAGE was performed. Dilutions 1:1 of Ixr1 protein were done and then, each dilution was treated with 150 µM glutaraldehyde to stabilize protein oligomerization state. Polyacrylamide gel (5% w/v, 0.5% SDS) (gel dimensions of 160 mm x 200 mm x 1.5 mm) was running at 150 V during 8 h and stained with Coomassie brilliant blue.


### Far-UV circular dichroism
  Circular dichroism (CD) experiments were carried out in a Jasco J-810 spectropolarimeter equipped with a Peltier PTC-423 S system. After dialysis (10 mM K2HPO4 pH 7, 50 mM NaF, 1 mM β- mercaptoethanol), samples were transferred to the cuvette (1-mm path length) and spectra were collected at 15 °C. Band width of 1 nm in continuous scanning mode, with 50 nm/s scanning rate, and a response time of 2 s was used. From raw data, collected spectra were buffer-subtracted and converted from millidegrees (Θobs, mdeg) to molar ellipticity ([Θ], degrees cm2 dmol−1 residue−1) using the equation [Θ] = (Θobs x M) / (10 x l x C), where M is the protein mean residue molecular weight, l is the optical path length of the cuvette in cm, and C is the concentration of the protein in mg mL−1. Estimations of secondary structure content were calculated by deconvolution of the far-UV CD spectra using the CDNN program developed by Gerald Bohm of the University of Halle, Germany70 as well as the CONTIN71, SECON372 and CDSSTR73 procedures contained in the Dichroweb utilities (http://dichroweb.cryst.bbk.ac.uk/html/ home.shtml)74,75. The SP43 basis set was used to deconvolute the CD spectrum. Estimates of percent secondary structure obtained from the four methods were averaged and standard deviations calculated. Titrations with 2,2,2-Trifluoroethanol (Sigma-Aldrich, United States) were done adding increasing concentrations to the same sample. The temperature dependence of the circular dichroism spectra of the different proteins was determined upon continuous heating with a rate of 1 K min−1 in the temperature range 5–95 °C, at 222 nm wavelength using a 0.1 cm path length sealed cell. Ellipticity signals were plotted as a function of temperature and a non-linear Boltzmann fit was performed; the melting temperature was calculated as the maximum of the first derivative of this curve using GraphPad Prism 6.0 software (GraphPad, United States).


### Limited proteolysis
  Ixr1 protein was prepared at 1 mg mL−1 and dialyzed into 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM β-mercaptoethanol and 0.2 mM EDTA (Proteinase K digestion buffer). Proteinase K (Sigma-Aldrich, United States) was dissolved at 1 mg mL−1 in the same digestion buffer and diluted 1:500 and 1:1000 (w/w). 10 µL of each proteinase K dilution was incubated with 10 µL of Ixr1 protein for 5, 10, 30 and 60 min on ice. The reactions were quenched with 4 x Laemmli-SDS loading buffer, and samples were boiled for 10 min, loaded onto a 12% SDS- PAGE gel and electrophoresed. Protein bands were stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue (Bio-Rad, United States) and images recordered in a Gel Doc™ XR + system (Bio-Rad, United States). Gel bands were manually excised and transferred to microcentrifuge tubes. Samples were in-gel reduced, alkylated and digested with trypsin according to the method of Sechi and Chait76. The samples were analyzed using the MALDI (Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization)-TOF (Time of Flight)/TOF mass spectrometer 4800 Proteomics Analyzer (AB Sciex, United States, United States) and 4000 Series Explorer™ Software (AB Sciex, United States). Data Explorer version 4.2 (AB Sciex, United States) was used for spectra analyses and for generating peak-picking lists. All mass spectra were internally calibrated using autoproteolytic trypsin fragments and externally calibrated using a standard peptide mixture (Sigma-Aldrich, United States). TOF/TOF fragmentation spectra were acquired by selecting the 10 most abundant ions of each MALDI-TOF peptide mass map (excluding trypsin autolytic peptides and other known background ions).


### Differential scanning fluorometry
  Differential Scanning Fluorometry (DSF) was used in order to analyze the thermal stability of the full-length protein and the HMG-box domains in tandem, and compare the results of melting temperature with the data obtained by circular dichroism77. A pre-screening of Sypro Orange dye (Sigma-Aldrich, United States) and protein was done in order to find the best dye and protein concentrations. This was found to be 20x Sypro Orange for 30 µM full-length protein and 20x Sypro Orange for 50 µM HMG-box domains protein. 96-well thin-wall PCR plates (Thermo-Scientific, United States) sealed with Optical-Quality Sealing Tapes (Bio-Rad, United States) were used. Samples were incubated for 5 min at 25 °C and then heated to 95 °C in increments of 0.5 °C x min−1 using a iCyclerIQ real-time PCR machine (Bio-Rad, United States). Fluorescence of the dye was monitored simultaneously using λex: 490 nm and λem: 530 nm filtered wavelengths. Fluorescence intensities were plotted as a function of temperature and a non-linear Boltzmann fit was performed using GraphPad Prism 6.0 software (GraphPad, Unites States). Melting temperature was calculated as the maximum of the first derivative of this curve77.

  Amyloid formation in vitro.

  In order to follow the rate of amyloid formation Thioflavin T (ThT) fluorescence was measured. Purified proteins in solution were heated for 5 min at 95 °C and diluted to 20 µM in assembly buffer (5 mM K2HPO4 pH 6.6, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 2 mM TCEP) plus 20 µM ThT (freshly prepared and added); samples (150 µL) were loaded in 96-well black with clear flat bottom polystyrene microplates (Corning Life Sciences, United States) and fluorescence (λex: 450 nm; λem: 482 nm) recorded with a Multi-modal plate reader (Synergy™ H1, Biotek, Italy). De novo and seeded amyloid assembly reactions were incubated at 25 °C, with double orbital continuous shaken, and readings were recorded every 2 min for a period 16 h.

  To generate amyloid seeds, proteins were assembled using continuous end-over-end agitation for 5 days; aggregates were then collected by centrifugation at 13,000 rcf for 1 h and re-suspended in fresh assembly buffer. To fragment the amyloid fibrils, samples were sonicated (Vibra-cell VCX130 sonicator, Sonic Healthcare Ltd., Australia) at 70% amplitude during 3 min (10 s on/10 seconds off cycles) on ice.

  Filter retardation assays78 were done after fluorescence measures to detect protein aggregates, which, due to their size, are retained on the membrane surface. of Nanosep 300 K Omega ultracentrifuge columns (Pall Corporation, United States). Then, 10 µL of the flow-through and the fraction retained was spotted on a nitrocellulose membrane (PROTRAN®, Whatman, United Kingdom) and revealed with Ponceau S (Sigma-Aldrich, United States). In parallel, aggregated proteins were treated with non-denaturing tween 20 0,1% (v/v) or denaturing SDS 2% (w/v) during 30 min at room temperature and processed in the same way.

  Amiloid visualization by Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) was as follows. PrDs of Ixr1 protein were purified and diluted to 20 µM in assembly buffer (5 mM K2HPO4 pH 6.6, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 2 mM TCEP) and incubated one week at 25 °C and continuous rotation. Aggregates were then collected by centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 1 h and re-suspended in fresh assembly buffer. 5 µL sample was incubated on top of a carbon-coated grid for 5 min. Excess solution was removed and the grid was washed in two sequential drops of ultrapure water (Sigma-Aldrich, United States) and subsequently stained in a 2% uranyl acetate (w/v) or 2% phosphotungstic acid (w/v) (Sigma-Aldrich, United States) drop (five minutes and thirty seconds of incubation time, respectively), followed by blotting off excess solution. Samples were examined using a transmission electron microscope (JEM-1010, Jeol, Japan) an accelerating voltage of 100 kV, recorded at different magnifications and images were digitalized using a CCD camera.


### Amyloid formation in vivo
  The different PrDs of Ixr1 protein fused to the Enhanced Green Fluorescent Protein (EGFP) were used to investigate the amyloid propensities of PrDs in vivo. The different pAG424GAL-PrD-EGFP constructions were transformed into the S. cerevisiae YJW509 and YJW584 strains and selected in CM-leu plates. The pAG426GAL_Sup35N_EGFP construct was used as a positive control of protein aggregation. Cells were grown in raffinose or galactose/raffinose-containing medium and subjected to fluorescence microscopy after 24, 48 and 72 h of growth, using a Nikon Eclipse 50i microscope (Nikon, Japan) and GFPHQ filter (λex: 455–485 nm; dichroic mirror: 495; λem: 500–545 nm). Fluorescence images were acquired with a Nikon Digital Sight D5-5 M camera (Nikon, Japan) and processed using NIS-Elements D v2.20 (Nikon, Japan) image analysis software.


### Semi-denaturing detergent-agarose gel electrophoresis (SDD-PAGE) and western blot
  Aliquots of YJW584 cell cultures overexpressing the different PrDs fused to EGFP were taken at 24, 48 and 72 h, harvested by centrifugation and resuspended in buffer (50 mM Hepes pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM EDTA, 1% (v/v) Triton X-100, 1 mM PMSF and 1 x Complete Protease Inhibitor from Roche, Switzerland). Cells were lysed using 425–600 μm glass beads (Sigma-Aldrich, United States) and were briefly spun at 3000 rcf to sediment cell debris. The protein concentrations (determined by the Bradford reagent from Bio-Rad, United States) of the cell lysate were adjusted and mixed with 4 x sample buffer (2 x TAE, 20% (v/v) glycerol, 4% (w/v) SDS and bromophenol blue). The samples were incubated at room temperature for 15 min and loaded onto a 1.8% (1.5 mm thickness) agarose gel containing 1 x TAE and 1% SDS. The gel was run in running buffer (1 x TAE, 1% SDS) at 50 V, followed by by blotting onto a PROTRAN® nitrocellulose membrane (Whatman International Ltd, United Kingdom). Anti-eGFP mouse IgG Antibody (MA1-952; Thermo Scientific, United States) and goat anti-mouse IgG-HRP (sc-2005; Santa Cruz Biotechnology) primary and secondary antibodies respectively, and BM Chemiluminiscence Western Blotting kit - Mouse/Rabbit (Roche, Switzerland) were used to detect the different PrD-fusion proteins in Amersham Hyperfilm™ ECL High performance chemiluminiscence films (GE Healthcare, United States). Membrane washes, incubation times and developing procedures were done following manufacturer’s instructions of the kit (BM Chemiluminiscence Western Blotting kit - Mouse/Rabbit) from (Roche, Switzerland).


### Sup35 prion assay
  A Sup35C tagging plasmid (pAG415ADH-ccdB-Sup35C) was used to generate different PrD-Sup35C expressing strains. The strain YSR100 was transformed with the cPrD-Sup35C expression plasmid, and then plasmid shuffling was performed by plating the transformed yeasts on 5-FOA (5-Fluorotic acid). This produced a strain that expressed a particular PrD-Sup35C fusion protein as the only source of functional Sup35. All strains were examined by Western blotting and SDD-AGE to evaluate expression and aggregation levels of the fusion proteins. To induce the prion state, the PrD-Sup35C strains were transformed with pAG424GAL-PrD-EGFP expression plasmid. Transformants were grown in SRafGal-Trp medium for 24 h and then plated on 1/4YPD plates (0.25 w/v yeast extract) with a cell number of 100 and 200 per plate, respectively. The same strains grown in SRaf-Trp were used as negative controls15. In parallel, cytoduction assays were performed following the protocol established by Tanaka79 For this purpose, spheroplasts prepared from YRS100 cells containing the pAG415ADH-PrD1-Sup35C construct were transformed with the different aggregates obtained from the “non-seeded” amyloid formation assays in vitro and subsequently plated on 1/4 YPD plates. Potential Ade+ colonies were cultured for 40 generations on YPD medium in the presence or absence of 5 mM guanidine hydrochloride45, and then plated on SD-Ade plates to check prion curing and restoration of Ade- phenotype.
