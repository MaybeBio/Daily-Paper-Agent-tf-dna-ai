# A novel molecular classifier enabling identification and prediction on immunotherapeutic response for MYCN-low pediatric neuroblastoma


## Purpose
  Neuroblastoma (NB) is a pediatric malignant solid tumor arising from peripheral neural crest cells, characterized by significant heterogeneity. Previous studies have stratified NB patients into risk groups based on pivotal genetic changes, but the suboptimal clinical outcomes of NB underscore the need for a more precise individualized grading system to guide the selection of novel therapeutic strategies.


## Methods
  In this study, we developed a dense neural network molecular classifier utilizing bulk transcriptomics data from the UCSC Treehouse database and applied it to a single-center cohort.


## Results
  The neural network molecular classifier on bulk transcriptomics refined the classification in both high-risk and low-risk groups by the traditional classification method. The classifier identified four molecular subtypes: High-risk MYCN-high NB (HR1), High-risk MYCN-low NB (HR2), Low/Intermediate-risk NB (LR1), and Low/Intermediate-risk GNB (LR2). By applying the new classifier, we identified factors such as PIK3R1, GATA2, and EYA1 that may be associated with a low-risk, differentiated ADRN-subtype NB, in addition to the classical adrenergic fate-determining factors PHOX2B, ASCL1, ALK, and GATA3. Additionally, we observed elevated GD2 and CTLA-4 expression in the High-risk MYCN-low NB group, which may serve as a potential clue for the development of personalized immunotherapeutic strategies.


## Conclusion
  Our model details the current NB risk stratification at the transcriptomic level with a molecular classifier and offers potentially more personalized immunotherapeutic strategies for High-risk MYCN-low NBs.

## Introduction
  Neuroblastoma (NB) is the most common pediatric solid tumor arising from the peripheral sympathetic nervous system in the first year of life, which accounts for 10% of childhood malignant cancers and contributes up to 15% of pediatric cancer fatalities [1, 2]. According to the Children’s Oncology Group (COG) risk classification system, patients are classified into different risk groups, including provisional low risk, low risk, intermediate risk and high risk, based on their clinical features like age, International Neuroblastoma Staging System (INRGSS), tumor MYCN status, pathology before initiating treatment [3]. Although combining surgery, chemotherapy, radiology and oral administration of retinoic acid (ATRA), the five-year survival rate of high-risk NBs is still lower than 50% [4, 5].

  Single-cell RNA transcriptomic study has demonstrated that NB is governed by two distinct gene expression profiles, ultimately resulting in either a committed adrenergic (ADRN) or an undifferentiated mesenchymal (MES) subtype [6, 7]. Numerous key transcription factors (TFs) involved in ADRN/MES binary fate determination have been identified, including MYCN, PHOX2B, ASCL1, GATA2 and PRRX1 [6–8]. Risk assessments based on important genetic signatures, such as MYCN amplification, ALK mutations or TERT rearrangements have systematically elucidated the relationship between differentiation origins and clinical heterogeneity [9, 10]. NB harboring MYCN gene amplification are conventionally classified as high-risk due to their aggressive progression and unfavorable therapeutic outcomes, rendering them a primary focus of extensive research [11]. However, recent investigations have revealed a distinct subset of high-risk NB lacking MYCN amplification [9, 12]. The poor clinical outcomes of these atypical high-risk cases have prompted intensified efforts to uncover their molecular mechanisms and identify potential therapeutic targets.

  Novel treatment modalities for NB are continuously evolving. Among these, monoclonal antibodies targeting ganglioside GD2 have emerged as a well-established form of NB immunotherapy. The regimen combining isotretinoin with GD2 monoclonal antibody Dinutuximab beta during the maintenance phase has become the standard regimen for HR-NBs, with clinical trials corroborating its efficacy [13–15]. Yet, the effectiveness of GD2 antibody monotherapy is constrained to approximately 45%, primarily due to the inherent low immunogenicity of NB [16, 17]. Similarly, immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapies, which have achieved notable success in adult solid tumors, demonstrated only limited efficacy as single-agent therapy in pediatric NB [18, 19]. Nonetheless, it is imperative to emphasize that preliminary preclinical studies in animal models suggest that the strategic combination of targeted immunotherapies may reverse the low immunogenicity state of NB, thus enhancing the host’s anti-tumor immune response and potentially improving therapeutic outcomes.

  In this study, we developed a novel molecular classifier for pediatric NB with bulk RNA-Seq data from the UCSC Treehouse database. Validation of the model was conducted utilizing an NB patient cohort from Shanghai Children’s Medical Center (SCMC, SCMC-NB-2024), encompassing transcriptomic, proteomic and clinical dimensions. Our investigation probed into the distribution of potential transcriptomic regulatory factors in ADRN-lineage NBs and indicated that employing anti-GD2 and a combined blockade of immune checkpoint molecules CTLA4 and PD1/PD-L1, may offer promising therapeutic strategies for atypical High-risk MYCN-low NBs.


## Materials and methods

### Inclusion and exclusion criteria
  All patients diagnosed with NB in the Department of Surgery from SCMC were retrospectively selected between January 2011 and July 2022. The cohort inclusion criteria include the following aspects: (1) Patients who underwent surgical treatment at the Department of Surgery at SCMC and were diagnosed by pathologic examination by at least one senior pathologist from the Department of Pathology at SCMC; (2) Patients and their families who agree to have the tumor tissue removed during surgery undergo transcriptome sequencing; (3) Patients who gave written informed consent signed by themselves and/or their guardians, agreeing to use tumor samples and clinical profiles for subsequent scientific research; (4) Patients who have relatively adequate clinicopathological profiles; (5) Patients who have complete follow-up data. The cohort exclusion criteria include the following aspects: (1) Patients who have received treatment at another hospital; (2) Patients who did not receive routine tumor treatment at our hospital after surgery; (3) Patients with other severe systemic diseases; (4) Patients whose tissue specimens cannot be effectively subjected to genetic sequencing; (5) Patients lost to follow-up.


### Clinicopathological characteristics
  Data on gender, age at initial diagnosis, tumor histopathology, COG risk classification, and MYCN gene amplification status were collected for analysis. MYCN gene amplification was assessed through FISH. Follow-up information encompassed recurrence, metastasis, and mortality. Tumor histopathological classification was conducted according to the International Neuroblastoma Pathology Classification (INPC) system [20], while risk stratification was performed using the 2021 COG Neuroblastoma Risk Classifier (Version 2) [3].


### Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) screening
  Bulk RNA-seq data were normalized and log2-transformed as log2(TPM + 1), and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between subgroups were identified using limma (adjusted P value < 0.05, |log2FC| > 1), with volcano plots generated via ggplot2. Details are listed in Additional file 2 (Tables 1 and 2).


### Gene ontology (GO), kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) and single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA)
  The clusterProfiler R package was employed to identify GO and KEGG enrichment terms, with significance thresholds defined by a P value and q value cut-off of 0.1. The GO enrichment analysis included evaluations of Biological Processes (BP), Cellular Components (CC) and Molecular Functions (MF). The ssGSEA scores were calculated by the GSVA R package.


### Identification of subtype hub genes
  Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed using the STRING database (https://string-db.org) and visually optimized with Cytoscape software (version 3.9.1), using node degree as the ranking criterion. Genes with the highest connectivity degree within the genome were identified as hub genes in the respective gene set. For the molecular panel heatmap, the top 25 genes from each subgroup, ranked by descending |log2FC| values, were selected. Genes that showed significant differential expression compared to the other three subgroups, as determined by Student’s t-test, were highlighted.


### Tumor composition and immune cell infiltration analysis
  We analyzed the proportion of stromal and immune cells proportion in NB with Estimate R package. The value of the ESTIMATE Score is equal to the sum of the Stromal Score and the Immune Score. To assess the 22 immune cell infiltration status of each subtype, we employed the Cibersort R package and visualized the results by ggplot2 R package.


### Detection of ADRN-associated genes
  The Groningen ADRN gene signature originally consists of 369 genes [6]. In the SCMC-NB-2024 bulk RNA-seq dataset, 356 of these genes were detected and retained for downstream analysis. The remaining 13 genes (ADGRB3, ADRBK2, CENPU, HMP19, LINC00999, LOC100507194, LOC101928409, MARCH11, MTCL1, NPY, RPPR5, SEPT3, and SEPT6) were not captured in the processed gene expression matrix, likely due to a combination of low or absent expression, poor transcript annotation, and standard pipeline filtering of low-count genes.


### Sample collection, storage and selection
  All tumor specimens were collected from patients in the SCMC-NB-2024 Cohort at diagnosis, after 4–6 chemotherapy or diagnosis of recurrence by open surgical biopsy or resection. Fresh tumor tissues were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80℃. Six samples from patients in each subgroup were selected randomly from the Sample Bank of Shanghai Children’s Medical Center for successive experimental validation.


### Quantitative PCR (qPCR)
  Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol and quantified by nanodrop 2000. Briefly, cells were lysed with TRIzol reagent at room temperature for more than 5 min and chloroform was added. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 15 min the aqueous phase was collected and mixed with isopropanol before centrifuging at 12,000 × rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. RNA was dissolved in RNase-free water and recovered by centrifuge. For analysis of mRNA expression, 200–500 ng of RNA was converted to cDNA using PrimeScript™ RT Regent Kit (TAKARA, Dalian, China). Quantitative real-time PCR using KAPA SYBR® FAST qPCR Kit Master Mix (2×) Universal (Applied Biosystems, USA) was performed on a 7500 Fast Real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems). Total RNA was isolated for qPCR analysis. Data were analyzed using the comparative Ct method (2 − ΔΔCt). GAPDH was used as a normalizing gene in all experiments.


### Immunofluorescence
  The excised tumor tissue was embedded in an optimal cutting temperature compound and rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen. Sections were cut using a cryostat and subsequently fixed in paraffin. The paraffin-embedded sections underwent deparaffinization in xylene and anhydrous ethanol, followed by rinsing with distilled water. The slides were subjected to high-pressure boiling in EDTA retrieval solution, covered, and heated for 3 min to facilitate antigen retrieval, after which the sections were washed three times with PBS. Cells were blocked with 3% BSA for 30 min to prevent nonspecific antibody binding. Diluted primary antibodies were incubated with the cells overnight at 4 °C in a humid chamber, with PD1 and CD8 marked for heterologous double staining, mixed prior to incubation. After removing the solution and washing the cells three times with PBS for 5 min each, secondary antibodies were applied in the dark at room temperature for 50 min. After discarding the secondary antibody solution, the cells were washed with PBS in the dark. DAPI staining was utilized for nuclear labeling, with the sections incubated at room temperature for 10 min in the dark, followed by the mounting of the specimens. Stained sections were stored in the dark at 4 °C and visualized using confocal microscopy. Six tumor tissue samples from each subgroup underwent immunofluorescence staining. Three randomly selected tumor fields of equal size were counted per sample and their average was used as the final result of this sample. For tumor cell quantification, 400x magnification images were randomly captured from three independent fields (96348.2 μm² per field) per tumor sample. Positive cells were manually counted and averaged, and cell nuclei were counted by QuPath [21]. The proportion of antibody-positive cells was defined as the number of antibody-positive cells divided by the number of nuclei per field. Fluorescent images were analyzed using CaseViewer (The Digital Pathology Company) and Photoshop (Adobe Systems Inc) software.


### Statistical analysis
  The expression data was loaded in Python package anndata (v0.11) and Scanpy (v1.10). The clustering, statistical analysis, and plotting functions during molecular classifier construction were derived from Python packages including scikit-learn (v1.5.2), pandas (v2.1), and seaborn (v0.13). We used GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software) and R software (version 4.2.1; http://www.r-project.org) to analyze and visualize the data. P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The student’s t test was used to compare the mean values of bulk RNA-Seq or the proportion of positively immunofluorescent cells between the two subgroups. Categorical associations between covariates were analyzed by the Pearson chi-square test (χ2) or Fisher’s exact test at the appropriate time.


## Results

### Training data preparation for NB molecular classifier
  We obtained Tumor Compendium v11 Public PolyA Training dataset and its corresponding metadata from Cancer Treehouse project website (https://treehousegenomics.soe.ucsc.edu/public-data/). The dataset contains over 12,000 samples and is standardized in Transcript per Million base pair (TPM). To maintain the integrity across multiple cancer types, we process all the samples as a whole. Since the sum of TPM in individual sample range between 1 × 107 and 1 × 108, we first normalized expression value to 1 × 107 and took log1p transformation. To validate whether the bulk RNAseq results are disease-specific, we reduced the dimension of features (genes) to the top 4000 highly variable genes, an amount that is sufficient to identify differential gene expression between groups. We then scaled the data to around zero, and performed principal component analysis (PCA). The PCA results served as a connectivity map for building the neighborhood graph. Step-wise Leiden clustering analysis were performed using resolutions of 0.4 and 0.8, which produced 29 and 37 clusters, respectively. The clustering results closely resemble the disease type of individual sample. Out of 201 NB samples, 198 were exclusively clustered in the same group under resolution of 0.8. Interestingly, under the resolution of 0.4, NB samples were clustered together with 183 samples diagnosed as pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma, two types of neuroendocrine tumors that are closely related to adrenal gland, suggesting a molecular similarity between these tumor samples. NB samples were extracted by disease name and re-select 2158 highly variable genes by the following criteria, minimum mean of log1p (TPM) = 0.7, maximum mean = 9, minimum dispersion = 1.5, and maximum dispersion = 4. Since the RNAseq data contains high-dimensional features, we selected Leiden algorithm for unsupervised clustering analysis for its robustness and scalability. Leiden clustering analysis were performed under different resolution and connectivity strategies. Silhouette score was used to evaluate the ideal number of clusters that allow maximum meaningful separations. Signature genes along the ADRN-MES trajectory were plotted in pair plots to show the correlation in their expressions. Pearson correlation coefficient r is calculated for each pair of genes.


### Construction of NB molecular classifier
  We trained our neural network classification model using PyTorch framework (v2.3). The cluster label was derived from aforementioned unsupervised clustering analysis. The classifier was built using a fully connected feedforward neural network with a Sequential architecture in PyTorch. The model architecture consists of the following layers. One input layer that accepts the normalized expression data of selected genes as input. Six fully connected (dense) layers with ReLU activation functions to capture non-linear relationships between the genes. We also included a dropout layer after the third hidden layer to prevent overfitting. Last we included a fully connected layer with a softmax activation function that outputs probabilities for four classes. In addition, we added a batch normalization step after the first two dense layers that stabilized and speed up the training steps. We also included dropout layer with 0.0001 dropout rate to prevent units from co-adapting too much. We set epoch number to 30 for each training loop, however, an early stopping strategy was also applied to prevent overfitting, monitoring both validation loss and validation accuracy. The average training epoch number was about 12. For hyperparameter tuning, we used Python optuna library (v4.0) to fine tune learning rate to 0.0005 (Adam optimizer) and batch size to 30. To evaluate the robustness of the model, we used k-fold cross-validation utilities from scikit-learn package (fold = 6). The average validation accuracy of k-fold cross-validation was over 84%. To stabilize the prediction outputs, we trained a total of 11 models and applied them on SCMC cohort. The final clustering results were inferred by the majority voting from these 11 models.


### Unsupervised clustering on public data enables a classification model predicting 4 clusters of pediatric neuroblastoma
  Bulk transcriptomics profiling is a cost-effective tool for providing comprehensive views on gene expression across mixed tumor cells and stromal cells in the tumor microenvironment. We collected 201 NB bulk transcriptomics data from the Cancer Treehouse repertoire. We first investigated whether these NB profiles show distinct ADRN or MES signatures. Pearson correlation between ADRN genes such as PHOX2B, ASCL, CHGA, and TH, as well as MES genes including PRRX1 and COL6A2, yielded a positive correlation between CHGA and TH (Pearson r = 0.62) and between PRRX1 and COL6A2 (Pearson r = 0.73) (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Both ADRN and MES genes showed gradient expression patterns and were not exclusively expressed in individual samples, suggesting these two types of cells are likely to co-exist in NB tumors. In addition, the expression of MYCN, a gene frequently found highly expressed in NB tumors due to focal duplication, exhibited a discrete binary pattern (Supplementary Fig. 1A), indicating that MYCN expression represents an independent molecular feature distinct from ADRN or MES status.

  To exploit the information hidden in the high-dimensional gene expression space and link gene expression to clinical traits, we next built a molecular classifier using dense neural network. To prepare the training data, we first performed an unsupervised clustering on the Treehouse NB dataset using the Leiden algorithm, which identified samples into distinct clusters. Silhouette scoring was highest when the number of clusters is set to 4, which allowed the highest degree of separation between clusters (Supplementary Fig. 1B, Fig. 1A). Interestingly, the unsupervised clustering results identified samples that had higher expression of MYCN (cluster 2) or lower expression of PHOX2B (cluster 3), suggesting that our clustering method captured known molecular features of the tumor. Furthermore, clusters 0 and 1 mostly overlapped along the ADRN-MES trajectory, suggesting that other molecular mechanisms might exist to further stratify NB tumors (Fig. 1B).

(B) Scatter plot matrix illustrates the transcriptional expression of ADRN and MES genes across 4 clusters in the Treehouse NB dataset.   To generalize our NB clustering model, we built an NB classifier using a dense neural network (DNN). The model consists of an input layer that accepts the normalized gene expression data, six hidden layers to capture non-linear relationships between the genes, a dropout layer to prevent overfitting, and an output layer that will classify NB clusters. To ensure a robust evaluation of the model, we employed 6-fold cross-validation. The average cross-validation accuracy was 84.6% (Supplementary Fig. 1C). Based on this, we established a robust and precise molecular classification of NB for subsequent individualized stratification of patient cohorts.


### SCMC-NB-2024 cohort demonstrated significant clinical heterogeneity before stratification
  Despite the volume of the Cancer Treehouse dataset, it lacks sufficient clinical information necessary for evaluating the effectiveness of molecular profiling in real-world clinical decision-making processes. To bridge this gap, we obtained 112 NB patient records from the General Surgery Department of SCMC and constructed our validation cohort (SCMC-NB-2024) (Additional File 2- Table 3) for our molecular classification model. The male-to-female ratio of the cohort was 1.24:1 (62 males and 50 females), with a median age at diagnosis of 2.77 years (ranging from 0.00 to 9.42 years). According to the International Neuroblastoma Pathology Classification (INPC) system [20], there were neuroblastoma (75.89%, N = 85), intermixed ganglioneuroblastoma (20.54%, N = 23), nodular ganglioneuroblastoma (2.68%, N = 3) and maturing ganglioneuroma (0.89%, N = 1) in the cohort. Based on the 2021 COG risk classification [3], the ratio of high-risk and non-high-risk patients was 0.70:1 (46 high-risk patients and 66 non-high-risk patients). Fluorescence in situ hybridization analysis of the MYCN gene was conducted for 102 patients and the results indicated that 15 patients exhibited MYCN gene amplification, while 87 patients did not. A significant statistical difference (P < 0.0001) in MYCN expression at the transcriptomic level was observed between these two groups (Supplementary Fig. 2A), suggesting a consistent trend in MYCN gene expression at both transcriptomic and cellular levels. Accordingly, patients with MYCN gene amplification were classified as the MYCN-high group, and those without as the MYCN-low group. The ratio of MYCN-high to MYCN-low patients was 0.15:1, comprising 15 and 97 patients, respectively. (Fig. 2A). The primary tumor sites of children in the cohort include posterior mediastinum (17.86%, N = 20), retroperitoneum (44.64%, N = 50), adrenal medulla (33.9%, N = 38) and pelvis (0.89%, N = 1). Additionally, three patients were concurrently diagnosed with lesions affecting two of the aforementioned sites at the initial evaluation. Throughout the disease, 50% of the children in the cohort did not experience distant metastasis (N = 56), 36.61% of patients developed bone metastases involving the spine, limbs, pelvis and skull (N = 41) and 12.5% had solitary or multiple liver infiltrations (N = 14). Additionally, one patient presented with concomitant gastric and pancreatic tumor lesions, one patient exhibited infiltration of the abdominal wall, and two patients showed no metastases in the sites above but only demonstrated regional lymph node infiltration (Fig. 2B-C). Overall, the SCMC-NB-2024 cohort exhibits a high degree of clinical heterogeneity, underscoring the importance of molecular stratification for subsequent analyses.

### Molecular subgroups show distinct MYCN status, COG risk and pathology profiles
  We applied the neural network classification model to our local cohort and assigned patients into 4 groups. Based on the non-parametric test (Supplementary Fig. 2B), we assigned clinical definitions to the four molecular subtypes: (1) High-risk MYCN-high neuroblastoma (HR1); (2) High-risk MYCN-low neuroblastoma (HR2); (3) Low/Intermediate-risk neuroblastoma (LR1); (4) Low/Intermediate-risk ganglioneuroblastoma (LR2). Since patients in HR1/2 have higher COG risk grades, HR1 and HR2 were designated as the high-risk NBs (HR-NBs), while LR1 and LR2 were designated as the non-high-risk NBs (NHR-NBs) (Fig. 2D).

  Adverse event incidence rates in each subtype showed that HR1 had recurrence, metastasis, and mortality rates of 46.15%, 76.92%, 30.77% , respectively, while HR2 showed rates of 42.85%, 100%. 23.81%, respectively (Fig. 2E). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed a significant difference in overall survival between the HR and NHR NBs (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2F). Further survival analysis among the four subgroups indicated no significant differences between HR1 and HR2 (P = 0.65), or between LR1 and LR2 (P = 0.38). However, a significant difference was observed between HR2 and LR1 (P < 0.05). Univariate Cox regression analysis showed that our refined molecular subtypes (P = 0.002), together with canonical clinical factors including MYCN gene status (P = 0.009), relapse (P < 0.001), metastasis (P = 0.03), and COG risk classification (P = 0.006), were significantly associated with patient prognosis (Fig. 2H). Thus, with our NB molecular classification, we stratified the transcriptional profiles of the SCMC-NB-2024 cohort and defined its corresponding subtypes, capturing the observed clinical heterogeneity.


### HR2 subgroup exhibits higher immune activity than HR1 in TME
  Besides clinical validation, we conducted a transcriptomic analysis to delineate distinct molecular subgroups within the cohort. With the cluster genes generated by the molecular classifier (Additional file 2 – Table 4), we identified and visualized the hub genes of each group (top25 genes ranked by log2FC) in a heatmap (Fig. 3A). Many genes exhibit markedly elevated expression levels in HR1/LR1/LR2. For instance, oncogenes MYCN, DDX1 and tumor stem cell-related gene SOX11 are prominently expressed in HR1, neuroimmune regulatory genes CLU and MPZ are significantly upregulated in LR2, and neuro-signaling transductor ADCY1 is upregulated in LR1. Quantitative PCR validation of these genes corroborates the expression patterns observed at the transcriptomic level (Supplementary Fig. 3B). However, in HR2, only four genes showed higher expression at the transcriptomic level- MUC15, FAM153A/B and SLCO1A2 (Fig. 3A). Among them, MUC15, a member of the transmembrane mucin family, has been shown to facilitate the migration of NB cells by suppressing c-MYC target MYCT1 expression and proved as an independent prognostic factor for metastasis in MYCN non-amplified NB [22].

  We conducted enrichment analysis on HR1 and HR2 within the SCMC-NB-2024 cohort to explore potential biological functional differences between the two high-risk NB subtypes. The analysis revealed significant upregulation of pathways, including cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, the PI3K-Akt and MAPK signaling pathway in HR2 subgroup (Fig. 3B-C). These findings suggest that, compared to the highly proliferative HR1 (Supplementary Fig. 4A-F), HR2 exhibits relatively enhanced antitumor immunity and a more suppressed state of genetic information transmission. Notably, the PPI network analysis for HR2 indicated that CCND1, a key regulatory factor for both ADRN and MES NB subtypes [23], remains critical in promoting proliferation. However, genes such as MMP9 and POSTN, identified as cancer-associated fibroblast markers known to drive tumor invasion, metastasis, and angiogenesis, displayed higher interaction degrees than CCND1 (Fig. 3D). This highlights an active TME remodeling process in the HR2 subgroup. Although the qPCR results did not confirm every significant upregulation in HR2 (Supplementary Fig. 3B), this discrepancy may be attributed to sample selection bias and limited resolution of bulk RNA data.

  To further assess differences in the TME between HR2 and HR1, stromal and immune scores for the four subgroups were calculated using the ESTIMATE R package. The analysis revealed that HR2 had higher Immune and ESTIMATE scores than HR1 (Fig. 3E), suggesting that HR2 tumors may harbor a more active immune microenvironment and potentially greater immune cell infiltration. HE staining demonstrated that LR2 specimens exhibited distinct histomorphological features, characterized by tumor lesions arranged in a conspicuous nodular, cluster-like pattern (Fig. 3F). The Cibersort R package was applied for immune cell infiltration analysis. HR2, similar to the remaining subgroups, was characterized by a high infiltration of macrophages M0 and M2, alongside a low infiltration of macrophages M1. Furthermore, HR2 had a higher estimated proportion of naïve B cells than HR1, which may suggest that HR2 exhibits a more robust adaptive immune response process compared to HR1 (Supplementary Fig. 4G). Collectively, these findings suggest that the HR2 displays relatively higher enrichment of immune response pathways and related genes compared with HR1, potentially indicating a more favorable responsiveness to immunotherapy.


### Transcriptional programs associated with better clinical outcomes in ADRN NBs
  Despite the similarities in pathological subtypes and MYCN gene status between HR2 and LR1, their divergent prognostic outcomes prompted us to undertake a more detailed investigation of their molecular distinctions. We utilized the single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) to assess the MES and ADRN scores across all NB samples in the SCMC-NB-2024 cohort, drawing on MES and ADRN gene panels as delineated by van Groningen et al. [6]. The original Groningen ADRN signature comprises 369 genes; however, only 356 genes were detected in our bulk RNA-seq dataset, as 13 genes were absent from the processed expression (see Methods for details). Except for the LR2 subtype, which displays a relatively elevated MES score, the HR1, HR2 and LR1 subtypes predominantly exhibit higher ADRN scores (Fig. 4A). Therefore, LR2 is designated as MES-NBs, while the other three subtypes are designated as ADRN-NBs. Upregulation of PHOX2B and PRRX1 from HR1 to LR2 is analogous to the expression changes observed in bipotent progenitor cells originating from NCCs, which transition from an autonomic neuronal lineage to a mesenchymal lineage (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Fig. 5A) [6–8]. Specifically, elevated expression of PHOX2B promotes the differentiation of NB cells towards an ADRN phenotype while the co-overexpression of PHOX2B and PRRX1, or the overexpression of PRRX1 alone, facilitates the progression of NB cells toward an MES phenotype (Supplementary Fig. 5A-B). This observation implies that the LR2 group may represent an undifferentiated MES lineage, while HR1/HR2/LR1-NBs appear to be aligned with a more committed ADRN lineage.

  Survival analysis revealed differing clinical outcomes among the three ADRN-type subgroups (Fig. 2G), highlighting the likely involvement of additional regulatory determinants in ADRN-NBs that deserve deeper investigation. We compared the bulk RNA-Seq profiles of the 356 previously identified ADRN-associated genes across our SCMC-NB-2024 cohort. Among the 356 genes, 139 genes were upregulated in the three ADRN subtypes (HR1/HR2/L1) compared to the MES subtype (LR2). Notably, within this subset, 50 genes, including the well-established ADRN regulators PHOX2B, ASCL1, CCND1 and ALK, exhibited no significant differences in expression across the three ADRN subtypes of NB (Fig. 4B). We speculate that these 50 genes may direct NB development toward the adrenergic fate at the MES-ADRN cell lineage crossroads but do not play a pivotal regulatory role in the further differentiation into distinct ADRN subtypes. Conversely, GATA2, EYA1, PIK3R1, SCG2, and HEY1 were expressed at significantly higher levels in LR1 compared with the HR1 and HR2 subgroups (Fig. 4C, Supplementary Fig. 5C), indicating a potential association between their elevated expression and the more favorable prognosis observed in ADRN-type NB. Furthermore, we also noticed that the SE-associated TF SOX11 significantly upregulated in HR1 (Fig. 4D), which is proved to sit atop the MYCN-related core regulatory circuits hierarchy in self-renewing NB. By leveraging our constructed molecular subtype, we portrayed a subset of TFs whose overexpression may promote the differentiation of MYCN-independent HR-NBs (HR2) from an intermediate state into a more differentiated intermediate/low-risk phenotype (LR1). This differentiation process has the potential to enhance the tumor’s responsiveness to clinical interventions, thereby improving therapeutic outcomes.


### Anti-GD2 and combination of anti-CTLA4 and anti-PD1/PD-L1 represent promising therapies for High-risk MYCN-low NBs
  Prior enrichment analysis indicated that antitumor immune function varies across subgroups (Fig. 3B). To further explore the efficacy of novel immunotherapies, we investigated the applicability of the currently most available therapeutic strategies- anti-GD2, anti-PD1/PD-L1 and anti-CTLA4 in the treatment of atypical High-risk MYCN-low NB (HR2).

  Differential analysis on the bulk transcriptomics profiles of two key enzyme-coding genes (B4GALNT1 and ST8SIA1) involved in the GD2 synthesis pathway [24, 25] (Fig. 5A-B), revealed that though B4GALNT1 expression did not differ significantly between HR1 and HR2 (P = 0.6465), ST8SIA1 expression was notably elevated in HR2 (P = 0.0167) (Fig. 5B). To corroborate these transcriptomic results, we performed GD2 immunofluorescence staining on tumor tissue samples from six NB patients within each subgroup (Fig. 5C, Supplementary Fig. 6A). The staining results indicated that both the number and proportion of GD2-positive cells in tumor regions were higher in HR2 than in HR1 (Fig. 5D-E). Although GD2 is regarded as a standard immunotherapeutic target for high-risk NB, our findings indicate that HR2 patients may also derive benefit from this strategy, potentially yielding superior outcomes compared to HR1 patients.

## Discussion
  NB originated from neural crest cells (NCCs) and underwent aberrant cellular differentiation during the development of the sympathetic-adrenal system, with the dysregulation of various intrinsic and extrinsic factors influencing cell fate determination. In this study, we performed clustering and dimensionality reduction on bulk RNA-seq profiles that are both cost-effective and clinically accessible. As a result, we stratified NB patients into four prognostically distinct subgroups, establishing a molecular classifier for pediatric neuroblastoma. Since this model’s construction is intrinsically linked to the developmental trajectory of NB, similar clustering methods could be applied to bulk RNA-Seq data of other malignant tumors with distinct differentiation patterns or high heterogeneity. This could enhance the correlation between clinical cohorts and shared transcriptomic profiles, aiding in identifying subgroup-specific therapeutic strategies.

  The molecular subgroups identified in our study using NB bulk RNA-Seq data exhibit strong concordance with those reported in previous investigations. For instance, our HR2-NB subgroup appears to correspond to the MYCN non-amplified high-risk (MNA-HR) category described by Gartlgruber [23] and bears resemblance to Subgroup 2 NB characterized by Hu et al. [12]. Although our novel clustering approach identified NB subgroups akin to those described in prior studies, this research places greater emphasis on exploring potential therapeutic strategies for High-risk MYCN-low NBs, particularly in the context of promoting differentiation of HR-NBs and predicting targets for immunotherapy.

  Our molecular classifier revealed a more favorable prognosis of LR1 in ADRN-subtype NBs. Researchers have proved that the heterogeneity of NB exists not only across different regions of the tumor at a given time point but also as a dynamic evolution of tumor characteristics throughout various stages of the disease course [26]. For example, chemotherapy can shift NB tumor cell states from ADRN to MES. To date, research into the mechanisms governing cell fate determination between the ADRN and MES subtypes during NB differentiation has largely elucidated the pivotal roles of transcription factors such as PHOX2B, ASCL1 and PRRX1. Our results indicate that transcription factors (e.g. GATA2 and HEY1), signaling regulators (PIK3R1 and EYA1), and neuropeptide-related molecules (SCG2) are more highly expressed in one ADRN-type NB, which is associated with a more favorable prognosis [27, 28]. GATA2 has been reported to be associated with NB differentiation and prognosis, and inhibition of the PI3K/mTOR signaling pathway can also induce apoptosis in NB cells [29]. Nevertheless, the potential roles of HEY1, EYA1, and PIK3R1 in NB warrant further investigation to better understand their functional relevance. This finding aligns with a recent study investigating the alterations in NB lineage-specific regulatory factors following ATRA intervention, demonstrating that ATRA treatment decreases the SE expression of SOX11, MYCN and GATA3 while simultaneously enhancing the SE-driven expression of GATA2 and SOX4 in NB [30]. Considering the plasticity of NB cell states, we speculate that the overactivation of genes such as PIK3R1, EYA1, GATA2, HEY1 and SCG2 may promote a transition of High-risk MYCN-low NB (HR2) cells from a self-renewing proliferative state to a differentiated state (LR1), thereby increasing their sensitivity to diverse therapeutic strategies and potentially improving treatment outcomes. Further investigations are warranted to explore this hypothesis.

  GD2 is a ganglioside predominantly expressed on the surface of NB cells, with minimal expression in normal tissues. Monoclonal antibodies targeting GD2, such as Dinutuximab beta and Naxitamab, exert their antitumor effects primarily through the formation of antigen-antibody complexes. These complexes enhance T cell proliferation and induce tumor cell lysis via antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC), thereby contributing to improved long-term outcomes in HR patients [31]. Our study revealed that HR2 exhibits enhanced infiltration of CD8 + T cells and elevated GD2 expression on tumor cell surfaces compared to HR1. These findings suggest that High-risk MYCN-low NB may respond more effectively to anti-GD2 monoclonal antibody immunotherapy than conventional MYCN-amplified HR-NBs. Furthermore, the observation that a subset of high-risk patients exhibit suboptimal clinical responses to GD2 therapy highlights the need for future studies to explore whether MYCN amplification status and the differentiation state of NB may contribute to variability in treatment efficacy.

  Low immunogenicity of NB attributed to the limited efficacy of anti-GD2 monotherapy [16]. To overcome this limitation, combination immunotherapy involving multiple agents has emerged as a central research focus. Studies have demonstrated that the combination of anti-GD2 monoclonal antibodies with anti-PD1 antibodies has produced favorable outcomes in two cases of relapsed refractory NB [15]. This therapeutic synergy may be attributed to the upregulation of PD-L1 expression by the anti-GD2 antibody, thereby enhancing the antitumor response when used in combination. Our results showed that PD-L1 expression at the transcriptome level is lower in HR1 compared to HR2. This suggests that in HR-NB patients with MYCN amplification, the combination of anti-GD2 and anti-PD1/PD-L1 immunotherapy could potentially counteract the low immunogenicity of NB, facilitating a more robust antitumor immune response.

  Agents of anti-CTLA4 are also clinically available by now. Similar to anti-PD1 antibodies, CTLA4 antibodies work by reversing the inhibitory effects of immune checkpoints on antitumor CD8+T cells, thereby activating the tumor immune response [19]. However, like anti-PD1 monotherapy, their efficacy in NB remains limited. Notably, prior studies demonstrated that combining anti-PD1/PD-L1 and anti-CTLA4 immediately following chemotherapy may stimulate the production of CD8+CD28+PD1+ T cells in mouse models, whose effect is closely linked to the upregulation of PD-L1 expression in non-tumor cells, particularly tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), after chemotherapy [32]. Moreover, the combination of ICB therapies targeting CTLA-4 and PD-1/PD-L1 has shown efficacy across various tumor types such as melanoma [33]. Evidence also showed that PD-L1 expression is higher in MYCN-non-amplified NB compared to MYCN-amplified NB, which is consistent with our results [32]. In our HR2, CD8+ T cell infiltration and PD-L1 expression are both elevated than HR1. Building on this observation, it would be valuable for future studies to explore whether the combined blockade of CTLA-4 and PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoints could potentially enhance the immunotherapeutic efficacy in MYCN-non-amplified high-risk NBs.

  Our study has several limitations. First, the number of RNA-seq samples at each clinical time point and within different risk groups was insufficient to allow separate analyses, so we analyzed the cohort as a whole. In the future, we aim to collect additional tumor samples across multiple time points and anatomical sites, including more high-risk cases, to rigorously evaluate the robustness and reliability of our molecular classifier over both temporal and spatial dimensions. Second, although MES-NB is generally considered a subgroup with relatively favorable prognosis compared with HR1 and HR2, the factors driving poor outcomes in a subset of these patients remain incompletely understood and warrant further investigation. Finally, our assessment of immunotherapy potential across subgroups is primarily based on transcriptomic and cellular expression of target genes, and additional preclinical studies are required to substantiate these findings.


## Conclusion
  We developed and validated a molecular classifier for pediatric NB using bulk RNA-Seq data from tumors in the UCSC Treehouse database and SCMC. This classifier stratifies NB patients into four distinct subgroups, each corresponding to specific clinical NB patient categories. Notably, in addition to the ADRN-type differentiation regulators PHOX2B and ASCL1, both transcription factors (GATA2, HEY1) and other functional regulators (PIK3R1, EYA1, SCG2) may play critical roles in guiding HR MYCN-low NB (HR2) toward a more differentiated and favorable phenotype. Additionally, our findings suggest that in HR MYCN-low NBs, anti-GD2 monoclonal antibody therapy may demonstrate greater efficacy compared to its use in HR MYCN-high NBs. An early combination of anti-PD1/PD-L1 and anti-CTLA4 therapies following chemotherapy in HR2 cases may elicit a durable antitumor immune response. Overall, our molecular classifier identified four clinical NB subgroups with distinct prognostic implications and provided novel therapeutic insights, particularly for High-risk MYCN-low NBs.
