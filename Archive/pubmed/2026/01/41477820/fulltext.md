# Machine learning–powered single-molecule cancer diagnosis using DNA origami tags


## Abstract
  Single-molecule detection (SMD) holds considerable promise in biomedical research. Although atomic force microscopy (AFM) provides an important technique with nanoscale resolution for SMD, its broader application is limited by labeling challenges and slow data processing. Here, we present a machine learning (ML)–powered strategy combining AFM and DNA nanotags for SMD and cancer diagnosis. Nickases are applied to create specific single-strand breaks in target DNA, allowing insertion of exogenous DNA to attach shape-distinct nanotags for AFM imaging. A YOLOv5l algorithm is adopted to automatically recognize target objects in AFM images, which can classify 370 structures in 1.21 seconds with 98% accuracy. The proof of concept of this strategy is confirmed by identifying nickase-edited sites on both linear and circular DNA. Its practical applicability is demonstrated by detecting KRAS Gly12Arg (G12R) and p53 Arg175His (R175H) mutations in samples from patients with pancreatic and colorectal cancer, with accuracy rivaling Sanger sequencing and quantitative polymerase chain reaction, opening avenues for SMD.


## INTRODUCTION
  Single-molecule detection (SMD) technology, which enables direct observation, quantification, or real-time tracking of target molecules at the single-molecule level, provides an indispensable tool for exploring molecular mechanisms in life sciences and medical research (1–3). Unlike traditional ensemble measurements that obscure molecular-level heterogeneity, SMD provides direct access to the structural variations, kinetic properties, and interaction dynamics of individual target molecules, offering superior spatial and temporal resolution and enhanced sensitivity (4–6). These advantages make SMD one of the robust tools for biological analysis and diagnostics with nanoscale spatial resolution. As a representative SMD technique, single-molecule fluorescence microscopy allows for noninvasive, multicolor tracking of individual biomolecules, along with quantitative analysis of molecular dynamic behaviors and interactions in physiologically living states (7–9). It has broken through the optical diffraction limit of conventional optical microscopy, achieving spatial resolution at the subnanometer and even angstrom scale (10, 11). However, its outstanding performance often depends on optimized optical configurations, photoswitchable fluorophores, and high-precision localization algorithms (12–14). Furthermore, this technique requires high-intensity excitation sources and fluorescent labeling, which may encounter spectral cross-talk, fluorophore photobleaching, and phototoxicity (15–17). In contrast, atomic force microscopy (AFM) has evolved into a powerful biophysical tool without the need of fluorescent labeling. It enables topographic imaging and nanomechanical mapping under ambient or liquid conditions, with nanoscale spatial resolution (1 to 10 nm) or even the angstrom level without algorithmic assistance (18–20), thereby providing a more comprehensive and localized information for SMD (21, 22). However, the lack of shape-specific molecular tags hinders the broad applications of AFM imaging in SMD studies.

  The development of DNA nanotechnology establishes a paradigm for the precise design and functional optimization of AFM molecular tags. By virtue of strict Watson-Crick base pairing, homogeneous DNA molecules can be assembled into stable and high-yield DNA tiles with defined connectivity (23). Alternatively, long DNA scaffold strands can be precisely folded by numerous staple strands to yield arbitrarily shaped objects (DNA origamis) with structural programmability and intrinsic nanoscale addressability (24, 25). These programmable DNA nanostructures provide a library of structurally defined tags for AFM-based SMD system. Their combination with gene-editing tools enables precise identification and manipulation, labeling, and analysis of specific gene sequences, presenting good potential for biomedical research especially molecular diagnosis of diseases.

  The rapid advancement of gene editing technologies (e.g., restriction endonuclease technology and CRISPR-Cas9 system) has revolutionized the field of molecular diagnostics (26–28). Among them, nickase-based gene-editing tools with high efficiency and accuracy may pave the way for the next generation of SMD technologies. Unlike Cas9 protein, Cas9 nickase creates nicks or single-strand breaks that could avoid off-target double-strand breaks (DSBs) (29–31), which has been applied to the detection of coronavirus COVID-19, bacteria, and gene mutations (32–34). In addition, nicking endonucleases (NEs), as one of the subunits of heterodimeric restriction endonucleases family, can specifically cleave one strand of the target double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) within a particular asymmetric sequence (35–37). Their site-specific cutting ability and high cleavage efficiency make NEs unique candidates for SMD. However, these gene-editing techniques often rely on molecular tags for signal output. Through specific chemical and/or physical interactions and base-complementary pairing, shape-resolved DNA nanotags specifically hybridize with the capture strands adjacent to the nickase-nicked sites. With the assistance of high-resolution AFM, these site-specifically labeled DNA nanotags can be directly identified under ambient conditions, enabling identification of target gene and location of edited sites. However, traditional manual data analysis of AFM images, such as contour distance measurement and image retrieval, cannot meet the requirements of massive data processing. It is labor intensive and prone to random measurement errors due to subjective factors. To address this bottleneck, the integration of machine learning (ML) into AFM image analysis promises automated, high-throughput object detection and classification (38–40). By leveraging ML algorithms, researchers can enhance data processing efficiency, accuracy, and comprehensiveness, which is critical for unlocking the full potential of AFM-based single-molecule gene analysis.

  In this study, we combined two kinds of nickases (NEs and Cas9 nickase), shape-resolved DNA nanotags, high-resolution AFM, and ML to build an analytical platform. This platform was denoted as NDAM (where “N” stands for nickase, “D” for DNA nanotag, “A” for AFM, and “M” for ML) and applied for SMD and disease diagnosis (Fig. 1). Nickases acted as “molecular scissors” to create nicks at specific sites on target DNA molecules, which facilitates the insertion of exogenous DNA. Specifically, the inserted exogenous DNA molecules were programmed with distinct sticky ends, which represent unpaired nucleotide overhangs that could connect shape-specific DNA nanotags through base pairing, enabling AFM observation. By further combining AFM imaging and ML-accelerated data processing, nickase-edited loci of target gene could be efficiently identified, and the feasibility of this approach was verified on the constructed linear and circular DNA. Small-sized DNA tetrahedral tag as a typical DNA tile was preferred for analysis of short DNA fragments, while DNA origami tag was preferred for long sequences. The practical applicability of this strategy was tested using NEs as the gene-editing tools for SMD of KRAS Gly12Arg (G12R) and p53 Arg175His (R175H) mutations in clinical samples from patients with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) and colorectal cancer (CRC). The mutation detection results showed that our method was comparable to Sanger sequencing and real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) in terms of detection accuracy. Overall, this analytical platform featured high specificity, high accuracy, and low false positives, opening up horizons for reliable molecular diagnosis of diseases and promoting the development of screening, early diagnosis, prognosis, and even new drug research.


## RESULTS

### SMD of NE-edited loci on short-stranded DNA
  Considering the simple structure and ease of manipulation of short-stranded DNA molecules, we constructed short-chain DNA fragments (denoted as DNA I, II, and III) with different NE cleavage sites to systematically evaluate the cutting efficiency of NEs (NE 1: Nb.BbvCI; NE 2: Nb.BssSI; NE 3: Nb.BtsI) (Fig. 2A). Formamide was used as a denaturant to destabilize the NE-nicked DNA duplex, thereby lowering the melting temperature and converting the cleaved dsDNA into single-strand state, which was characterized by urea–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (urea-PAGE) (41, 42). As analyzed by ImageJ software, the cutting efficiencies of NEs at various target sites were determined to be ~97.9, 91.1, 76.5, 82.3, and 68.9%, respectively (Fig. 2A and fig. S1).

  Given the high cleavage efficiency of NEs, we then explored the potential of this NDAM platform in gene SMD by localizing the NE-edited loci. A 96–base pair (bp) dsDNA (DNA 1) was chosen as the target gene, one strand of which is 5′ phosphorylated and contains an NE 1 recognition sequence. After NE treatment, exogenous DNA fragments were hybridized with the intact single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) template for primer extension under the action of DNA polymerase, followed by PAGE assay (figs. S2 and S3). After a series of manipulation on DNA 1, DNA tetrahedral nanotag with each edge length of 20 bp (denoted as T20) was assembled (figs. S4 and S5) and adopted to hybridize with the overhang of the inserted DNA to label the gene editing products. As characterized by AFM, T20 DNA tetrahedron was labeled on the NE 1–edited site with the contour distance close to that predicated from the base numbers (~0.34 nm for one base) (Fig. 2B and fig. S6). The above results confirm the accuracy of NE 1–mediated gene editing, highlighting the potential of this NDAM platform in SMD.

  In addition to linear short-stranded DNA, small circular DNA was also constructed to validate the broad applicability of this approach. Circular DNA, a closed-loop structure without free 5′ or 3′ ends, played a pivotal role in biological systems and biotechnological applications such as gene therapy and vaccine development (43, 44). Its closed-loop configuration conferred resistance to exonuclease degradation, resulting in superior stability compared to linear DNA (45, 46). This inherent advantage made it promising in future biomedical innovation. Therefore, to include this stable form in our validation, we constructed circular DNA. First, DNA 1 was amplified using asymmetric PCR to obtain 5′-phosphorylated ssDNA (DNA 2), which was characterized by PAGE assay (Fig. 2C). Next, a DNA splint complementary to both ends of DNA 2 was introduced to allow cyclization of DNA 2 by T4 DNA ligase, which was further digested by exonuclease I to yield circular ssDNA (DNA 3). This construction process was monitored by urea-PAGE assay (Fig. 2D). Then, primer extension was operated to form circular dsDNA (DNA 4). Last, after NE 1 cleavage and inserted DNA-guided extension, T20 DNA tetrahedron was applied to detect the gene editing results. It can be seen from AFM images that the resultant gene editing product was successfully labeled by the T20 DNA tetrahedral nanotag (Fig. 2E and figs. S7 and S8).

  To assess the labeling accuracy by contour distance, we designed a circular DNA containing two prescribed NE recognition sites for localization. In the presence of DNA splint and T4 DNA ligase, an NE recognition site–containing ssDNA (E-DNA) was coupled with DNA 2 to form DNA 7 with two cleavage sites for NE 1 (fig. S9). As shown in Fig. 3A, after primer extension, gene editing, and inserted DNA-guided extension, the circular gene editing product was imaged using AFM (fig. S10). Considering the relatively large size of circular DNA 7, we assembled a larger DNA tetrahedron structure (denoted as T37) with each edge length of 37 bp as the detection tag to more localize the two NE 1 recognition sites on DNA 7 (figs. S4 and S11). This nanotag was then used to label the NE 1 cleavage site on DNA 7, followed by AFM observation (fig. S12). The contour distance distribution between two labeled DNA tetrahedrons measured from AFM images was mostly close to the theoretical value, demonstrating the precision of NE 1–mediated multisite editing (Fig. 3A and fig. S12). The above successful gene editing and DNA nanotag labeling on short-stranded DNA encourage further studies on SMD of more complex genes.


### ML-accelerated AFM data processing for SMD of gene editing results
  To shorten analysis time and avoid uncertain subjective judgment, we developed an ML algorithm for automated and precise classification of target objects in AFM images (Fig. 3B). Circular DNA samples with dual DNA tetrahedron labeling at the NE 1 recognition sites in the AFM image were selected as the recognition objects and defined as the 192-ring category. First, a large number of AFM images of the target objects were collected from experimental data and existing databases. Next, to expand the training dataset for enhanced performance of the model, we subjected the original images to diverse data augmentation processing including rotation, scaling, and flipping. Then, a professional annotation tool (i.e., LabelMe) was used to precisely annotate the target objects in the AFM images. Last, YOLOv5l model was chosen for training due to its fast computational speed and high recognition accuracy (Fig. 3C). The YOLOv5l model consisted of three core components: backbone, neck, and head networks (Fig. 3C). The backbone network was responsible for feature extraction, progressively transforming the images into high-level data representations. The neck network enhanced the feature expression performance by multiscale fusion of the features extracted from the backbone part and transmits these features to the head network. The head component, which was composed of classification and regression subnetworks, was responsible for outputting classification confidence score and parameters of bounding boxes. The confusion matrix, precision, recall, F1 score, and training loss (i.e., bounding box and class loss) curves collectively demonstrated the high predictive performance of YOLOv5l model in identifying the 192-ring object (Fig. 3, D to F). The final detection results showed that the established YOLOv5l model could accurately predict all the 192-ring objects presented (Fig. 3G). To further evaluate the classification performance of YOLOv5l model, we selected shape-resolved DNA origamis as the training data and used LabelMe software for data annotation. Differentially shaped DNA origamis (triangular, cross, and rectangular origamis) were assembled by folding M13 bacteriophage DNA with numerous staple strands, which were verified by agarose gel electrophoresis and AFM imaging (figs. S4 and S13). After training the AFM data with YOLOv5l model, a series of AFM images containing the above three types of origamis was input to evaluate the model performance in terms of target identification and classification (Fig. 3H and fig. S14). The detection results in Fig. 3I highlight the substantial advantages of YOLOv5l model in automated, time-/labor-saving, and accurate AFM data processing, as it can recognize and classify 370 DNA origamis from 10 AFM images within 1.21 s, a speed that is difficult to achieve manually (fig. S15).


### NDAM strategy for multilocus detection on long-stranded DNA
  Encouraged by the success of specific SMD on short-stranded DNA through the integration of DNA nanotag, NE, and ML, we further tested its applicability on long-stranded DNA. Circular viral phiX 174 ssDNA was selected as the target gene, which was converted into double-stranded form via primer extension to address the lack of structural stability and linearization during manipulation (Fig. 4A). Two types of nickases namely Cas9 nickase and NE 1 were chosen as the gene-editing tools to create site-specific nicks on phiX 174 dsDNA (Fig. 4B and fig. S16). Notably, for better comparison, the physical location of the recognition loci on the phiX 174 dsDNA was programmed to be the same for both nickases through the rational design of guide RNA (gRNA) molecules (Fig. 4B). It is observed in AFM images that the three predetermined loci of phiX 174 dsDNA were simultaneously labeled with triangular origamis in both cases, demonstrating the reliability of this nickase-based NDAM strategy (Fig. 4, C and D, and fig. S17). In addition, confusion matrix, precision, recall, and F1 score collectively confirmed the good prediction performance of YOLOv5l model for identifying three triangular origami–labeled phiX 174 dsDNA (phiX tri) (Fig. 4, E and F, and fig. S17). The above results demonstrated the feasibility of this NDAM strategy in detecting nickase-edited loci on long-stranded circular DNA.

  In addition to circular long-stranded DNA, we further explored the potential of this strategy for SMD on long-stranded linear DNA (Fig. 5A). Considering the potential off-target risks associated with Cas9 nickase systems, NEs were selected as the primary gene-editing tools for the following study. Three primer sets were designed to amplify lambda DNA, generating 10.00, 5.00, and 1.57 kb DNA fragments (termed gene 1, gene 2, and gene 3, respectively) (Fig. 5B). In particular, the amplified gene 1, gene 2, and gene 3 contain three, two, and one cleavage sites for NE 1, respectively. After NE 1–mediated gene editing, the products were detected by our method with triangular DNA origami as the tag. According to AFM imaging and measurement of contour distances between adjacent tags (Fig. 5, C to E, and figs. S18 to S21 and S23), all the NE 1 recognition sites on long DNA fragments were identified by this method. Furthermore, the reliability was proven by detecting the recognition sites of NE 2 and NE 3. Specifically, cross-shaped and rectangular DNA origami tags were used to label the NE 2– and NE 3–edited sites, respectively, enabling precise discrimination of different gene loci (Fig. 5E and figs. S22 and S24).

  On this basis, we further tested the feasibility of differently shaped DNA origamis as multiplex tags in simultaneous multilocus detection using different NEs to act on the same DNA fragment. Benefiting from the shape controllability of DNA origamis, each gene-editing site can be identified by the specially labeled shape-resolved DNA origami tag under AFM imaging (Fig. 5E). By comparing the theoretical and actual contour distances between the adjacent tags, it is concluded that this NE-based NDAM strategy can realize high-precision multilocus detection, which offers a promising tool for SMD of complex genes (figs. S25 to S29).


### NDAM strategy for SMD of KRAS (G12R) mutation
  KRAS gene is an oncogene that is frequently mutated in solid tumors, which has a mutation incidence of ~17% among patients with cancer, especially in PDAC (47–49) and CRC (50–52) with a high mutation frequency of ~90 and ~40%, respectively. For PDAC and CRC, G12R mutation as a common type of KRAS mutations can render RAS protein continuously active, leading to uncontrolled cell proliferation and tumorigenesis (53–55). Traditional analysis tools such as Sanger sequencing or pyrosequencing fail in identifying low-frequency mutations owing to their limited sensitivity. Therefore, it is urgent to develop efficient, affordable, and reliable detection techniques to identify these key mutations.

  Given the existence of NE 2 recognition sequence on G12R-mutated gene sequence, we applied the NDAM strategy to detect the G12R mutation. In the experiment, formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections from 37 patients with PDAC and 29 patients with CRC were sampled. First, DNA was extracted from the FFPE samples by spin-column method and subjected to PCR amplification to obtain sufficient target DNA fragments (Fig. 6A). However, the presence of residual organic solvents in the extracted DNA solution may affect the amplification efficiency. In this regard, we selected Phanta Flash Super-Fidelity DNA polymerase for amplification. As characterized by agarose gel electrophoresis, 2.26-kb target DNA fragment of KRAS gene was successfully amplified under the action of designed primers and DNA polymerase (fig. S30).

  Given that FFPE DNA undergoes substantial fragmentation during sample fixation and processing, we designed two sets of PCR primers to generate amplicons of distinct lengths to ensure the detection accuracy. Amplification using the forward and reverse PCR primers (denoted as F1 and R1, respectively) in the first set yielded 0.50-kb target DNA amplicon. Specifically, F1 is terminated with 15 adenylic acid residues (A) at its 5′ end, which introduced a polyadenylate [poly(A)] tail on 0.5-kb target DNA to facilitate the attachment of gold nanoparticle (AuNP). The forward and reverse primers in the second set were designated as F2 and R2, which were designed to yield 2.26-kb target DNA fragment (Fig. 6B). Subsequently, we introduced NEs to perform gene editing on these target DNA molecules, followed by inserted DNA-guided extension. For 0.50-kb target DNA, 5-nm AuNP served as a reference marker to interact with the terminal poly(A) while triangular DNA origami was used to localize the G12R mutation. As observed in AFM images (Fig. 6C), only AuNP labeling was observed in the samples without G12R mutation, while simultaneous labeling of AuNP and triangular DNA origami was observed in the case of G12R mutations. The contour distances between the adjacently labeled AuNP and triangular origami is in good agreement with the predicated value (Fig. 6C), proving the high accuracy of the DNA origami–based SMD method. Triangular origami labeling has also been demonstrated to accurately detect the G12R mutation on 2.26-kb target DNA (Fig. 6D). As a practical application of this method, we assessed the G12R mutation status in samples from 37 patients with PDAC and 29 patients with CRC. The detection results showed that six patients with PDAC had the G12R mutation, while no such mutation was identified in patients with CRC (Fig. 6E and figs. S31 to S35). The above patient samples were all verified by qPCR, demonstrating the high reliability of our method in single-molecule cancer diagnosis (Fig. 6F and figs. S36 to S49).


### SMD of p53 (R175H) mutation in patients with CRC by NDAM strategy
  For CRC, p53 (R175H) mutation is prone to pathological aggregation and disrupts p53 wild-type function, which fails to initiate cell cycle arrest or apoptosis under cell stress, causing genomic instability and tumor development (56–58). Given the important role of R175H mutation in p53 dysfunction and the existence of NE 3 recognition sequence in R175H mutation, we conducted an in-depth analysis of R175H mutation, aiming to reveal the potential genetic variation information in CRC (59–61). In this study, FFPE tissue sections of 22 patients with CRC were sampled, and 0.72-kb target DNA fragment containing R175 region was efficiently amplified using specifically designed primers (denoted as F3/R3) (Fig. 7, A and B). Subsequently, the amplified R175H mutation was identified by AFM based on the site-specific labeling of cross DNA origami. The detection accuracy was verified by counting the contour distance between the two colabeled origamis in AFM images (Fig. 7, C and D). In contrast, in the case of R175H-unmutated sample, only one cross DNA origami anchored on the sample was observed under AFM imaging (Fig. 7E). As determined by this approach, 2 of the 22 patients with CRC carried the R175H mutation (Fig. 7F and figs. S35, S50, and S51), which were all confirmed by Sanger sequencing, demonstrating the reliability of our diagnostic method (Fig. 7, G and H, and figs. S52 to S57). In addition, this DNA nanotag labeling strategy not only achieved high-precision diagnosis of G12R and R175H mutations but also realized disease risk prediction caused by these two gene mutations using differentially shaped DNA nanotags (Fig. 7I). In general, this method demonstrated high specificity and high sensitivity, which opens up avenues for the field of single-molecule disease diagnosis.


## DISCUSSION
  In this study, we integrated nickases, DNA nanotags, AFM, and ML to construct an innovative platform NDAM, which enables precise detection of specific genes and in vitro SMD of gene mutations. Compared with single-molecule fluorescence detection technology, NDAM strategy overcomes the shortcomings such as limited fluorophore choices, photobleaching, and spectral overlap. Meanwhile, it can detect disease-related mutations more rapidly than Sanger sequencing (which takes several days) (table S1). Unlike DSB-generating CRISPR-Cas9 systems that may induce large-scale gene rearrangement, nickases (Cas9 nickase and NEs) were chosen as molecular scissors due to their single-strand cleavage capability. These nickases have unique advantages: Cas9 nickase allows high-efficiency multiplex loci editing of any genomic region, while NEs demonstrate high recognition accuracy by cleaving single strands of dsDNA within asymmetric sequences. A series of self-assembled DNA nanostructures serves as shape-resolved nanotags for high-resolution AFM imaging. YOLOv5l-based algorithm enables automated and high-throughput object recognition, which notably simplifies AFM data processing. The feasibility of this analysis strategy for detecting specific genes was validated on both short-/long-chain and circular/linear DNA molecules (table S2).

  Furthermore, the clinical applicability of this strategy is proven in mutation detection of samples from patients with cancer. As for cancer molecular diagnosis, this study focused on two common malignant tumors (i.e., PDAC and CRC). After DNA extraction from clinical FFPE samples and region-of-interest amplification, KRAS (G12R) and p53 (R175H) mutations were precisely edited by site-specific NEs. Subsequently, the edited sites were labeled by shaped-resolved DNA nanotags with each shape corresponding to specific NE recognition site. Combining with high-resolution AFM and ML, the mutation sites of these key mutated genes were observed under AFM imaging and automatically identified by well-trained YOLOv5l model. As revealed by the experimental results, the detection accuracy of this method is comparable to that of Sanger sequencing and qPCR (table S3). Moreover, it enables high-specificity and high-sensitivity disease risk prediction using shape-resolved DNA nanotags.

  Despite notable achievements, the NDAM-based SMD strategy still has room: (i) further optimizing existing NEs and exploring new NEs to increase the diversity of editable sites in gene editing; (ii) developing more efficient and precise gene-editing tools with minimized off-target effect; (iii) integrating analysis process into an automated AFM detection platform to pursue automated, time-/labor-saving, and high-throughput detection; (iv) designing more shape-resolved DNA nanostructures to construct tag libraries, which will help to improve the broad applicability of disease diagnosis and achieve molecular-scale typing of different stages and types of diseases; (v) exploring a new strategy for determining the ratio of mutant–to–wild-type cells, which enables quantifying the proportion of mutant versus wild-type sequences in a sample.

  In summary, the combination of gene editing, multiplex DNA tags, imaging technology, and ML makes this NDAM platform attractive for mutation detection, which enriches the applications of AFM imaging. In addition, this method demonstrates the potential of disease risk prediction, providing a valuable strategy for screening, early diagnosis, prognosis, and new drug development.

### Preparation of DNA nanotags
  Construction of T20 DNA tetrahedron: Equal proportions (10 μl and 10 μM) of four ssDNA strands (denoted as S1, S2, S3, and S4) were added to 60 μl of tris-MgCl2 buffer [10 mM tris-HCl and 50 mM MgCl2 (pH 8.0)]. The solution was placed in a thermal cycler, heated at 95°C for 10 min, promptly cooled to 4°C, and kept for 20 min.

  Construction of T37 DNA tetrahedron: Equal proportions (10 μl and 10 μM) of eight ssDNA strands (denoted as A1, A2, B1, B2, C1, C2, D1, and D2) were added to 20 μl of tris-MgCl2 buffer [10 mM tris-HCl and 50 mM MgCl2 (pH 8.0)]. The solution was placed in a thermal cycler, heated at 95°C for 10 min, promptly cooled to 4°C, and kept for 20 min.

  Preparation of DNA origamis: Triangular, cross, and rectangular origamis were assembled following a previously reported procedure. M13 bacteriophage DNA and staple strands were mixed at a molar ratio of 1:5 in 1 × TAE-Mg2+ buffer [40 mM tris, 20 mM acetic acid (HAc), 12.5 mM Mg(Ac)2, and 2 mM EDTA (pH 8.0)]. Notably, some staple strands have sticky ends that can specifically capture DNA origamis at predetermined locations. Then, the mixture underwent a programmed annealing process from 95° to 20°C with a cooling rate of −1°C min−1 to form the desired structures. The resulting origamis were purified by agarose gel electrophoresis and lastly extracted using Freeze 'N Squeeze DNA Gel Extraction Spin Columns.


### Asymmetric PCR
  A total of 50 μl of PCR reaction mixture, which consisted of template DNA (1 μl and 0.01 μM), forward primer (4 μl and 10 μM), reverse primer (4 μl and 0.1 μM), LA Taq (5000 U ml−1 and 1 μl), 10 × LA PCR buffer II containing Mg2+ (5 μl), deoxynucleoside triphosphate (dNTP) mixture (4 μl and 10 mM), and H2O (31 μl), was freshly prepared on ice. Forward primer was phosphorylated at the 5′ end. Then, the PCR reaction proceeded in a thermal cycler with predenaturation at 95°C for 1 min and 30 cycles of amplification. Each cycle includes denaturation at 98°C for 10 s, annealing at 49.3°C for 20 s, and extension at 65°C for 10 s. The PCR process was completed after an extension step at 65°C for 1 min. Last, the PCR products were purified by PAGE and extracted using Freeze 'N Squeeze DNA Gel Extraction Spin Columns.


### Circularization of linear ssDNA
  First, 2 μl of linear ssDNA (1 μM) was mixed with 2.5 μl of DNA splint (100 μM) and 15.5 μl of 1 × TE buffer [10 mM tris-HCl and 1 mM EDTA (pH 7.8 to 8.2)]. The mixture was placed in a PCR instrument, incubated at 95°C for 5 min, and annealed from 95° to 20°C at a rate of −1°C min−1. Next, 2.5 μl of T4 DNA ligase (350,000 U ml−1) and 2.5 μl of 10 × T4 DNA ligase buffer [50 mM tris-HCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM adenosine 5′-triphosphate, and 10 mM dithiothreitol (pH 7.5)] were added, and the mixture was incubated at 16°C for 16 hours in a PCR instrument. Then, 3.5 μl of exonuclease I (5000 U ml−1) and 3.5 μl of exonuclease I buffer were added to the circularization system, which was further incubated at 37°C for 16 hours. Last, the obtained circular ssDNA was subjected to 10% denaturing PAGE and extracted by Freeze 'N Squeeze DNA Gel Extraction Spin Columns.


### Synthesis of cyclic dsDNA
  Template DNA (1 ng), primer (0.4 μM), LA Taq (5000 U ml−1, 1 μl), 10 × LA PCR buffer II containing Mg2+ (5 μl), and dNTP mixture (10 mM and 4 μl) were mixed in a total volume of 50 μl. Then, the PCR reaction was carried out in a thermal cycler with the following steps: predenaturation at 95°C for 1 min and extension at 55°C for 50 min.


### Site-specific cleavage of target DNA
  Site-specific cleavage of target DNA was carried out in an experimental system containing 1 μl of DNA (5 μg ml−1), 1.5 μl of NEs (10,000 U ml−1), 5 μl of 1× NEBuffer r3.1 (100 mM NaCl, 50 mM tris-HCl, and 10 mM MgCl2), recombinant albumin (100 μg ml−1, pH 7.9), and 42.5 μl of ultrapure water. The mixture was incubated in a thermal cycler at 37°C for 1 hour, followed by heating at 80°C for 20 min to deactivate the NEs.

### Conjugation of the sticky-ended exogenous DNA to the nicked dsDNA
  An experimental system (50 μl) containing the nicked DNA product (0.1 μg ml−1 and 25 μl), exogenous DNA (10 μM and 5 μl), Vent (exo-) DNA polymerase (2,000 U ml−1 and 2 μl), 10 × ThermoPol reaction buffer (5 μl), MgSO4 solution (100 mM and 3 μl), dNTP mixture (10 mM and 1 μl), and H2O (9 μl) was prepared. Then, the mixture was placed in a thermal cycler to perform primer extension with the following process: a hot start at 95°C for 1 min, annealing at 48°C for 40 s, and extension at 48°C for 30 min. The PCR amplification products were purified by agarose gel electrophoresis and then extracted using Freeze 'N Squeeze DNA Gel Extraction Spin Columns.


### Hybridization of DNA nanotags with the target DNA
  An experimental system (20 μl) containing the differentally shaped DNA nanotags (3 nM and 1 μl), 1 μl of target DNA (2.0 ng μl−1), 2 μl of 10 × TAE-Mg2+ buffer [40 mM tris, 20 mM HAc, 12.5 mM Mg(Ac)2, and 2 mM EDTA (pH 8.0)], and ultrapure water (15 μl) was incubated at 45°C for 3 min and then cooling to 20°C at a rate of −0.5°C min−1. The as-prepared products were stored at 4°C for further characterization.


### AFM imaging
  The samples were diluted to ~3 ng μl−1 using 1× TAE-Mg2+ buffer. Ten microliters of the samples were dropped onto a freshly cleaved mica surface. After 3 min of incubation, the mica surface was gently washed thrice with ultrapure water to rinse off the residual salt and then dried under a gentle airflow from an ear-washing bulb. Last, the dried mica surface was scanned by a MultiMode 8 (Bruker) instrument. Specifically, the DNA tetrahedron–labeled samples were scanned in tapping mode (fluid) with an ScanAsyst-Fluid+ probe, while DNA origami–labeled samples were scanned in ScanAsyst Air mode with a ScanAsyst-Air probe.


### Measurement of contour distances between adjacent DNA nanotags
  (i) Open NanoScope Analysis 1.0 software, click the “File” option in the menu bar, and then select “Open” to import the AFM image file. (ii) Select the “Line” tool displayed as a straight line in the left panel of the software interface. (iii) Left-click to define the starting point of the curve to be measured. Drag the mouse along the curve to draw a tangent line, and then release the mouse button to complete the curve drawing. (iv) Detailed curve information, including contour distance, will be displayed in the right panel of the software interface.


### Agarose gel electrophoresis
  A total of 0.4 g of agarose powder was dispersed into 43 ml of 1× TAE-Mg2+ buffer, followed by the addition of Gelblue dye (5 μl). The mixture was then heated to boiling, poured into a gel casting tray, inserted with a well-forming comb, and allowed to solidify at room temperature. Last, agarose gel electrophoresis was carried out in 1× TAE-Mg2+ buffer at a constant voltage of 90 V for 50 min.


### Urea–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis
  Two milliliters of 30% acrylamide, 1.5 ml of 5× TBE buffer [450 mM tris-borate buffer and 10 mM EDTA (pH 8.0)], 55 μl of 10% ammonium persulfate, 5 μl of tetramethylethylenediamine, 3.6024 g of urea, and 3.94 ml of water were combined and vortexed until thoroughly mixed. The mixture was then poured into a gel casting tray to allow solidification. Last, the electrophoresis was performed in 1× TBE buffer at a constant voltage of 80 V for 45 min.


### ML-powered automated analysis of AFM images
  (i) Acquire an image dataset of AFM images of the target objects. (ii) Use LableMe tool to manually annotate the target objects in AFM images within the image dataset to create the labeled dataset. (iii) Establish a fusion object detection algorithm based on the YOLOv5 model. (iv) Train the fusion object detection algorithm using the labeled dataset to optimize the object detection model. (v) Input the AFM image to be detected into this trained model for object recognition and classification, which outputs detection results along with confidence scores.


### PCR amplification of DNA from paraffin sections of patients with PDAC and CRC
  DNA was extracted from FFPE samples of patients with CRC and PDAC according to the manufacturer’s instructions for the QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue kit. PCR reaction mixture with a total volume of 50 μl was prepared by mixing template DNA (1 ng), forward and reverse PCR primers (each at 0.4 μM), Phanta Flash Super-Fidelity DNA polymerase (1,000 U ml−1 and 1 μl), and 2 × Phanta Flash buffer (25 μl) together. Then, PCR reaction was performed in a thermal cycler according to the following procedure: predenaturation at 98°C for 30 s, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 98°C for 10 s, annealing at 52.5°C for 10 s, and extension at 72°C for 20 s. Last, the PCR protocol was completed after an extension step at 72°C for 1 min.


### qPCR assay of the KRAS (G12R) and p53 (R175H) mutations in FFPE samples of patients with CRC and PDAC

### Cas9 nickase–mediated gene editing on phiX 174 dsDNA
  Equal amounts of trans-activating RNA (TracrRNA) and CRISPR RNA (crRNA) were mixed in a buffer solution containing 20 mM tris, 10 mM NaCl, and 5 mM MgCl2. The resulting mixture was heated at 95°C for 10 min and then cooled slowly to 25°C at a rate of 0.1°C s−1 to yield gRNA. Three gRNAs, namely, gRNA1, gRNA2, and gRNA3, responsible for editing the corresponding site on phiX 174 dsDNA were mixed together and subsequently diluted to a final concentration of 300 nM using nuclease-free water. Afterward, Cas9 nickase (1 μM) was incubated with gRNAs (300 nM) for 15 min to produce ribonucleoprotein complexes. Last, Cas9 nickase–mediated gene editing on phiX 174 dsDNA was operated by incubating with these ribonucleoprotein complexes for 60 min.
