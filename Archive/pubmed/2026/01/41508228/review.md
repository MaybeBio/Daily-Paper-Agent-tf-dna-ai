## Reviewer

- **Overall assessment** The abstract presents a plausible and potentially useful finding that CcWRKY40 regulates alkaloid biosynthesis by activating two pathway genes. However, the evidence as presented is insufficient to fully evaluate the robustness of the claims. Key experimental validations, quantitative data, and controls are not described. The work is of interest to the specialized community studying medicinal plant secondary metabolism, but its broader significance and technical completeness cannot be assessed from the abstract alone.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in plant secondary metabolism, transcription factor regulation, and medicinal plant biotechnology would be interested. The identification of a regulatory node controlling alkaloid biosynthesis in Coptis chinensis could inform metabolic engineering or breeding strategies for improved alkaloid production.
- **Major strengths** The study addresses a relevant gap in understanding the regulatory layer of alkaloid biosynthesis. The combination of multiple assays (yeast one-hybrid, dual luciferase, subcellular localization, tissue-specific expression) provides a multi-angle approach. The identification of two target genes with a defined binding domain offers a concrete mechanistic hypothesis.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **Concern ID** R1-M1
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Technical soundness
  - **Claim pointer** CcWRKY40 binds to the promoters of CcCNMT and Cc4'OMT and activates their transcription.
  - **Evidence pointer** Abstract, yeast one-hybrid and dual-luciferase assays; location not provided
  - **Concern** The abstract states that CcWRKY40 binds to and activates the promoters of CcCNMT and Cc4'OMT, but no quantitative results are provided. It is unclear whether the activation is statistically significant, what magnitude of activation was observed, or whether appropriate controls (e.g., empty vector, mutated promoter) were included.
  - **Why it matters** Without quantitative data and controls, the claim of transcriptional activation cannot be distinguished from weak or non-specific binding. This is the central mechanistic conclusion of the study.
  - **Resolution test** Provide representative data from yeast one-hybrid and dual-luciferase assays, including fold activation, statistical significance, and negative controls. If possible, include promoter mutation experiments to confirm binding specificity.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M2
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Technical soundness
  - **Claim pointer** The binding sites were predicted at the core domain (RKYGQK) of CcWRKY40.
  - **Evidence pointer** Abstract, molecular docking; location not provided
  - **Concern** The binding site prediction is based solely on molecular docking, which is a computational method with limited predictive accuracy. No experimental validation (e.g., electrophoretic mobility shift assay, site-directed mutagenesis) is described.
  - **Why it matters** The claim that the RKYGQK domain is the functional binding site is a specific mechanistic assertion. If not validated experimentally, it remains speculative and could mislead downstream studies.
  - **Resolution test** Provide experimental validation of the predicted binding site, such as mutagenesis of the RKYGQK domain followed by binding and activation assays, or an electrophoretic mobility shift assay with the predicted DNA motif.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M3
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Reproducibility
  - **Claim pointer** CcWRKY40 was most highly expressed in the fine roots in 1- to 5-year-old C. chinensis.
  - **Evidence pointer** Abstract, RT-PCR; location not provided
  - **Concern** The abstract reports tissue-specific expression but does not provide any quantitative data, such as relative expression levels, statistical comparisons, or the number of biological replicates. The claim of "most highly expressed" is therefore not verifiable.
  - **Why it matters** Tissue-specific expression is used to infer biological relevance. Without statistical support, the conclusion that fine roots are the primary site of action is not robust.
  - **Resolution test** Provide quantitative RT-PCR data with error bars, statistical tests, and replicate numbers. Clarify whether expression was compared across all tissues and ages.
