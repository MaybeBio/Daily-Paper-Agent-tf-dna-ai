# Harnessing nanomedicine to target NF-κB signalling in cancer: at the intersection of inflammatory signalling, metabolic reprogramming, and therapeutic innovation


## Abstract
  The nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signalling pathway is a pivotal orchestrator of inflammation, tumour progression, immune modulation, and metabolic reprogramming within the dynamic landscape of the tumour microenvironment (TME). Aberrant and sustained activation of NF-κB is frequently associated with tumour proliferation, resistance to therapy, and poor clinical outcomes. However, conventional NF-κB inhibitors face significant limitations, including systemic toxicity, poor bioavailability, and lack of tumour specificity. Nanomedicine offers a transformative approach to overcome these barriers by enabling the targeted delivery of NF-κB modulators, such as small molecules, RNA interference agents, and gene-editing systems, directly to tumour sites. Recent advances in nanocarriers, including liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, exosomes, and dendrimers, have demonstrated improved therapeutic indices, enhanced stability, and reduced off-target effects. This review highlights the convergence of nanotechnology, inflammatory signalling, and cancer metabolism, with a focus on how nanomedicine can precisely modulate NF-κB signalling to reprogramme tumour-associated macrophages and remodel the TME. It also discusses preclinical evidence supporting NF-κB-targeted nanomedicine, innovative delivery strategies, and the translational challenges that impede clinical adoption. Collectively, these insights highlight the potential of nanomedicine in advancing NF-κB-targeted cancer therapy by enhancing drug specificity, safety, and therapeutic responsiveness. Future developments integrating artificial intelligence, theranostic design, and personalised nanomedicine are expected to accelerate clinical translation and establish NF-κB-targeted nanotherapeutics as a cornerstone of next-generation precision oncology.

## Background
  Cancer remains a significant global health burden, with an estimated 20 million new cases and 9.7 million cancer-related deaths reported in 2022 [1]. Alarmingly, this figure is projected to rise to 35 million new cases annually by 2050, highlighting the urgent need for comprehensive and targeted strategies that encompass prevention, early detection, and therapeutic intervention [1]. Metastases are estimated to account for the vast majority of cancer-related deaths, with studies indicating that it is responsible for up to 90% of mortality among cancer patients [2]. Cancer pathogenesis is driven by a multifaceted interplay of mechanisms, including uncontrolled cellular proliferation, immune evasion, chronic inflammation, and intratumoral heterogeneity. These processes are fuelled by metabolic reprogramming, subcellular alterations, and epigenetic modifications such as chromatin remodelling [3–7]. The tumour microenvironment (TME) plays a pivotal role in promoting key cancer hallmarks such as sustained proliferation, resistance to apoptosis, angiogenesis, invasion, and immune escape, making it an attractive target for therapeutic development [8]. Despite considerable advances in the molecular understanding of cancer, the disease continues to outpace existing treatment modalities in terms of its complexity and adaptability [9]. Historically, cancer therapy was confined to surgical resection and cytotoxic chemotherapy. However, the identification of oncogenes and tumour suppressor genes catalysed the emergence of targeted therapies aimed at specific molecular drivers [10]. More recently, the advent of next-generation sequencing and integrative multi-omics approaches has facilitated the delineation of intricate signalling networks, paving the way for personalised and more precise therapeutic interventions [10]. The major signalling pathways implicated in cancer progression include the p53, phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/Protein kinase B (Akt)/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), RAS/mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), Wnt/β-catenin, and nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) pathways [10–14]. Therapeutic modulators that target these pathways have reached various stages of development, with some, such as mTOR, MEK and RAS inhibitors, already approved by the FDA, while others are currently being evaluated in preclinical and clinical trials [15–19]. Among these molecular targets, dysregulation of the NF-κB signalling pathway has garnered considerable attention due to its central role in linking inflammation, immunity and tumorigenesis [20, 21]. Alteration of this pathway is not only associated with cancer but also implicated in a wide spectrum of pathological conditions, including autoimmune diseases, chronic inflammation, and cardiometabolic disorders [21–26].

  NF-κB is a pivotal transcription factor that regulates the expression of genes involved in cellular proliferation, inflammation, and immune responses [27–29]. In addition, NF-κB modulates the expression of extracellular matrix components, such as matrix metallopeptidases (MMPs), and pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), both of which contribute to endothelial dysfunction [30, 31]. During tumour initiation, NF-κB function as an oncogene after cellular immortalisation and facilitates immune evasion of Ras-induced-transformed cells, thereby promoting tumour progression [32]. NF-κB functions through two principal signalling cascades, the canonical and non-canonical pathways. The canonical pathway is rapid and broadly responsive to stimuli, involving the inhibitor of nuclear factor-κB (IκB) kinase (IKK)-mediated degradation of IκB, which permits nuclear translocation of NF-κB and activation of target gene transcription. Conversely, the non-canonical pathway is activated by a specific subset of tumor necrosis factor (TNF) receptors and regulates more specialised cellular responses [33–36]. Several therapeutic strategies have been developed to modulate aberrant NF-κB signalling in cancer, including monoclonal antibodies, IKK inhibitors, proteasome inhibitors, phytochemicals, tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), agents blocking nuclear translocation and DNA binding, non-coding RNAs, immunotherapies, and chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) cell therapies [37–45]. The therapeutic potential of these agents is often curtailed by inefficient tumour targeting and systemic dissemination, leading to unintended toxicity and subpar treatment outcomes [46–50]. For example, bortezomib, a first-generation proteasome inhibitor that suppresses NF-κB signalling and is approved for haematological malignancies, is associated with dose-limiting peripheral neuropathy, for which no approved prophylactic or therapeutic interventions currently exist [51, 52]. Other adverse effects include thrombocytopenia and cardiovascular complications such as congestive heart failure and hypotension [53]. Moreover, antibodies that target programmed cell death 1 (PD-1) and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4), both of which are functionally linked to NF-κB signalling, have demonstrated potent anticancer activity. However, their use is frequently associated with immune-related adverse events (irAEs), including colitis, dermatitis, hepatitis, neuropathies, and endocrinopathies [54–58]. A comprehensive analysis of 14 phase I–III clinical trials revealed that 64.2% of patients with advanced melanoma experienced irAEs following treatment with CTLA-4 inhibitor ipilimumab [56, 59]. Non-coding RNA-based therapies have also encountered clinical setbacks, primarily due to off-target effects, recognition by toll-like receptors (TLRs), poor cellular uptake, and instability of oligonucleotides [60]. Additionally, CAR-T cell therapies are hampered by severe toxicities, limited efficacy in solid tumours, resistance mechanisms in B-cell malignancies, antigen escape, inadequate persistence and trafficking, and the presence of an immunosuppressive TME [61]. TKIs, while effective, may produce adverse effects in multiple organs, including the liver, kidneys, lungs, skin, thyroid, and heart [47]. The cumulative drawbacks of current modalities demand the exploration of novel and mechanistically precise interventions to safely and effectively inhibit NF-κB signalling in malignancies.

  In recent decades, the integration of nanotechnology with disciplines such as biology, materials science, and pharmacology has led to the development of innovative strategies for cancer treatment [62–64]. Nanotechnology has addressed several limitations associated with conventional chemotherapy by improving drug solubility, enhancing bioavailability, and facilitating tumour-selective delivery [62–64]. For example, nanocarriers typically less than 500 nm in size, enhance the efficacy and stability of therapeutic agents by preferentially accumulating in target tissues, thereby minimising systemic toxicity [65, 66]. Nanomedicine utilises a diverse range of nanomaterials, including liposomes, dendrimers, metallic nanoparticles, and quantum dots, to facilitate controlled drug release, targeted delivery, and alternative therapeutic modalities such as photothermal and photodynamic therapies [67, 68]. These platforms have been utilised to deliver chemotherapeutic agents, nucleic acids, and immunotherapies directly to tumours. To date, over 80 nanomedicines are under investigation in more than 200 clinical trials, with more than 15 nanomedicine-based cancer therapies already approved for clinical use worldwide [69]. Employing nanomedicine to target the NF-κB signalling pathway in cancer is an effective and rational approach to address the limitations associated with conventional NF-κB inhibitors. This therapeutic approach has progressed beyond the preclinical stage, with several nanocarrier systems, particularly liposomal and polymeric formulations of curcumin, currently under evaluation in clinical trials. Building upon these advancements, this review will focus on current and emerging nanomedicine-based strategies designed to modulate NF-κB signalling in cancer. Given the central role of NF-κB in tumour initiation, progression, immune evasion, and therapy resistance, targeting this pathway through nanotechnological interventions offers a potential avenue for enhancing treatment specificity and efficacy while mitigating systemic toxicity. This review will encompass various nanocarriers engineered for the delivery of small-molecule inhibitors, nucleic acids, and immunomodulatory agents aimed at disrupting NF-κB activation within the TME. This review also comprehensively explores how nanomedicine-based strategies targeting the NF-κB signalling pathway can modulate inflammatory, metabolic, and immune processes in cancer, highlighting their therapeutic potential, biopharmaceutical considerations, and translational challenges toward clinical application.


## NF-κB signalling pathway
  The NF-κB signalling pathway is critically involved in regulating cellular processes central to cancer biology, including proliferation, inflammation, apoptosis, and metastasis [70, 71]. The seminal work of Sen and Baltimore first described this protein as a transcription factor present in B lymphocytes, identifying it through its specific binding to a conserved DNA element [72, 73]. The functionality of this transcription factor is defined by its highly selective nature, whereby it activates a restricted set of target genes [73, 74]. The NF-κB family of transcription factors comprises five members, namely, RelA (p65), RelB, c-Rel, p50, and p52, which form various homodimeric and heterodimeric complexes that selectively regulate distinct sets of target genes [73, 74]. The transcriptional activity of these proteins is mediated by their binding to a specific consensus sequence, 5′-GGGRNWYYCC-3′. This motif, termed the κB site, is a characteristic feature of the upstream regulatory regions of their various target genes [72, 75, 76]. Among the NF-κB family members, RelA (p65), RelB, and c-Rel contain a transcriptional activation domain (TAD), enabling them to directly initiate the transcription of target genes upon nuclear translocation [77, 78]. In contrast, p50 and p52 lack a TAD and are generally considered transcriptionally repressive when forming homodimers. However, their transcriptional activity can be restored through heterodimerisation with TAD-containing NF-κB subunits or through interaction with specific transcriptional co-activators [77, 78]. In addition, p50 and p52 homodimers modulate the activation threshold of target genes either by competing with TAD-containing NF-κB subunits or by constitutively binding to κB sites, thereby exerting a dual repressive function [77, 78]. A central aspect of NF-κB signalling regulation is mediated by the IκB family of inhibitory proteins. IκB proteins, including IκBα, IκBβ, IκBɛ, IκBζ, Bcl-3 (B-cell lymphoma 3), and IκBNS, as well as the precursor proteins p100 (NF-κB2) and p105 (NF-κB1), are characterised by the presence of multiple ankyrin repeat (AnkRs) domains [77, 78]. IκBα, the most extensively studied member of the IκB family, undergoes rapid degradation during canonical NF-κB signalling, leading to the release of NF-κB dimers, primarily the p50:p65 heterodimer [79–81]. The NF-κB signalling system is principally divided into two distinct routes, the canonical and the non-canonical. The canonical pathway activation involves the assembly of signalling complexes that link upstream signal-generating components to the IKK complex, with ubiquitin modifications serving as critical scaffolding elements [38]. Phosphorylation of IκB proteins by the IKKβ (IKK1) targets them for proteasomal degradation, thereby releasing NF-κB dimers to translocate into the nucleus and activate target gene expression [38, 73, 77, 78]. In the cytoplasm, IKKβ forms a complex with IKKα (IKK2) and NEMO (IKKγ), constituting the core of the IKK complex [36]. Activation of the IKKβ is stimulated upon ligand binding by cytokine receptors, such as the TNFR and interleukin-1 receptor (IL-1R), as well as pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), including TLR4, and antigen receptors [38]. The non-canonical NF-κB signalling pathway is primarily activated by a subset of TNFR superfamily members, including CD40, BAFF-R, LTβR, and receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa B (RANK) [82]. Unlike the canonical pathway, it relies on the stabilisation and activation of NF-κB-inducing kinase (NIK), which plays a central regulatory role. Under basal conditions, NIK is constitutively degraded via a complex involving TNF receptor-associated factor 2 (TRAF2), TNF receptor-associated factor 3 (TRAF3), and cellular inhibitors of apoptosis proteins (cIAP1/2) [82]. Upon receptor stimulation, TRAF3 is degraded, leading to the accumulation of NIK and subsequent phosphorylation and activation of IKKα homodimers. This triggers the processing of the p100 precursor into p52, which then forms transcriptionally active heterodimers with RelB and translocates to the nucleus to regulate gene expression [82]. Termination of NF-κB signalling involves the nuclear export and degradation of NF-κB subunits, along with the dissociation of transcriptional co-activators [77, 78]. NF-κB also induces the expression of IκBα, which is newly synthesised and binds to NF-κB dimers, sequestering them in the cytoplasm. This mechanism contributes to the attenuation of transcriptional activity and constitutes a key component of the negative feedback regulation within the NF-κB signalling pathway [83–86]. These pathways are not independent but are intricately interconnected through complex crosstalk mechanisms (Fig. 1). The interplay between the canonical and non-canonical NF-κB pathways involves the indirect crosstalk via gene induction, dimerisation of various NF-κB subunits, upstream signalling crosstalk, and NIK activation of canonical NF-κB [87, 88]. This dynamic interplay regulates inflammatory and survival signalling in cancer cells, thereby promoting tumour progression and therapeutic resistance [87, 88].

## Role of NF-κB signalling pathway in cancer
  Aberrant activation of NF-κB is frequently observed across multiple cancer types and is associated with enhanced tumour progression, metastatic potential, and therapeutic resistance [70, 71]. The NF-κB signalling pathway can be activated by a diverse array of stimuli, including extrinsic factors such as viral and bacterial components, carcinogens, and cytokines, as well as intrinsic factors such as DNA damage, oncogene activation, cellular stress and hypoxia [77, 78]. One of the primary causes of aberrant NF-κB activation is the disruption of its negative feedback loop, resulting in the constitutive expression of target genes [89]. The NF-κB signalling pathway regulates several hallmarks of cancer. It promotes cell proliferation by inducing cyclins and other proto-oncogenes, and modulates apoptosis by balancing the expression of genes involved in cell survival and programmed cell death [38, 70, 90–92]. NF-κB promotes angiogenesis in cancer by regulating the expression of proangiogenic factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), placental growth factor (PGF), and platelet derived growth factor (PDGF), adhesion molecules, namely intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1) and VCAM-1, pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor- α (TNF-α) and IL-6, MMPs, and other proteins such as HIF-1α, OPA-1, etc [38, 93, 94]. NF-κB-induced cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 mediate reciprocal crosstalk among tumour-associated macrophages (TAMs), fibroblasts, and malignant cells, establishing a self-sustaining inflammatory niche that promotes tumour survival and immune suppression [95]. NF-κB also promotes cancer metastasis by inducing the expression of epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT)-related genes such as SNAIL, twist family bHLH transcription factor 1 (TWIST1), and cadherin 2 (CDH2), as well as metastasis-associated proteins including MTA-2 [96, 97]. It further regulates cell adhesion molecules such as integrins and selectins, and contributes to the formation of the pre-metastatic niche [96, 97]. Cytokines activated by NF-κB further contribute to the establishment of an immunosuppressive TME, affecting not only cancer cells but also various cellular components such as CAFs, MDSCs, TAMs, etc., within the TME [38]. In addition, NF-κB regulates the reprogramming of cellular metabolism by modulating enzymes such as 6-phosphofructo-2-kinase/fructose-2,6-biphosphatase 3 (PFKFB3), glutamate dehydrogenase 1 (GDH1), carboxylesterase 1 (CES1), glutamine-fructose-6-phosphate transaminase 2 (GFPT2), etc., which are involved in metabolic pathways including glycolysis, glutaminolysis, oxidative phosphorylation, etc., particularly under nutrient-deprived conditions [20]. Moreover, this pathway exhibits persistent and aberrant activation in cancer stem cells, where it plays a crucial role in maintaining their key properties of self-renewal and a stem-cell-like phenotype [98, 99]. Persistent activation of NF-κB promotes cell survival and inhibits apoptosis by modulating the expression of pro- and anti-apoptotic proteins, including caspase-8 and FADD-like apoptosis regulator (c-FLIP), Bcl-2, and Bcl-2-like 1 (Bcl-xL), thereby contributing to the development of therapeutic resistance [100, 101]. Therapeutic targeting of the NF-κB signalling cascade has shown considerable promise across multiple disease contexts, notably in cancer. Given its central role in regulating inflammation, cell survival, immune modulation, and therapeutic resistance, the NF-κB signalling cascade has emerged as a compelling target in cancer therapy [36]. Conventional strategies targeting the inhibition of NF-κB have demonstrated therapeutic potential. However, challenges such as systemic toxicity, poor specificity, and limited bioavailability have hindered their clinical success [49, 50]. These limitations highlight the need for more precise and effective delivery systems. Several strategies have been proposed to enhance the targeting of NF-κB signalling, including the use of multi-targeted NF-κB inhibitors, improving the safety profile, bioavailability, and stability of small-molecule therapeutics, increasing drug specificity, and harnessing the potential of naturally derived compounds [38]. In this context, nanomedicine provides a promising platform for modulating NF-κB signalling with enhanced specificity and reduced off-target effects. The following section examines recent developments in nanotechnology-based approaches aimed at selectively targeting the NF-κB pathway in cancer.


## Nanomedicine approaches for targeting NF-κB signalling pathway in cancer
  Nanoparticles are a class of agents with a size in the range of nanoscale, ranging from 1 to 100 nm [102]. It is also defined as “nano-objects with all external dimensions in the nanoscale, where the lengths of the longest and the shortest axes of the nano-object do not differ significantly” by The International Organization for Standardization. However, if there is a change in the dimension of more than three times, it is termed as nanofibers or nanoplates [102]. It exists in different shapes, sizes, structures and compositions, and can be either crystalline or amorphous. It is generally synthesised using bottom-up and top-down approaches. These particles exhibit various physical, optical, magnetic, mechanical, electrical, and chemical properties at the nanoscale compared to their bulk counterparts due to their composition, crystallography, surface charge, surface area, and quantum confinement effects [102, 103]. Nanoparticles are generally classified based on their composition, including organic, inorganic, and carbon-based materials, dimensions, pore dimensions, origin, and potential toxicity, as discussed elsewhere [102, 103]. Nanoparticle utilisation has applications in various fields, including medicine, the environment, military, energy, electronics, semiconductors, scientific instruments, water treatment, catalysis, and agriculture [104–112]. In cancer therapy, nanomedicine as therapeutic agents possess advantages such as enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect protection, drug protection, prolonged drug release, overcoming biological barriers, selective targeting, enhanced solubility and biocompatibility, bypassing drug resistance, enabling multi drug delivery, combination therapy, theranostic property, precise activation of therapeutic agents, large specific area, electromagnetic properties, etc. as discussed elsewhere [69, 113]. Given the pivotal role of NF-κB in promoting various hallmarks of cancer, the subsequent sections will focus on nanotherapeutic strategies developed to target this critical signalling pathway (Fig. 2).

### Overview of nanomaterials in cancer drug delivery
  The development of nanomedicines targeting the NF-κB pathway leverages a diverse range of nanomaterials, each with unique physicochemical properties, as delivery systems to enhance the therapeutic precision and stability of NF-κB-targeted agents in cancer. These nanocarriers are designed to overcome the limitations of conventional small-molecule drugs, such as poor solubility, rapid degradation, off-target effects, and low tumour accumulation. Based on their composition, nanoparticles can generally be classified as organic, inorganic, and carbon-based [102]. Organic nanoparticles, such as liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, dendrimers, and micelles, are primarily composed of biodegradable and biocompatible materials [123, 124]. These systems can encapsulate drugs, protecting them from enzymatic degradation and facilitating controlled or stimuli-responsive release [102, 123]. Lipid- and polymer-based nanocarriers, including those made from PLGA, PEG, or chitosan, can be functionalised with tumour-specific ligands or antibodies, thereby improving cellular uptake and targeted delivery of NF-κB inhibitors [125, 126]. Inorganic nanoparticles such as gold, silica, zinc oxide, and superparamagnetic iron oxide possess unique optical, magnetic, and electronic properties [67, 123, 127]. These properties make them suitable not only as drug carriers but also as diagnostic and imaging agents. Their structural stability and ease of surface modification allow for co-delivery of multiple therapeutic agents and real-time monitoring of NF-κB activity, making them highly suitable for theranostic applications [128, 129]. However, the risk of long-term accumulation and cytotoxicity requires careful assessment of their safety profiles [102]. Carbon-based nanoparticles, such as carbon nanotubes, quantum dots, graphene oxide, and fullerenes, have recently gained prominence due to their exceptional mechanical strength, high surface area, and electrical conductivity [130, 131]. These nanostructures facilitate efficient drug loading, penetration of biological membranes, and modulation of intracellular signalling pathways, including NF-κB. Moreover, functionalisation of carbon nanomaterials with hydrophilic polymers or biomolecules enhances their solubility, biocompatibility, and tumour-targeting efficiency [132, 133]. These materials exhibit low toxicity and excellent biocompatibility, rendering them attractive candidates for use in drug delivery and tissue engineering [102, 134]. Together, these nanomaterial classes provide a versatile toolbox for designing NF-κB-targeted nanotherapeutics that integrate selective delivery, imaging capability, and multifunctional therapeutic potential to address the complex biological landscape of cancer.


### Targeting strategies of nanomedicine in cancer
  Traditional chemotherapeutics often lack specificity, resulting in systemic toxicity and reduced efficacy [135]. Nanocarriers overcome these limitations by exploiting tumour-specific physiological and molecular features that enhance drug localisation and retention [136]. Understanding these targeting strategies is fundamental to designing nanomedicines that can precisely modulate critical signalling pathways such as NF-κB in cancer. The preferential accumulation of therapeutic agents within the tumour is primarily achieved through two distinct strategies, namely active and passive targeting [137–139]. Active targeting is a strategy that relies on the surface modification of nanocarriers with high-affinity ligands such as antibodies, peptides, aptamers, or small molecules to selectively recognise and bind to cognate receptors or antigens that are characteristically overexpressed on the surface of cancer cells, thereby facilitating efficient cellular internalisation via receptor-mediated endocytosis [140–143]. However, passive targeting that takes advantage of the unique physiological features of specific tissues, such as tumours, to enhance the effective accumulation of nanoparticles [141, 144, 145]. This utilises the enhanced permeability and retention effect, in which the tumour blood vessels, capillaries, and lymphatic drainage are abnormal, leading to a leaky tumour vasculature that permits the entry and accumulation of macromolecules and nanoparticles within the tumour tissues [146–149]. In addition, the effectiveness of the EPR effect is influenced by complex interactions between tumour cells and the surrounding stromal compartment, as well as by the aberrant production of cytokines and angiogenic mediators such as VEGF, HIF-1α, nitric oxide, interleukins, interferons, etc., within the TME [69, 147]. Moreover, the physicochemical characteristics of nanocarriers, including particle size, surface properties, biocompatibility, and morphology, also critically determine their EPR-dependent accumulation and retention in tumour tissues [150–152]. Despite the presence of the EPR effect, the overall permeability of tumour endothelium remains a major limiting factor for the uptake of macromolecules such as antibodies, resulting in intratumoral antibody concentrations that are typically 100-1000 times lower than those observed in plasma [147, 153]. The abnormal tumour vasculature, high interstitial pressure, growth-induced solid stress, and solid stress from the abnormal matrix exert greater resistance, referred to as the capillary wall’s resistance, when compared to the tumour interstitium as discussed elsewhere [147, 154]. In addition, the magnitude of the EPR effect exhibits considerable variability, not only between patients but also across different tumour types and even within the same tumour or patient over time [69, 155]. This is due to the heterogeneous and complex nature of tumour pathophysiology, including type, tumour aetiology, size, location, microenvironment, stage, vascular density, and status of blood perfusion [69, 155, 156]. It has also been reported that the EPR effect is a dynamic rather than a static phenomenon, characterised by transient and fluctuating features [69]. Several strategies have been investigated to augment the EPR effect, thereby overcoming its inherent heterogeneity and improving the often-suboptimal accumulation of nanoparticles in tumours [157, 158]. These interventions include both pharmacological approaches, such as the administration of vasoactive agents like angiotensin-II and TNF-α, and physical modalities, such as the application of hyperthermia, radiotherapy, or sonoporation to the tumour site [69, 159–161]. Furthermore, several EPR-independent strategies have been developed to enhance targeted delivery, including the exploitation of overexpressed vascular markers such as VEGFR2, integrins αvβ3, and CD105 within the TME. Additional approaches comprise cellular hitchhiking using living cells such as leukocytes, stem cells, or engineered erythrocytes, as well as iRGD-mediated tumour targeting and locoregional administration of nanomedicines [69].

  Apart from this, nanocarriers can be functionalised on the surface with various targeting ligands such as antibodies, antibody fragments, proteins, peptides, aptamers, small molecules, and carbohydrates that can bind selectively to their receptors, leading to increased retention in tumour tissues and enhanced internalisation in tumour cells [69, 139]. In addition, the capacity for enhanced tumour-specific targeting can be conferred upon nanocarriers by coating them with cell plasma membranes. By retaining the surface adhesion molecules of their source cells, such as stem cells, blood cells, fibroblasts, cancer cells or bacteria, these biomimetic systems can engage in homotypic or heterotypic interactions within the TME [69]. The ability to target specific intracellular organelles represents a major advancement beyond conventional tissue- and cell-level approaches, characterising the third generation of nanomedicines [162, 163]. These nanomedicines offer superior therapeutic outcomes by delivering payloads directly to the intracellular site of action, allowing for greater efficacy at reduced dosages and, most significantly, providing a powerful mechanism to circumvent or even reverse multidrug resistance [164, 165]. Consequently, these nanocarriers are designed to facilitate endosomal escape and ensure robust delivery to organelles such as the nucleus, lysosomes, mitochondria, Golgi apparatus, and endoplasmic reticulum [164, 166]. Moreover, superior therapeutic outcomes, including high tumour accumulation, deep tissue penetration, efficient cellular internalisation, and precise organelle localisation, can be achieved through the dynamic, multi-stage targeting of cancer nanomedicines [69, 167, 168]. This advanced approach utilises nanocarriers engineered to undergo specific physicochemical transformations in response to either endogenous stimuli within the TME, such as changes in pH, redox gradients, enzyme concentrations, etc., or exogenous physical triggers such as temperature variations, magnetic fields, ultrasound, light, etc., thereby enabling functions such as size reduction, charge conversion, or the exposure of concealed targeting ligands [69, 169]. Building upon these targeting principles, nanomedicine approaches can be strategically designed to modulate the NF-κB signalling pathway, a central regulator of tumour progression and therapy resistance. By integrating passive accumulation within tumour tissues and active molecular recognition of cancer-specific receptors, these nanosystems achieve enhanced localisation, internalisation, and controlled release of therapeutic agents. Such multi-layered targeting not only improves the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profiles of NF-κB inhibitors but also minimises off-target toxicity. The following sections provide a detailed discussion of how diverse nanocarrier systems utilise these strategies to selectively inhibit NF-κB activity and suppress cancer hallmarks.

#### Organic nanoparticles
  Within the broad landscape of nanotherapeutic systems, organic nanoparticles have gained significant attention due to their superior biocompatibility and versatility in delivering NF-κB inhibitors [123, 124]. Their ability to integrate targeting ligands and respond to tumour microenvironmental cues further enhances their potential to modulate NF-κB-mediated inflammatory signalling. For instance, Bisht et al. have investigated the effects of the pegylated liposomes encapsulating 3,5-bis(2-fluorobenzylidene)−4-piperidone, a curcumin analogue, against the treatment of pancreatic cancer cells [201]. Upon treatment, this formulation reduced cell viability and colony formation, and induced apoptosis, as evidenced by increased expression of cleaved poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase 1 (PARP). It also inhibited NF-κB activation by inhibiting IκBα phosphorylation and preventing p65 nuclear translocation [201]. In addition, Zhang et al. have evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of PLGA nanoparticles loaded with EGCG in the treatment of lung cancer [192]. Treatment with this nanoparticle resulted in increased apoptosis, mediated by the inhibition of NF-κB activation and the downregulation of its associated target genes, including Bcl-2, Bcl-xL, cyclooxygenase-2 (COX2), TNF-α, cyclin D1, c-Myc, TWIST1, and MMP2. Molecular interaction assays also revealed that EGCG exhibits high-affinity binding to NF-κB. These findings were consistent in a murine model of lung cancer and were further enhanced compared to treatment with free EGCG [192]. Further, another study has evaluated the anti-inflammatory effects of folic acid-conjugated, 1,5-diaryl pyrazole-loaded chitosan nanoparticles against a breast cancer model [180]. The administration of this nanoparticle significantly increased the apoptosis rate, improved cognitive function, and reduced immune cell infiltration, accompanied by the downregulation of oncogenes, including Bcl-2, MDM-2, COX2, and NF-κB, in the DMBA-induced breast cancer model [180]. Furthermore, Luong et al. have conjugated the 3,4-difluorobenzylidene diferuloylmethane (CDF), an anticancer flavonoid analogue, with PAMAM and folic acid (FA-PAMAM-CDF) specifically to target cervical cancer that overexpresses folate receptor [184]. Treatment with FA-PAMAM-CDF to cervical cancer cells decreased cell viability, phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) expression, and NF-κB expression, along with an increase in apoptosis [184].


#### Inorganic nanoparticles
  Inorganic nanoparticles possess exceptional structural rigidity and multifunctionality, making them attractive candidates for NF-κB-targeted therapy [123, 127]. Materials such as gold, silica, and iron oxide nanoparticles have demonstrated significant potential to modulate NF-κB signalling through controlled drug delivery, photothermal effects, and magnetic targeting. For instance, Abdellatif et al. have demonstrated that treating breast cancer cells with silver citrate nanoparticles inhibits TNF-α expression by suppressing NF-κB signalling in phorbol myristate acetate-stimulated cells [176]. Another study reported that treatment with Zn-doped CuO nanoparticles induced cytotoxicity, ROS generation, DNA damage, apoptosis, and autophagy in pancreatic and liver cancer cell lines [114]. These effects were mediated through the modulation of key molecular markers, including Bax, caspase-3, Beclin 1, LC3 I/II, mitochondrial membrane potential, Bcl-2, p-p65, p-IKKα/β, and p-IκBα [114]. In addition, Lai et al. have explored the therapeutic potential of gold nanoparticles conjugated with a cell-penetrating peptide in activated B-cell-like diffuse large B-cell lymphoma cells, providing insight into the underlying molecular mechanisms [170]. Treatment with this nanoparticle induced apoptosis, activated thioredoxin reductase, and increased ROS production. These effects were mediated through modulation of both canonical and non-canonical NF-κB signalling pathways, as evidenced by changes in the expression of key regulatory components, including decreased IKK, and increased nuclear p65, p-p65, p-IκB, NIK, p-IKKα, p-p100, p52, as well as the NF-κB target genes cyclin G2 (CCNG2) and growth arrest and DNA damage inducible alpha (GADD45α) [170]. This elevated ROS production led to a decrease in the mitochondrial membrane potential and promoted the release of cytochrome-C, which in turn leads to cell apoptosis [170]. Moreover, silver-based nanoparticles upon treatment inhibited the growth and viability of colon cancer cells by inducing apoptosis, DNA damage, and S-phase cell cycle arrest as evidenced by the increase in the Bax/Bcl-xL ratio, p53, p21, cleaved PARP, and caspases-3,−8, and − 9, alongside downregulation of Akt and NF-κB signalling [115]. Further, another study has evaluated the therapeutic effect of gold nanoparticles loaded with linalool conjugated to the CALNN peptide against ovarian cancer cells [199]. In these cells, the nanoparticle demonstrated potent cytotoxic and pro-apoptotic activity, evidenced by an elevated apoptosis rate, significant DNA damage, and increased ROS generation. Mechanistically, these outcomes were linked to high cellular uptake, a reduction in mitochondrial membrane potential, and the suppression of NF-κB nuclear translocation [199].


#### Carbon-based nanoparticles
  Carbon-based nanoparticles, including graphene oxide, carbon nanotubes, and fullerenes, have emerged as promising candidates for cancer nanotherapy owing to their exceptional mechanical strength, electrical conductivity, and large surface area [130, 131]. These properties enable efficient drug loading and cellular uptake, making them attractive tools for modulating NF-κB signalling in tumour cells. For instance, Hu et al. have reported the selective inhibition of the NF-κB signalling pathway by using aqueously synthesised quantum dots in pancreatic cells [200]. Treatment with these quantum dots resulted in the inhibition of both canonical and non-canonical NF-κB signalling pathways, where it inhibited the nuclear translocation of p65, which further led to the suppression of anti-apoptotic genes and promoted apoptosis in cancer cells. Moreover, these quantum dots showed enhanced effects against viral and bacterial endotoxin-induced inflammatory responses; however, these effects were diminished by treatment with N-acetylcysteine, which reduced cellular uptake of the quantum dots [200]. Interestingly, another study has investigated the anti-inflammatory effects of oral fullerene tablets against colorectal cancer (CRC) [116]. Treatment of cancer cells with these tablets led to a reduction in intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, NF-κB protein expression, and pro-inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α and iNOS, while enhancing the expression of anti-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-10 and transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β), in response to LPS stimulation. Similarly, treatment of macrophages (Mϕ) with these tablets elicited a comparable anti-inflammatory response [116]. Administration of these tablets in a CRC mice model significantly enhanced the therapeutic outcomes of AOM/DSS treatment, as evidenced by reductions in tumour number and volume, neutrophil infiltration, Ki-67 expression, M1 Mϕ population, and ROS levels. Furthermore, the treatment downregulated the expression of NF-κB, phosphorylated IκBα, TNF-α, IFN-γ, signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), IL-6, and iNOS, while upregulating wild-type p53 expression and increasing colorectal length [116].

  Targeting NF-κB-mediated inflammation through nanomedicine holds promise in disrupting key pro-tumoural pathways. These emerging approaches may ultimately lead to improved clinical outcomes through immune modulation and reduced tumour aggressiveness.


### RNA-based nanomedicine strategies for direct Inhibition of NF-κB in cancer
  RNA-based therapeutics have emerged as powerful tools for precise gene silencing and modulation of specific pathways [205]. In the context of cancer, these RNA molecules can directly inhibit key components of the NF-κB signalling cascade, thereby suppressing the hallmarks of cancer [38]. However, their clinical application is often limited by poor stability, endonuclease degradation, and ineffective cellular delivery, among other factors [206]. These are challenges that nanocarrier-based systems are particularly well-suited to address. Nanomedicine-based RNA delivery thus offers a promising strategy for achieving targeted, efficient, and durable inhibition of NF-κB in cancer therapy [207]. Nanomedicine-based strategies have emerged as effective tools to directly inhibit NF-κB activity, enhancing therapeutic efficacy while minimising systemic toxicity (Table 1). Interestingly, an in vivo study has reported that the treatment of the lymphoma allograft mice model bearing patient-derived xenografts with peptide p5RHH-based delivery of small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) against p65 and p100 resulted in the significant reduction of the tumour size, spleen size, and peripheral blood lymphocyte count, along with the decrease in the expression levels of p65, p100 and CD47 [194]. It has also been reported that the expression of CD47 was regulated by the TNF-NFKB1 signalling pathway in breast cancer [194]. Another study has utilised the nano micelles containing siRNA against p65 conjugated with polyethylene glycol-polycaprolactone and functional peptide CH2R4H2C and evaluated its therapeutic effect in melanoma models [195]. It has been reported that treatment with this micelle enhanced cellular uptake, reduced the migratory capacity of melanoma cells, and decreased the number of lung nodules in a murine model, compared to naked siRNA [195]. In addition, Ke et al. have evaluated the therapeutic potential of carbamate–mannose-modified polyethylenimine as a delivery system for short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting NF-κB in breast cancer cells [175]. Treatment with this shRNA significantly enhanced transfection efficiency and reduced cell viability, migration, invasion, mammosphere formation, and the proportion of ALDH⁺ cells in breast cancer cells [175]. Subsequently, another investigation has examined the effect of directly inhibiting NF-κB using nanoparticle antibody complexes in cancer models [117]. Treatment with this complex has decreased p65 nuclear translocation, which in turn has decreased cell growth and the expression levels of COX2 and TNF-α in the head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) cell line. Similarly, administration of this complex in a breast cancer murine model resulted in reduced tumour volume, an effect that was further enhanced by co-treatment with doxorubicin [117]. Further, another study evaluated the therapeutic potential of mesoporous silica nanoparticles loaded with anti-miR-155 and surface-modified with polymerised dopamine and the AS1411 aptamer for targeted treatment of CRC [186]. Treatment with this nanoformulation has significantly reduced cell proliferation, viability, and colony formation ability, along with a decrease in the expression levels of NF-κB p65 in breast cancer cells [186]. Notably, this formulation also sensitised adriamycin-resistant CRC cells to 5-FU therapy by downregulating P-gp expression, a target of the NF-κB signalling pathway [186]. RNA-based nanomedicine strategies offer a precise and efficient approach for directly silencing NF-κB signalling in cancer cells. These interventions hold significant potential to overcome therapeutic resistance and enhance the specificity of cancer treatment.


### Modulating tumour metabolism via NF-κB-targeted nanotherapeutics
  Aberrant tumour metabolism is intricately linked with NF-κB signalling, which regulates key metabolic pathways that support cancer cell survival and proliferation [20, 208–210]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that the NF-κB signalling pathway transcriptionally regulates the spatial expression of a wide array of genes involved in metabolic pathways, which are further implicated in oncogenesis, immune regulation, energy homeostasis, ageing, stem cell renewal, and inflammasome activation [20, 208–210]. Mechanistically, NF-κB suppresses oxidative phosphorylation and ATP production by promoting mitochondrial DNA instability through its translocation mediated by Mortalin and STAT3 in p53-deficient tumours [95, 211, 212] Moreover, the IKKβ/NF-κB pathway plays a crucial role in upregulating the glucose transporter GLUT3, thereby enhancing both glucose uptake and glycolytic activity [95, 213]. The IKK/NF-κB pathway and glycolysis form a positive feedback loop, and aberrant activation of this loop, in the absence of p53, plays a pivotal role in the process of Ras-induced cell transformation [95, 214]. Apart from the cancer cells, the NF-κB signalling pathway also reprograms the metabolism of the immune cells. It has been reported that tumour-derived exosomes polarise Mϕ towards an immunosuppressive phenotype through NF-κB-dependent metabolic reprogramming, which further increases PD-L1 expression [95, 215]. Further, it has been reported that upon prolonged treatment with tyrosine kinase inhibitors, cancer cells exhibit a metabolic shift towards increased glycolysis and lactate production, which subsequently leads to the production of HGF in an NF-κB-dependent manner [95, 216]. The resultant activation of MET-dependent signalling, driven by increased growth factor levels, conferred resistance to tyrosine kinase inhibitors in the cancer cells [95, 216]. Targeting tumour metabolism has emerged as a promising strategy to disrupt the energy and biosynthetic demands of rapidly proliferating cancer cells (Table 1). Nanotherapeutics directed at NF-κB signalling offer a potential means to modulate these metabolic circuits, thereby disrupting tumour growth and improving treatment efficacy. For instance, a study has reported targeting the metabolic crosstalk between the pancreatic stellate cells and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cells with a nanosystem [217]. It was designed based on TME-responsive liposomes encapsulating TPCA-1, an NF-κB inhibitor, and a CD71 aptamer linked to siGLUT1 via a disulfide bond (AsiG). Treatment with this nanosystem has resulted in the disassembly of this nanosystem in acidic TME, which further led to the release of TPCA-1. The released TPCA-1 reversed the activated stellate cells to the quiescent stage, aiding the secondary delivery of AsiG deep inside the tumour tissue, which then binds to tumour cells that overexpress CD71 and releases the siGLUT1 [217]. Silencing of the GLUT1 in the tumour cells resulted in a ~ 40% decrease in glucose uptake and a ~ 100% reduction in lactate excretion and blocked the oxidative phosphorylation. In addition, treatment with this nanosystem in the pancreatic cancer mice model significantly reduced the tumour volume, tumour weight, along with the decrease in the percentage expression of GLUT1, p65, and Ki-67 [217]. NF-κB-targeted nanotherapeutics present a latent approach to rewire tumour metabolism and disrupt cancer cell adaptability. By interfering with metabolic reprogramming, these strategies may enhance therapeutic outcomes and limit tumour progression.


### Reprogramming the immune suppressive microenvironment through NF-κB modulation
  TAMs are a type of immune cell found in TME and are also involved in cancer initiation, progression, metastasis, therapeutic resistance, and relapse. TAMs originate from two different lineages, including Mϕ-derived cells, which are resident in tissues, and monocyte-derived cells [218]. It is one of the critical components of the TME and contributes to almost 50% of the mass of tumour tissue [218, 219]. The TAMs are commonly classified into two functional phenotypes, namely tumour-suppressive M1 and tumour-promoting M2, based on the model proposed by Alberto Mantovani [218, 220]. There is a scientific consensus that the NF-κB signalling pathway is instrumental in modulating the phenotypic polarisation of TAMs during tumour progression [221–224]. Targeting NF-κB through nanomedicine offers a potential avenue to modulate immune cell polarisation, diminish tumour-promoting inflammation, and reinvigorate anti-tumour responses (Table 1) (Fig. 3). For instance, a recent study by Zhang et al. describes the design of a pH-responsive selenium-based core–satellite nanoassembly with the ability to reprogramme TAMs [174]. Treatment with this nanoassembly resulted in a significant reduction in cell viability, colony formation ability, invasion, and migration, along with a decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential, and increased apoptosis and ROS levels mediated by the p53 signalling pathway, as well as induced DNA damage [174]. In addition, treatment of Mϕ with the nanoassembly activated the NF-κB signalling pathway, induced polarisation towards an M1 phenotype, enhanced their phagocytic activity by interacting with calreticulin, and increased cancer cell-killing capacity through the secretion of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α, as well as their phagocytic ability [174]. Administration of this nanoassembly to the bilateral breast cancer murine model reprogrammed the immunosuppressive to immune-reactive microenvironment by repolarising the M2 Mϕ to M1, increasing the infiltration of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the tumour, along with the increase in the pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 and immunoactivated cytokines such as IL-2, IL-12, and Interferon γ (IFN-γ) [174]. Another study has reported that prostate cancer cells, when treated with mangiferin-functionalised gold nanoparticulates, resulted in decreased cell viability, angiogenesis, and induced early-stage apoptosis by modulating the NF-κB signalling pathway [202]. Upon treatment, these nanoparticles also induced the polarisation of Mϕ towards the anti-tumour M1 phenotype. In addition, when administered to a prostate cancer mice model, this treatment reduced the tumour volume and angiogenesis [202]. Another study has examined the immune reprogramming ability of the nanoparticles composed of amino-modified ferumoxytol (FMT) combined with poly I: C (PIC) in the progression of melanoma [225]. Treatment with FMT-PIC reprogrammed Mϕ towards an anti-tumour phenotype, characterised by elevated production of TNF-α and iNOS, increased NO secretion, and enhanced phagocytic activity mediated by NOX2-derived ROS, collectively contributing to the suppression of melanoma cell proliferation [225]. Mechanistically, this treatment polarised M1 Mϕ to M2 Mϕ, as evidenced by the upregulated expression of M1 Mϕ markers, such as iNOS and CD86, and the downregulated expression of M2 Mϕ markers, including CD206 and Arg1, in an NF-κB-dependent manner. Similarly, administration of this nanoparticle in a murine model significantly reduced tumour volume, weight, and lung metastasis, while increasing the proportion of CD11b⁺ cells, CD45+ cells, lung Mϕ, and the expression levels of iNOS and TNF-α [225]. Another study has evaluated the effect of silica nanoparticles on the TME of lung tumours [118]. Treatment with this nanoparticle in a co-culture system of Mϕ and lung cancer cells inhibited tumour cell growth by promoting Mϕ polarisation towards the M1 phenotype by activating the NF-κB pathway and glycolysis. Marker expression analysis of Mϕ and the co-culture system revealed elevated levels of CXCL10R, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, along with an increased frequency of CD86⁺ cells. This treatment also modulated inflammatory and apoptotic factors associated with NF-κB activation, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and NOS2, as well as XIAP and CCL4, respectively [118]. Additionally, another study investigated the reprogramming of Mϕ using micelles encapsulating MG132, a proteasome inhibitor, in breast cancer models [177]. Administration of this micelle in a murine model resulted in reduced tumour volume and NF-κB expression, and promoted the polarisation of M2 to M1 Mϕ, as indicated by an increased proportion of CD68⁺NOS2⁺ cells compared to free drug [177]. Moreover, Shan et al. have developed a carrier-free nanoparticle that is a co-assembly of vitamin E succinate, hydroxychloroquine, and vitamin E succinate conjugate, as well as bortezomib, and evaluated its capacity to modulate lysosomal pH in TAMs and induce immunogenic cell death in breast cancer models [181]. Treatment with this nanoparticle increased lysosomal pH levels, thereby disrupting lysosomal acidification. This alkalisation subsequently led to a decrease in the production of TGF-β and a reduction in the population of Treg cells. In addition, treatment with this nanoparticle promoted calreticulin exposure, ATP release, and HMGB1 efflux, while suppressing IL-6 secretion via modulation of the NF-κB signalling pathway in breast cancer cells [181]. Administration of this nanoparticle in a murine model elicited dual immunological and anti-tumour responses. The immunological effects included reduced expression of IL-6 and TGF-β, decreased populations of immunosuppressive CD3⁺CD4⁺Foxp3⁺ Treg cells and M2 Mϕ, and increased proportions of dendritic cells (CD11c⁺CD80⁺CD86⁺) within lymph nodes. Concomitantly, anti-tumour responses were evident from reduced tumour volume and improved survival outcomes [181].

#### Potentiating immunotherapy via NF-κB-targeted nanomedicines
  Therapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors constitutes a breakthrough in the field of oncology, establishing immunotherapy as a principal modality in cancer care [226, 227]. Despite its efficacy, a substantial proportion of patients experience limited clinical benefit, resulting in disease progression due to therapeutic resistance [228, 229]. A principal strategy to overcome the challenge of immunotherapy resistance is the development of synergistic combination therapies. This has led to the emergence of a research field focused on pairing immune checkpoint inhibitors with nanomedicines, which can co-deliver therapeutic agents or re-engineer the TME to render it more susceptible to immune-mediated attack. For instance, Xiao et al. have examined the therapeutic effect of pH-sensitive nanocarriers containing curcumin surface-coated with anti-PD-1 against melanoma models [196]. In response to the acidic TME, the nanocarrier releases its anti-PD-1 payload, which subsequently binds to circulating PD-1-positive T cells. Concurrently, a surface charge conversion of the nanocarrier facilitates the internalisation of curcumin into both tumour cells and TAMs. This nanocarrier administration markedly reduced tumour volume, suppressed CD4⁺FOXP3⁺ T cell infiltration, and prolonged survival in the murine model [196]. Another study has evaluated the synergistic effect of MMP2-responsive and tumour-targeting nanogels containing a PD-1 antibody, along with IMD-0354, an NF-κB pathway inhibitor, loaded in mannose-modified lipid nanoparticles against a melanoma model [230]. This nanogel successfully co-delivered PD-1 antibody and IMD-0354 nanoparticles to T cells and M2 Mϕ separately. In addition, treatment with this nanogel enhanced the efficacy of PD-1 blockade immunotherapy and mitigated immune resistance within the TME of the melanoma model by repolarising M2 TAMs and concurrently upregulated the expression of PD-L1 [230]. In addition, Yang et al. have examined the synergistic repolarisation of TAMs using miR-155-loaded layered double hydroxide nanoparticles (LDH@155) against the cervical cancer mouse model [231]. Intratumoral administration of LDH@155 potentiates PD-1 antibody immunotherapy by remodelling the tumour immune microenvironment through the suppression of STAT3 and ERK1/2 phosphorylation and activation of NF-κB in TAMs, which leads to a depletion of immunosuppressive MDSCs and an increase in CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell infiltration and activity [231]. More recently, the synergistic enhancement of immune checkpoint inhibitors using hydrophilic micelles encapsulating astragaloside IV (PPA) surface-coated with PD-L1 antibody (PPA@aPD-L1) has been evaluated against bladder cancer [172]. Administration of PPA@aPD-L1 to mouse models has remodelled the immunosuppressive landscape, characterised by increased CD8+ T cell infiltration and decreased Treg cell infiltration, through the inhibition of the NF-κB and STAT3 pathways in tumour cells, compared to PPA or aPD-L1 alone [172]. Moreover, another study has demonstrated the combined anti-cancer and immunomodulatory effects of methotrexate loaded in a PLGA carrier covered with polyethyleneimine and hyaluronic acid against a breast cancer mouse model [182]. Administration of this nanoparticle, combined with the PD-L1 antibody, has polarised the Mϕ towards the M1 phenotype, which further leads to a reduction in inflammation, immunosuppressive activity, and pro-tumour signalling by downregulating NF-κB and STAT3. In addition, this treatment also downregulated Bcl-2, MDR-1, survivin, vimentin, CXCR4, IL-10, and PD-L1, while increasing the expression of FADD, APAF-1, caspase-3, IFN-γ, and E-cadherin in malignant cells [182]. Further, Zhang et al. have evaluated the synergistic effect of the glycocalyx-mimicking nanoparticles and an anti-PD-L1 antibody on lung cancer [232]. Treatment with these nanoparticles induced lectin receptor-mediated internalisation in TAMs, subsequently reprogramming them towards an antitumour phenotype [232]. This polarisation was characterised by increased expression of IL-12 and reduced levels of IL-10, CCL22, and arginase-1, mediated through activation of NF-κB and suppression of p-STAT6 signalling. Additionally, the combined treatment of the glycocalyx-mimicking nanoparticles with the anti-PD-L1 antibody in the lung cancer mouse model has reduced the tumour burden and also improved the immunosuppressive microenvironment by increasing CD8+ T cells and decreasing CD4 + T cells [232]. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that combining gadofullerene nanoparticles with an anti-PD-L1 inhibitor has synergistically exhibited an anti-cancer effect in a breast cancer mouse model [233]. Targeting NF-κB signalling through nanotherapeutics offers a compelling strategy to disrupt tumour metabolic reprogramming and suppress cancer progression. Furthermore, combining NF-κB-targeted nanotherapeutics with immune checkpoint inhibitors has demonstrated synergistic enhancement of antitumour immune responses. These combinatorial strategies show potential for overcoming immune evasion and achieving therapeutic effectiveness in cancer immunotherapy.


### Nanomedicine strategies targeting NF-κB to overcome cancer cell plasticity and EMT
  Cancer cell plasticity, characterised by the ability of tumour cells to dynamically shift between phenotypic states, is a major hallmark contributing to cancer progression, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance [7]. This adaptive behaviour enables tumour cells to evade cytotoxic therapies and regenerate aggressive subpopulations, posing a significant challenge in clinical oncology [7]. One of the central signalling pathways implicated in regulating cancer cell plasticity is the NF-κB signalling pathway. NF-κB orchestrates intraclonal communication and modulates the expression of key stem cell-associated markers, including CD44, sex determining region Y-box 2 (SOX2), and Nanog, thereby promoting tumour heterogeneity and resilience [98, 234, 235]. The NF-κB signalling pathway also plays a pivotal role in orchestrating various stages of EMT [97]. These include the transcriptional repression of E-cadherin leading to the dissolution of cell–cell junctions, reorganisation of the cytoskeleton, loss of apical–basolateral polarity, enhanced motility, and acquisition of an invasive phenotype. NF-κB further regulates EMT through interactions with key signalling molecules and pathways, such as the PTEN/PI3K/AKT axis, the CXCL12/CXCR4 axis, matrix metalloproteinases, and a range of cytokines and chemokines [97]. Targeting this pathway offers an opportunity to limit the adaptability and survival of malignant cells (Table 1). Nanomedicine-based strategies are emerging as powerful tools to precisely inhibit NF-κB signalling, thus offering a means to overcome cancer cell plasticity and associated resistance mechanisms. For example, a study has reported that the administration of PLGA nanoparticles encapsulating disulfiram significantly reduced the tumour volume and inhibited the expression of NF-κBp65, ALDH, and Ki-67 in glioblastoma xenografts [188]. In addition, Wei et al. have evaluated the therapeutic effects of curcumin conjugated with cholesteryl-hyaluronic acid against drug-resistant breast cancer cells [179]. In vitro studies have demonstrated that this nanogel promotes apoptosis in breast cancer cells through the suppression of key inflammatory markers, namely NF-κB, TNF-α, and COX2. These anti-tumour effects were subsequently replicated in a murine model [179]. Another study has investigated the effect of zinc-aspartic acid nanofibers on lung cancer cells [191]. Treatment with these nanofibers specifically inhibited HSP90, leading to the suppression of NF-κB signalling molecules such as p-IKKα/β (Ser176/180), p-IκBα (Ser32), and p-p65, which resulted in decreased cell proliferation, invasion, and migration of lung cancer cells [191].

  Moreover, another study has investigated the effect of nano-hydroxyapatite and poly-L-lysine carrying siTNFAIP2 on the OSCC mice model [198]. Administration of this siRNA to the mice model significantly reduced the tumour lesions and lymph node metastasis, along with the reduction in TNFAIP2, p65, IKKβ, and LYVE1 expression [198]. Interestingly, another study has evaluated the effects of nanostructured lipid carriers loaded with citral on breast cancer models [119]. Treatment with this formulation resulted in decreased cell proliferation, colony formation, migration, invasion, and induced apoptosis in breast cancer cells. In addition, this treatment also resulted in a substantial reduction in the expression of metastasis-associated genes, including MMP9, iNOS, NF-κB, c-Myc, and ICAM, as well as pro-angiogenic genes such as G-CSF, M-CSF, EOTAXIN, VEGF, bFGF, and IL-1α in breast cancer cells, when compared to free citral [119]. More recently, Wu et al. have investigated the anti-cancerous potential of rutin-loaded chitosan nanoparticles in hepatoma cells [190]. Treatment with this nanoparticle significantly increased mitochondrial membrane potential and ROS levels, which subsequently reduced cell viability through the modulation of autophagy, NF-κB signalling, and ULK1 pathway activity [190]. These molecular modulations were indicative of the treatment’s multifaceted mechanism of action, as evidenced by the upregulation of autophagy-related markers, such as ULK1, ATG3, ATG5, and LC3II, along with increased expression of the tumour suppressor p53, NF-κB subunits (NF-κB1 and NF-κB2), and the epithelial marker E-cadherin. Concurrently, there was a notable downregulation of the mesenchymal markers SNAIL and Vimentin, suggesting a potential reversal of EMT [190]. Nanomedicine-based interventions targeting NF-κB have shown promise in mitigating cancer cell plasticity and EMT by disrupting key regulatory pathways involved in these processes. These approaches not only hinder tumour aggressiveness and metastasis but also restore treatment sensitivity. Future research is crucial for refining and advancing these strategies to ensure successful clinical implementation.


### Nanotherapeutic approaches for Chemo- and Radio-sensitisation by NF-κB modulation
  Tumour resistance to chemotherapy and radiotherapy remains a major obstacle in effective cancer treatment [7]. A significant contributor to this resistance is the aberrant activation of the NF-κB signalling pathway, which promotes cell survival, inflammation, and DNA repair mechanisms [38, 186, 236]. NF-κB upregulates anti-apoptotic proteins and enhances the expression of drug-efflux transporters, thereby diminishing treatment efficacy. Modulating NF-κB can therefore resensitise tumour cells to therapeutic agents and improve treatment outcomes [38, 186, 236]. Nanomedicine-based strategies offer a promising platform for delivering NF-κB inhibitors or co-therapeutic agents directly to tumour sites with enhanced specificity (Table 1).


#### Chemotherapy
  Given the pivotal role of NF-κB in driving chemoresistance, nanotherapeutic interventions have been developed to suppress its activation and potentiate the cytotoxic effects of standard chemotherapeutic drugs [101]. Such nanocarrier-mediated co-delivery systems have demonstrated improved bioavailability, reduced off-target effects, and enhanced anti-tumour responses in preclinical models. For instance, Eskandari et al. has reported that the administration of a PLGA–levan–curcumin formulation in combination with gemcitabine in a murine breast cancer model significantly reduced tumour volume and downregulated NF-κB expression along with its associated subunits [120]. Another study has revealed that the treatment of p5RHH-based delivery of siRNAs against p65 and p100 in combination with etoposide, a conventional chemotherapeutic agent, resulted in the sensitisation of late-stage aggressive tumours to this therapy [194]. Consequently, Siddique et al. have investigated the effects of the caffeine-coated nanoparticles in combination with doxorubicin in a leukaemia mouse model [171]. Administration of these nanoparticles to a mouse model had a better effect as an anti-depressant and anti-coagulant when compared to doxorubicin treatment alone. In addition, this treatment restored hepatic and renal biomarker levels, suppressed stathmin 1 (STMN1) expression, glycolytic activity, and the mechanistic target of rapamycin kinase (mTOR) pathway, while upregulating p53 expression. It also exhibited anti-proliferative effects through inhibition of the non-canonical NF-κB pathway and induction of apoptosis, as evidenced by reduced expression of Rel-A, Rel-B, cFLIP, TNF-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL), and cytochrome c, alongside increased DR5 expression [171]. Interestingly, another study has investigated the therapeutic efficacy of platinum-based drugs when delivered by glutathione-stabilised gold nanoparticles conjugated with neuropilin-1 receptor targeting peptide for prostate cancer treatment [203]. Upon treatment, these nanoparticles demonstrated enhanced cytotoxicity and cellular uptake, and induced apoptosis via modulation of the NF-κB signalling pathway when compared to free drug [203]. Further, Sesarman et al. have investigated the synergistic effects of curcumin and doxorubicin encapsulated in long-circulating liposomes on colon cancer cells [237]. This treatment exhibited enhanced anti-proliferative effects compared to its free form, attributed to NF-κB-dependent suppression of angiogenic and inflammatory protein expression [237]. It has been reported that treatment with nano-pirarubicin in combination with nano-curcumin sensitised breast cancer cells to pirarubicin by downregulating NF-κB expression [238]. Consequently, Zhang et al. have evaluated the synergistic effect of the cell-penetrating peptide, CB5005, an NF-κB inhibitor, conjugated with PEGylated liposomes loaded with doxorubicin against glioma [239]. Treatment with this liposome has been shown to decrease cell viability and increase cellular uptake in glioma cells. In addition, administration of this liposome to the murine glioma model has exhibited improved survival compared to free doxorubicin [239]. Interestingly, it has been demonstrated that treatment with polymeric micelles co-encapsulating curcumin and doxorubicin, surface-coated with anti-GLUT1 scFv, significantly reduced cell viability through the synergistic inhibition of NF-κB activation in glioblastoma cells and a murine model [240].

  Moreover, Xiao et al. have developed a photothermal nanomissile by integrating bortezomib, black phosphorus quantum dots, erythrocyte membrane, and anti-BCMA against the multiple myeloma models [197]. Upon treatment with these nanomissiles, myeloma cells underwent apoptosis, as evidenced by decreased expression of Bcl-2 and phosphorylated p65, and increased levels of Bax and cleaved caspase-3. Similarly, administration of these nanomissiles significantly reduced the tumour volume and improved the survival of the myeloma model mice [197]. Interestingly, Vyas et al. have examined the chemosensitising effect of superparamagnetic nanoparticles conjugated with doxorubicin and hyaluronan against the metastasised and chemoresistant breast cancer [178]. The treatment of this nanoparticle exhibited a significant increase in apoptosis and downregulated the proinflammatory mediators such as IL-6 and NF-κB, along with a decrease in vimentin expression levels in breast cancer cells [178]. Another study has explored the combination treatment of Fe3O4 magnetic nanoparticles in combination with docetaxel against prostate cancer [204]. The combined treatment enhanced the efficacy of docetaxel by increasing apoptotic activity and suppressing NF-κB expression [204].


#### Radiotherapy
  Despite its clinical success, radiotherapy often encounters limitations due to the activation of NF-κB signalling, which facilitates cellular repair and resistance mechanisms within tumour cells [241–243]. By integrating nanocarrier systems capable of delivering NF-κB inhibitors directly to tumour sites, it is possible to augment radiation efficacy while minimising damage to surrounding healthy tissue. For illustration, Cen et al. have developed a peptide/nucleic acid nanoparticle by self-assembling siRNAs against the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) and RelA/p65 genes, using the peptide cRGD-GR9G-(LLHH)3, and evaluated its therapeutic ability in radiosensitisation [121]. Treatment with this nanoparticle in combination with irradiation enhanced the efficacy of radiotherapy, as evidenced by reduced colony-forming ability, increased apoptosis, and inhibition of DNA double-strand break repair pathways. This was characterised by reduced expression of p-EGFR, p-RelA/p65, p-DNA-PKcs, and p-ATM, alongside elevated levels of p-H2AX. Similarly, administration of this nanoparticle to the glioblastoma mouse models significantly reduced the tumour volume, proliferating cell number, improved the survival, and increased the radiotherapy-induced DNA breaks, evidenced by the increased expression of p-H2A.X [121]. These approaches not only reduce off-target toxicity but also enhance the synergistic potential of conventional therapies.


### Exosome-based nanotherapeutics for NF-κB modulation in cancer
  Exosomes are small extracellular vesicles ranging in size from 30 to 150 nm, secreted by a wide variety of cell types [244]. They encapsulate a diverse cargo of biologically active molecules, including proteins, lipids, mRNAs, microRNAs (miRNAs), and long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) [244]. It originates from intraluminal vesicles (ILVs), which arise via inward budding of the endosomal membrane, through a process mediated by the endosomal sorting complex required for transport (ESCRT) [245]. Exosomes play a crucial role in maintaining physiological homeostasis by modulating intercellular communication involved in development, immune responses, and reproduction [246–248]. In recent years, exosomes have gained considerable attention as promising drug delivery vehicles owing to their intrinsic characteristics, such as nanoscale size, low immunogenicity, minimal toxicity, high physicochemical stability, tissue penetration capabilities, and ability to facilitate long-range intercellular communication [249, 250]. Notably, multiple studies have demonstrated the efficacy of exosome-based delivery systems in cancer therapy, particularly for targeting the NF-κB signalling pathway within TME (Table 2) (Fig. 4). For instance, in osteosarcoma, the expression of fibroblast growth factor-inducible 14 (FN14), a transmembrane protein, was reported to be downregulated and correlated with poor survival outcomes [256]. FN14-overexpressing exosomes derived from bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells showed significant anticancer activity in osteosarcoma cells by inhibiting proliferation, migration, and invasion, inducing apoptosis, and arresting cells in the G0/G1 phase. Mechanistically, these exosomes activated the NF-κB signalling cascade through upregulation of p50 and p65 and downregulation of IκB, thereby triggering PANoptosis, a cell death program that integrates pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis [256]. This was confirmed by the elevated expression of NLRP3, cleaved caspase-1, -3, and -8, cleaved GSDMD, Bax, phosphorylated MLKL and RIPK, and pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, alongside the suppression of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2. This exosome, when administered in vivo, exerted significant antitumour effects characterised by reduced tumour volume and improved survival [256].

For example, Pan et al. developed a nanovector (Exo-PMA/Fe-HSA@DOX) by isolating exosomes from the urine of prostate cancer patients and modifying them with nano-sized Fe₃O₄ (inorganic nanoenzyme), doxorubicin, and human serum albumin (HSA) through electrostatic adsorption [257]. Treatment of prostate cancer cells with this engineered nanovector inhibited cell viability by suppressing the NF-κB/Akt signalling pathway and promoting apoptosis, as indicated by increased cleaved PARP and reduced caspase-3 levels. In vivo, Exo-PMA/Fe-HSA@DOX significantly reduced tumour burden and improved survival, without triggering systemic inflammation or adverse effects, and showed excellent cellular internalisation and tumour penetration [257]. In another study, Li et al. encapsulated TRAIL and a synthetic steviol derivative (SD9) within mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes, which demonstrated potent cytotoxic effects across eight different cancer cell lines [255]. In preclinical models of lung cancer, this formulation significantly increased the levels of cleaved caspases while concurrently downregulating key anti-apoptotic proteins, including Bcl-2, MCL1, XIAP, and cIAP1. Notably, treatment with SD9@EV-Ts also resulted in a marked reduction in phosphorylated p65 levels, suggesting effective inhibition of the NF-κB signalling pathway. Importantly, no cytotoxic effects were observed in normal cells, indicating the specificity and safety of this nanotherapeutic strategy [255]. Further, bovine milk-derived exosomes loaded with celastrol, a plant-derived triterpenoid and NF-κB inhibitor, significantly inhibited the viability of lung cancer cells in preclinical models [254]. This encapsulation reversed TNF-α-induced NF-κB activation in cancer cells. Mechanistically, celastrol treatment induced apoptosis via ER stress and unfolded protein response, evidenced by increased expression of p-PERK, Grp78, and Grp94 [254]. Additionally, M1 macrophage-derived extracellular vesicles with a size range of 100–120 nm were employed as nanocarriers for the delivery of oxaliplatin, retinoic acid, and Libidibia ferrea, either alone or in combination, for the treatment of CRC [253]. The combinatorial treatment notably inhibited the polarisation of Mϕ towards the pro-tumorigenic M2 phenotype and significantly enhanced apoptosis in CRC cells. Mechanistically, this strategy led to the downregulation of NF-κB, activation of FADD, and increased expression of APAF1 and E-cadherin, while suppressing tumour-promoting factors such as Survivin, MDR1, STAT3, Akt, PI3K, and vimentin [253]. These findings were corroborated in an in vivo model, where the combination therapy resulted in a significant reduction in peritoneal metastases [253]. Collectively, these studies demonstrate the remarkable safety profile and therapeutic potential of exosomes as targeted delivery vehicles for modulating NF-κB signalling within the TME. Engineered exosomes have demonstrated the ability to transport a diverse array of therapeutic cargos, including small molecule inhibitors, and proteins, with high specificity and minimal off-target toxicity. As a result, exosome-mediated NF-κB targeting represents a potential and innovative platform in next-generation cancer therapeutics.

  Additionally, mounting evidence suggests that exosome-based therapies play a crucial role in reprogramming the TME toward a pro-inflammatory, antitumour macrophage phenotype [251–253]. For instance, Jang et al. demonstrated that Mϕ isolated from murine tumour grafts, when treated with exosomes derived from epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG)-pretreated 4T1 cells, underwent a phenotypic shift towards an M1-like state [252]. This treatment upregulated miR-16 levels in both the cellular and exosomal fractions of the tumour cells. Subsequent exposure of TAMs to these exosomes reduced IKKα expression and attenuated NF-κB signalling, thereby preventing M2 Mϕ polarisation and suppressing tumour growth [252]. In addition, Gunassekaran et al. engineered exosomes from M1 Mϕ, co-loaded with siRNA targeting NF-κB and miR-511-3p, and surface-modified with IL4 receptor binding peptide 1 (IL4RPep-1) to target and reprogram TAMs toward an M1 phenotype [251]. This treatment reduced NF-κB p50, ROCK2, and M2-associated markers, including Arg1 and IL-10, while upregulating M1 markers such as IL-12p40 and iNOS. In breast cancer xenograft models, IL4R-Exo(si/mi) reduced tumour growth, suppressed immunosuppressive markers, and increased miR-511-3p levels in tumour-infiltrating lymphomononuclear cells. Moreover, the exosomes demonstrated tumour-homing capabilities, revealing their targeting specificity [251]. Despite promising preclinical outcomes, several challenges hinder the clinical translation of exosome-mediated cancer therapy. These include limitations in isolation and purification techniques, heterogeneous cargo composition, insufficient targeting efficiency, quality control issues, high production cost, and time-consuming protocols. Moreover, there is limited reproducibility in preclinical models, rapid clearance from systemic circulation, and the potential for off-target effects [250, 260, 261]. Exosomes are often co-isolated with other extracellular vesicles, resulting in significant heterogeneity [249]. Notably, many commercially available exosome preparations exhibit mismatches in exosome subtypes and lack comprehensive analytical characterisation [262]. Moreover, the molecular profiling of exosomes is influenced by microenvironmental conditions, leading to batch-to-batch variations in exosomal cargo composition and, consequently, reduced clinical translational efficiency [263, 264]. Several studies have shown that exosomes may carry oncogenic precursors, contributing to tumorigenesis rather than suppression [265–267]. In addition, exosomes exhibit a short biological half-life, often accumulating in clearance organs such as the liver, spleen, and kidneys, or being removed by phagocytic cells, like Mϕ [268]. Their low zeta potential also predisposes them to aggregate formation, potentially increasing immunogenicity and shortening circulatory stability [268]. Overcoming these limitations through the standardisation of isolation techniques, enhanced targeting strategies, and rigorous dose-response and pharmacokinetic studies is crucial for harnessing the full therapeutic potential of exosomes. Interestingly, several clinical trials are currently underway to explore the potential of exosome-based therapies in improving cancer treatment (NCT06536712, NCT01294072, NCT03608631). For instance, exosomes have been used to mitigate the adverse effects, such as anastomotic leak and myelosuppression, in cancer patients caused by conventional cancer therapies, including surgery and chemotherapy (NCT06536712, NCT06245746). Additionally, it has also aided in improving the delivery of curcumin by overcoming obstacles such as bioavailability, stability, and solubility in patients with colon cancer (NCT01294072). Notably, trials have also shown that exosomes loaded with therapeutic payloads have been utilised in the treatment of metastatic pancreatic cancer and unresectable non-small cell lung cancer patients (NCT03608631, NCT01159288).


## Biopharmaceutics of nanomedicine targeting NF-κB in cancer

### Improving bioavailability
  Nanoparticles have emerged as promising carriers to enhance the bioavailability and biopharmaceutical properties of chemotherapeutic drugs and phytochemicals [269–272]. By improving solubility, stability, and permeability, they overcome physiological barriers that limit therapeutic efficacy. Additionally, nanoparticle-based delivery systems can prolong circulation time and facilitate targeted release, thereby optimising pharmacokinetic profiles [269–271]. For example, disulfiram encapsulated in PLGA demonstrated an extended half-life of 13.4 min, whereas the free drug became undetectable immediately post-administration [188]. Moreover, the liposomal delivery of 3,5-bis(2-fluorobenzylidene)-4-piperidone, a curcumin analogue, significantly altered its pharmacokinetic profile, resulting in prolonged systemic circulation and reduced overall exposure compared to the free compound, which exhibited rapid clearance and a higher systemic burden [201].

  Curcumin, despite its potential therapeutic properties, suffers from poor aqueous solubility and low oral bioavailability, which severely limit its clinical application [273–277]. Rapid metabolism, poor absorption, and systemic elimination further reduce its therapeutic efficacy. These pharmacokinetic drawbacks necessitate innovative formulation strategies to enhance its stability and biological performance [273–276]. To overcome these challenges, various nanoparticle-based formulations have demonstrated significant improvements in the solubility and bioavailability of curcumin in preclinical cancer models. For instance, Eskandri et al. have reported that the loading of curcumin into the PLGA and levan has improved the bioavailability of the curcumin, along with increased cellular uptake, which led to a 10,000-fold increase in its solubility [120]. Another study has reported that the galactosylated BSA nanoparticles have improved the solubility of curcumin after galactosylation, as galactose is hydrophilic, when compared to BSA nanoparticles [189]. Interestingly, Wei et al. demonstrated that curcumin, when encapsulated within a cholesteryl-hyaluronic acid nanogel, exhibited enhanced stability and solubility under gastrointestinal conditions [179]. In addition, this nanogel showed increased area under the curve (AUC), longer circulation time, and gradual clearance from the model [179].


### Stability of RNA molecules
  siRNAs face major challenges in therapeutic application due to their susceptibility to enzymatic degradation by serum nucleases [206, 278, 279]. Additionally, their short half-life and rapid renal clearance limit their systemic circulation time and hinder effective delivery to target tissues. These limitations necessitate the development of delivery systems that can enhance siRNA stability and prolong their bioavailability in vivo [206, 278, 279]. Nanomedicine offers potential solutions to these challenges by encapsulating or complexing siRNAs within nanoparticles, thereby protecting them from degradation, prolonging their systemic retention, and facilitating targeted delivery. For instance, the p5RHH peptide, developed by Hou et al., has been shown to form complexes with siRNAs, effectively shielding them from enzymatic degradation in serum and extending their circulation half-life to 159 min [194, 280, 281]. Another study has reported that conjugating the siRNAs against GLUT1 with the nanosystem prolonged the circulation time of siRNAs when compared to the non-conjugated siRNAs in the orthotopic pancreatic cancer mouse model [217].


### Toxicology and safety of nanomedicines
  Despite their therapeutic potential, nanomedicines raise important concerns regarding toxicity and biosafety [282–284]. Accumulation in non-target organs, induction of oxidative stress, and activation of immune responses can pose significant risks. Variability in size, shape, surface chemistry, and composition further influences their biocompatibility and clearance. Therefore, comprehensive preclinical evaluation is essential to ensure the safety and clinical translation of nanomedicine-based therapies [282–284]. For instance, a study reported that folic acid-conjugated chitosan nanoparticles mitigated mutagen-induced effects and significantly enhanced the survival rate of zebrafish embryos compared to control [180]. Similarly, another study reported that rutin-loaded chitosan nanoparticles demonstrated selective cytotoxicity, exerting minimal effects on normal fibroblasts while inducing significant cytotoxicity in hepatocarcinoma cells, along with favourable haemocompatibility [190]. Xiao et al. demonstrated that a bortezomib-loaded nanomissile was well-tolerated and effectively restored the levels of leukocytes, monocytes, and platelets in a murine model [197]. In addition, it has been demonstrated that the combined treatment of caffeine-coated nanoparticles with doxorubicin effectively restored liver, heart, and kidney weights, as well as normalised red and white blood cell and platelet counts induced by benzene treatment, compared to treatment with the nanomedicine alone [171]. Subsequently, Zhao et al. reported that carbon-centred free radicals exhibited excellent biocompatibility, as evidenced by unchanged blood biochemical parameters in murine models [173]. Similarly, oral administration of fullerene tablets did not induce any significant toxicity in the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, or kidneys in the CRC model mice [116]. Moreover, Cen et al. demonstrated that stimuli-responsive nanoparticles exhibited neither significant toxicity to the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, or kidneys, nor did they elicit immunogenic responses in murine models [121]. It has also been reported that a nanosystem incorporating siGLUT1 exhibited favourable biosafety and biocompatibility, as evidenced by the absence of significant alterations in serum biochemical markers, including AST, ALT, BUN, and CREA, in an orthotopic murine model [217]. Similarly, Ibaraki et al. have reported that treatment with nanomicelles containing siRelA exhibited no cytotoxic effects on rat retinal pigment epithelial cells and did not induce systemic weakness in a melanoma mouse model [195]. Additionally, Bisht et al. reported that administration of EF-24-loaded liposomes to a pancreatic cancer murine model did not elicit any observable toxicity or significant changes in body weight [201]. Xin et al. have demonstrated that zinc nanofibers exerted minimal inhibitory effects on lung fibroblast cells, likely due to the absence of tumour-associated protein expression [191]. Further, another study has reported that the pomegranate nanoparticles treatment in combination with cisplatin has reduced the cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity by reducing oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, and also decreased induced inflammation by reducing the expression levels of NF-κB, IL-1β, and TNF-α in the Ehrlich solid carcinoma mice model [187].

  Inorganic nanoparticles may adversely affect normal cells by inducing oxidative stress, membrane disruption, and mitochondrial dysfunction [285–287]. Additionally, concerns regarding their haemocompatibility include potential haemolysis, platelet aggregation, and complement activation. Therefore, comprehensive in vitro and in vivo evaluations are critical to ensure their safety for clinical application. For instance, gold and platinum nanoseeds have demonstrated negligible toxicity on normal urothelial cells up to 0.5µM [122]. Another study reported that gold nanoclusters conjugated with a cell-penetrating peptide exhibited favourable biosafety, inducing no detectable toxicity in normal Mϕ and keratinocytes, while exerting significant cytotoxic effects on lymphoma cells at the same dose [170]. In addition, Khoobchandani et al. have reported that mangiferin-functionalised gold nanoparticles exhibited minimal cytotoxicity towards aortic endothelial cells while demonstrating selective cytotoxic effects against prostate cancer cells [202]. Interestingly, it has also been reported that treatment with a selenium-based nanoassembly exhibited potent anti-tumour activity alongside favourable biosafety, as evidenced by the absence of pathological alterations, changes in body weight, aberrant blood biochemical parameters, or toxic immune responses in a breast cancer mouse model [174].

  Collectively, these studies demonstrate that the use of nanocarriers has significantly enhanced the pharmacological characteristics of various therapeutic agents, including improved solubility, stability, and bioavailability (Fig. 5). Importantly, these nanomedicines demonstrated minimal or no cytotoxic effects on normal cells, indicating a favourable biosafety profile. Moreover, their ability to achieve selective and efficient delivery to tumour cells spotlights their therapeutic advantage in modulating NF-κB-driven oncogenic pathways. In addition, these nanomedicine strategies signify a transition from conventional cytotoxic therapies to precision oncology approaches that exploit molecular specificity, real-time monitoring, and personalised intervention. By enabling combinatorial delivery of chemotherapeutics, immunomodulators, or RNA-based inhibitors, NF-κB-targeted nanomedicines enhance therapeutic synergy while reducing systemic toxicity. Furthermore, the integration of diagnostic and therapeutic functionalities within a single nanoplatform represents a significant step towards theranostic precision in cancer treatment.

## Translational challenges in nanomedicine therapy
  Despite substantial progress in nanoformulations targeting NF-κB signalling, their translation from preclinical promise to clinical reality remains limited. FDA-approved nanomedicines such as Doxil® (PEGylated liposomal doxorubicin) demonstrate the feasibility of nanocarrier-based cancer therapy [288, 289]. In addition, nanocarriers designed to directly inhibit NF-κB, such as various liposomal and polymeric formulations of curcumin, are being actively evaluated in numerous clinical trials [290, 291]. Another formulation known as Onivyde®, a liposomal version of irinotecan, has been utilised in the treatment of various cancers [292]. However, similar NF-κB-targeting formulations have yet to achieve clinical approval, largely due to inconsistencies between preclinical efficacy and human pharmacokinetics, biocompatibility, immunogenicity, and reproducibility on a large scale [292, 293]. Other challenges associated with the clinical development of nanomedicines include large-scale manufacturing, a lack of a globally approved regulatory protocol, government regulations, intellectual property, and cost-effectiveness when compared to current therapies [294, 295]. Unlike small-molecule drugs, the physicochemical properties of nanomedicines, such as size, charge, and ligand density, can vary between batches, profoundly impacting their biological performance [155, 296, 297]. This lack of robust, cost-effective production methods remains a significant barrier to commercialisation and clinical adoption for next-generation, actively targeted systems [155, 296, 297]. Biological challenges dominate the translation of nanomedicines because successful delivery depends on a deep understanding of disease-specific pathophysiology and how it governs the accumulation, distribution, retention, and efficacy of nanomedicines [155, 294, 296, 297]. Traditional formulation-driven development often overlooks the heterogeneity of human diseases, resulting in unpredictable biodistribution and limited therapeutic benefits. Thus, a disease-driven approach that leverages pathological changes is crucial for enhancing clinical outcomes [155, 294, 296, 297]. Moreover, a principal safety challenge is the establishment of comprehensive toxicological assessments to fully evaluate biocompatibility and immunotoxicity [294]. The safety profile of a nanomedicine is also critically dependent on its long-term stability, ensuring that aggregation or premature drug release does not occur during storage. This process requires thorough validation of the final product as well as any changes made to the manufacturing processes, including synthesis, raw materials, or sterilisation, as these modifications can significantly alter the physicochemical properties and toxicological and biocompatibility profiles [294]. Additionally, the relationship between nanomaterial properties and in vivo behaviour, such as protein corona formation, immune recognition, and clearance by the reticuloendothelial system, must be systematically characterised to ensure safety and reproducibility across patient subgroups [155, 294, 297]. In addition, the large-scale production of nanoparticle formulations is hindered by poor quality control, scalability complexities, and incomplete purification, resulting in batch-to-batch variability and higher costs [294]. Also, the lack of specialised infrastructure, stringent sterility requirements, and low batch-to-batch reproducibility further delay scale-up and increase development expenses [294]. Moreover, regulatory challenges for nanoparticle-based medicines include the absence of specific guidelines for nanomedicines, leading agencies to apply conventional drug or device rules on a case-by-case basis (e.g., the FDA’s lack of a nanotechnology definition and the EMA’s reliance on existing pharmaceutical legislation) [298, 299]. This creates uncertainty over classification, required data packages, and appropriate approval pathways, especially for follow-on or “nanosimilar” products [298, 299]. Assessment of these challenges is essential for guiding future research and realising the therapeutic potential of this approach. Consequently, scientists must invest additional time and resources to generate extensive safety, toxicology, and physicochemical characterisation data, which delays clinical trial initiation and raises development costs, ultimately hindering timely market entry [298, 299].


## Future perspectives in nanomedicine therapy
  Nanomedicine has emerged as a transformative approach in therapeutic delivery, offering targeted, controlled, and efficient drug administration across a range of diseases. Despite substantial progress, challenges remain in enhancing clinical applicability, safety, and personalisation of these systems. This section outlines key future directions aimed at refining nanomedicine through precision integration, improved biocompatibility, regulatory advancement, and digital innovation.


### Role of AI in nanomedicine targeting NF-κB in cancer
  The rational design of nanomedicines has historically been a complex, resource-intensive process, often reliant on empirical optimisation and serendipitous discovery. The convergence of artificial intelligence (AI) with nanoscience, however, is heralding a paradigm shift towards predictive, data-driven development [300]. These computational tools are now being leveraged to accelerate material discovery, predict biological interactions, and engineer bespoke nanotherapeutics with unprecedented precision [300]. The AI tools are effectively used for the loaded drugs, synergy, drug targeting and dosing, drug potency, theranostics, and nanotoxicity, as discussed elsewhere [300]. Traditionally, the development of a nanocarrier involves a laborious cycle of synthesis and in vitro/in vivo testing, where incremental modifications are made to refine its physicochemical and pharmacological properties [301–306]. This approach is not only slow but also struggles to navigate the vast multidimensional space of possible material combinations, surface modifications, and drug payloads [301–306]. As emphasised by recent studies, AI, particularly machine learning (ML), presents a promising solution to this challenge [307, 308]. By leveraging existing datasets, ML algorithms can efficiently screen virtual libraries of nanomaterials to identify candidates with a high likelihood of success, thus prioritising and expediting subsequent experimental validation [301–303, 305]. For instance, Shan et al. employed the physicochemical properties such as hydrophile-lipophile balance value, log of the partition coefficient of a solute between octanol and water, Van der Waals molecular surface area, solvent accessible molecular surface area and polar surface area, of various compounds reported in the literature to train a ML model that enables the identification of key factors governing the self-assembly behaviour of these compounds [181]. The model achieved an AUC exceeding 0.9, indicating high predictive accuracy, and identified the hydrophile-lipophile balance as a key determinant of self-assembly, followed by the logarithm of the partition coefficient. Subsequently, a pharmacophore hybridisation strategy was employed to design the self-assembling nanoparticle VHEB, comprising lipophilic vitamin E succinate, lysosome-alkalising hydroxychloroquine, and the proteasome inhibitor bortezomib [181]. This carrier-free nanoparticle effectively reprogrammed M2 Mϕ by alkalising TAM lysosomes, induced immunogenic cell death, and exerted antitumour effects through the inhibition of NF-κB signalling in tumour cells [181]. Future research should harness the transformative potential of AI to optimise the design of nanomedicines that selectively and effectively target cancer cells. By integrating large-scale biological, chemical, and clinical datasets, AI can identify molecular patterns and predict therapeutic outcomes with remarkable precision. This approach can accelerate the development of personalised nanomedicines with enhanced efficacy, reduced off-target effects, and improved safety profiles. Ultimately, leveraging AI-driven design strategies may lead to the next generation of intelligent, tumour-specific nanotherapeutics in oncology.


### Theranostic nanoplatforms for NF-κB monitoring and targeted intervention
  Theranostic nanoplatforms represent an emerging paradigm in cancer treatment, combining diagnostic and therapeutic capabilities within a single nanostructure [309–312]. In the context of NF-κB signalling, these platforms enable real-time monitoring of pathway activity while simultaneously delivering targeted interventions [313]. This dual functionality offers a potential strategy to enhance treatment precision, reduce systemic toxicity, and improve therapeutic outcomes [313]. Recent advances in material science and molecular imaging have significantly contributed to the development of such intelligent systems tailored for NF-κB modulation. For instance, Luong et al. have engineered a nanocarrier consisting of superparamagnetic iron oxidenanoparticles with a core surface coated with PAMAM dendrimer and folic acid, loaded with 3,4-difluorobenzylidene-curcumin, and assessed its theranostic ability in ovarian and cervical cancer cells [185]. It has been demonstrated that treatment with this nanoparticle enhances MRI contrast by increasing the relaxation rate, functioning effectively as a T₂ shortening agent. This treatment also demonstrated cytotoxic and pro-apoptotic effects, accompanied by upregulation of PTEN expression and downregulation of NF-κB expression [185]. In addition, Hsu et al. developed superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles conjugated with erlotinib and investigated their selective therapeutic efficacy in NSCLC models [193]. It has been demonstrated that changes in MR ΔR2* signal intensity correlate strongly with intratumoral iron and erlotinib concentrations, indicating the potential of MR imaging for non-invasive estimation of nanoparticle uptake in tumours [193]. Additionally, treatment with these nanoparticles resulted in increased apoptosis, modulated by the NF-κB signalling pathway and its components, including p-NF-κB p65, c-FLIP, Bcl-2, and MCL-1, in lung cancer cells. Similarly, the administration of this nanoparticle effectively decreased the tumour volume by inhibiting NF-κB activity, decreasing Bcl-2, c-FLIP, and MCL-1, while increasing the expression levels of cleaved caspase-3, cleaved caspase-8, and cytochrome-C in a lung cancer mouse model [193]. Inclusively, theranostic nanoplatforms represent a transformative approach that bridges diagnostics and targeted therapy within a single nanosystem. By enabling simultaneous NF-κB monitoring and controlled therapeutic delivery, these systems pave the way for more precise, adaptive, and effective cancer treatment strategies. As these nanosystems continue to evolve, their integration with patient-specific data and biomarker profiling holds immense potential for individualised therapy design. The convergence of theranostics and personalised nanomedicine thus represents the next frontier in tailoring NF-κB-targeted interventions to the molecular and physiological heterogeneity of each patient. The implementation of personalised nanomedicine strategies represents a critical step towards optimising NF-κB-targeted therapies. Patient stratification based on NF-κB activation profiles, gene expression signatures, and associated biomarkers such as p-p65, IκBα degradation levels, and inflammatory cytokine patterns can guide the selection of appropriate nanotherapeutic formulations and dosing regimens. Such biomarker-guided approaches could be integrated into adaptive clinical trial designs to improve response prediction and reduce interpatient variability in treatment outcomes. Further, combining multi-omics profiling with AI-assisted modelling may enable the development of dynamic, patient-specific nanomedicine frameworks that enhance both efficacy and translational precision.


## Conclusion
  The NF-κB signalling pathway plays a central role in cancer biology by regulating genes involved in inflammation, proliferation, apoptosis resistance, and metastasis. Its persistent activation has been implicated in tumour progression and therapeutic resistance across various cancer types. Consequently, targeting NF-κB through nanomedicine offers a strategic advantage by disrupting this key oncogenic signalling axis with enhanced precision and reduced off-target effects. Nanomedicine serves both as a therapeutic and delivery platform, improving the biopharmaceutical limitations of small molecule inhibitors while enhancing their anti-tumour efficacy. Surface modification with targeting moieties such as antibodies, ligands, and cell-penetrating peptides has further refined nanoparticle specificity to tumour cells and the TME. Additionally, combination therapies that integrate nanomedicine with conventional chemotherapy, immunotherapy, or radiotherapy have shown synergistic benefits in preclinical models. Recent advances in theranostic nanomedicine have yielded integrated platforms capable of simultaneous therapy and diagnostic imaging, which fundamentally improve the precision of treatment delivery and efficacy monitoring. In addition to improving drug solubility, circulation time, and tumour accumulation, nanocarriers have enabled the co-delivery of NF-κB inhibitors alongside other agents that modulate the TME or immune checkpoints. Notably, the co-delivery of such agents facilitates the phenotypic switching of TAMs towards a pro-inflammatory, anti-tumour phenotype, which in turn potentiates the cytotoxic immune response against the tumour. This multimodal therapeutic approach is specifically designed to address the significant clinical hurdles of tumour heterogeneity and resistance mechanisms. Moreover, stimuli-responsive nanocarriers that release their cargo in response to tumour-specific pH, enzymes, or redox conditions have enhanced treatment selectivity while minimising systemic toxicity. Further, the incorporation of AI and ML is expected to revolutionise the design and optimisation of nanomedicines targeting NF-κB, paving the way for personalised and adaptive cancer therapeutics. AI-driven models can accelerate the prediction of nanoparticle behaviour, optimise formulation parameters, and streamline preclinical validation processes. As research advances, the integration of nanotechnology, systems biology, and computational modelling will be vital in translating these innovations into clinical success. Collectively, nanomedicines targeting NF-κB represent a potential frontier in oncological therapy, offering multifunctional platforms that not only suppress pro-tumorigenic signalling but also enable real-time monitoring, immune modulation, and reduced treatment-associated toxicity. Future clinical translation will depend on addressing regulatory hurdles, conducting long-term safety evaluations, and developing scalable production methods, thereby realising the full potential of nanomedicine in the era of precision oncology.
