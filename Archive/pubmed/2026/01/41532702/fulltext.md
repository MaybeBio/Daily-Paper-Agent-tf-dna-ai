# Integrated Transcriptomics and Experimental Validation Reveal That Ellagic Acid Alleviates Fuchs Endothelial Corneal Dystrophy via PLAU/NF-κB Signaling


## Purpose
  The purpose of this study was to investigate the role of ellagic acid (EA) in alleviating oxidative stress and inflammation in Fuchs endothelial corneal dystrophy (FECD) and to evaluate its therapeutic potential by targeting urokinase-type plasminogen activator (PLAU) and modulating NF-κB signaling.


## Methods
  We integrated bioinformatics analyses using multiple databases to identify EA target genes and FECD-related differentially expressed genes (DEGs), along with functional enrichment. Experimental validation was carried out in H2O2-stimulated and UVA-irradiated human corneal endothelial cell (CEC) line B4G12. Molecular docking, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, immune infiltration, and siRNA-mediated knockdown were applied to confirm key targets and pathways.


## Results
  Our study identified 1284 EA target genes and 297 FECD-related DEGs, with 21 overlapping genes. From this intersection, a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network identified 10 hub genes. Functional enrichment analysis revealed that these hub genes were significantly associated with key pathways, including NF-κB signaling, inflammatory regulation, and oxidative stress responses. Among these hub genes, PLAU was selected for further investigation due to its key role in the PPI network, its strong correlation with immune cell infiltration, and its high diagnostic value (area under the curve [AUC] = 0.933). Molecular docking predicted a strong interaction between EA and PLAU. Subsequent in vitro experiments demonstrated that EA treatment attenuates inflammation and oxidative damage by targeting PLAU and modulating the NF-κB signaling in CECs.


## Conclusions
  These findings demonstrate that EA alleviates oxidative stress and inflammation in FECD by targeting PLAU and regulating NF-κB signaling. This study supports the therapeutic potential of EA and PLAU as a novel intervention for corneal endothelial diseases.


## Materials and Methods

### Identification of Potential Targets of EA
  The potential target genes related to EA were screened using the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD; https://ctdbase.org/), Binding Database (Binding DB; https://bindingdb.org/rwd/bind/index.jsp), and TargetNet database (https://targetnet.scbdd.com/). After the targets were acquired, they were converted to gene names by utilizing the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/).


### Functional Enrichment Analysis
  Disease Ontology (DO), Gene Ontology (GO), and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis were performed using the R packages “clusterProfiler” (version 4.2.2) and “DOSE” (version 3.20.1) to assess functional annotation, biological functions, and signaling pathway enrichment. The GO analysis encompassed three main categories: biological processes (BPs), cellular components (CCs), and molecular functions (MFs). Adjusted P values less than 0.05 were used as the screening threshold.


### Identification of DEGs in FECD
  Transcriptome data were obtained from the publicly accessible Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. The GSE142538 dataset was selected as the primary discovery dataset as it represents the largest available dataset with well-characterized corneal endothelial tissue samples.15 According to the original sample classification, our analysis included 10 FECD samples comprising 6 with TCF4 trinucleotide repeat expansion (FECD_REP) and 4 without expansion (FECD_NR), along with 9 healthy controls. We excluded the six pre-symptomatic TCF4 expansion-positive samples (Pre_S) that lack the definitive FECD clinical phenotype. This refined cohort allows for a clearer comparison between established FECD and control transcriptomes, consistent with approaches used in subsequent studies of this dataset.16,17

  For validation purposes, the GSE112039 dataset was used, comprising six FECD cell samples and six non-pathological CECs obtained from eye bank donor corneas.18 This dataset does not include TCF4 genetic subtyping information. Additionally, two RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) datasets were retrieved from the DNA Data Bank of Japan (DDBJ) Sequence Read Archive (DRA), accessed via the DDBJ Genomic Expression Archive (GEA) under Accession IDs E-GEAD-399 and E-GEAD-564.19,20 Together, these included 10 FECD samples and 7 healthy control samples. Although TCF4 subtyping information is available for these two RNA-Seq datasets,21 all FECD samples were analyzed without genetic stratification to identify common pathogenic mechanisms underlying the general FECD phenotype.

  Normalization of gene count data was performed using the “edgeR” package (version 3.36.0). For the RNA-seq datasets (GSE142538, E-GEAD-399, and E-GEAD-564), gene expression levels were presented as log2-transformed counts per million (log2-CPM), which were normalized using the trimmed mean of M values (TMM) method. A prior count was added prior to log2 transformation to avoid undefined values. The microarray data GSE112039 were analyzed using the provided robust multi-array average (RMA)-normalized expression values in the log2 space. DEGs were identified based on a threshold of adjusted P values < 0.05 and |log2 fold change (FC)| > 1. To visualize the DEGs, volcano plots were generated using the “volcano3D” R package (version 2.0.9).


### Construction of Protein-Protein Interaction Network of Common Target Genes of FECD and EA
  A Venn diagram was generated to illustrate the intersection targets of EA and FECD. The common target genes of EA and FECD were uploaded to the STRING database to construct a PPI network, which was subsequently visualized using Cytoscape (version 3.9.0). The CytoHubba plugin (version 0.1) in Cytoscape was then used to identify hub genes from this network. Hub genes were selected based on the maximal clique centrality (MCC) algorithm, which is one of the top-performing methods in CytoHubba for identifying influential nodes in a network.22 The top 10 genes ranked by the MCC score were defined as the hub genes for subsequent analysis. Normalized transcript counts were used as input for the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis performed with the “pROC” R package (version 1.19.0.1). The area under the curve (AUC) was then calculated to assess the diagnostic power of the candidate genes in distinguishing FECD samples from controls.


### Analyses of Immune Landscape and Immune Correlation
  In this study, immune cell infiltration profiles in the GSE142538 dataset were analyzed using the CIBERSORT algorithm in R software (https://cibersort.stanford.edu/), which quantified the proportions of 22 distinct immune cell types in each sample. The immune cell types evaluated included activated dendritic cells (aDCs), naïve B cells, plasma cells, eosinophils, resting mast cells, CD8 T cells, memory B cells, M0 macrophages, activated natural killer (NK) cells, monocytes, gamma delta T cells, resting dendritic cells, Tregs, activated mast cells, M2 macrophages, M1 macrophages, neutrophils, activated CD4 memory T cells, resting CD4 memory T cells, follicular helper T cells, and naïve CD4 T cells. CIBERSORT analysis was performed to estimate the relative proportions of 22 immune cell subtypes between FECD and control samples, using the CIBERSORT web portal. Subsequent statistical comparisons of cell fractions between the two groups were conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test. Additionally, a comprehensive analysis was conducted to evaluate the correlation coefficients between 10 hub genes and individual immune cells. Pearson's correlation method was used for this analysis, and the results were visualized in a matrix diagram created using the “corrplot” R software package (version 0.95).


### Molecular Docking
  To investigate the molecular interactions between PLAU and EA, a computational docking approach was used. The protein structure of PLAU was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) database (https://www.rcsb.org/) and subjected to modifications including hydrogenation and charge balancing using the Autodock tools software (version 4.2.6). Furthermore, the Grid Option tool was used to process the protein structure and delineate the dimensions of the ligand binding pocket.23 Subsequently, the Autodock Vina software (version 1.1.2) was utilized to simulate and analyze the binding mode of EA with PLAU.


### Cell Culture and Treatment
  The human CEC line B4G12 (Cat no. CC4039; Cellcook Biotech, China) was utilized instead of primary CECs. This cell line was chosen for its well-documented advantages over other available CEC models for investigating FECD pathogenesis. Unlike primary CEC cultures, which have limited proliferative capacity and are prone to endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition, B4G12 cells maintain a stable, homogeneous population with a functionally mature phenotype essential for reproducible experimental outcomes.24 Compared with other immortalized HCEC lines, such as HCEC-12 and HCEC-H9C1, B4G12 cells exhibit superior characteristics, including stronger cell-cell adhesion, higher density confluent monolayers, proper localization of tight junction proteins (ZO-1) and ion pumps (Na+/K+-ATPase), and a broader expression profile of ion channels and receptors.25–27 This closely mimics the in vivo organization and function of human corneal endothelium.

  Cells were cultured in human endothelial serum-free medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; #11111044), supplemented with 10 ng/mL of recombinant human basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) (MedChemExpress, USA; #HY-P7004), in a humidified incubator set to 37°C with 5% CO2. B4G12 cells were cultured in 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 106 cells/well and exposed to 200 µM hydrogen peroxide (H2O2; Sigma Aldrich, USA; #H1009) for 2 hours to establish an oxidative stress-related model of FECD, as described in the literature.28 Following this exposure, the cells were treated with EA at concentrations of 1 or 10 µM (TargetMol, China; #T0465) for 24 hours. To model photo-oxidative stress, B4G12 cells were also exposed to UVA radiation (365 nm, 5 mW/cm2) using a UVA lamp (UVL-26; UVP, USA), as previously described.29 Following UVA exposure, the cells were treated with 10 µM EA for 24 hours before analysis.

  For gene knockdown, B4G12 cells were transfected with 100 nM siRNA targeting PLAU in 6-well plates, following the manufacturer’s protocol (Genomeditech, China). As controls, cells were treated with an equivalent concentration of non-targeting siRNA (siNC). The sequence of the PLAU siRNA (siPLAU) was as follows: 5ʹ− CCUCUCCUCCAGAAGAAUUAA(dT)(dT)−3ʹ (forward) and 5ʹ− UUAAUUCUUCUGGAGGAGAGG(dT)(dT)−3ʹ (reverse).


### Cellular ROS Measurement
  The detection of cellular ROS was conducted using a fluorescence probe 2,7-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA; Beyotime, China; #S1105S) under a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan). For quantification, three random images per group were analyzed using ImageJ software (version 1.54p). Images were converted to 8-bit grayscale. A consistent threshold was set to identify fluorescent areas, and the “Measure” function was used to calculate the integrated density (IntDen) for each field. The average IntDen for each treatment group was then calculated. Additionally, ROS was also detected using flow cytometry (BD Biosciences, FACSuite, USA) through the FITC channel for mean fluorescence intensity.


### Superoxide Dismutase Assay
  The activity of superoxide dismutase (SOD) enzyme in cells was determined utilizing a commercially available assay kit (Beyotime, China; # S0101S). Cells were subjected to gentle homogenization within a 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) on ice, then centrifuged at 12,000g at 4°C for 10 minutes. The subsequent analysis was conducted using the resulting supernatants following the manufacturer’s instructions, which reported the SOD activity numerically, expressed as units per milligram of protein.


### Real-Time Quantitative PCR
  Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA; #15596026). The RNA concentration was assessed with NanoDrop 2000 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). For cDNA synthesis, 2000 ng of RNA was reverse transcribed using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara Bio, Japan; #RR037A), in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using Hieff UNICON SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen, China; #11184ES03) on a real-time PCR detection system (Applied Biosystems, USA). The relative expression levels of the target genes were expressed as fold changes relative to the control. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as the housekeeping gene, as its Ct values showed no significant variation among different treatment groups (Supplementary Fig. S1), confirming its stability under our experimental conditions. The primer sequences (Sangon Biotech, China) are detailed in Supplementary Table S1.


### Protein Extraction and Western Blotting
  Lysates of cell samples were prepared in RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China; #P0013E) supplemented with 1 mM PMSF (Beyotime, China; #ST505) and 1 × protease inhibitor cocktail (NCM Biotech, China; #P001). The resultant protein extract was fractionated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore, USA; #IPVH08100). To minimize nonspecific binding, the membrane was blocked with 5% non-fat milk. Primary antibodies against PLAU (Proteintech, China; #17968-1-AP), NF-κB p65 (Abcam, UK; #ab32536), phospho-NF-κB p65 (S536; Abcam, UK; #ab76302), GAPDH (ABclonal, China; #AC002), and β-Actin (ABclonal, China; #AC004) were applied overnight at 4°C. Following this incubation, the membrane was exposed to HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Goat Anti-Rabbit/Mouse IgG HRP; Proteintech, China) at room temperature for 1 hour. For detection, an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate (Beyotime, China; #P0018HS) was applied to the membranes, and the signal was captured using the ECL imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA). The intensities of the protein bands were quantified with ImageJ software (version 1.54p). The “Gels” analysis function was used to plot the lane profiles and measure the peak area for each band. The intensity of the target protein band was normalized to that of the corresponding β-Actin or GAPDH loading control band from the same lane.


### Statistical Analysis
  Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.2) and GraphPad Prism (version 8.0). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For qPCR data, statistical comparisons were made on ΔCt values between groups using 1-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test for normally distributed data. For non-parametric data, the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc test was applied. Results are presented as relative fold changes (2^–ΔΔCt). For immunofluorescence intensity and Western blot protein levels, statistical comparisons were conducted on raw data using 1-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey's post hoc test for normally distributed data. Fold changes were then calculated and presented in the figures. For SOD activity, statistical analyses were performed using 1-way ANOVA. For the bioinformatic analysis of transcriptome data, differential expression between the two groups was assessed using the two-sample Wilcoxon test. Pearson's correlation coefficient was used to assess statistical relationships. The Benjamini-Hochberg method was applied to adjust P values for multiple comparisons. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001).


## Results

### Identification of Multi-Target Mechanisms of EA and Functional Annotations
  Using the CTD, BindingDB, and TargetNet databases, we identified 1284 target genes of EA (Fig. 1A). Functional enrichment analyses—including GO, DO, and KEGG pathway assessments—revealed that these genes are significantly associated with multiple ocular and systemic disorders. DO enrichment highlighted links to corneal diseases, conjunctival pathologies, Sjögren's syndrome, and age-related conditions such as cataracts, macular degeneration, diabetic retinopathy, and rheumatic diseases (Fig. 1B).

  GO analysis revealed involvement in key biological processes such as positive regulation of the MAPK cascade, ERK1 and ERK2 signaling, responses to oxidative and chemical stress, apoptotic signaling, and calcium ion transport. These genes were also associated with aging and inflammatory processes, including regulation of inflammatory responses, leukocyte migration, and cytokine production (Fig. 1C). Enriched cellular components included membrane rafts, microdomains, neuronal cell bodies, synaptic membranes, and the external plasma membrane. Molecular functions featured amide binding, protein serine/threonine kinase activity, and G protein-coupled peptide receptor activity (see Fig. 1C).

  KEGG pathway analysis indicated significant enrichment in PI3K-Akt, MAPK, calcium, and cAMP signaling pathways, along with pathways involved in chemical carcinogenesis, apoptosis, sphingolipid signaling, neurotrophin signaling, and VEGF signaling. Immune-related pathways, such as chemokine and T-cell receptor signaling, were also prominent (Fig. 1D). These findings suggest that EA exerts its effects through multi-target mechanisms relevant to FECD and other diseases, supporting its potential as a therapeutic agent.


### Transcriptomic Alterations in FECD and Pathway Enrichment
  Analysis of the GSE142538 dataset, comprising corneal endothelial tissues from patients with FECD and healthy controls, identified 297 DEGs including 167 upregulated and 130 downregulated (Fig. 2A). GO enrichment highlighted BPs including positive regulation of cytokine production, regulation of cell-cell adhesion, positive regulation of cell-substrate junction organization, Fc-gamma receptor signaling pathway, activation of immune response, and calcium-mediated signaling (Fig. 2B). Significant CCs included the collagen-containing extracellular matrix, specific granule membrane, dopaminergic synapse, laminin complex, and specific granule (see Fig. 2B). Key MFs involved immune receptor activity, IgG binding, integrin binding, extracellular matrix structural constituent, and cytokine receptor activity (see Fig. 2B). KEGG analysis revealed enrichment in cell adhesion molecule (CAM) interaction, cGMP-PKG signaling pathway, ECM-receptor interaction, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, and Wnt signaling pathway (Fig. 2C). Together, the findings from both the EA target genes and the DEGs in FECD indicate shared molecular mechanisms and pathways, highlighting the potential of EA as a therapeutic strategy for FECD.


### EA Attenuates Oxidative Stress and Inflammation in Corneal Endothelial Cells
  To evaluate the therapeutic potential of EA in FECD, we utilized B4G12 human CECs under oxidative stress induced by H2O2 to simulate the in vivo degenerative processes, as previously reported.28,30 Intracellular ROS levels were significantly elevated following H2O2 exposure, and treatment with EA at 1 and 10 µM effectively reduced ROS in a dose-dependent manner, with 10 µM showing the most robust effect (Figs. 3A, 3B). This concentration was selected for subsequent experiments.

  Flow cytometry confirmed the reduction in ROS following EA treatment (Fig. 3C). We further assessed antioxidant capacity by measuring SOD activity. Whereas H2O2 markedly suppressed SOD, EA treatment restored its activity, underscoring its antioxidative role (Fig. 3D). Moreover, EA significantly downregulated the expression of key inflammatory mediators that were elevated by H2O2, including IL-1β, IL-8, and IL-17 (Fig. 3E). These results demonstrate that EA exerts both antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, supporting its potential as a therapeutic agent for FECD.


### Shared Molecular Targets of EA and FECD Implicate NF-κB Signaling
  Intersection of the 1284 EA-targeted genes and 297 FECD-related DEGs identified 21 shared targets (Fig. 4A). A PPI network constructed from these genes revealed 10 hub genes: ATF3, JUN, CASP1, VCAM1, CCND1, PTPRC, PLAU, PTAFR, ALOX5AP, and CTSS (Fig. 4B). Functional enrichment analysis highlighted key BP including positive regulation of cell-cell adhesion, MAPK cascade, ERK cascade, regulation of inflammatory response, response to endoplasmic reticulum stress, regulation of DNA-templated transcription in response to stress, cellular response to oxidative stress, and response to UV-A (Fig. 4C). Enriched CCs and MFs included synaptic, presynaptic, and nuclear membranes, as well as neurotransmitter and G protein-coupled amine receptor activities (see Fig. 4C). KEGG analysis further emphasized the NF-κB signaling pathway, along with various immune-related pathways, including TNF, T-cell receptor, and toll-like receptor signaling. Additionally, pathways related to second messengers, such as cAMP and cGMP-PKG signaling, as well as calcium signaling, apoptosis, and neuroactive ligand–receptor interactions, were highlighted (Fig. 4D). These findings highlight NF-κB signaling as a key pathway through which EA exerts its protective effects in FECD, while also implicating several associated pathways as potential targets for intervention.


### PLAU as a Key Therapeutic Target of EA in FECD
  To identify core therapeutic targets of EA in FECD, we performed a multi-step screening process of the 10 hub genes. First, ROC analysis using the GSE142538 dataset assessed their diagnostic value. Among them, VCAM1, PTPRC, JUN, and ALOX5AP each achieved an AUC of 1.000, indicating excellent discriminative power between FECD and healthy endothelial tissue. This was followed by PTAFR (AUC = 0.978), CTSS (AUC = 0.967), CASP1 (AUC = 0.956), PLAU (AUC = 0.933), CCND1 (AUC = 0.922), and ATF3 (AUC = 0.844; Supplementary Fig. S2). Whereas PLAU expression was significantly upregulated in FECD samples in the metadata set (Supplementary Fig. S3A), its expression patterns varied across independent validation datasets (Supplementary Figs. S3B, S3C), reflecting the known biological heterogeneity of FECD and the limited sample sizes of the available cohorts. Beyond expression patterns, our selection of PLAU for further investigation was substantiated by its strong correlation with key immune cell subtypes in the FECD microenvironment (Fig. 5A). Immune infiltration analysis revealed significant differences between FECD and normal samples in several immune cell types, including plasma cells, macrophages M0, macrophages M2, activated dendritic cells, and neutrophils (Fig. 5B). Correlation analysis further demonstrated significant correlations between specific hub genes, including CASP1, CTSS, and PLAU, and immune cell subtypes, such as plasma cells, activated dendritic cells, M2 macrophages, activated NK cells, and naïve CD4+ T cells (Fig. 5C). Given the central role of NF-κB signaling in EA-mediated protection, we focused on PLAU, a known modulator of NF-κB activity. Further validation in independent datasets confirmed the consistent diagnostic relevance of PLAU, with AUC values of 0.686 in GSE112039 (Supplementary Fig. S3D) and 0.667 in E-GEAD-399 and E-GEAD-564 (Supplementary Fig. S3E).

  To elucidate the direct targeting of PLAU by EA, molecular docking was performed. The analysis revealed strong binding affinity between EA and PLAU, with specific hydrogen bonds formed at the binding site, indicating stable and selective molecular interactions (Fig. 5D). These results provide structural evidence supporting PLAU as a high-affinity target of EA.


### EA Targets PLAU to Attenuate NF-κB Activation In Vitro
  To functionally validate PLAU as a therapeutic target of EA in FECD, we conducted in vitro experiments using both H2O2-induced and UVA-induced oxidative stress models in B4G12 cells. In the H2O2 model, Western blot analysis demonstrated that EA treatment significantly downregulated PLAU expression (Figs. 6A, 6B; Original Western blot bands shown in Supplementary Fig. S4) and concurrently reduced the phosphorylation of NF-κB p65 (see Figs. 6A, 6C), indicating effective suppression of NF-κB pathway activation. This protective effect was consistently observed in the biologically relevant UVA irradiation model. UVA exposure markedly upregulated PLAU expression and enhanced NF-κB p65 phosphorylation, and these effects were effectively reversed by EA treatment (Figs. 6D–F; Original Western blot bands shown in Supplementary Fig. S5). To further confirm the central role of PLAU in this mechanism, siRNA-mediated knockdown of PLAU was performed. Silencing PLAU expression (Figs. 6G, 6H; Original Western blot bands shown in Supplementary Fig. S6) led to a marked decrease in NF-κB activation, as evidenced by reduced phospho-NF-κB p65 levels (see Figs. 6G, 6I). Collectively, these results demonstrate that both EA treatment and direct PLAU knockdown inhibit NF-κB signaling, positioning PLAU as a functional target through which EA exerts its anti-inflammatory effects in CECs.

  A proposed model illustrates the dual antioxidant and anti-inflammatory mechanisms of EA in FECD, focusing on PLAU inhibition and subsequent modulation of the NF-κB pathway (Fig. 6J). These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of EA in alleviating oxidative stress and inflammation in FECD by targeting PLAU and mitigating NF-κB-driven responses.


## Discussion
  This study provides the first comprehensive evidence that EA exerts protective effects in FECD by targeting PLAU and modulating NF-κB-mediated inflammation and oxidative stress. Through an integrated approach combining bioinformatics and experimental validation, we identified 21 overlapping genes between EA targets and FECD-related DEGs, with PLAU emerging as a central hub gene due to its significant diagnostic value and immune correlation. In vitro, EA treatment markedly reduced ROS levels and suppressed the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Molecular docking confirmed a high-affinity interaction between EA and PLAU, and functional assays demonstrated that both EA treatment and PLAU silencing inhibited NF-κB activation. Our findings offer new insights into the potential use of EA as a therapeutic agent for FECD and encourage further preclinical research.

  Our results align with and extend the established understanding of FECD pathogenesis, which is characterized by chronic oxidative stress and inflammation leading to corneal endothelial degeneration. Elevated ROS levels and impaired antioxidant defenses have been widely documented in patients with FECD, contributing to mitochondrial dysfunction and cell death.31 The activation of NF-κB, a master regulator of inflammation, perpetuates a vicious cycle of cellular damage by inducing the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and matrix-degrading enzymes.32 Our observation that EA suppresses both oxidative and inflammatory pathways supports the notion that targeting these interconnected mechanisms is critical for effective intervention. Moreover, the involvement of PLAU suggests a novel link between proteolytic activity, ECM remodeling, and immune activation in FECD, processes that may contribute to guttae formation and endothelial barrier failure.

  PLAU, or urokinase-type plasminogen activator (uPA), is a serine protease that plays a critical role in the fibrinolytic system. It is recognized as a key mediator in protein hydrolysis and extracellular matrix degradation, contributing to tumor migration, invasion, and metastasis.33 Moreover, PLAU significantly influences inflammatory, vascular, and fibrotic diseases by regulating the inflammatory response and maintaining immune homeostasis.34 Previous studies indicate that the AQR/PLAU pathway is implicated in both inflammation and cellular senescence in endothelial cells through the canonical NF-κB p65 pathway.35 Additionally, PLAU interacts with various signaling pathways, including the AKT/NF-κB pathway, thereby regulating the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6.36 Recent research has also highlighted its involvement in oxidative stress-related pathways, particularly in cellular responses to tissue injury and inflammation.37 This inflammatory environment may exacerbate endothelial cell damage, leading to corneal edema and visual impairment. The protective role of EA, through its targeting of PLAU and inhibition of the NF-κB signaling cascade, suggests a multifaceted approach to counteract these detrimental effects.

  Current management of FECD relies heavily on surgical interventions, such as EK, which are limited by donor tissue availability and risks of graft rejection. Pharmacological therapies, including Rho kinase (ROCK) inhibitors, have demonstrated efficacy in enhancing CEC proliferation, migration, and wound healing in preclinical and clinical studies. However, they do not fully address the underlying oxidative stress and chronic inflammation that are central to FECD pathogenesis.38 EA, as a naturally derived agent, offers a complementary approach by effectively tackling oxidative stress and inflammation. In studies of dermal fibroblast senescence, EA has been shown to mitigate oxidative damage by upregulating the Nrf2 pathway, which enhances the expression of antioxidant enzymes such as SOD and catalase.39 Moreover, its anti-inflammatory properties are evidenced by its ability to suppress NF-κB activation and subsequent production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, in models of neuroinflammation and metabolic disorders.40 These findings reinforce our study’s conclusion that EA can address the pathological mechanisms in FECD, particularly by targeting the PLAU/NF-κB signaling pathway to alleviate oxidative stress and inflammation in CECs. By simultaneously addressing both oxidative stress and inflammatory pathways, EA and PLAU inhibitors could represent novel therapeutic candidates for FECD, warranting further investigation into their efficacy and safety in preclinical models.

  Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the analysis of FECD transcriptomic data was conducted on individual datasets rather than a combined cohort, as technical batch effects between different platforms (microarray and RNA-seq) precluded the direct merging of raw data. Although our use of independent discovery and validation cohorts strengthens the findings, a meta-analysis of larger, harmonized datasets in the future could provide even greater statistical power and generalizability. Second, the genetic heterogeneity of the FECD samples in the public datasets represents a limitation. Whereas partial genetic subtyping was available for the metadata set, it predominantly comprises FECD samples as a mixed population reflecting the general clinical phenotype. TCF4 trinucleotide repeat expansion status was not available in the other datasets (GSE112039, E-GEAD-399, and E-GEAD-564). Future studies with larger and genetically stratified cohorts will be valuable for confirming and refining these findings. Third, the generalizability of our transcriptomic findings, particularly the variability in PLAU expression, is limited by the biological heterogeneity in FECD and the small sample sizes in public datasets. Future studies utilizing larger and genetically stratified cohorts are essential to obtain more reliable molecular signatures. Fourth, candidate molecules were identified through transcriptome analyses; however, some potential biomarkers that may exhibit alterations solely at the protein or epigenetic level could have been excluded from our analysis. Therefore, further research should integrate proteomics and epigenetic studies to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the biomarkers involved. Fifth, whereas molecular docking indicates a strong binding between EA and PLAU, experimental validation through direct binding assays faces technical constraints. Conventional pull-down assays require chemical modification of EA through biotin tagging or other linkages. However, as a polyphenolic compound, chemical tagging risks altering EA’s native conformation or blocking its access to the PLAU binding pocket, potentially yielding false-negative results. We therefore used functional validation as an alternative approach to support the predicted interaction. Future studies utilizing label-free biophysical techniques, such as surface plasmon resonance (SPR), will be essential to provide direct quantitative confirmation of this interaction. Sixth, the transcriptomic response of CECs to EA treatment under oxidative stress remains uncharacterized. A comprehensive RNA-seq analysis in the future could provide a systems-level view of EA’s effects and identify additional pathways contributing to its therapeutic potential. Finally, examining PLAU expression and NF-κB activation status in corneal endothelium specimens from human patients with FECD is crucial for validating the clinical relevance of our findings and establishing their potential as biomarkers for disease progression or treatment response. This will be a key focus of our future research efforts.

  In conclusion, this study elucidates a novel molecular mechanism through which EA exerts potent therapeutic effects against FECD. By integrating bioinformatics with experimental validation, we identified PLAU as a key target of EA and demonstrated that EA binding suppresses PLAU-mediated NF-κB activation, ameliorating oxidative stress and inflammatory responses in human CECs. This work provides a foundation for the development of EA-based pharmacotherapies and highlights the potential of targeting the PLAU/NF-κB axis as an innovative strategy for treating FECD and other oxidative stress-related corneal disorders.
