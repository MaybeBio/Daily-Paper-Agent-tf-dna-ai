# Quercetin induces ferroptosis in ovarian cancer through regulating HSPB1/Notch1 pathway


## Objective
  Ferroptosis, characterized by iron dependency and lipid peroxidation, is essential in tumor development and chemotherapy resistance. Quercetin, a natural flavonoid, exhibits anticancer activity in various tumors. The study aimed to explore whether quercetin suppresses ovarian cancer by inducing ferroptosis, as well as to uncover the associated regulatory mechanisms.


## Methods
  The impact of quercetin on promoting ferroptosis, affecting cell apoptosis, growth, and lipid peroxidation, was assessed in the human ovarian cancer cell lines A2780 and SKOV3. Cell proliferation was evaluated by CCK-8 and colony formation experiments, whereas apoptosis and lipid peroxidation were measured through flow cytometry and malondialdehyde assays, respectively. Bioinformatics analysis identified potential targets of quercetin-induced ferroptosis, and molecular docking predicted the binding interaction between quercetin and HSPB1. The expression levels of ferroptosis-related genes at the mRNA level were quantified by qRT-PCR, while the protein levels of heat shock protein beta-1 (HSPB1), glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), Notch1, Notch intracellular domain (NICD) and its downstream effectors HEY1 and HES1 were assessed by western blotting. Ferrostatin-1 was co-treated with quercetin to determine the role of ferroptosis in quercetin-mediated anti-proliferative effects. The Notch1 inhibitor tangeretin was used to explore mechanistic pathways. An in vivo study was conducted using a xenograft mouse model.


## Results
  Quercetin inhibited cell proliferation and colony formation, triggered apoptosis, and promoted ferroptosis in A2780 and SKOV3 cells, marked by GPX4 downregulation and malondialdehyde levels increase. Quercetin decreased HSPB1 expression and was predicted to bind HSPB1. Silencing HSPB1 enhanced quercetin-induced cell death and malondialdehyde accumulation, while its overexpression reversed these effects. HSPB1 regulated Notch1/NICD expression, and Notch1 inhibition amplified ferroptosis. In vivo, quercetin suppressed tumor growth and reduced HSPB1, NICD, and GPX4 levels, and increased 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE).


## Conclusions
  This study demonstrates that quercetin promotes ferroptosis and suppresses ovarian cancer progression through the HSPB1/Notch1 axis, highlighting its potential as an adjuvant in ovarian cancer therapy.

## Introduction
  Ovarian cancer is among the most deadly cancers affecting the female reproductive system, with the five-year survival rate remains below 45%, representing a global threat to women’s health [1, 2]. The standard first-line treatment involves primary cytoreductive surgery followed by chemotherapy. However, post-treatment recurrence, chemoresistance, especially to platinum-based agents, and severe side effects often contribute to disappointing therapeutic efficacy [3, 4]. Thus, it is imperative to explore innovative therapeutic strategies to improve treatment efficacy and ultimately prolong patient survival.

  Since the discovery of ferroptosis in 2012, this unique type of programmed cell death has attracted growing interest in the field of cancer research [5]. Ferroptosis differs from apoptosis and necrosis due to its iron dependency and lipid peroxidation, marked by intracellular iron accumulation and excessive oxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids within cellular membranes [6]. Glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), an essential antioxidant enzyme, prevents ferroptosis by using glutathione (GSH) to reduce lipid hydroperoxides [7]. Abnormal changes in ferroptosis levels not only contributes to tumorigenesis but is also closely linked to cancer chemoresistance [8]. For instance, our previous study [9] showed that dihydroorotate dehydrogenase inhibitors sensitize cervical cancer cells to cisplatin via ferroptosis activation, while Shi et al. [10] reported that targeting ferroptosis effectively sensitizes ovarian cancer cells to cisplatin. Collectively, the findings highlight the therapeutic potential of ferroptosis modulation in cancer treatment [11].

  In recent years, the application of traditional Chinese medicine in cancer treatment has received considerable attention due to its low toxicity and ability to enhance therapeutic efficacy while alleviating the adverse effects of chemotherapy. Quercetin, a natural flavonoid compound, exhibits notable anticancer activities in various types of cancer [12]. Increasing evidence indicates that ferroptosis is an important mechanism underlying the anticancer potential of quercetin [13]. Recent studies has shown that quercetin promotes ferroptosis in multiple malignancies, such as gastric, colorectal, hepatocellular, breast, and oral squamous carcinomas [14–16]. Nevertheless, it is still unclear if quercetin regulates ferroptosis in ovarian cancer.

  As a small heat shock protein, heat shock protein beta-1 (HSPB1) protects cells from ferroptosis by regulating iron metabolism and lipid peroxidation processes [17, 18]. Overexpression of HSPB1 has been shown to stabilize GPX4 while reducing ferritin degradation, thereby suppressing ferroptosis [19]. Conversely, HSPB1 degradation increases intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) and triggers ferroptosis in HER2-low breast cancer cells [20]. Therefore, suppressing HSPB1 could be a potential target in promoting ferroptosis. The highly conserved Notch signaling pathway, which participates in cell differentiation, proliferation, and survival, is often dysregulated in cancer and contributes to tumor progression and therapy resistance [21–23]. The pathway functions through the Notch intracellular domain (NICD), which is released inside the cell following proteolytic cleavage of the Notch receptor. Subsequently, NICD translocates into the nucleus to regulate the transcription of target genes, such as HEY1 and HES1 [24]. Among the Notch receptors, Notch1 can be suppressed by HSPB1 inhibition, and has been shown to influence ferroptosis regulation, suggesting that inhibition of the HSPB1/Notch1 axis may play a major role in promoting ferroptosis [25, 26]. Despite the recognized roles of HSPB1 and Notch1 in ferroptosis, the mechanistic link between quercetin and this axis remains unclear. In other cell types, quercetin has been shown to affect HSPB1 or Notch1: it downregulated Notch1 and inhibited proliferation in U937 cells when combined with shHSPB1, and sensitized glioblastoma cells to t-AUCB via HSPB1 inhibition [26, 27]. These findings suggest that quercetin may regulate HSPB1 and Notch1, but its precise effects on ferroptosis in ovarian cancer have not been systematically investigated. In our study, the biological activities of quercetin in ovarian cancer were examined both in vitro and in vivo. Bioinformatics techniques were applied to screen target genes to illustrate the underlying mechanism. Our results showed that quercetin could suppress ovarian cancer progression by inducing ferroptosis through regulating the HSPB1/Notch1 pathway, a mechanism that has not been reported in ovarian cancer before. These findings reveal a new mechanism for quercetin’s anti-cancer effect and suggest that quercetin might be a potential treatment option for ovarian cancer.


## Materials and methods

### Cell culture
  A2780, SKOV3, and HEK293T cell lines were preserved by CAS Cell Bank (Shanghai, China). DMEM medium (Meilunbio, China) plus 10% FBS (Gibco, USA) was used for cell growth. Cells were incubated at 37℃ with 5% CO₂.


### Reagent and antibodies
  Quercetin was from Sigma (St. Louis, MO, USA), while ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1), tangeretin, and Z-VAD-FMK, which act as inhibitors of ferroptosis, Notch1 signaling, and caspase-dependent apoptosis, respectively, were from MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). The reagents were initially dissolved in dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) as stock solutions and then diluted using DMEM medium, with the final DMSO concentration maintained within a non-cytotoxic range (< 0.1%). Cells treated with this amount of DMSO alone served as the negative control. The concentrations and durations of drug treatments for each cell lines are detailed below. A2780 and SKOV3 cell lines were treated with quercetin (200 µM and 80 µM, respectively), Fer-1 (2 µM), Z-VAD-FMK (20 µM) or tangeretin (20 µM) for 48 h. In this study, the primary antibodies included mouse anti-HSPB1 monoclonal antibody (Proteintech, 66767-1-Ig), rabbit anti-Notch1 monoclonal antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 3608), rabbit anti-NICD monoclonal antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 4147), mouse anti-GPX4 monoclonal antibody (Proteintech, 67763-1-Ig), rabbit anti-HEY1 Polyclonal antibody (Proteintech, 19929-1-AP), mouse anti-HES1 monoclonal antibody (Santa Cruz, sc-166410), mouse anti-4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) monoclonal antibody (Abcam, ab48506), mouse anti-Ki67 monoclonal antibody (Santa Cruz, sc-23900) and mouse anti-GAPDH monoclonal antibody (Affinity Biosciences, #T0004). Secondary antibodies, namely peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit and anti-mouse, were from Biosharp (Shanghai, China).


### Cell viability assay
  A2780 and SKOV3 cells were cultured in the 96-well plates (2 × 103 cells/well) overnight. After cell treatment, CCK-8 (Meilunbio, China) working solution was freshly prepared following manufacturer’s guidelines. Then, each well received 100 µL CCK-8 working solution after removal of the initial medium. After 2 h, optical density at 450 nm was detected by the microplate absorbance reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) to evaluate cell viability. The IC50 values were obtained through nonlinear regression fitting.


### Colony formation assay
  Briefly, A2780 and SKOV3 cells were cultured in the 6-well plates (800 cells/well), then A2780 cells were treated with 100, 200 and 400 µM quercetin and SKOV3 cells with 40, 80 and 120 µM quercetin treatment, respectively. After 48 h, the previous medium was gently replaced with normal DMEM medium. After 14 days, the cells were fixed with 4% freshly prepared paraformaldehyde, and then stained using 0.25% crystal violet (Beyotime, China). The experiments were conducted in triplicate, and the numbers of colony formation were analyzed by the light microscope.


### Flow cytometry assay
  A2780 and SKOV3 cells were grown in the 6-well plates (3 × 105 cells/well), then A2780 cells were treated with 100, 200 and 400 µM quercetin, while SKOV3 cells with 40, 80 and 120 µM quercetin treatment, respectively. After 48 h, the cells were stained using Annexin V-FITC apoptosis detection reagent (Beyotime, China), and CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) was employed for apoptosis detection.


### Real-time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR)
  For RNA extraction, RNA-easy Isolation Reagent (Vazyme, R701-01) was applied. Following the manufacturer’s guidelines, cDNA was synthesized utilizing the reverse transcription kit (TransGen Biotech, P20607, China), and qRT-PCR was implemented with the SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Primer sequences (Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) are listed in Table 1. Relative mRNA levels were quantified by the 2−ΔΔCT method, normalized to GAPDH.

### Western blotting
  RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China) plus 1 mM PMSF (Beyotime, China) was applied for extracting proteins. Cells were subjected to lysis on ice for 30 min. Following lysis, the lysates were centrifuged at 12 000 × g under 4 °C for 20 min. After collection, the protein concentration was determined using BCA assay. Proteins, loaded in equal amounts, were separated via SDS-PAGE method, and then transferred onto the polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Boston, MA, USA). The obtained membranes were subsequently incubated with 5% non-fat milk to block nonspecific binding. After blocking, membranes were soaked in primary antibodies at 4 °C for 16 h. The dilutions of primary antibodies were listed below: HSPB1 (1:5000), Notch1 (1:1000), NICD (1:1000), GPX4 (1:1000), HEY1(1:1000), HES1(1:500), and GAPDH (1:4000). Following washing, membranes were soaked in corresponding secondary antibodies for 60 min. Through ChemiScope imaging platform (Clinx, China), protein signals were visualized. Then, their intensities were quantified with ImageJ software, normalized against GAPDH. Each experiment was repeated at least three independent replicates, and statistical analyses are shown graphically.


### Lipid peroxidation measurement
  Lipid peroxidation levels were assessed utilizing malondialdehyde (MDA) assay kit (Solarbio, China). At the beginning, cells were seeded into the 6-well plates (5 × 10⁵ cells/well), then incubated with quercetin (200 µM and 80 µM for A2780 and SKOV3 cells, respectively) alone or in combination with tangeretin (20 µM) for 48 h. After harvesting, cells were gently suspended using MDA extraction buffer. The sample was centrifuged at 4 °C and 8 000 × g for 10 min, then the supernatant was obtained. After determining protein concentration, remaining lysate was proportionally mixed with fully dissolved MDA working solution and incubated at 100 °C for 60 min. After the samples cooled, they underwent centrifugation at 10 000 × g under room temperature for 10 min, and the supernatant’s absorbance values at 532 nm and 600 nm were measured. The MDA levels were calculated based on the absorbance difference (ΔA = ΔA532 − ΔA600) and normalized to protein concentration.


### Identification of potential ferroptosis-related targets modulated by Quercetin in ovarian cancer
  To explore potential ferroptosis-associated targets modulated by quercetin in ovarian cancer, gene sets were retrieved from three publicly available databases. Quercetin-associated targets were identified using the traditional Chinese medicine systems pharmacology database (TCMSP, http://tcmspw.com/), ovarian cancer-related genes were obtained via GeneCards (http://www.genecards.org), and ferroptosis-associated genes were gathered through FerrDb V2 (http://www.zhounan.org/ferrdb/current/). All retrieved target lists were standardized and exported into Microsoft excel for subsequent analysis. The intersection targets of quercetin treated ovarian cancer, regulated by ferroptosis, were identified using Venn diagrams (http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/static/others/jvenn/example.html).


### Molecular Docking analysis
  To investigate the interaction of quercetin with the active sites of HSPB1, docking simulations were performed through the AutoDock software (http://autodock.scripps.edu/). The 3D structure of quercetin (ID:5280343) was from PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), and HSPB1 (ID: 3Q9P) X-ray crystallographic structure was obtained from RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB, http://www.rcsb.org/). Docking simulations were conducted using AutoDock software, and the resulting docking poses were visualized and analyzed by Pymol software.


### Cell transfection
  HSPB1 short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) plasmids were provided by Shanghai Jiao Tong University, and the HSPB1 overexpression vector (HSPB1-pCDH-GFP + Puro) was obtained from Youbio Biosciences Inc. The shRNA sequences were described as sh1: 5ʹ-CGGTGCTTCACGCGGAAAT-3ʹ, and sh2: 5ʹ-GAGCTGACGGTCAAGACCA-3ʹ. To produce lentiviral particles, the packaging plasmids psPAX2 and pMD2.G were co-transfected into HEK293T cells. The collected viral supernatants were subsequently used to infect A2780 and SKOV3 cells. After infection, puromycin was applied to enrich stable transduced cells, and the efficiency of transfection was validated by western blotting.


### Animal studies
  Twenty female BALB/c nude mice (4 weeks) were sourced from the Zhejiang Weitonglihua Animal Experiment Centre. After 1 week, the animals were allocated randomly into two groups (n = 10 mice/group). A total of 4 × 106 A2780 cells, either overexpressing HSPB1 or carrying the control vector, suspended in 100 µL cold PBS were injected subcutaneously into the right dorsal region of every mouse. After 14 days, animals within each group were allocated to two subgroups (n = 5 per group). Interventions for groups were as below: (i) vector group (sterilized saline); (ii) HSPB1 overexpression group (sterilized saline); (iii) vector + quercetin group (50 mg/kg); (iv) HSPB1 overexpression + quercetin group (50 mg/kg). Quercetin was dissolved in PEG300, Tween-80, and saline, followed by ultrasonic treatment until fully dissolved, and then intraperitoneally injected every two days. Mice were weighed and tumor volumes measured every three days, and tumor volume was determined by the following formula: volume = width2 × length× 1/2. On the 15th day post-injection, mice were sacrificed, followed by tumor dissection and weighing. The in vivo ferroptosis-inducing effects of different treatments were assessed by measuring MDA and GPX4 levels. Ethical approval of animal experiments was obtained from the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Wenzhou Institute, Chinese Academy of Sciences (WIUCAS24110502).


### Immunohistochemistry
  Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were prepared from xenograft tumors in nude mice. After dewaxing, graded dehydration, and antigen retrieval, the slides were washed with PBS three times. Excess liquid was removed, and 3% hydrogen peroxide was applied for 15 min. The sections were then blocked with 5% BSA for 30 min. Primary antibodies against Ki-67 (1:50) and 4-HNE (1:50) were added, and the slides were incubated overnight at 4 °C. On the following day, the slides were allowed to return to room temperature for 1 h and then incubated with the secondary antibody for 1 h. After DAB chromogenic development and hematoxylin counterstaining, the sections were observed and imaged under a light microscope (Leica).


### Statistical analysis
  Cellular experiments were independently repeated in triplicate, with statistical analyses executed via GraphPad Prism 8.0.1. Data with normal distribution are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Differences between two groups were analyzed by Student’s t-test, while one-way ANOVA was applied to assess differences among multiple groups and then Tukey’s post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. Differences were considered significant when P < 0.05. The levels of significance were represented by *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001.


## Results

### Quercetin inhibited proliferation and promoted apoptosis in ovarian cancer cells
  To assess the anticancer potential of quercetin, different concentrations of quercetin were applied to A2780 and SKOV3 cells across multiple time points. CCK-8 analysis indicates that quercetin displayed a significant inhibitory effect on ovarian cancer cell viability, and the 48-hour IC50 values of quercetin were 192.1 µM and 83.55 µM for A2780 and SKOV3 cells, respectively. (Fig. 1A). Based on these values, concentrations of 100, 200, and 400 µM were selected for A2780 cells, and 40, 80, and 120 µM for SKOV3 cells in subsequent studies.

  From the colony formation results, there is a marked reduction in colony numbers in quercetin-treated ovarian cancer cells, indicating significant inhibition of cell proliferation (Fig. 1B).

  Additionally, flow cytometry analysis demonstrated a notable enhancement of apoptosis in A2780 and SKOV3 cells subjected to quercetin treatment (Fig. 1C). These data demonstrate that quercetin inhibits proliferation and promotes apoptosis in ovarian cancer cells.

A Cell viabilities of A2780 and SKOV3 cells treated with various concentrations of quercetin for diverse time periods were detected by CCK-8 (n = 3). B After treating A2780 and SKOV3 cells with quercetin for 14 days, the colony formation and related quantitative analyses were performed (n = 3). C Apoptosis was evaluated in A2780 and SKOV3 cells after 48 h of treatment with quercetin (n = 3). (Data are presented as Mean ± SD **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001)


### Quercetin promoted ferroptosis in ovarian cancer cells
  To assess the induction of ferroptosis by quercetin in ovarian cancer, A2780 and SKOV3 cell lines received quercetin treatments at 200 µM and 80 µM, respectively. Initial qRT-PCR analysis revealed a reduction in GPX4 mRNA expression in the quercetin-treated cells compared to the control (Fig. 2A). Western blotting further confirmed that quercetin treatment led to a significant reduction in the levels of GPX4 protein in A2780 and SKOV3 cells (Fig. 2B).

  Additionally, MDA assay results demonstrated increased lipid peroxidation in the quercetin-treated cells (Fig. 2C). Moreover, quercetin treatment markedly reduced cell viability, and this reduction was significantly reversed by Fer-1 (Fig. 2D), but not by the apoptosis inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK (Fig. S1), indicating that quercetin primarily induces cell death via ferroptosis.

### Quercetin downregulates HSPB1 with potential binding interaction
  To identify potential ferroptosis-related targets of quercetin in ovarian cancer, a Venn diagram analysis was performed, resulting in 18 intersection targets (Fig. 3A). Next, mRNA expression changes of candidate genes were analyzed by qRT-PCR in ovarian cancer cells underwent quercetin treatment. As illustrated in Fig. S2, quercetin significantly decreased the mRNA expression of HSPB1, ALOX5, AR, GSTM1, HIF1A, PARP1, PPARG, and PRKCA in both cell lines, while suppressed the expression of CAV1, HSF1, IL6, TP63 only in A2780 cells, inhibited NQO1 expression only in SKOV3 cells.

  HSPB1, which negatively regulates ferroptosis, was therefore selected for further study. Western blotting analysis confirmed that quercetin reduced HSPB1 protein expression in a concentration-dependent response in A2780 and SKOV3 cells (Fig. 3B). Molecular docking analysis was performed to investigate whether HSPB1 is a direct target of quercetin. The results suggested a potential interaction between quercetin and HSPB1, with a binding affinity of − 6.8 kcal/mol. This relatively low binding energy indicates a spontaneous and potentially stable interaction under physiological conditions, suggesting that quercetin may directly bind to HSPB1 and modulate its function. Specific binding sites between quercetin and various regions of HSPB1 protein were shown by the predicted model (Fig. 3C). Altogether, the data indicate that HSPB1 might serve as a suppressor of ferroptosis induced by quercetin in ovarian cancer cells.

C The interaction between quercetin with HSPB1 was predicted by molecular docking. (Data are presented as Mean ± SD *P < 0.05)


### Quercetin promoted ferroptosis in ovarian cancer cells through regulating HSPB1 expression
  To further explore the function of HSPB1 in quercetin-induced ferroptosis, A2780 and SKOV3 cells lines stably overexpressing or silencing HSPB1 were established. Western blotting analysis was performed to verify successful modulation of HSPB1 in ovarian cancer cells (Fig. 4A and B). The quercetin-mediated suppression of protein levels of GPX4 in ovarian cancer cells was partially reversed after HSPB1 overexpression (Fig. 4C and D). Moreover, HSPB1 overexpression significantly restored cell viability decreased by quercetin (Fig. 4E). In addition, the quercetin-induced increase in MDA levels was effectively reduced by HSPB1 overexpression (Fig. 4F). In contrast, HSPB1 knockdown sensitized A2780 and SKOV3 cells to quercetin (Fig. 4G). Correspondingly, silencing HSPB1 markedly elevated quercetin-induced MDA levels in A2780 and SKOV3 cells (Fig. 4H). The findings highlight HSPB1 is essential for the regulation of ferroptosis induced by quercetin in ovarian cancer cells.

### Quercetin promoted ovarian cancer cells ferroptosis by regulating HSPB1/Notch1 pathway
  To explore whether the HSPB1/Notch1 signaling axis is involved in quercetin-induced ferroptosis, the role of HSPB1 in regulating Notch1 signaling in ovarian cancer cells was examined. The protein expression levels of Notch1 and its cleaved active form NICD were examined in A2780 and SKOV3 cells via western blotting after HSPB1 overexpression or knockdown. These data show that HSPB1 overexpression significantly increases Notch1 and NICD protein levels (Fig. 5A), while HSPB1 knockdown decreases the protein expression of both proteins (Fig. 5B), indicating that HSPB1 positively regulates Notch1 signaling activity in ovarian cancer.

  Fig. 5HSPB1 positively regulated Notch1 signaling activity in ovarian cancer cells. A Notch1 and NICD protein levels in A2780 and SKOV3 cells overexpressing HSPB1 (n = 3). B Notch1 and NICD protein expressions in A2780 and SKOV3 cells after HSPB1 knockdown (n = 3). (Data are presented as Mean ± SD *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001)

  To investigate the function of HSPB1/Notch1 pathway in ferroptosis induced by quercetin, A2780 and SKOV3 cells lines with stable HSPB1 overexpression, along with their corresponding controls, were incubated with quercetin. In parallel, HSPB1-overexpressing cells were co-treated with quercetin and the Notch1 inhibitor tangeretin. Western blotting analysis showed that quercetin treatment reduced NICD and GPX4 protein levels, while HSPB1 overexpression partially reversed this effect. Co-treatment with tangeretin further decreased the expression of NICD and GPX4 (Fig. 6A and B). In addition, we independently assessed HEY1 and HES1 protein levels, both of which exhibited alterations parallel to the changes observed in NICD (Fig. S3). CCK-8 results showed that HSPB1 overexpression restored cell viability suppressed by quercetin, while tangeretin exerted an additional inhibitory effect (Fig. 6C). The MDA assay showed increased lipid peroxidation upon tangeretin co-treatment. This effect was attenuated by quercetin treatment combined with HSPB1 overexpression (Fig. 6D). The results indicate that HSPB1 activates Notch1 pathway, while quercetin promotes ferroptosis by blocking this pathway through reducing NICD levels.

### Quercetin promoted ovarian cancer ferroptosis in vivo
  To further investigate whether quercetin promotes ferroptosis in vivo, xenograft model was constructed in nude mice, and quercetin was administered by intraperitoneal injection every two days. During the treatment period, tumor volumes gradually increased in both the control and treatment groups. However, treatment with quercetin in the HSPB1 vector group significantly reduced tumor growth, showing the greatest reduction in both tumor volume and tumor weight. Notably, these suppressive effects of quercetin were partially reversed by HSPB1 overexpression (Fig. 7A-D). No statistically differences were observed in body weight across the groups, suggesting minimal systemic toxicity (Fig. 7E). Consistent with the in vitro findings, MDA levels were elevated in the quercetin-treated group, and this increase was attenuated by HSPB1 overexpression (Fig. 7F). In tumor tissues, quercetin markedly reduced the protein expression of HSPB1, NICD and GPX4, and this suppression was significantly reversed by HSPB1 overexpression (Fig. 7G). In addition, immunohistochemical staining showed that quercetin significantly reduced Ki-67 expression, and this reduction was clearly reversed by HSPB1 overexpression. Quercetin also increased the level of 4-HNE, indicating higher lipid peroxidation, while HSPB1 overexpression caused a slight decrease in 4-HNE levels (Fig. 7H). Collectively, these in vivo results confirm that quercetin induces ferroptosis and inhibits tumor growth by targeting the HSPB1/Notch1 signaling pathway.

## Discussion
  Ferroptosis, distinguished by iron overload and lipid peroxidation, has attracted growing attention in cancer research. It not only regulates tumor progression but also sensitizes chemo-resistant cells to chemotherapy drugs, making it an attractive candidate for therapeutic intervention [11, 28]. Although ferroptosis shows promise, there are few clinically approved drugs that induce it. Quercetin, a natural compound, has been reported to promote ferroptosis in various cancers through multiple mechanisms, including downregulation of SLC1A5 and modulation of the Nrf2/xCT pathway in gastric cancer [16], suppression of the mTOR/S6K pathway and SLC7A11 in oral squamous cell carcinoma [14], and inhibition of the PI3K/Akt and Notch1 pathways in breast cancer [15]. In ovarian cancer, previous studies have primarily focused on quercetin’s combination with chemotherapeutics, its ability to induce apoptosis, or its potential to overcome cisplatin resistance [29, 30]. The role of quercetin in inducing ferroptosis remains largely unexplored, and the present study provides an in-depth investigation of its underlying mechanisms. GPX4, a key enzyme in the glutathione peroxidase family, is essential for reducing phospholipid hydroperoxides in cellular membranes [31]. As the central negative modulator of ferroptosis, inhibition or loss of GPX4 function causes accumulation of ROS and lipid peroxides (LPO), ultimately triggering ferroptosis [32, 33]. In this study, CCK-8 assays revealed that ferroptosis inhibition partially rescued the viability of quercetin-treated ovarian cancer cells. In parallel, MDA assays indicated a significant increase in lipid peroxidation upon quercetin treatment. Furthermore, qRT-PCR and western blotting analyses confirmed quercetin significantly downregulated GPX4 expression at mRNA expression and protein levels. Collectively, together with existing evidence, the findings suggest that quercetin exerts its antitumor effects in ovarian cancer through the induction of ferroptosis.

  To further elucidate the mechanism underlying quercetin-induced ferroptosis in ovarian cancer, bioinformatics analyses were conducted to identify potential target genes, followed by qRT-PCR validation. We found that quercetin significantly downregulated HSPB1 mRNA expression. Earlier investigations have implicated HSPB1 negatively regulates ferroptosis [18]. He et al. [34] reported that HSPB1 interacts with PRDX1 to reduce ROS and protect pancreatic acinar cells from oxidative stress. Zhang et al. [35] found HSPB1 to be enriched in pancreatic cancer-derived exosomes, where it promoted tumor growth, invasion, and resistance to ferroptosis. Similarly, Liang et al. [17] showed that HSPB1 suppresses ferroptosis and promotes doxorubicin resistance in breast cancer. Consistent with these findings, our western blotting analysis confirmed that quercetin markedly downregulated HSPB1 protein expression. Molecular docking further suggested a potential binding interaction between quercetin and HSPB1. Functional assays revealed that HSPB1 overexpression partially reversed quercetin-induced ferroptosis, supported by elevated cell viability and reduced MDA levels, whereas HSPB1 knockdown enhanced these effects. The study reveals that HSPB1 functions to negatively regulate ferroptosis induced by quercetin in ovarian cancer cells.

  Given that HSPB1 has also been reported to regulate the Notch signaling pathway in various cancers, we further explored whether the Notch1 axis was involved in quercetin-induced ferroptosis. The Notch1 signaling pathway is critical for ovarian cancer cell survival and migration, with downstream effectors such as HEY1 and HES1 promoting proliferation and inhibiting cell death [36, 37]. Li et al. [38] showed that blocking Notch1 enhances the efficacy of erastin, a ferroptosis inducer, in salivary gland cancer. Similarly, Xing et al. [25] showed that emodin promotes ferroptosis in renal cancer through inhibiting Notch1. In our study, quercetin increased total Notch1 levels but significantly reduced NICD expression. A possible mechanism is that quercetin impairs γ-secretase-mediated cleavage of Notch1, reducing NICD generation. Certain flavonoids have been reported to interfere with this step, which could lead to precursor Notch1 accumulation [39]. Consistent with this hypothesis, quercetin treatment led to downregulation of NICD downstream target genes, HEY1 and HES1. Furthermore, treatment with the Notch1 inhibitor tangeretin suppressed both Notch1 and NICD expression, downregulated GPX4, decreased cell viability while elevating MDA levels. The findings indicate that quercetin induces ferroptosis in ovarian cancer through targeting NICD and inhibiting GPX4.

  Although our findings provide important insights into quercetin-induced ferroptosis in ovarian cancer, several limitations should be acknowledged. This study relied on only two epithelial ovarian cancer cell lines, without validation in drug-resistant models or patient-derived samples, which may limit the applicability of the results. Additionally, the direct interaction between quercetin and HSPB1, predicted by molecular docking, requires further experimental confirmation. Moreover, future studies should explore the in vivo pharmacokinetics and comprehensive systemic toxicity of quercetin.

  In summary, our findings demonstrate that quercetin exerts anticancer effects in ovarian cancer by inducing ferroptosis. This study uncovers a novel mechanism of quercetin in ovarian cancer. Quercetin induces ferroptosis via the HSPB1/Notch1 axis and inhibits cancer progression, highlighting its potential as an adjuvant therapeutic strategy.
