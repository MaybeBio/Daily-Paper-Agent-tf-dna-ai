# Hydrogel with cell-cell adhesion cues enhances neural regeneration


## Abstract
  Cell-cell adhesion is crucial for maintaining cell functions and the integrity of tissue structure in organisms. However, cell-cell adhesion cues have not been effectively replicated in biomaterials and the associated mechanisms that enhance neural regeneration remained largely unexplored. Here, we present a diffusive N-cadherin functionalized hydrogel system, which provided cell-cell adhesion cues to modulate intercellular communications to significantly promote the formation of active neural network via thrombospondin-1 mediated neural communication and activation of TGF-β/Smad pathway. The dynamic assembly of N-cadherin at cell-hydrogel interface driven by adhered neurons effectively facilitated the reshaping of membrane protrusions to initiate intercellular adherens junctions. Further, this hydrogel system promisingly promoted neurological function recovery in rats following traumatic brain injury. Our study provides the principle of replicating diffusive cell adhesion molecules to mediate cell-cell adhesion in hydrogels, which may have broad applications in developing engineered biomaterials aimed at modulating cell fates in regeneration of various tissues.


## Introduction
  Besides cell-extracellular matrix (ECM) interactions, cell-cell adhesion and communication play critical roles in maintaining cellular functions and the structural integrity of tissues in organisms1–3. Cell-cell adhesion, mediated by membrane proteins, such as adhesion proteins and receptor-ligand pairs, is essential for initiating intercellular communication that sustains tissue homeostasis and drives key physiological processes, including cellular proliferation, survival, migration, and differentiation4–7. Particularly in the central nervous system, cell-cell adhesion-dependent neuron-neuron and astrocyte-neuron interactions can facilitate synaptogenesis and the functionality of neural networks, which are significant for brain functions, including motor function, memory, and learning8–12. However, the mechanisms of cell-cell adhesion cues that enhance neural regeneration remain largely unexplored.

  Cell adhesion molecules are crucial in facilitating cell-cell adhesion and recognition by bringing apposed cell membranes into contact, thereby activating tissue development or regeneration processes13. For instance, favored by the dynamic flow of the membrane, diffusive cadherin monomers can assemble side-by-side to form clusters and induce intercellular adherens junctions (AJ) for establishing effective cell-cell adhesion14–16. Recently, based on the self-assembling of the phospholipids into membranes on polymer surfaces in aqueous conditions, artificial lipid bilayers mimicking cell membranes have been developed to favor protein lateral mobility, which offer possibilities for investigating interactions of cell adhesion molecules in biological process17–19. However, the inability to adapt to three-dimensional tissue structures limits the application of such artificial systems in vivo, especially in filling and repairing irregular trauma in the brain or spinal cord. On the other hand, although cell adhesion molecules, such as N-cadherin, have also been covalently conjugated to biopolymer chains without diffusivity in recent studies, the therapeutic effect on repairing CNS injury was significantly limited, due to cell-cell adhesion significantly relies on free diffusion of N-cadherin in CNS20,21. Moreover, the biological mechanism of N-cadherin based hydrogel systems mimicking cell-cell adhesion on promoting neurological function recovery was largely unexplored (Supplementary Table 1).

  In this study, we present a hydrogel system characterized by dynamic diffusion of N-cadherin to modulate intercellular communications in the central nervous system. The successful cell contact-driven assembly of N-cadherin within the hydrogel provides cell-cell adhesion cues to enhance the formation of an active neural network. The neural regrowth-enhancing effects of this system are validated across various hydrogel platforms. The results of RNA-seq and Western blot indicate that this hydrogel system could effectively inhibit apoptosis by significantly down-regulating the Bax/Bcl-2 protein level; meanwhile, the TGF-β/Smad and AKT/mTOR pathways are also activated through THBS-1-mediated neural communication by significantly up-regulating phosphorylation levels of Smad, AKT, mTOR, and S6K proteins, resulting in the promotion of neural growth. Furthermore, the hydrogel system applied to traumatic brain injury (TBI) models not only effectively promotes neural regeneration and synapse formation, but also notably inhibits microglia over-activation to hinder glial scar formation, thereby facilitating neurological function recovery in rats following TBI. This finding provides a strategy for developing materials for the treatment and regeneration of the nervous system trauma. ## Results and discussion

### Preparation and characterization of lipid modified hydrogel
  To prepare lipid-modified hydrogel (L@SFMA), 1 mL solution of 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DOPC) and 1,2-dipalmitoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphothioethanol (DPTE) lipid mixture was mixed with 2 mL SFMA solution (15% w/w) containing photoinitiator (0.5% w/w). Upon UV irradiation (365 nm, 30 mW/cm2), methacryloyl moieties on silk fibroin underwent photo-crosslinking, facilitating the gelation of the precursor solution (Fig. 2A and B). DPTE was conjugated to the silk fibroin chain through the addition reaction between thiol groups on DPTE and methacryloyl moieties on silk fibroin chain. DOPC and DPTE self-assembled within the hydrogel. To demonstrate the rapid process of photo-induced formation of fluid lipid bilayers modified hydrogel, single-distilled water or lipid solution was dripped onto the surface of the SFMA hydrogel with UV irradiation (365 nm, 30 mW/cm2). The contact angles of lipid solution droplets on SFMA hydrogels significantly decreased within 30 seconds of UV irradiation compared to those in the single-distilled water group, due to the rapid photo-reaction of DPTE with silk fibroin chain and the self-assembling of lipid vesicles forming lipid bilayer coating on hydrogel, which enhanced the hydrophilicity of the L@SFMA hydrogel (Fig. 2C). The gentle decrease of contact angles in lipid solution group without irradiation may be due to physical adsorption of vesicles at the interface by silk fibroin. To further demonstrate whether the vesicles can self-assemble to form lipid bilayer in L@SFMA hydrogel upon irradiation, fluorescence correlation spectroscopy (FCS) was used to investigate the diffusion of lipid molecules. The FCS autocorrelation curve indicated that the movement of lipid molecules in the L@SFMA hydrogel conformed to a two-dimensional (2D) diffusion law22,23, demonstrating that the self-assembled lipids uniformly distributed within L@SFMA hydrogel (Fig. 2D). Three-dimensional reconstruction fluorescent images revealed that the red fluorescence dye-labeled lipids were uniformly distributed within the L@SFMA hydrogel (Fig. 2G). The scanning electron microscope (SEM) images demonstrated that L@SFMA hydrogel held interconnected microporous structures. Notably, the L@SFMA hydrogel exhibited a wrinkled surface of microporous structures (indicated by yellow pentagrams) and none lipid vesicles were found, due to the lipid bilayer uniformly distributed within L@SFMA hydrogel, while the SFMA hydrogel displayed a flat surface, indicating the successful lipid bilayer loading (Fig. 2E). The pore size of L@SFMA hydrogel was 146.3 ± 3.842 μm, providing ample space for cell growth and elongation24 (Fig. 2F). Furthermore, the introduction of lipids into the hydrogel system did not significantly affect the storage modulus (G’) and loss modulus (G”) of the hydrogel (Fig. 2H–K). The efficient photocuring process (gelation time of less than 5 min) enabled the hydrogel system to effectively fill irregular damage25. ### Diffusive N-cadherin in NL@SFMA hydrogel
  Cell-cell adhesion significantly relies on free diffusion of adhesion molecules based on dynamic flow of the membrane27. In our L@SFMA hydrogel system, we hypothesized that the diffusivity of lipids within the hydrogel could be modulated by altering the ratio of DPTE (non-mobile lipids, conjugated to the hydrogel network) and DOPC (mobile lipids). Therefore, lipid samples were labeled by Texas Red-DHPE and the diffusivity of lipids with various ratios (DPTE/(DPTE + DOPC): 0%, 5%, 50%, 100%) in the hydrogels was evaluated by fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) assay. As shown in Fig. 3A and B, hydrogels containing 0% or 5% DPTE exhibited desirable fluorescence recovery after photobleaching within 20 min, indicating that lipids maintained diffusive in hydrogels containing 5% DPTE. As the content of DPTE increased to 50% or 100%, the diffusivity of lipids in hydrogels was decreased dramatically, due to the covalent binding of DPTE to the silk fibroin chain, which restricts lipid movement. Because the interaction between the lipid bilayer and the hydrogel network within the hydrogel system containing 0% DPTE was unstable, the L@SFMA hydrogel containing 5% DPTE was selected for further study.Fig. 3Diffusive N-cadherin in NL@SFMA hydrogel.A, B Diffusivity of Texas Red-DHPE labeled lipids with various ratios (DPTE/(DPTE + DOPC): 0%, 5%, 50%, 100%) in the hydrogels evaluated by fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) assay (n = 3 hydrogels), values represent means ± S.E.M.   Next, in order to equip hydrogel with cell-cell adhesion cues, N-cadherin, a transsynaptic adhesion protein, was linked to 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-[(N-(5-amino-1-carboxypentyl) iminodiacetic acid succinyl] (DGS-nta) via nickel-coupled His tags and introduced to L@SFMA hydrogel system through self-assembling to obtain a diffusive N-cadherin functionalized hydrogel (NL@SFMA hydrogel). Within the membrane, N-cadherin monomer can freely diffuse with the dynamic flow of the interfaces and form intercellular adhesion junctions in intercellular interactions15. Therefore, N-cadherin was labeled by Alexa Fluor™ 488 and the diffusivity of N-cadherin in NL@SFMA hydrogels was evaluated by FRAP assay. Compared to N-cadherin directly conjugated in hydrogel network (N-SFMA group), N-cadherin was freely diffusive in NL@SFMA hydrogels, which may further facilitate N-cadherin-mediated adhesive roles and intercellular interactions (Fig. 3C and D). To further evaluate the stability of this coating, NL@SFMA hydrogels were immersed in PBS for 7 days followed by fluorescence bleaching recovery assays. It was found that N-cadherin in the NL@SFMA hydrogels still maintained effective diffusivity even after 7 days, indicating the high stability of this hydrogel supports subsequent studies in biological environments, including those in the presence of cells (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B).


### Cell-cell adhesion cues in hydrogel promote neural growth
  N-cadherin plays a crucial role in neural development and regeneration by facilitating processes such as neuronal migration, axonal elongation, and synapse formation28. Diffusive N-cadherin on the cell surface can assemble side-by-side to form clusters via cadherin-cadherin interaction to induce cell-cell adherens junction and initiate cell-cell contact (Fig. 3E), which allows cells to migrate, differentiate, mature, and ultimately to form neural circuits29. Therefore, to demonstrate whether the diffusive N-cadherin in NL@SFMA hydrogel can form clusters at membrane protrusions to promote neuronal growth, N-cadherin in NL@SFMA hydrogel was labeled by Alexa Fluor™ 488 and PC12 cells were seeded on the hydrogel. As shown in Fig. 3F and G, N-cadherin at the cell membrane-hydrogel interface assembled into clusters. Within 30 min of incubation, dynamic formation and disassembly of N-cadherin clusters occurred, coinciding with the reshaping of membrane protrusions that facilitate neuronal migration, due to free diffusion of N-cadherin in NL@SFMA hydrogel (Fig. 3H, I and Supplementary Movie 1). Furthermore, to investigate the capacity of NL@SFMA hydrogel on promoting axonal elongation, primary cells from cerebral cortex of E16 Sprague-Dawley rat pups were seeded on SFMA, L@SFMA, N-SFMA or NL@SFMA hydrogels respectively for 3 days, and the neural cytoskeleton was labeled with NF200 antibody at 1, 2, 3 days. Attributed to cell-cell adhesions mediated by N-cadherin, neurons cultured on the N-SFMA and NL@SFMA hydrogels showed a significant increase in neurite length, which was more rapid than previously reported30,31. More importantly, compared to neurons in the N-SFMA groups, the NL@SFMA group exhibited significantly enhanced axonal elongation and improved intercellular connections between neurons during 3 days of incubation (Fig. 4A–C), indicating that the hydrogel with diffusive N-cadherins can better promote axonal elongation and neural communication. In addition, neurons in the NL@SFMA group migrated farther with a wider range of direction (Fig. 4D, E and Supplementary Movie 2–5). To further demonstrate the validity of NL@SFMA hydrogel in enhancing synaptic activity and neuronal communication, electrophysiological recordings were employed to investigate synaptic transmission between neurons32. Spontaneous excitatory postsynaptic currents (sEPSCs) were observed in autaptic cultures of cortical neurons grown on NL@SFMA hydrogels for 14 days (Fig. 4H–I). The frequency (Fig. 4J), rather than the amplitude (Fig. 4K), of sEPSCs was significantly increased compared to that of neurons cultured on N-SFMA hydrogels. This indicates that the NL@SFMA hydrogel, which incorporates diffusive N-cadherin, can effectively enhance synaptic activity and neuronal communication primarily by increasing either the presynaptic release probability or the number of synapses, rather than altering postsynaptic receptor function. It has been reported that the initial neurite outgrowth is typically determined by N-cadherin localization following cell division, and increased concentration of N-cadherin at one pole of the neuron can result in neurite formation33. Therefore, the enrichment of diffusive N-cadherin in NL@SFMA provided cell-cell adhesion cues that enhance neuronal migration, neurite sprouting, and neural communication, thereby benefiting the formation of the neural network. Moreover, it was known that the central nervous system (CNS) has a limited ability to regenerate after injury, due to the pathophysiological challenges in axonal regeneration and the reconstruction of functional connections between neurons34. Therefore, to verify the effect of this hydrogel system (NL@SFMA) on promoting the axonal regeneration of cortical neurons, SND450 neural culture plates were used to isolate neuronal cytoplasm and axons to mimic the scenario of axonal rupture after TBI.   In order to further demonstrate the three-dimensional application of the hydrogel platform, PC12 cells were encapsulated in the hydrogel system and stained after 5 days of 3D cultured. The results showed that PC12 cells could survive normally in the hydrogel following 5 days of 3D culture, and almost no dead cells were observed. Moreover, the length/width ratio of PC12 cells in the NL@SFMA group was significantly higher than that in other groups, indicating that the NL@SFMA hydrogel can successfully promote the axonal growth of PC12 cells in 3D culture (Supplementary Fig. 1C-D). Next, the effect of cell-cell adhesion cues on enhancing neural growth in other commonly used light-responsive hydrogel platforms was also determined. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 2, significantly improved neurite outgrowth was observed in neurons cultured on various light-responsive hydrogels containing diffusive N-cadherin, such as gelatin, alginate, hyaluronic acid based hydrogel platforms, indicating that the principles of replicating diffusive cell adhesion molecules mediated cell-cell adhesion in hydrogels can be broadly applied to various biomaterials and tissue outcomes.


### Biological mechanisms of NL@SFMA hydrogel in promoting neural growth
  Next, the underlying biological mechanisms by which NL@SFMA hydrogel promotes neurite growth and neural communication were investigated using RNA-Seq analyses (Supplementary Fig. 3). A Gene Ontology (GO) Chord plot for clustering enrichment analysis revealed significantly upregulated biological processes, including synapse organization, neuron migration and cell-cell adhesion, as well as downregulated process of mitochondrial apoptosis in primary cells from the cerebral cortex of E16 Sprague-Dawley rat pups cultured on NL@SFMA hydrogel after 24 h of incubation (Fig. 5A). The proteins Bax and Bcl-2, members of the Bcl-2 family, play critical roles in regulating apoptosis by modulating mitochondrial membrane permeability35. Bax is a pro-apoptotic protein and Bcl-2 is a protein inhibiting apoptosis36. A significantly reduced ratio of Bax to Bcl-2 expression was observed in cells of the NL@SFMA group, indicating that NL@SFMA, with diffusive N-cadherin, effectively inhibited cell apoptosis by regulating mitochondrial activity (Fig. 5B and C). To further explore mitochondrial activity, mitochondrial membrane potential fluorescence staining assay and ATP content measurements were conducted.   Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis indicated the activation of the transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) signaling pathway via thrombospondin-1 (THBS-1) in cells cultured on the NL@SFMA hydrogel for 24 h, compared to those cultured on the N-SFMA hydrogel with fixed N-cadherin (Fig. 5A). THBS-1, a member of the thrombospondin family, can act as an activator of latent TGF-β by the interaction of a specific sequence in THBS-1 (K412RFK415) with the latent TGF-β complex37. THBS-1 is also a synaptogenic factor that mediates cell-cell communications in the brain by engaging with membrane receptors to promote synaptogenesis38. The significantly upregulated expression of THBS-1 and TGF-β were confirmed in cells cultured on the NL@SFMA hydrogel through quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) assays (Fig. 5G). To verify whether the effect of NL@SFMA hydrogel on promoting neurite growth is mediated by activating THBS-1-associated cell-cell communication, primary cortical cells were cultured on the NL@SFMA hydrogel with different concentrations of Gabapentin (an antagonist of THBS-1 receptor α2δ-1 on neurons38) or LSKL (Leu-Ser-Lys-Leu peptide, a competitive antagonist of THBS-139). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 4, the effect of NL@SFMA hydrogel on promoting neurite growth was significantly attenuated by 30 μM Gabapentin or LSKL. Therefore, 30 μM Gabapentin (Gab) was chosen for use in subsequent experiments as a THBS-1 inhibitor. To further eliminate the interference of off-target effects of inhibitors, the THBS-1 gene in neurons were knocked down using siRNA and then cultured on NL@SFMA hydrogels. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 5, the length of neurites in the siTHBS-1 group was significantly reduced, which further confirms that the effect of the NL@SFMA hydrogel on promoting neurite growth was mediated by activating THBS-1-associated cell-cell communication. Given that the classical TGF-β/Smad pathway is crucial for neuronal growth and synaptogenesis40,41, the state of Smad2, Smad3, and Smad4 within this pathway was investigated. The phosphorylation of Smad2 and Smad3 was significantly activated, and the expression of Smad4 proteins was notably increased in cells cultured on the NL@SFMA hydrogel; these effects were attenuated by Gabapentin (Fig. 5H–J). Furthermore, it is well known that the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway plays a crucial role in axonal regeneration42,43 and KEGG enrichment analysis also indicated the activation of the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway in cells cultured on the NL@SFMA hydrogel for 24 h. Therefore, to further explore the underlying biological mechanisms by which NL@SFMA hydrogel promotes axonal regeneration, we investigated the status of hub molecules associated with this signaling axis. AKT is a serine/threonine-specific protein kinase highly expressed in the nervous system. Phosphorylation of Thr308, Thr309, and Thr305 sites in the kinase domain, as well as Ser473, Ser474, and Ser472 sites in the regulatory domain, can lead to complete activation of AKT. Western blot results showed that compared to the SFMA, L@SFMA, and N-SFMA groups, the expression levels of AKT and pAKT in the NL@SFMA group were significantly upregulated, with the phosphorylation level of Ser473 site (Fig. 5K) being significantly higher. mTOR (mammalian target of rapamycin protein) is one of the important downstream targets of the AKT signaling pathway. Activated AKT further phosphorylates mTOR, which participates in biological events such as neuronal development and differentiation, axonal regeneration, and synaptic plasticity by regulating overall or local protein synthesis. While studying the phosphorylation level of mTOR, we further investigated activation of its downstream signaling molecule, ribosomal protein kinase S6K. Western blot results showed that compared to the SFMA, L@SFMA, and N-SFMA groups, the pmTOR/mTOR ratio (Fig. 5L) and pS6K/S6K ratio (Fig. 5M) were significantly upregulated in the NL@SFMA group. These results indicated that NL@SFMA hydrogel can activate AKT/mTOR signaling pathway to promote axon regeneration. These results indicate that NL@SFMA hydrogel with diffusive N-cadherin can provide cell-cell adhesion cues to facilitate cell vitality and promote THBS-1 mediated neural communication and neuronal growth by activating TGF-β/Smad and AKT/mTOR pathway.


### NL@SFMA hydrogel exhibits favorable biocompatibility
  Prior to in vivo application, the cytocompatibility and hemocompatibility of the SFMA, L@SFMA, and NL@SFMA hydrogels were assessed. PC12 cells were seeded on hydrogels, respectively, and the cell viability was evaluated by CCK8 assay. Cells in all three hydrogel groups exhibited normal proliferation and good cell viability during 3 days of incubation. Meanwhile, all of these hydrogels displayed a hemolysis ratio of less than 1% (Supplementary Fig. 6A–C). These results indicate that these hydrogels held good blood compatibility and cytocompatibility.


### NL@SFMA hydrogel enhances traumatic brain injury repair
  Dysregulated interregional neural communication is a critical issue in traumatic brain injury (TBI)44. NL@SFMA hydrogel has been shown to enhance neuronal migration, neurite outgrowth, and neural communication in vitro by providing cell-cell adhesion cues. Therefore, the capacity of this hydrogel on facilitating neurological function recovery in TBI model was further examined. A TBI model was established in Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats using a free fall striking device by the modified Feeney’s free-fall method applied to the center of the right parietal bone (parameters: an impact depth of 2 mm, a weight of 40 g, and a height of 20 cm)45. All TBI rats were divided into four groups: the control group (treated with normal saline), the SFMA group (treated with SFMA hydrogel), the L@SFMA group (treated with L@SFMA hydrogel), and the NL@SFMA group (treated with NL@SFMA hydrogel) (Fig. 6A). To evaluate the neurological functional recovery, the modified neurological severity score (mNSS) and rotarod test were performed on days 1, 3, 7, 14, and 21 post treatment46. As shown in Fig. 6B and C, the mNSS scores in each group exhibited a decreasing trend over time. Notably, rats in the NL@SFMA group exhibited significantly lower mNSS scores compared to the other three groups on days 21 and 28 post treatment. Additionally, in the rotarod test, while the duration for which rats remained on the rotarod increased progressively across all groups following TBI, rats in NL@SFMA group achieved a significantly longer duration compared to the other groups on days 21 and 28 post treatment.   Microglia are a type of glial cell and the main immune cells in the central nervous system45. When injury occurs, microglial activation can be divided into two processes. Firstly, microglia polarize toward the pro-inflammatory (M1) phenotype, producing pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and expressing high levels of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) for NO production. Then, microglia polarize into the M2 phenotype, expressing anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-4, IL-10, TGF-β and etc) to promote injury recovery. However, overactivated M1 microglia can further activate reactive astrocytes by secreting factors, causing the formation of glial scars that impair recovery47,48. In order to explore the effect of these hydrogel systems on regulating microglia polarization to inhibit glial scar formation, BV2 cells were cultured on 4 types of hydrogels and observed via immunofluorescence staining. The results showed that the M2/M1 fluorescence intensity ratio in the NL@SFMA group was significantly higher than that in the other groups (Supplementary Fig. 7A, B), indicating that the NL@SFMA hydrogel can promote the polarization of BV2 cells to the M2 type. Meanwhile, qPCR results also showed that the NL@SFMA hydrogel promoted the expression of M2-related genes (IL-4 and TGF-β) but inhibited the expression of M1-related genes (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) in BV2 cells (Supplementary Fig. 7 C). Moreover, in in vivo experiments, immunostaining assays were conducted to characterize microglial polarization in the injured area at 7 days post-treatment. Images of immunofluorescent staining for iNOS (M1 phenotype) and CD206 (M2 phenotype) revealed that the M2/M1 fluorescence intensity ratio was significantly higher in the injured area of the NL@SFMA group (Fig. 7C and D). Further immunostaining assay was conducted to characterize the regenerated cells in the injured area at 28 days post treatment. Images of immunofluorescent staining for GFAP and Tuj1 revealed a large number of Tuj1 positive neurons in the injured area of the NL@SFMA group, indicating the regeneration of dense neural networks following treatment (Fig. 7E and F). Increased astrogliosis can lead to glial scars and secondary neuronal damage following TBI49. The control group exhibited significant reactive astrocyte scarring and hippocampal tissue damage, whereas the NL@SFMA group showed significantly less reactive astrocyte scarring around the injured sites, further facilitating neurological function recovery (Fig. 7G). These results indicated that the NL@SFMA hydrogel holds a strong capacity to inhibit microglia overactivation to modulate the inflammatory microenvironment and glial scar formation, thereby facilitating neural regeneration.

  Subsequently, to investigate the effect of hydrogel treatment on brain tissue apoptosis, we collected brain tissues from TBI rats at 3 days post-treatment to extract proteins and detect the expression of apoptosis-related proteins. Western blot results were consistent with those from the in vitro experiments, which showed that TBI rats treated with NL@SFMA hydrogels had significantly reduced Bax/Bcl-2 expression (Fig. 8A and 7B). Meanwhile, TUNEL staining also showed that there were significantly fewer apoptotic positive cells in the NL@SFMA group than in other groups (Fig. 8C–E). These results all proved that treatment with NL@SFMA hydrogel can effectively inhibit brain tissue apoptosis in TBI rats. Previous studies have indicated that THBS-1 may be beneficial for maintaining blood-brain barrier (BBB) integrity in the early phase and facilitating functional recovery in the later phase following traumatic brain injury (TBI)50.   Here, we presented a diffusive N-cadherin-equipped hydrogel system that provides cues for cell-cell adhesion, thereby modulating intercellular communications to promote the formation of active neural networks following neural injury. The dynamic assembly of N-cadherin at the cell membrane-hydrogel interface occurred on a timescale of minutes, facilitating the reshaping of membrane protrusions that initiate intercellular adherens junctions and significantly enhance long-term cell-cell communication. Although this research primarily focuses on N-cadherin and neural regeneration, the principles of replicating diffusive cell adhesion molecules to mediate cell-cell adhesion in hydrogels may shed light on the development of engineered biomaterials aimed at modulating various cell fates in the regeneration of diverse tissues, as well as investigating the roles of other cell surface molecules across different organs during development.


## Methods

### Preparation of vesicle solutions
  Solutions of 25 mg/mL DOPC (1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine, Avanti, AL, USA), 25 mg/mL DPTE (1,2-dipalmitoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphothioethanol sodium salt, Avanti, AL, USA), and 0.01 mg/mL DGS-nta (1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-[(N-(5-amino-1-carboxypentyl)iminodiacetic acid)succinyl] nickel salt, Avanti, AL, USA) were prepared in chloroform. The solutions were combined in varying quantities to achieve DPTE: (DOPC + DPTE) at molar ratios of 0%, 5%, 50%, or 100%.

  The lipid mixtures were dried under nitrogen gas, and excess chloroform was removed by vacuum drying. The lipid mixtures were then rehydrated in 1 mL of rehydration buffer (1 mM sucrose-sodium bicarbonate solution) to a concentration of 1 mg/mL. The mixture was allowed to swell at 75 °C for 2 h and subsequently extruded through a 50 nm polycarbonate membrane (Whatman® Nucleopore Track-etched membrane, Avanti Polar Lipids, AL, USA).


### Preparation of hydrogels
  Raw silk fiber was obtained from Xianjida Cocoon Silk Products Co., Ltd. (Nantong, China). Following previously established procedures,53 the silk fiber was degummed in a sodium carbonate (Na2CO3) solution (0.5% w/w) (Macklin, China) for 30 min, repeated three times to effectively remove sericin proteins. A total of 10.0 g of degummed silk was dissolved in 50 mL of lithium bromide solution (9.3 M, Meryer, China) at 60 °C and stirred for 30 min. Subsequently, 2.0 mL of methacrylic anhydride was gradually added to the mixture, which was stirred for an additional 3 h and then dialyzed against distilled water for 3 days at 4 °C to yield a methacrylated silk fibroin (SFMA) solution.

  SFMA hydrogel: The SFMA solution was concentrated in air at 4 °C to a concentration of 10% w/w, and then mixed with 0.5% w/w photoinitiator (2-hydroxy-1-(4-(hydroxyethoxy)phenyl)-2-methyl-1-propanone, RGACURE 2959, CIBA Chemicals, Basel, Switzerland). The mixture was irradiated with ultraviolet light (365 nm, 30 mW/cm2) for 5 min to obtain SFMA hydrogel.

  SFMA hydrogel: 10 μg/mL N-cadherin was added to the above SFMA hydrogel pre-solution, then irradiated with ultraviolet light for 5 min to obtain N-SFMA hydrogel.

  L@SFMA hydrogel: 1 mL vesicle solution prepared previously containing DOPC and DPTE in various ratios was mixed with 2 mL SFMA solution (15% w/w) containing photoinitiator (0.5% w/w). The mixture was irradiated with ultraviolet light (365 nm, 30 mW/cm2) for 5 min to obtain L@SFMA hydrogel.

  NL@SFMA hydrogel: 30 μg of N-cadherin (His-tag) was dissolved in 1 mL lipid mixture solution containing DOPC:DPTE:DGS-nta (95:5:0.1). The lipid mixture solution was mixed with 2 mL SFMA solution (15% w/w) containing photoinitiator (0.5% w/w) and irradiated with ultraviolet light (365 nm, 30 mW/cm2) for 5 min to obtain NL@SFMA hydrogel.


### Scanning electron microscope observation
  A field emission scanning electron microscope (SEM, Hitachi S-3400N II, Tokyo, Japan) was employed to observe the internal architecture of the hydrogels. A representative freeze-dried sample from each group (SFMA and L@SFMA) was selected for imaging. After gold sputtering at a working voltage of 30 kV, images were captured. The pore diameter was calculated using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).


### Rheological properties
  The photocuring times of the hydrogels were assessed by rheologically determining the sol-gel transition, also referred to as the gel point, which is defined as the moment when the storage modulus (G’) exceeds the loss modulus (G”). Time sweeps were conducted at 20 °C using a Haake MARS rotational rheometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with P20 parallel plates and a 1.0 mm gap (CD mode), at a strain of 150%, a frequency of 1 Hz, and under UV irradiation (365 nm, 180 mW/cm²). The final moduli of the hydrogels were also determined based on the results of the rheological tests.


### Photo-induced formation of lipid bilayer within the hydrogel
  To demonstrate the rapid photo-reaction of DPTE with silk fibroin chain and the self-assembling of the small vesicles forming lipid bilayer on hydrogel, contact angles of single-distilled water and a lipid solution droplet on SFMA hydrogel with/without UV irradiation were recorded using a JY-PHA instrument (Chengde Jinhe Instrument Manufacturing Co. Ltd., Chengde, China). The contact angle for each sample was determined as the average value from three independent experiments.


### Fluorescence Recovery after Photobleaching (FRAP) measurements
  To facilitate analysis of the samples using fluorescence microscopy, lipid samples were labeled with fluorescent dye (Texas Red-DHPE or NBD-PE), or N-cadherin was labeled with Alexa Fluor™ 488. FRAP measurements were conducted using an Axioscope 5 (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) or a DMI3000B (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). The initial state was recorded prior to bleaching, after which a designated region was bleached by setting the laser intensity to 100%. Images were captured immediately after bleaching and subsequently at 5-minute intervals. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).


### Stability of NL@SFMA hydrogels
  The NL@SFMA hydrogels with Alexa Fluor™ 488 labeled N-cadherin were immersed in PBS solution refreshed every 24 h at room temperature. Then the fluidity of N-cadherin was detected by FRAP measurement at day 0, 1, 3, 5, and 7.


### Cell culture
  PC12 cells (obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, TCR 9, China) were seeded at a density of 8000 cells/mL/well on the surfaces of NL@SFMA hydrogels with Alexa Fluor™ 488 labeled N-cadherin in DMEM (11965092, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (10099-141 C, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin liquid (PS, C0224, Beyotime, China). A Laser confocal microscopy LSM 900 with Airyscan 2 (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) was used to examine the dynamic formation and disassembly of N-cadherin clusters coinciding with the reshaping of membrane protrusions immediately after PC12 cells adhered to the hydrogels. ImageJ software was used to analyze the fluorescence intensity and cell movement.

  BV2 cells (obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, SCSP-5208, China) (5 × 104 cells/well) were seeded on hydrogels and cultured for 24 h in DMEM with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% PS. Subsequently, the samples were stained with the primary antibodies iNOS (1:400, Proteintech, 18985-1-AP) and CD206 (1:400, Proteintech, 60143-1-lg), the secondary antibodies IgY Alexa Fluor 488 (1:500, Abcam, ab150113), and DAPI (1:1000, MBD0015, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and then imaged using a fluorescence microscope.

  Primary cells from the cerebral cortex of E16 SD pups (8 × 103 cells/well) were seeded on hydrogels and cultured for 3 days in Neurobasal (21103-049, Gibco, USA) with 2% B27 (17504044, Gibco, USA) and 1% PS. Subsequently, the samples were stained with the primary antibodies anti-NF200 (1:500, Proteintech, 60331-1-Ig), the secondary antibodies IgY Alexa Fluor 488 (1:500, Abcam, ab150113), and DAPI (1:1000, MBD0015, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and then imaged using a fluorescence microscope. Subsequently, 10 neurons were randomly selected from each of the three hydrogel samples. Image J software was used to count the length of each neurite of these 30 neurons, and the average value was taken as the neurite length of the neuron for statistical analysis. Moreover, the ratio of neurons forming cell junctions on each hydrogel sample was calculated for statistical analysis.

  After being cultured on hydrogels for 24h, primary cells were incubated with JC-1 dye (5 μg/mL, HY-15534, MCE) at 37°C for 30min to observe the mitochondrial membrane potential by fluorescence microscope, and the ATP contents of them were detected using ATP assay kit (S0026B, Beyotime).

  For further qPCR and Western blot analysis, 30 μM Gabapentin (HY-A0057, MCE) was used to treat primary cells to investigate the effect of THBS-1 mediated signaling pathways on neural growth, which acted as a THBS-1 inhibitor.


### Microfluidic culture assay
  The standard neuron device with 450 μm microgroove barrier (SND450, Xona) was used to detect the axon extension following the previous method54. The primary neurons from cerebral cortex were seeded on the somal compartment. After culturing for 7 days, the axons crossing the 450 μm-microtunel were cut off by suction. After 24 h of culture again, the regenerative axons were stained with NF200 and the total regenerative axonal length was calculated.


### Three-dimensional (3D) cultured cells
  For 3D cell culture, PC12 neuronal cells were mixed with the precursor solution of hydrogels with a final concentration of 1 × 106 cells mL−1. The mixture was spreaded on small round glass slides to gelate under ultraviolet light for 2 min, then placed in 24-well plates. The obtained PC12 cells encapsulated in hydrogels were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% PS. Following the above cell culture for 5 days, live/dead staining was performed on PC12 using Calcium AM/PI (Bestbio), and imaged under confocal microscopy with ZEN3.5 software. Images at various depths were captured for “z-stacked” compilation images.


### Whole-cell Patch clamp recordings in vitro
  The cultured neurons on NL@SFMA and N-SFMA hydrogels were swiftly removed and incubated in oxygenated normal ACSF (125 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 1.3 MgSO4, 26 NaHCO3, 2.5 CaCl2, and 10 D-glucose) at a rate of 2 mL/min, maintained at 32 ± 0.5 °C. Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were performed using borosilicate glass pipettes (Sutter Instruments), which typically exhibit a resistance of 4-6 MΩ. The internal solution employed for voltage-clamp recordings of spontaneous excitatory postsynaptic currents (sEPSCs) consisted of the following components (in mM): 125 CsMeSO3, 10 Na2-phosphocreatine, 5 Tetraethylammonium chloride, 1 QX-314 Cl, 5 HEPES, 0.25 EGTA, 2 Mg2ATP, and 0.5 Na3GTP; the pH was adjusted to 7.25 with CsOH.

  Neurons were visualized using an Olympus BX51WI microscope (Tokyo, Japan) equipped with infrared differential interference contrast optics. All images were captured with a CoolSNAP cf Photometrics camera (Roper Scientific, Rochester, NY) and displayed on a laboratory computer. Patch-clamp recordings were conducted employing an Axopatch-700B amplifier (Axon Instruments, Sunnyvale, CA), with signals transmitted to a computer via a Digidata-1440 interface (Axon Instruments) for data acquisition and analysis utilizing pClamp version 10.0 software (Axon Instruments). Whole-cell current recordings underwent low-pass filtering at 2 kHz and digitization at 10 kHz; membrane potential recordings were low-pass filtered at 5 kHz and digitized at 20 kHz. Neurons were maintained at a membrane potential of -70 mV and characterized by the injection of rectangular voltage pulses (5 mV, 50 ms) to evaluate whole-cell membrane capacitance, series resistance, and membrane resistance. Spontaneous excitatory postsynaptic currents (sEPSCs) were recorded in the presence of SR95531 (10 μM, Tocris Bioscience), which effectively blocks GABAA receptor-mediated spontaneous inhibitory postsynaptic currents (sIPSCs). Neurons exhibiting unstable series resistance or those with values exceeding 30 MΩ were excluded from the study. Additionally, neurons in which seal resistance varied by more than 30% from its initial value were also discarded from further analysis.


### Inhibitor treatment
  Primary cells from the cerebral cortex of E16 SD pups (8 × 103 cells/well) were seeded on NL@SFMA hydrogels, and cultured for 24h in neuron culture medium with LSKL (L303323, Aladdin) or gabapentin (HY-A0057, MCE) of different concentrations (0, 30, 100μM) after adhering. Subsequently, the samples were stained with NF200 and imaged by fluorescence microscope.


### RNA sequencing
  Primary cells cultured on different hydrogels for 24 h for the sequencing of RNA. Total RNA was extracted with Trizol (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and assessed with NanoPhotometer® spectrophotometer (IMPLEN, CA, USA), Qubit® RNA Assay Kit in Qubit® 2.0 Flurometer (Life Technologies, CA, USA), and Agilent Fragment Analyzer 5400 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). A total amount of 1.5 µg RNA per sample was used as input material for the RNA sample preparations, and the sequencing libraries were generated using NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (NEB, USA). The clustering of the index-coded samples was performed on a cBot Cluster Generation System using TruSeq PE Cluster Kit v3-cBot-HS (Illumina) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After cluster generation, the library preparations were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, and 150 bp paired-end reads were generated.

  Gene expression levels were estimated by using HTSeq v0.13.5 to count the read numbers mapped to each gene, and differential expression analysis was conducted using the DEGseq R package (1.28.0). The heatmap and volcano plot were subsequently generated to visualize the differentially expressed genes between NL@SFMA and N-SFMA groups. An adjusted P value of < 0.05 and |FoldChange | ≥2 were used as the threshold for significance.

  Subsequently, GO (Gene Ontology) analysis was employed to analyze the biological roles of core genes, while Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways analysis was employed to identify core gene signaling pathways. GO enrichment analysis was performed using clusterProfiler R package (v3.12.0), and KEGG analysis was conducted using KOBAS v3.0 software, with a significance threshold of p-value  <  0.05.

  The TRIzol method was used to extract total RNA from primary cells or BV2 cells cultured on hydrogels for 1 day for qPCR analysis. The RNA concentration was then measured using a micro-nucleic acid protein analyzer (Thermo Scientific, USA). Subsequently, the RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the Omniscript Reverse Transcription Kit (Takara, Japan). ### Western blot analysis

Different volumes of the mixture of RIPA, PMSF, and phosphatase inhibitor were added to the samples according to their concentration to adjust the overall sample concentration. Sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) sample loading buffer (Beyotime) was added to the samples, which were then boiled for 10 min. The protein samples were electrophoresed on an 8–12% SDS/PAGE gel and then electroblotted onto presoaked polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore). The PVDF membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat powdered milk (Beyotime) in Tris-buffered saline with Tween (TBST) for 2 h at room temperature and then separately incubated with the primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The PVDF membranes were washed with TBST and incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1.5 h. After sufficient washing, the ECL chemiluminescence system (Clinx Science Instruments, China) was used to detect the immunoreactive bands. The antibody information can be found in Supplementary Table 3.

### Animal experiments
  A total of 80 Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (females, aged 8–9 weeks, weighing 180 ± 10 g) were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Nantong University. The rats were housed under controlled conditions with access to food and water ad libitum, at a humidity level of 50% and a temperature of 25 °C, during a 12-h dark-light cycle.


### Traumatic brain injury (TBI) model
  Traumatic brain injury (TBI) rat model was established using a free-fall striking device (ZH-ZYQ, Zhenghua Biological Instrument Equipment Co., Ltd, China) based on a modified version of the Feeney free-fall method38. Briefly, the rats were immobilized on a brain stereotaxic apparatus, and the cranial bone was exposed after After anesthetized using 0.3% pentobarbital sodium (0.3 mL/100 g). A circular bone window with a diameter of 5.00 mm was created at the center of the right parietal bone (2 mm lateral to the sagittal suture and 3 mm posterior to the coronal suture) to expose the complete dura mater. The impact parameters were set as follows: a depth of 2 mm, a weight of 40 g, and a height of 20 cm. Following the impact, the rats were randomly divided into four groups. The rats were respectively treated with saline (Control group), in situ formed SFMA, L@SFMA, or NL@SFMA hydrogel upon irradiation for 5 min to fit with the injured sites. Subsequently, the scalp was sutured and disinfected with iodophor. All procedures were conducted on a heated pad to maintain the rats’ normal body temperature. The rats were then housed in separate cages for feeding over a period of four weeks.


### Modified neurological severity score (mNSS)
  The motor function of SD rats was assessed on days 1, 3, 7, 14, 21, and 28 following treatment using the modified Neurological Severity Score (mNSS). Basic neurocognitive functions, including motor, sensory, and reflex capabilities, were quantified on a scale from 0 to 18 points, where a higher score indicates more severe brain damage.


### Rotarod test
  Three days prior to preparing the traumatic brain injury (TBI) model, rotarod training was performed to enable the rats to learn how to navigate the rotating rods. The rats underwent two trials per day, with each trial consisting of three rounds. Each training session lasted 10 min, during which the speed of the rotating rod was uniformly increased from 5 revolutions per minute (r/min) to 40 r/min over the same duration. If a rat fell off the rod during training, it was promptly placed back on the rod and allowed to continue training until the session concluded. On days 1, 3, 7, 14, 21, and 28 post-surgery, the rotarod test was administered. The settings of the rotarod apparatus during the test were consistent with those used during training. The duration of time until the rat fell from the rod was recorded, and the average score was calculated based on three trials for each rat.


### Morris water maze (MWM) test
  The Morris Water Maze (MWM) experiment was performed to assess the learning and memory abilities of the rats in each group following TBI. A 5 + 1 training protocol was employed, consisting of a positioning cruise phase lasting 5 days (from days 23 to 27 post TBI) and a space exploration phase on day 28 post TBI. During the positioning cruise phase, rats were trained to locate an underwater platform within 90 seconds from randomly assigned starting positions in four quadrants, without interruption, for 5 consecutive days. After successfully locating the platform, the rats were allowed to remain on it for 15 seconds to facilitate memorization. In contrast, rats were also guided to the platform and allowed to stay for 15 seconds. The escape latency was measured using the SLY-WMS Morris analysis system (Beijing Shuolinyuan Technology Co., Ltd, China). On day 28 post TBI, the space exploration phase was conducted by removing the platform, and rats were subjected to a probe trial lasting 90 seconds.


### Histologic analysis
  Rats were deeply anesthetized and subsequently transcardially perfused with normal saline, followed by 4% paraformaldehyde dissolved in phosphate buffer solution. The brains were then removed and incubated in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, after which gradient sucrose dehydration was performed. The brains were processed into 12 μm-thick coronal sections using a cryostat after being embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound, followed by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining.


### Immunofluorescence staining
  The sections were washed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 10 min and then blocked with bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 2 h at room temperature. The sections were treated with the primary antibodies (iNOS: 1:400, Proteintech, 18985-1-AP; CD206: 1:400, Proteintech, 60143-1-lg; Tuj1: 1:1000, Abcam, ab78078; GFAP: 1:1000, Abcam, ab7260; THBS1: 1:500, Proteintech, 18304-1-AP; GAP43: 1:500, Invitrogen, 33-5000; SYN: 1:1000, Proteintech, 17785-1-AP) at 4 °C overnight. After three washes with PBS, the sections were incubated with 488/CY3-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500) at room temperature for 2 h. Following this, the sections were stained with DAPI (1:1000) for 5 min and washed three times with PBS. Images of the whole brain tissue sections were observed using a fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany).


### Statistical analysis
  All experimental results were analyzed using ImageJ software and GraphPad Prism 10 software. Results are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (S.E.M.). One-way ANOVA was used to compare means of multiple groups, n.s. indicates p > 0.05, ★ indicates p < 0.05, ★★ indicates p < 0.01, ★★★ indicates p < 0.001; whereas two-tailed, unpaired student’s t-test was used to compare means between each two groups.
