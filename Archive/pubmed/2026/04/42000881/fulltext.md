# Wogonin attenuates psoriasis through anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting reactive oxygen species production and the AKT/NF-κB signaling pathway


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-49477-0.


## Introduction
  Psoriasis is a common, chronic, and immune-mediated skin disorder that affects an estimated 125 million individuals globally1, with the incidence increasing each year. The disease is often prolonged and recurrent, making it difficult to achieve complete cure. Patients usually endure heavy physical and mental burden2. Psoriasis can affect the skin, nails, joints and is associated with various comorbidities, including metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disease, and psychiatric disorders3. The characteristic psoriatic lesions present as well-demarcated, erythematous plaques with silvery-white scales, resulting from hyperproliferative and poorly differentiated epidermal keratinocytes (KCs)4. Histologically, these lesions demonstrate significant immune cell infiltration comprising KCs, dendritic cells (DCs), and activated T lymphocytes. Epidermal KC injury triggers local inflammation and enhances T cell and neutrophil chemotaxis, leading to KC activation and vascular endothelial dysfunction. This process further stimulates the production of circulating inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-17 (IL-17), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interferon-γ (IFN-γ), and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)5.

  In recent years, new therapies for psoriasis, particularly biologics, have achieved good efficacy in the clinical treatment of moderate and severe psoriasis by targeting specific inflammatory pathways, such as the IL-23 / Th17 axis and TNF-α signaling pathways, although they are less effective for some patients. In addition, compared to biologics, traditional systemic therapies for psoriasis are still the mainstay of treatment, owing to economic factors, dosage regimens, and adverse effects. Systemic therapies such as glucocorticoids and methotrexate also have significant side effects6,7. Therefore, there is a need to develop new treatment strategies for psoriasis that are more effective, have fewer side effects, and are more economical.

  Chinese herbal medicine8 and extracts9,10 have demonstrated significant therapeutic effects on psoriasis, with fewer side effects. Wogonin (5,7-dihydroxy-8-methoxyflavone, WG), a natural flavonoid derived from Scutellaria Baicalensis root extracts, has shown therapeutic effects on a variety of inflammatory diseases. Experimental studies using a murine model of lipopolysaccharide-induced acute lung injury have demonstrated that WG has been proven to reduce neutrophil infiltration, pro-inflammatory cytokine production, and adhesion molecule expression by inhibiting Akt phosphorylation and RhoA activation11. Additionally, WG acts as an anti-inflammatory and chondroprotective agent in human osteoarthritic chondrocytes by inducing low levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and modulating the redox-mediated Nrf2/ARE pathway12. In a recent study, WG was shown to be beneficial in treating colitis by activating PPARγ signaling in colonic tissues, restoring normal metabolism in colonic cells, alleviating hypoxia in intestinal epithelial cells, and decreasing intestinal nitrate levels, thereby inhibiting the abnormal expansion of Enterobacteriaceae bacteria13.WG has also been reported to inhibit oxidative stress in mouse models of ulcerative colitis, and to suppress inflammatory responses by inhibiting COX-2 and iNOS14. In a mouse model of diabetes, WG demonstrated anti-oxidative stress and anti-inflammatory effects, reducing hyperglycemia, improving cardiac function, and mitigating diabetes-induced apoptosis and necrosis of cardiomyocytes in mice. This was achieved by increasing the activity of iso-antioxidant enzymes, decreasing the production of ROS, inhibiting the expression of inflammatory factors such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNFα, and inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway15. Moreover, WG has shown anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting the production of cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), and prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) in a mouse model of dermal inflammation16–18. Despite these findings, the specific therapeutic potential and underlying molecular mechanisms of WG in psoriasis pathogenesis remain to be fully elucidate.


## Materials and methods

### Identifying “psoriasis-WG” target genes
  Psoriasis-related target genes were systematically retrieved from multiple biomedical databases using the standardized search term “psoriasis”: the Genecards database (https://www.genecards.org/), “Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man”database (https://www.omim.org/), Therapeutic Target Database(https://db.idrblab.net/ttd), Pharmacogenetics and Pharmacogenomics Knowledge Base (https://www.pharmgkb.org/) and DrugBank database (https://go.drugbank.com/). Psoriasis-related target genes were identified after removing reproducible targets.

  The corresponding targets of WG were collected from three databases: Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology Database and Analysis (TCMSP; https://old.tcmsp-e.com/tcmsp.php), PharmMapper Server (http://www.lilab-ecust.cn/pharmmapper/), and Swiss Target Prediction (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch). After removing reproducible targets, the corresponding WG targets were collected. Access date: June 2024.

  Next, “psoriasis-WG” target genes were identified by intersecting psoriasis-related target genes and WG targets.


### Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network construction
  The Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING) (https://string-db.org/) database was used to construct the PPI network to explore the relationships between “psoriasis-WG” targets, setting the species as “Homo sapiens”. The constructed PPI network was then imported into Cytoscape 3.8.2, and further analyzed by CytoNCA. The core network consisted of hub genes identified based on the top 50% highest scores in six centrality metrics: degree centrality (DC), betweenness centrality (BC), closeness centrality (CC), local average connectivity-based method (LAC), eigenvector centrality (EC), and network centrality (NC).


### Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) Enrichment Analysis
  GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were carried out on the intersection gene set utilizing the “clusterProfiler” R package. Molecular functions (MF), cellular components (CC), and biological processes (BP) were included in the GO biological function study. R packages “clusterProfiler,” “org.Hs.eg.db”, “enrichplot”, and “ggplot2” were used to perform the GO and KEGG pathway enrichments. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value and q-value threshold of less than 0.05.


### Molecular Docking
  In this study, AutodockTools-1.5.6 was employed to conduct molecular docking. The Protein Data Bank (PDB) (www.rcsb.org) provides the X-ray crystal structures of key targets. Prior to docking, all protein structures were prepared by removing water molecules, adding partial charges and nonpolar hydrogen atoms, and assigning appropriate atomic parameters using AutoDockTools-1.5.6. The 2D structures of WG were obtained from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and subjected to energy minimization using Chembio3D. Next, the structures of hub targets were prepared using AutoDockTools. Both the prepared structures of the targets and the WG were saved in the PDBQT format. Molecular docking simulations were then conducted using AutoDockTools-1.5.6, to explore interactions between hub targets and WG. The docking results were analyzed and visualized using AutoDockTools 1.5.6 and PYMOL.


### Cell culture and treatment
  HaCaT cells (spontaneously transformed aneuploid immortal keratinocytes) were obtained from ATCC and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Biological Industries, Israel) and 1% antibiotics (penicillin-streptomycin). The cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. To induce a psoriatic model in vitro, HaCaT cells were stimulated with M5 (10 ng/ml), a cocktail of cytokines, containing TNF-α, IL-17 A, IL-22, IL-1α, and Oncostatin-M (Peprotech, USA) for 24 h.

  WG (A0502, Purity > 98%) was purchased from CHENGDU MUST BIO-TECHNOLOGY CO.,LTD (Chengdu, Sichuan, China). WG was initially dissolved in DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) and then added in cell culture medium. Notably, the final concentration of DMSO in the culture medium did not exceed 0.1%.


### Cell viability assay
  Cell viability was assessed using the MTT (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) assay (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). HaCaT cells were plated in 96-well plates at a density of 3000 cells per well, which were then incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO2. After 24 h of incubation, cells were treated with various concentrations of WG (0–100 µM) for 24 and 48 h. At the indicated time points, 10% volume of MTT solution (5 mg/ml) was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. After carefully removing the medium, 200 µL of DMSO was added to dissolve the formazan crystals. Absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader.


### RNA isolation and quantitative real time-PCR analysis (RT-qPCR)
  Total RNA was extracted from skin samples or HaCaT cells using TRIzol (Accurate Biotechnology, China) and then reverse-transcribed to cDNA using Evo M-MLV RT Premix (Transgen, Beijing, China). RT-PqCR was performed on a real-time PCR system (Eppendorf, Germany) using the SYBR Green qPCR SuperMix (Transgen, China). The thermal cycling conditions are as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min (1 cycle), followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 90 °C for 30 s, annealing at 60 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 20 s, with a final extension step at 72 °C for 10 min. Gene expression (2 − ΔΔCt method) was normalized to β-actin. Primers (Shanghai Shenggong Bioengineering Co., Ltd.) are listed in Table S1.


### Western blot
  Total cellular proteins were extracted using IP lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) containing 1 mM PMSF (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Protein concentration was quantified using BCA (bicinchoninic acid) Protein Assay Kit (Shanghai Epizyme Biotech, China). Equal aliquots of protein samples were loaded onto 10% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to Polyvinylidene Fluoride membranes (PALL, USA). The Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature and incubated with the primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Next, the membranes were washed and incubated with peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (Jackson, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence system. The primary antibodies used were Akt (#9272), p-Akt (Ser473) (#4060T), NF-κB p65 (10745-1-AP), p-NF-κB p65 (Ser 536) (#3033S), and GAPDH (#5714).


### Cell ROS detection
  HaCaT cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 10^5 cells per well for 24 h. Following this, the cells were stimulated with M5 and co-treated with or without WG for 24 h. Then, the cells were harvested and incubated with DCFH-DA (10 µM) (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 30 min in the dark. After washed twice using phosphate buffered saline (PBS), the cells were resuspended with 200ul PBS for flow cytometry analysis (AECA NovoCyte from Agilent) at 490 nm excitation and 525 nm emission. Untreated cells served as the control.

  After the treatment of M5 and WG, DHE (10 µM) (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was added into the cells in the plates for 30 min in the dark. Then the cells were washed three times and shot by fluorescence microscopy (Nikon Eclipse) at wavelengths of 300 nm (excitation) and 610 nm (emission).


### Measurement of the Mitochondrial Membrane Potential (Δψm) by JC-1 Probe
  HaCaT cells were seeded into a 96-well plate and incubated with M5 or WG for 24 h. Next, the cells were treated with 100 µL JC-1 (5 mg/ml) at 37 °C for 60 min in the dark, which was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). Then the cells were washed three times with PBS to remove excess dye and the fluorescence value was detected by a fluorescence spectrometer (FlexStation 3, CA, USA) at 488 nm excitation and 525/590 nm emission.


### Establishment of Imiquimod (IMQ) -Induced Psoriasis Mouse Models
  Six-week-old female BALB/c mice (18–22 g) were obtained from Gempharmatech Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China) and kept in a specialized pathogen-free environment. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the policies and procedures established by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Xi’an Jiaotong University School of Medicine (Approval No. XJTUAE2024-1815). The mice were kept for a week before their backs were shaved. Mice were randomly allocated into three groups (n = 5/group): control group, IMQ model group (IMQ), and WG treatment group (IMQ + WG). From days 0 to 6, the IMQ and IMQ + WG groups were applied 62.5 mg of IMQ cream (Mingxin Pharmaceutical, Chengdu, China) daily on the shaved back, and the mice in the IMQ + WG group received an intragastric administration of 25 mg/kg/day WG additionally. All mice were sacrificed on day 7, and skin samples were collected. Mice were anesthetized by inhalation of 3% isoflurane (MedChemExpress, USA) and euthanized by cervical dislocation.


### Enzyme Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)
  Skin tissue homogenates were prepared, normalized to a concentration of 2000 ng/µL, and then were tested using commercial ELISA kits according to the manufacturers’ protocols. Mouse interleukin (IL)-1β, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, IL6, and IL-17 A kits (Jiangsu Jingmei Biotechbology Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China) were used for evaluation of protein expression levels of IL-1β, TNF-α, IL6, and IL-17 A.


### Hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) Staining and Immunohistochemical (IHC) Analysis
  Fresh skin tissues were fixed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 24 h and subsequently embedded in paraffin. Next, the paraffin blocks were cut into slices of 5 μm thickness. For histological analysis, sections were stained with H&E following standard protocols. Immunohistochemical staining was performed using anti-Ki-67 primary antibody (1:200 dilution; Servicebio, GB111499, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. A Hamamatsu digital pathology system was used to scan stained sections. Epidermal thickness was measured from H&E-stained sections by analyzing six randomly selected fields per sample using NDP.view2 software. Ki-67 + cells were quantified in 5 random fields/section using ImageJ software with standardized thresholds.


### Statistical analysis
  All quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.3.0 software. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to analyze the statistical significance among multiple groups, and unpaired Student’s t-test was used to compare two groups. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001.


## Results

### Identifying gene targets for WG in psoriasis
  A total of 4644 targets of psoriasis were obtained by filtering out reproducible genes from five databases. Based on the corresponding databases, 211 WG targets were identified by using similar filtration steps. By intersecting these datasets, 117 genes were identified as “psoriasis-WG” target genes (Fig. 1a). These intersecting genes are potential therapeutic targets for WG in psoriasis.

(b) Construction of protein-protein interaction (PPI) network. (c) Establishment of core network by hub targets. (d) GO enrichment analysis and (e) KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of “psoriasis-wogonin” targets. For KEGG uses in this study, the Kanehisa laboratory have happily provided permission60–62.


### PPI network analysis of “psoriasis-WG” target genes and identification of hub genes
  The “psoriasis-WG” targets were integrated into the STRING database to perform PPI network analysis and then imported into Cytoscape for further analysis (Fig. 1b). Next, the hub nodes were obtained by screening the top 50% of DC, BC, CC, EC, NC, and LAC one by one using the CytoNCA plugin. Nine target genes were identified as hub genes and used to construct the core network: SRC proto-oncogene, non-receptor tyrosine kinase (SRC), heat shock protein 90 alpha family class B member 1 (HSP90AB1), Jun proto-oncogene (JUN), heat shock protein 90 alpha family class A member 1 (HSP90AA1), tumor protein p53 (TP53), AKT serine/threonine kinase 1 (AKT1), RELA proto-oncogene (RELA), mitogen-activated protein kinase 8 (MAPK8), and estrogen receptor 1 (ESR1) (Fig. 1c). These hub genes represent central nodes within the PPI network and are crucial for understanding the molecular interactions influenced by WG during psoriasis treatment.


### GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis
  GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses of “psoriasis-WG” were performed to elucidate therapeutic mechanisms. The screening criteria were a p-value < 0.05, and a q-value < 0.05.

  GO enrichment analysis comprised of three parts: biological process (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular function (MF). The top 5 terms of each category are shown in Fig. 1d and Table S2. The BP category of the GO analysis results showed that the intersection targets were markedly associated with response to oxidative stress, cellular response to chemical stress, and cellular response to oxidative stress. In terms of CC category, the targets showed a strong relationship with vesicle lumen, secretory granule lumen, and cytoplasmic vesicle lumen. In the MF category, the target genes were significantly associated with endopeptidase activity, ubiquitin-like protein ligase binding, and serine hydrolase activity.

  The KEGG enrichment analysis results showed that the potential genes of wogonin against psoriasis were predominantly enriched in “PI3K-Akt signaling pathway”, “IL-17 signaling pathway”, and “TNF signaling pathway” et al. (Fig. 1e and Table S3).


### Verification of the interaction between WG and Hub targets
  Nine hub genes identified from the PPI network (Fig. 1c) were subjected to molecular docking analysis with WG using AutoDock Tools 1.5.6. Binding affinity energy below 0 kcal/mol indicates spontaneous binding. All target proteins demonstrated strong binding potential with WG, exhibiting docking scores < -5.0 kcal/mol (Table 1). The predicted binding conformations were visualized and analyzed using PyMOL1.8.x software. The docking results are shown in Fig. 2. These findings support the potential mechanisms through which wogonin exerts its therapeutic effects by binding to these key proteins.

### WG suppressed the proliferation of keratinocytes
  The chemical structure of WG is depicted in Fig. 3a (PubChem CID: 44584733). To investigate the effects of wogonin on keratinocytes proliferation, HaCaT cells were treated with various concentrations of WG. There was no significant on the viability of HaCaT cells when the concentration of WG was below 10 µM at 24 h and below 5 µM at 48 h (Fig. 3b). Wogonin had an effective inhibitory effect on HaCaT cells proliferation in a dose-dependent manner. The concentrations of 5 µM, 10 µM and 20 µM were employed in following experiments. These concentrations were determined because they did not significantly reduce cell viability over 48 h (survival rate > 70%).

### WG inhibited inflammation in M5-treated HaCaT cells
  According to previous studies demonstrating WG’s anti-inflammatory effects19,20, we tested whether WG could inhibit inflammatory response in M5-induced HaCaT cells. As expected, mRNA expression levels of IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17 A, TNF-α and NLRP3 were significantly upregulated after induced with M5 for 24 h21,22, while WG could downregulate the mRNA expression levels of these pro-inflammatory cytokines (Fig. 3c-h). WG effectively suppressed M5-induced inflammatory cytokine secretion while maintaining optimal cell status, indicating that the observed anti-inflammatory effects were not due to non-specific cellular damage. The results suggested that WG may relieve psoriasis via decreasing the expression of inflammatory factors.


### WG reduced ROS and prevented mitochondrial dysfunction in M5-treated HaCaT cells
  As mentioned before, WG has demonstrated the ability to modulate ROS production in various diseases. In this study, we assessed ROS levels and mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP). In mitochondrial diseases, a reduction in MMP is typically accompanied by an increase in ROS production23. MMP was decreased in M5-induced HaCaT cells compared to control group while WG could reverse the decrease (Fig. 4a). To further investigate ROS levels, we utilized DCFH-DA to measure overall ROS levels in HaCaT cells. The whole ROS level was upregulated in M5-treated group and WG treatment suppressed the increase (Fig. 4b and d). We also utilized DHE staining to detect ROS level and the results were consistent with DCFH-DA, confirming the reduction of ROS levels with WG treatment (Fig. 4c and e). These results were consistent with GO enrichment analysis, suggesting that WG’s therapeutic effects may be related to modulation of ROS levels in psoriasis-related cellular models.

### Effects of WG on psoriasis-like lesions in IMQ-induced mice
  WG was orally administrated at dose of 25 mg/kg/day to IMQ-induced mice to confirm the therapeutic effects of WG in vivo. Compared to the control group, IMQ-induced mice exhibited evident symptoms of psoriasis-like lesions, including erythema, scales, and infiltration, while WG treatment could remarkably improve these symptoms (Fig. 6a). PASI scores and were also decreased in WG group compared to IMQ group (Fig. 6b-e). H&E staining revealed that WG treatment inhibited epidermis thickness, hyperplasia, and parakeratosis in IMQ-induced mice (Fig. 7a, c). The expression level of Ki67 was detected by IHC staining and reduced by WG treatment on IMQ-induced lesions (Fig. 7b, d). Throughout the study, the weights of mice were recorded every day. No extra weight loss was observed after WG administration compared to IMQ group (Fig. 7e), indicating the safety of WG. Moreover, WG treatment also inhibited the splenomegaly induced by IMQ and significantly decreased spleen weight index in IMQ-induced mice (Fig. 7f, g), suggesting a potential role of WG in suppressing systemic inflammation associated with psoriasis.

## Discussion
  Psoriasis, characterized as a chronic, inflammatory and autoimmune skin disease, is considered as a “chronic, non-communicable, painful, disfiguring and disabling disease” by WHO in 201426. Psoriasis imposes a substantial physical, psychological, and socioeconomic impact on patients. While traditional therapies are effective, psoriasis remains challenging to cure and often recurs, highlighting the need for safe and long-term effective treatments. A variety of Chinese herbal medicines and corresponding effective extracts have shown therapeutic effects for psoriasis27,28, with fewer side effects compared to conventional drugs. For instance, recent studies have shown that Tetrastigma hemsleyanum polysaccharides alleviated psoriasis by modulating the JAK/STAT3 signaling pathway29. WG is a flavonoid isolated from Scutellaria baicalensis, which has gaenered attention in recent scientific studies due to its potent anti-inflammatory effects30–35. This study aimed to explore the therapeutic effects and underlying mechanisms of WG in treating psoriasis.

  Network pharmacology and molecular docking have emerged as novel methods to perform pharmacology workings of Chinese medicine studies36. Utilizing network pharmacology, we identified that the targets of WG in treating psoriasis were enriched in pathways such as the “PI3K-Akt signaling pathway”, “IL-17 signaling pathway”, “TNF signaling pathway”, and showed strong relationship to oxidative stress. These findings indicated the possible mechanisms through which WG exerted anti-psoriatic effects. Both in vivo and in vitro experiments indicated that WG could significantly inhibit inflammation in psoriasis and reduce ROS production. As a natural product, WG is a promising therapeutic agent, offering enhanced efficacy, cost-effectiveness, and lower toxicity compared to traditional synthetic agents.

  Multiple inflammatory factors contributed to the pathogenesis of psoriasis. Among the inflammatory factors, the IL-23/Th17 axis is the most important driver of psoriasis37. IL-23 is an important proinflammatory cytokine, mainly produced by DCs and activated macrophages38. DCs release IL-6 and transforming growth factor β (TGF-β), which promotes naïve T cells differencing into Th17 cells and then sustained by IL-23, leading to production of several proinflammatory mediators including IL-17, IL-22, TNF-α 39–41. These pro-inflammatory factors help maintain psoriasis inflammation environment and promote KCs proliferation. Moreover, several cytokines (IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-18, IL-33, and IL-36) of IL-1 family are also involved in the pathogenesis of psoriasis42. Strategies aimed at inhibiting or blocking the production of these inflammatory factors are crucial in psoriasis treatment. Our results demonstrated that WG could suppress the expression levels of IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17 A and TNF-α in vitro (Fig. 3c-h), highlighting its potent anti-inflammatory effects. This suggested that WG may offer therapeutic potential by targeting key inflammatory pathways involved in psoriasis pathophysiology. Notably, the lack of a dramatic dose-response in certain inflammatory factors may be related to a potential saturation effect at these concentrations.

  Redox imbalance is a crucial factor in the onset and progression of psoriasis. Skin lesions of psoriasis patients exhibit abnormal production and accumulation of ROS, resulting in changing several cell signaling pathways 43. Elevated levels of TNF-α and Munro microabscesses formed by neutrophils infiltrate contribute to overproduction of ROS 44,45. Studies have demonstrated that WG could regulate oxidative stress in various conditions. For instance, Lu et al. found that WG treatment reduced ROS level and promoted heme oxygenase-1in vascular smoot muscle cells, mitigating vascular calcification 46. In osteoarthritis models, WG protects chondrocytes through inhibiting oxidative stress, inflammation and matrix degradation via activating the Nrf2 pathway 47. In the present study, GO enrichment analysis showed markedly association with oxidative stress, suggesting its potential in alleviating psoriasis by targeting oxidative stress. Consistent with these findings, we observed a marked increase in ROS level in M5-induced HaCaT cells, which was effectively attenuated by WG treatment (Fig. 4b-e), supporting that WG exerted anti-inflammatory effects in treating psoriasis possibly related to the decrease of ROS production. The PI3K/AKT and NF-κB signaling pathways play important roles in inflammatory response and oxidative stress 44,48. Activation of PI3K/AKT pathway can induce proinflammatory factors production, aggravating psoriatic lesions 49. Previous research has identified WG as a potent modulator of both AKT and NF-κB signaling pathways50–52. In the present study, our data that M5 treatment upregulated the phosphorylation of AKT and p65 in HaCaT cells while WG could inhibit the upregulation (Fig. 5a, b). The causal link between ROS and AKT/NF-κB signaling pathways warrants further consolidation via pathway-specific rescue experiments, these findings offer a robust mechanistic basis for the therapeutic potential of WG.

  To further explore the potential key targets of WG in treating psoriasis, we conducted molecular docking to investigate the binding affinities between these targets and WG. The docking results showed strong binding activities between WG and these key proteins (Fig. 2; Table 1). Subsequently, we evaluated the mRNA expression levels of these targets in M5-induced HaCaT cells. Among the identified targets, the mRNA expression of MAPK8, RELA, HSP90AA1, HSP90AB1, and JUN could be downregulated by WG in HaCaT cells under M5 stimulation (Fig. 5c-k), suggesting that MAPK8, RELA, HSP90AA1, HSP90AB1, and JUN may serve as potential therapeutic targets of WG in psoriasis. Conversely, no significant transcriptional alterations were observed for AKT1, ESR1, SRC and TP53 following WG administration. Notably, our Western Blot analysis (Fig. 5a, b) revealed that WG markedly suppressed the phosphorylation of Akt. This discrepancy suggests that for certain core targets like Akt, WG may exert its pharmacological effects via modulating post-translational modifications (PTMs) rather than altering transcriptional abundance. Consequently, further validation at the protein level and of specific activation states is warranted to fully elucidate these regulatory mechanisms. MAPK8, also known as c-JUN N-terminal kinase (JNK), belongs to the MAPK family and plays crucial roles in regulating inflammatory responses, cell differentiation, cell proliferation, and cell death. Inhibition of the MAPK pathway is considered a potential therapeutic strategy for psoriasis 53. RELA is a subunit of NF-κB that is crucial in the pathogenesis of psoriasis by regulating inflammatory cytokine production 24. HSP90AA1, one of the four isoforms of Hsp90, is an ATPase-dependent molecular chaperone and involved in the maturation, activation and stabilization of various transcription factors 54. HSP90AA1 has been found to be downregulated in IMQ-induced mice lesions than control 55 while it was upregulated in M5-induced HaCaT cells, which need further researches in psoriasis. HSP90AB1 is crucial for IL-17 A-mediated signaling pathways, which are pivotal in psoriasis pathogenesis 56. JUN, also known as c-JUN, exhibits obviously upregulated expression in human psoriatic lesions compared to normal controls, contributing the development of psoriasis through promoting inflammatory cells infiltration 57,58. Overall, MAPK8, RELA, HSP90AA1, HSP90AB1, and JUN may emerge as promising targets of WG in treating psoriasis.

  To further investigate the therapeutic effects of WG in vivo, we employed IMQ-induced mouse models. Our results showed that oral WG significantly alleviated psoriasis–like skin lesions in mouse models (Fig. 6). Importantly, WG led to a reduction in the expression of inflammatory cytokines both at mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 8). Additionally, WG treatment downregulated Ki67 expression level in skin tissues of the mouse models (Fig. 7b, d), indicating that WG effectively suppressed KCs proliferation in psoriatic lesions 59. Moreover, our data showed that WG treatment induced no significant changes in the body weight of the treated mice, indicating the safety of WG (Fig. 7e).

  Overall, our study focuses on oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling as key mechanisms underlying the effects of WG in psoriasis. Specifically, we identified ROS modulation and AKT/NF-κB signaling as key processes and validated these findings through both in vitro (M5-induced HaCaT cells) and in vivo (IMQ-induced mouse model) experiments. In addition, we examined both transcriptional changes of hub genes and phosphorylation levels of signaling proteins, providing multi-level evidence linking bioinformatic prediction with functional outcomes.


## Conclusion
  In conclusion, our study demonstrated that WG treatment exerted anti-inflammatory effects in both psoriasis cell and mouse models, suggesting its therapeutic effects on psoriasis. WG effectively inhibited inflammatory cytokine production, likely through the suppression of cellular oxidative stress and the AKT and NF-κB pathways. These findings indicated that WG maybe serve as a novel therapeutic agent for psoriasis. However, several issues and limitations remain in this research: (1) although we have delineated the potential signaling pathways and targets of WG, further researches are necessary to identify the specific therapeutic mechanisms underlying its actions in psoriasis; (2) the therapeutic efficacy and safety of WG need to be further evaluated in human clinical trials to validate its potential as a clinical treatment for psoriasis. Addressing these aspects will be crucial for advancing WG from experimental studies to clinical application, potentially offering new avenues for the treatment of psoriasis.
