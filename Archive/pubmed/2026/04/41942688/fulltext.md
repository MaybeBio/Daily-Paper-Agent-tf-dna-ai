# Peiminine attenuates inflammatory bowel disease by suppressing TLR4 mediated NFƙB pathway: an integrated network pharmacology and experimental validation


## Abstract
  Ulcerative colitis and Crohn’s disease are two of the complex and recurring inflammatory conditions that comprise inflammatory bowel disease. There are currently relatively few therapeutic alternatives available for its treatment, and those that are available have serious side effects. It is also in a very progressive and prevalent phase. Therefore, it is necessary to find safer and more effective therapeutics for its treatment. Targeting different molecular pathways, peiminine has antioxidant and anti-inflammatory qualities. Integrated network pharmacology and molecular docking depicted various targets, amongst them NFƙB and TNF-α were the targets where peiminine elicited maximum binding affinity. Validating the in silico data, further in in vivo study, mice were randomly allocated into six groups: Normal control (NC), Disease control (DC-TNBS, 2,4,6-trinitrobenzene sulfonic acid), STD (TNBS + sulfasalazine 100 mg/kg, p.o.), PEI1, PEI2 & PEI3 (TNBS + Peiminine 1, 2.5, & 5 mg/kg, p.o.), respectively. A sharp fall in disease activity index (DAI) and changes in histopathological examination revealed the colonoprotective effect of peiminine. It also depicted anti-oxidant activity through reducing the levels of myeloperoxidase (MPO) and nitric oxide (NO) and elevating superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase (CAT). It downregulated various pro-inflammatory mediators such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) in in vitro and in vivo studies, depicting its anti-inflammatory activity. Peiminine has also been shown to have strong anti-inflammatory properties by downregulating the gene expression of TLR4 (1.96-fold), NFƙB (1.70-fold), iNOS (1.27-fold), COX-2 (2.40-fold), and IL-1β (0.80-fold). Meanwhile, it increases the expression of anti-inflammatory mediator IL-10 (2.55-fold). Collectively, peiminine likely decreased colon damage and TNBS-induced colitis through the TLR4-mediated NFƙB pathway.


## Introduction
  Inflammation of the mucosal lining of the gastrointestinal system is a characteristic of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). This umbrella term includes two main forms: Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC)1. The hallmark of UC is inflammation, which starts in the rectum and can spread to other parts of the proximal colon. There are multiple factors responsible for the cause and progression of UC. However, the etiology of UC is still unknown. According to the epidemiological studies of IBD, it is anticipated that India will have one of the greatest populations of IBD patients globally2. Currently, there is no “gold standard” treatment for IBD. UC is typically treated with 5-aminosalicylic acid (5-ASA), immunosuppressants, TNF-α inhibitors, steroids, and targeted molecular drugs, the use of which varies based on the extent and degree of the disease3. These medications reduce inflammation, but they come with several unfavourable side effects. Consequently, when UC worsens, people with the illness often have a lower quality of life4.

  Medicinal herbs have been used for centuries to treat a wide range of diseases5. Phytochemicals with anti-oxidant, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective properties can be found in botanical sources6. Peiminine is an alkaloid compound that naturally occurs in the plant Fritillaria bulbs (family: Liliaceae) from a traditional Chinese medicine7. In China, Japan, Turkey, and other Asian nations, it is frequently utilised due to its strong anti-inflammatory effect, and other pharmacological actions like neuroinflammation, atopic dermatitis, ulcer healing, osteoclastogenesis, pulmonary diseases, and mastitis8. Notably, peiminine is reportedly having anti-inflammatory properties via acting on various targets, including NFƙB, MAPK & PI3K/Akt and having ulcer healing properties, suggesting its application in GIT disorders.

  The implementation of computational pharmacology has become increasingly popular in determining a bioactive’s ability to alleviate a disease9. Network pharmacology (NP) is a state-of-the-art method used to find possible therapeutic drugs and learn more about the therapeutic mechanisms behind complex illnesses10. It has been widely used for newer drug discovery due to its ability to identify relevant genes and target several sites in signalling pathways. NP is essential for the development of herbal medicines as therapeutic agents11. To validate the computational work, the identified hub genes are evaluated in an in-vivo animal model.

  The researchers have suggested, nuclear factor kappa B (NFƙB) is a key target responsible for the development and progression of UC. Inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) are two transcriptional enzymes that researchers have discovered12 are activated by it, potentially leading to colon inflammation13. Thus, targeting NFƙB would be favourable in battling against inflammatory complications. Peiminine attenuated LPS-induced acute lung injury in mice by acting through NF-κB pathway. It also showed anti-inflammatory effects in LPS-induced mastitis and lung injury models, eliciting significant suppression of NF-κB and associated signaling pathways in mice. Peiminine has been shown in research to have anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties in the treatment of acne and myocardial injury14,15. Researchers have studied the effect of peiminine on IBD through various models. It attenuated colitis by enhancing the function of the intestinal epithelial barrier through Nrf2/HO1 signalling pathway. Peiminine elicited anti-inflammatory activity in acetic-acid induced colitis model by targeting various proinflammatory mediators16,17.

  However, we hypothesized that peiminine could functionally suppress the TLR4–NF-κB inflammatory signaling axis and could alleviate IBD as predicted from the computational network pharmacology approach. The current study is the first study in our knowledge to showcase the targeted relationship of peiminine and IBD through integrated network pharmacology, molecular docking, in vitro and in vivo TNBS-induced colitis model via targeting TLR4-mediated NFƙB pathway. In line with this, various methods were utilised to investigate the potential of peiminine in combating IBD.


## Materials and methods

### Computational pharmacology

#### Identification of potential targets
  The target fishing for peiminine, as an identified bioactive phytoconstituent can be done by exploring various databases. The simplified molecular-input line-entry system (SMILES) of peiminine was acquired from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/)18. The canonical SMILES or 2D structure of the query molecule was imported into the databases. Peiminine’s targets had been retrieved using Super-PRED (https://prediction.charite.de/subpages/target_prediction.php) and the Swiss Target Prediction Database (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/). Further downstream operations were carried out using the provided targets.


#### UC and CD-related target prediction
  Several databases, such as DisGeNET (https://disgenet.com/), DrugBank (https://go.drugbank.com/), and GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/), were used to identify the targets implicated in the pathophysiology of IBD19. Every target was validated using the UniProtKB database (https://www.uniprot.org/). To obtain all of the IBD targets, the combined UC and CD targets were utilized.


#### Protein–protein interaction network
  STRING version 12.0 (https://string-db.org/) was used to process the overlapping data on peiminine and UC target genes in order to obtain the data required for the KEGG pathway, gene ontology, and protein–protein interaction (PPI) studies. To obtain a more visible network diagram20, the PPI values that were obtained from STRING 12.0 were then imported into Cytoscape 3.10.3. The Cytoscape Network Analyzer set up was used to analyze this network. The top ten peiminine intended genes were then processed using the CytoHubba plugin and MNC (Maximum Neighbourhood Component).


#### Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis
  The protein–protein interaction data obtained from STRING 12.0 were further analysed through functional enrichment using DAVID (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) and INPUT http://cbcb.cdutcm.edu.cn/INPUT/Z). The three categories of Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment were molecular function (MF), cellular component (CC), and biological process (BP). The enrichment analysis encompasses a total of 72 expected targets. Using the Bioinformatics web platform (http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/), the top 10 phrases from BP, CC, and MF as well as the top 20 KEGG pathways, were chosen for enrichment analysis.


#### Molecular docking
  Molecular docking is a widespread computational technique that predicts a ligand’s capacity to bind with a particular protein at a specific location. The hub genes discovered by network analysis and putative proteins implicated in the pathophysiology of IBD were subjected to molecular docking. Peiminine’s three-dimensional structure was obtained in SDF format from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). NFƙB, AKT, and EGFR are among the important targets whose crystal structures were obtained in PDB format from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) database (https://www.rcsb.org/). To determine peiminine’s binding affinity for the target, docking was done via AutoDock Vina using the PyRx platform. A higher ligand-target binding stability is indicated by a more negative Vina score. The docked structures were visualized using BOVIA Discovery Studio Visualizer v21.0.20298 (https://discover.3ds.com/discovery-studio-visualizer-download).


### Experimental pharmacology

#### In vitro assessment of peiminine
  From NCCS Pune, the RAW 264.7 mouse monocyte-macrophage cell line was acquired. Raw cells were cultivated at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 and 95% air in DMEM (Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium) supplemented with 5% heat-inactivated foetal bovine serum, 100 g/ml streptomycin, and 100U/ml penicillin to prevent bacterial contamination. It was standard procedure to switch the medium every 2 days. After reaching about 80% confluence, the RAW 264.7 cells were passaged21.

  Cytotoxicity assessment MTT (3-[4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]-2,5 diphenyl tetrazolium bromide) was used to assess the vitality of RAW cells after peiminine treatment. RAW cells were treated in 96-well plates with varying doses of peiminine (10, 20, 40, 80, and 120 μM) and STD (sulfasalazine) as a standard treatment for 24 h. After that, 200 μg/mL of MTT solution was added. The supernatant was removed after an extra 4 h of incubation at 37 °C, and 100 μl of DMSO was used to dissolve the reduced formazan. The absorbance was determined using a microplate reader at 570 nm. Cell viability for the untreated cells was regarded as 100%. Comparing the results to the untreated group, the results are shown as a percentage of cell viability22.

  Determination of NO:The cells of RAW 264.7 cell line were grown in 96-well plate plates for 24 h following treatment with different doses of peiminine (10, 20, 40, 80, and 120 μM) as well as 5-ASA as a reference treatment and LPS (1 μg/mL)21. After extracting 100 μL of the supernatant, the mixture was mixed with 100 μL of Griess reagent, which is a combination of equal amounts of reagents A and B. Reagent A was 1% sulphanilamide in 5% H3PO4, while Reagent B was 0.1% naphthyl ethylene diamine dihydrochloride. The supernatant was shaken for 20 min at ambient temperature. The nitrite concentrations were then calculated by measuring the absorbance at 540 nm and creating a standard calibration curve. It was expressed as µM of the nitrites that the cells secreted23.

  Determination of TNF-α and IL-6 levels RAW 264.7 cells were seeded in 96-well plates, and they were either pre-treated with peiminine at 10, 20, 40, 80, and 120 μM or left untreated for 24 h before being exposed to 1 μg/mL of LPS for 6 h. TNF-α and IL-6 concentrations in the supernatant were determined with the help of an ELISA kit in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.


#### In vivo assessment of peiminine
  Experimental study design Six to eight-week-old Balb/c mice, weighing 20–30 gm, were procured from Zydus Research Centre. The cages held eight animals each, and the bedding was made of corn cobs. Polypropylene cages were utilised to house the animals. Temperature (22–250 °C), humidity (60%), and lighting schedule (12 h of light and 12 h of darkness) were all maintained within a 5% deviation. The research protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee (IAEC) (RPCP/IAEC/2023-24/R2). All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the National Institutes of Health, and ARRIVE guidelines. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The animals were split up into six groups. NC (Normal Control, Saline), DC (Disease Control, only 2,4,6-trinitrobenzene sulfonic acid (TNBS), STD (sulfasalazine 100 mg/kg/day, p.o.), PEI1 (Peiminine 1 mg/kg/day, p.o.), PEI2 (Peiminine 2.5 mg/kg/day, p.o.), and PEI3 (Peiminine 5 mg/kg/day, p.o.).

  Induction of IBD through TNBS In animals, the haptenizing agent TNBS in ethanol is found to cause immunogenic reactions in the colon wall that resemble human colitis when given intracolonically24. After 2 days of post-treatment (PEI and Sulfasalazine), the mice were fasted for a full day. The mice were lightly anaesthetized with a cocktail of ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) by intraperitoneal injection before induction. 100 μL of TNBS (2.5% TNBS in 50% ethanol) was progressively injected into the descending colon using an 18-gauge cannula that was placed 3.5–4 cm proximal to the anus. The normal control group is provided with only 50% ethanol. On 7th day post TNBS induction the mice were euthanized by inhalation of excess CO2 and colon samples were collected. Scoring parameters, colon length and weight were measured. For histological analysis, the proximal portion of the colon was preserved in a 10% formaldehyde solution. Colon homogenate was utilised for measuring various pro-inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress parameters24.

  Disease activity index (DAI) scoring Post TNBS administration, weight loss, stool consistency, and rectal hemorrhage were measured on a daily basis. The DAI was calculated by dividing the sum of the stool consistency score, stool occult blood score, and body weight loss score by 3. The DAI was determined by combining these parameters and applying the scoring scheme shown in Table 125.Table 1The scoring of the disease activity index (DAI).Body weight loss (%)Stool consistencyBleedingScore < 0NormalNegative01–516–10Loose stoolsHemoccult positive211–153 > 15DiarrheaGross bleeding4

  Histological assessment of the colon After being embedded in paraffin, the colon samples were kept in a 10% formalin solution. Colon was cut up into tiny pieces, each 5 μm thick, and stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E). To determine the extent of inflammatory infiltration, the slices were next examined under a microscope. The injury to the intestinal mucosa was scored26.

  Estimation of oxidative stress The samples of colon were homogenized with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and after centrifugation, the levels of catalase, superoxide dismutase (SOD), nitric oxide (NO), and malondialdehyde (MDA) were measured in the supernatant27. The MDA level was measured at 523 nm as per the description of Ohkawa et al.28. The Griess reagent is used to measure the NO level, according to a study by Green et al. and the results were expressed as μM/g of protein29. Using the technique outlined by Misra et al. the SOD was examined. The results were measured at 450 nm and reported as U/g of tissue30. The activity of catalase was measured spectrophotometrically by recording the changes in absorbance at 240 nm, as units/mg Protein.

  Estimation of TNF‑α and IL‑6 levels Colon tissue samples were homogenized with PBS solution and then centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. After that, the supernatant was moved to fresh tubes. TNF-α and IL-6 levels were determined using a standard protocol of the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) utilizing mice ELISA kits from Krishgen BioSystems, India. The results were expressed as pg/mL.

  Quantitative real‑time PCR Colon tissues were preserved in RNA Protector (Takara Bio, USA) and stored at − 80 °C until further processing. The NucleoSpin RNA isolation kit (Takara Bio, USA) was used to purify the total RNA after it was extracted using TRIzol reagent. A QIAxpert nanodrop spectrophotometer was used to measure the concentration and purity of RNA. Then, using the PrimeScript First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Takara Bio, USA), 2 µg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA. As directed by the manufacturer, quantitative real-time PCR was performed using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Tli RNase H Plus, Takara Bio, USA). Gene expression levels were analysed by recording cycle threshold (Ct) values, and relative expression was calculated using the 2 − △△CT method with GAPDH serving as the internal control24.


### Statistical analysis
  GraphPad Prism (version 9.0.0, CA, USA, Trial version) was used for data processing. The disease activity index and histological scoring parameters were analysed using Kruskal-Wallis with Dunn’s multiple comparison test. Whereas, all other parameters were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), Tukey’s multiple comparison test was employed. The mean ± Standard Error of Mean (SEM) was used to display the data. p < 0.05 was considered a significant level.


## Results

### Computational pharmacology

#### Target identification of peiminine
  The target fishing of peiminine was done using Super-PRED and Swiss Target Prediction databases. In total, 198 targets of Peiminine were identified. The network was constructed using Cytoscape and was further utilised for finding common targets of peiminine and IBD.


#### Retrieval of potential targets of IBD
  A keyword-based search was used to find a total of 1459 UC-related genes and 1383 CD-related genes from the DisGenet database. A total of 5140 UC-related genes and 5401 CD-related targets were retrieved from GeneCards, OMIM, and TTD databases. With a total of 95 targets from peiminine and 6974 targets to UC and CD, they shared 95 targets in common.


#### Protein–protein interactions
  For protein–protein interaction, 198 targets of peiminine were put into a string database. The PPI network of peiminine comprises of 71 nodes and 114 edges in an interaction network; PPI enrichment p-value was < 1.0e−16 (Fig. 1A). The confidence interval of 0.9% with the highest confidence was selected for the analysis of PPI. The PPI network was analysed using Network Analyzer tool in Cytoscape, and the targets for higher than 7 degrees were selected for further analysis. ## Discussion
  Colon ulcers are caused by ulcerative colitis (UC), a chronic inflammatory disease. Although the precise mechanisms of UC are still unknown31. Although there are several categories of drugs for the treatment of IBD, they have several limitations, such as serious side effects and exorbitant prices. Recent pharmacological findings, however, suggest that peiminine-like phytoconstituents are a good alternative for treating UC with fewer side effects. The alkaloid peiminine has several pharmacological properties, including anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects brought about via the downregulation of specific pro-inflammatory cytokines.

  Network pharmacology (NP) has become a useful technique in modern drug discovery and development as it shifts the focus from the traditional “one drug-one target” model to a more realistic “multi-target, multi-pathway” perspective10. In this study, the top ten hub targets of peiminine were evaluated by using the CytoHubba and Cytoscape network analysis plugins. Accordingly, the molecular docking results revealed that peiminine has a greater binding affinity for IBD targets. Overall, computational network pharmacology matches these potential targets of peiminine to the various components of IBD. KEGG pathway revealed the correlation of TLR4-NFƙB pathway, demonstrating its probable mechanism in attenuating IBD.

  Researchers have depicted the correlation of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and TLR4 as it binds to TLR4 and activates the NFƙB pathway, further releasing various pro-inflammatory mediators and more nitric oxide formation32. The anti-inflammatory effects of peiminine were first examined by exposing RAW 264.7 macrophage cells to 1 μg/mL LPS, which induced inflammatory responses. RAW 264.7 cells were not cytotoxically affected by peiminine, which also successfully reduced inflammation by inhibiting pro-inflammatory cytokines, including NO, TNF-α, and IL-6. Nitric Oxide (NO) is a well-known indicator of both acute and persistent inflammation, and iNOS controls the expression and synthesis of NO33. Consequently, mononuclear macrophages are encouraged to infiltrate the site of inflammation, and inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α, and IL-6, and pain-producing chemicals can invade34. Nitric oxide plays a vital role in practically every stage of the inflammatory process. Thus, one of the main objectives of anti-inflammatory medications is to inhibit NO. In this study, similar results were observed, and the anti-inflammatory qualities of peiminine were demonstrated by a significant reduction in NO, TNF-α, and IL-6 production in RAW 264.7 cells exposed to LPS.

  In an in vitro investigation, peiminine showcased remarkable anti-inflammatory activity. Based on this, the Trinitrobenzenesulfonic acid (TNBS)-induced colitis model was utilized to validate the activity of peiminine through in vivo studies. An increase in neutrophil infiltration and macrophage activation is a hallmark of Th1-mediated immune responses, which are triggered by a hapten molecule known as TNBS that binds to the host protein35. Numerous pro-inflammatory mediators are produced as a result of these interactions, which cause inflammation in the colon. The well-established model has characteristics similar to those of human CD, including severe transmural inflammation, diffuse necrosis, thickening of the large intestine wall, bloody diarrhea, and gradual weight loss36. Similar results were observed in the present study, indicating the successful establishment of the model. However, treatment groups had depicted a colono-protective effect.

  TNBS causes immune cells that resemble macrophages to become activated and infiltrate the intestinal mucosa, producing reactive oxygen species (ROS) and eventually NO. This results in oxidative stress and contributes to the pathogenesis of ulcerative colitis. Researchers stated that peiminine has shown antioxidant activity in IBD37 and similar results are observed in our study. It has been shown to have an antioxidant effect by lowering NO levels. SOD is an antioxidant that cells need to defend themselves against ROS and the effects of free radical chain reactions. MDA rises and SOD levels fall in the TNBS-induced colitis model, indicating a rise in ROS production and oxidative damage. Fan et al. reported that osthole has showcased antioxidant activity, reducing MDA levels and elevating SOD levels34. Similar results were observed in this study, peiminine therapy has shielded against oxidative damage and, consequently, ROS production by preventing the reduction of SOD and preventing the rise in MDA levels and nitric oxide levels.

  According to the results of a prior study, UC is well known for altering the architecture of the colon in animals with colitis38, which is characterized by a damaged mucosal barrier39 and an enhancement in neutrophil infiltration in the mucosa. The intestinal mucosa produces reactive oxygen species (ROS) as a result of these activated neutrophils40, which results in oxidative stress and contributes to the root cause of colitis. Oliveira et al. stated that Bergenine at the dose of 25 mg/kg demonstrated colonoprotective effect by reducing neutrophil infiltration and maintaining the colonic architecture41. Similar alterations in colon histology have been observed in the current investigation. where peiminine at 5 mg/kg dose have improved the colonic architecture and depicted the reduction in neutrophil infiltration, colonic mucosal damage, and red blood cell accumulation, eliciting its colonoprotective effect.

  The precise mechanism by which peiminine protects the colon was uncertain and exploring the signaling pathways implicated in ulcerative colitis has been the main focus of recent research. To detect the presence of certain bacterial antigens, the innate immune system employs a broad family of pattern recognition receptors (PRRs)42. TLR4 is a PRR that identifies LPS, a key constituent of gram-negative bacteria’s outer membrane, and triggers the release of pro-inflammatory mediators such as NO, which ultimately leads to production of ROS resulting in the expression of the inflammatory response. Intestinal epithelial cells from IBD patients and the colonic mucosa of animals with experimental colitis also exhibit higher levels of it43 Several studies have shown that inhibiting TLR4 signaling molecules results in the inhibition of pro-inflammatory cytokines and the inactivation of NFƙB14. Similarly, in our study, peiminine significantly decreased the NO production in LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 cells and also inhibited various proinflammatory cytokines like TNF-α and IL-6 in the cell supernatant.

  NFƙB acts as a central regulator of intestinal inflammation by regulating the transcription of several essential mediators, including iNOS, COX-2, and IL-1β44. In order to determine the exact mechanism by which peiminine reduces TNBS-induced colitis, our study evaluated this pathway, as it was predicted via network pharmacology and molecular docking studies. The TLR4-NFƙB signalling pathway was activated, as shown by the experimental results, which showed that the Disease Control (DC) group had considerably greater mRNA levels than the Normal Control (NC) group. When NFƙB is activated, pro-IL-1β is transcriptionally induced and then matures into IL-1β, which intensifies the inflammatory response45. Treatment with peiminine efficiently suppressed this signalling pathway and ameliorated inflammatory signals by significantly suppressing the expression of these pro-inflammatory genes. Conversely, IL-10 expression was substantially increased in the peiminine-treated group. Considering IL-10 is a crucial anti-inflammatory cytokine integrally engaged in IBD regulation, as widely reported, its elevation further substantiates the anti-inflammatory activity of peiminine46.

  The present findings align with earlier research, wherein Yang S et al. demonstrated that chicoric acid (10 mg/kg) mitigates colitis by inhibiting the TLR4/NFƙB signalling axis47. Building on this premise, the main objective of our investigation was to discover whether peiminine similarly ameliorates IBD by modulation of this route. Collectively, peiminine showcased the highest binding affinity with NFƙB and TLR4 and in vivo studies supported the prediction and proved the antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-colitic and colono-protective effect of peiminine against TNBS-induced colitis by suppressing TLR4-NFƙB signalling.

  While the current work focused on transcriptional regulation of the TLR4/NF-κB pathway, earlier studies have shown that peiminine inhibits nuclear translocation and p65 phosphorylation, which mitigates NF-κB activation at the protein level. As a result, the modifications in gene expression demonstrated in this study coincide with the known molecular mechanism of peiminine. Importantly, the current study employed an integrated experimental strategy combining network pharmacology, molecular docking, biochemical assays, histopathological analysis, and in vivo gene expression profiling. The convergence of computational target prediction with transcriptional and biochemical findings strengthens the mechanistic interpretation of the anti-inflammatory activity of peiminine in combating IBD. Further research utilizing Western blot or immunohistochemical analysis can verify that this signaling pathway is modulated at the protein level.


## Conclusion
  Network pharmacology analysis identified TLR4 and NF-κB as key hub targets of peiminine, which was substantiated by high network centrality and strong enrichment of TLR and NF-κB signaling pathways in KEGG analysis—both of which are essential to the pathophysiology of inflammatory bowel disease. Molecular docking depicted the highest binding affinity of peiminine to TLR4 and NF-κB. Experimental validation has been offered for these computational predictions. Peiminine markedly decreased nitric oxide, TNF-α, and IL-6 in LPS-stimulated inflammatory models, suggesting functional suppression of TLR4–NF-κB signaling. Peiminine increased IL-10 and decreased colonic gene expression of TLR4, NF-κB, iNOS, COX-2, and IL-1β in TNBS-induced colitis, which is in line with pathway-level suppression anticipated in silico. Taken together, network pharmacology, KEGG pathway enrichment, in vitro cytokine modulation, and in vivo transcriptional regulation created a cohesive mechanistic framework in which peiminine suppresses the TLR4-mediated NF-κB axis to produce anti-colitic effects (Fig. 10).
