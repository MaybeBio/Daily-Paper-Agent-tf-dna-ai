# A competing TMPO-AS1-let-7b-5p-kinesin superfamily RNA network predicts poor lung cancer patient survival


## Background
  The high morbidity and mortality rates of lung cancer associated with smoking underscore the need for a deeper understanding of prognosis-related kinesin family–microRNA–long non-coding RNA–competitive endogenous RNA (KIFs-miRNA-lncRNA-ceRNA) networks.


## Materials and methods
  Survival analysis was performed using Kaplan-Meier (KM) Plotter (log-rank test, p < 0.05), while differential expression was analyzed using The University of ALabama at Birmingham CANcer data portal (UALCAN), On-coDB, Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA), and The Encyclopedia of RNA Interactomes (ENCORI) databases (|log2FC|>1). Transcription factor analysis was conducted using Enrichment Analysis Resource (Enrichr), and the microRNA Network (miRNet) database was used to construct the ceRNA network. The miRWalk and RNA22 databases predicted folding energy and binding affinities between KIF genes and miRNAs. Additionally, molecular docking was performed to evaluate the binding affinities of KIF proteins with natural compounds, chemotherapeutic agents, and carcinogenic inducers.


## Results
  KIF18B, KIF20A, KIF2C, KIF4A, and KIFC1 were significantly upregulated in lung cancer, particularly in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) (p < 0.05), and strongly associated with poor survival [hazard ratio (HR) = 1.5–2.0]. Transcription factor analysis revealed eukaryotic transcription factor 1 (E2F1) as a potential key regulator. These genes showed positive correlations with long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) thymopoietin antisense transcript 1 (TMPO-AS1) (R = 0.6) and negative correlations with miRNA homosapiens microRNA family (hsa-let-7b-5p) (R = −0.4 to −0.3). Targeting this regulatory axis, especially by enhancing hsa-let-7b-5p expression, could improve patient prognosis and suppress aggressive tumor growth. Strong folding energies were observed between KIF genes and hsa-let-7b-5p (−15.2 to −18.4 kcal/mol), while docking analysis demonstrated higher binding affinities of natural compounds compared to conventional chemotherapeutic agents.


## Conclusions
  Our findings identify the KIF18B/KIF20A/KIF2C/KIF4A/KIFC1/TMPO-AS1/E2F1/hsa-let-7b-5p regulatory axis as a potential therapeutic target in LUAD, particularly among high-risk smokers. This suggests that its regulatory mechanisms could lead to new targeted therapies.


## Introduction
  Uncontrolled proliferation of lung cells leads to the development of lung cancer, resulting in tumors that impair lung function. According to data from the Global Cancer Observatory (GCO), lung cancer ranks as the most common disease, affecting approximately 2 million individuals, half of whom have a high mortality rate [1]. Risk factors include smoking, exposure to radon gas, asbestos, and other environmental pollutants [2]. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and small cell lung cancer (SCLC) are the two main types of lung cancer, further subdivided into lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC). Diagnosis methods for lung cancer include chest X-rays, computed tomography (CT) scans, positron emission tomography (PET) scans, magnetic resonance images (MRIs), and other diagnostic methods [3]. Treatment strategies include surgery, radiation therapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and monoclonal antibodies that target specific molecules in cancer cells. However, these treatments can have side effects, such as damage to healthy tissues in the chest and resistance to conventional radio- and chemotherapies [4]. Confusion in diagnosis remains a major challenge for lung cancer patients, as it often spreads to other parts of the body, making treatment more difficult. Symptoms of lung cancer include persistent cough, chest pain, shortness of breath, and coughing blood, which could be another reason for misdiagnosis [5].

  Therefore, it is crucial to determine specific biomarkers that can easily detect lung cancer at an early stage. Many biomarkers, such as epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS), B-Raf proto-oncogene (BRAF), anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), ROS proto-oncogene 1 (ROS1), and programmed death-ligand 1 immunohistochemistry (PD-L1 IHC), are mutation-based and show less specificity and sensitivity for advanced-stage diseases [6]. Recently, competitive endogenous ribonucleic acid (ceRNA) networks have gained significant attention as potential regulators of tumorigenesis and promising source of novel biomarkers. In this model, long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) act as molecular sponges that bind and sequester microRNAs (miRNAs), thereby preventing them from repressing their target messenger RNAs (mRNAs) [7]. This dynamic crosstalk among lncRNAs, miRNAs, and mRNAs can influence the expression of oncogenes and tumor suppressors, ultimately affecting tumor initiation, progression, and therapy response [8]. Consequently, exploring ceRNA-mediated regulation in LUAD could provide valuable insights into the identification of novel diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers. The kinesin family (KIF) proteins are molecular motors that play a role in numerous cellular functions, including mitosis, meiosis, and transporting macromolecules [9]. Changes in KIF expression and function can lead to cancer development and progression. KIFs are also crucial for spindle formation, chromosomal segregation, and cytokinesis during normal cell proliferation and genetic homeostasis [10]. Abnormal expression of these genes can result in abnormal spindle assembly, chromosomal aneuploidy, and mitotic retardation, ultimately leading to tumor initiation and progression [11]. KIF consists of 37 kinesin members and holds prognostic significance and a regulatory mechanism that influences the survival rate [12]. Understanding these factors can help identify novel prognostic biomarkers and improve lung cancer treatment. Targeting KIFs could disrupt abnormal processes, preventing tumor cell division and proliferation. Recent research has underscored the clinical relevance of KIF family members in LUAD: for example, KIF11, KIF15, KIF18B, KIF20A, KIF2C, KIF4A, and KIFC1 have been consistently shown to be significantly overexpressed and associated with poor overall survival in LUAD patients [13]. However, most existing studies have focused on individual KIF genes, leaving the broader regulatory mechanisms unexplored. Our study provides a novel integrative framework by identifying the Thymopoietin Antisense Transcript 1/homosapiens microRNA family/kinesin superfamily proteins (TMPO-AS1/hsa-let-7b-5p/KIF) axis as a key regulatory network driving LUAD progression. We further show that reduced hsa-let-7b-5p levels and elevated TMPO-AS1 expression are linked to adverse prognosis, particularly among smokers, highlighting the clinical relevance of this ceRNA-mediated regulation. By uncovering this axis, our findings not only improve prognostic risk stratification but also open avenues for developing targeted therapeutic strategies in LUAD. Furthermore, developing small molecule inhibitors or monoclonal antibodies against dysregulated KIFs could enhance existing therapies and improve overall survival rates in lung cancer patients.


## Materials and methods

### Survival analysis
  We explored the 37 genes of the KIF using the Kaplan-Meier (KM) plotter database (https://kmplot.com/analysis/) to determine the prognostic significance of all the members [14]. Multiple genes were selected using the Affy id/gene symbol: 204444_at (KIF11), 229258_at (KIF12), 202962_ at (KIF13B), 236641_at (KIF14), 219306_at (KIF15), 219570_at (FLJ20135/KIF16), 222144_ at (KIF17), 221258_s_at (KIF18A), 222039_at (KIF18B), 1553314_a_at (KIF19), 225878_at (KIF1B), 238477_at (KIF1C), 218755_at (KIF20A), 205235_s_at (MPP1/KIF20B), 202183_s_at (KIF22), 204709_s_at (MKLP-1/KIF23), 232069_at (KIF26A), 232514_at (KIF27), 203086_at (KIF2A), 223926_at (KIF2B), 209408_at (KIF2C), 228680_at (KIF3A), 225205_at (KIF3B), 203389_at (KIF3C), 218355_at (KIF4A), 205318_at (KIF5A), 224662_ at (KIF5B), 203130_s_at (KIF5C), 1552538_a_at (KIF6), 229405_at (KIF7), 203333_at (KIFAP3), 209680_s_at (KNSL2/KIFC1). The study analyzed genes based on overall survival (OS), first progression (FP), and post progression survival (PPS) to determine their association with survival status. The hazard ratio (HR) was used to filter out significant genes, and genes with a significant association with poor survival were included for further analysis. The multivariate analysis included variables like histology, smoking history, and gender, and only genes with an HR > 1 and significant p-values were included for further expression analysis.


### Differential expression analysis and transcription factor
  The study utilized databases such as The University of ALabama at Birmingham CANcer data portal (UALCAN) (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/) [15], OncoDB (https://oncodb.org/) [16], Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis 2 (GEPIA2) (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/#index) [17], OncoMX [18] (https://oncomx.org/#hero) and The Encyclopedia of RNA Interactomes (ENCORI) (https://rnasysu.com/encori/index.php) [19] to analyze gene expression in normal and LUAD samples. We used the TCGAnalyzeR (http://tcganalyzer.mu.edu.tr/) [20], a transcriptomic technique-based database, to analyze single-cell RNA expression. We used the UALCAN database to analyze gene expression in LUAD samples based on clinicopathological parameters. The Enrichment Analysis Resource (Enrichr) database [21] was used to identify transcription factors (TFs) associated with genes. Closely associated TF was finalized by analyzing gene correlations using ENCORI, OncoDB, and GEPIA databases; KM Plotter for survival status; and UALCAN for differential expression analysis in normal vs. tumor, LUAD stages, and smoking history.


### Competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) regulatory network analysis
  The study analyzed the impact of non-coding RNAs on mRNAs, focusing on lncRNAs associated with selected KIF genes. Differential expression analysis was performed using ENCORI and UALCAN databases, and clinico-pathological status analysis was done using UALCAN. The KM Plotter database was used to identify the effect of lncRNA expression on LUAD patient survival. The MicroRNA Network (miRNet) database (https://www.mirnet.ca/miRNet/home.xhtml) [22] was used to retrieve a network involving KIF genes, lncRNA, and miRNA, and the prognostic significance of miRNA was observed using the KM Plotter database. Differential expression of miRNA was also analyzed using the CancerMIRNome (http://bioinfo.jialab-ucr.org/CancerMIRNome/) [23], UALCAN, and ENCORI databases. Database miRWalk (http://mirwalk.umm.uni-heidelberg.de/) [24] and RNA22v2 (https://cm.jefferson.edu/rna22/Interactive/) [25] were used to predict the folding and binding energy between the KIF genes and miRNA.


### Protein and ligand data acquisition analysis and molecular docking
  Three KIF genes’ crystal structures were retrieved from Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/) with ID: KIF2C (PDB ID: 4UBF), KIF20A (PDB ID: 8BJS), KIFC1 (PDB ID: 5WDH) and the KIF18B gene’s (UniProt ID: Q86Y91) crystal structure was retrieved from AlphaFold protein structure (https://alphafold.com/) database. The chemical structures of six ligands, namely: hesperidin (PubChem CID: 10621), quercetin (Pub - Chem CID: 5280343), paclitaxel (CID: 36314), docetaxel (CID: 148124), nicotine (CID: 89594), and nitrosamine (NNK) (CID: 47289) were obtained from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Ligand structure minimization and protein structure cleaning was done using the University of California, San Francisco (UCSF) Chimera database (https://www.cgl.ucsf.edu/chimera/). Auto Dock Tools-1.5.7 (https://ccsb.scripps.edu/mgltools/downloads/; https://autodock.scripps.edu/download-autodock4/) was used to analyze the binding interactions of KIF genes with the ligands (natural compounds, chemotherapeutic agents and carcinogenic inducers). Ten docked conformations were generated for each ligand and the topmost hit with best docking score was selected and further visualized using Discovery Studio (http://163.15.166.20:9944/DS/) to generate 2D plots.


### Gene enrichment and cell cycle analysis
  The Gene ENrichment Identifier (GENI) database (https://yoavshaul-lab.shinyapps.io/gsea-geni/) [26] was used to study the role of KIF genes, TF, and lncRNA in biological, cellular, and molecular processes. The ENCORI database was used to study the correlation between cell cycle checkpoints and KIF genes and regulatory molecules.


### Statistical analysis
  Survival analyses were performed using the KM Plotter database, where hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated, and log-rank tests determined significance (p < 0.05). Genes with HR > 1 were considered high-risk. Differential expression analyses were conducted using UALCAN, GEPIA2, OncoDB, OncoMX, and ENCORI databases, applying built-in Student’s t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests where appropriate. Sample sizes varied by database (n = 59–515). Correlation analyses among KIF genes, transcription factors, lncRNAs, and miRNAs were performed using Spearman’s correlation. For molecular docking, binding affinities were ranked based on AutoDock Vina scores, and the best conformations were visualized using Discovery Studio. A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was applied throughout.


## Results

### Prognostic significance of the KIF family
  A study analyzed 37 KIF family genes based on their prognostic significance in lung cancer cases using the Kaplan-Meier Plotter database. Three survival statuses were assessed: overall survival (OS), first progression (FP), and post-progression survival (PPS). The genes with significant results were selected for further analysis, resulting in 12 genes: KIF11, KIF15, KIF18A, KIF18B, KIF1C, KIF20A, KIF20B, KIF23, KIF2C, KIF3B, KIF4A, and KIFC1 (Supplementary File — Tab. S1). A multivariate analysis showed that 11 KIF genes were significantly associated with poor survival in LUAD cases but not in lung squamous cell carcinoma cases (Supplementary File — Tab. S2). Various inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied to assess these KIF family genes for their prognostic significance. These factors included OS, LUAD, smoking history, non-smokers, and the patient’s gender. We also checked the fold change of the selected 5 genes (KIF18B, KIF20A, KIF2C, KIF4A, and KIFC1) using the OncoMX database, finding that these genes were significantly overexpressed in lung cancer, with a log fold change greater than 3, as mentioned in Supplementary File — Table S3 and S4.

  Furthermore, the selected values reveal a significant correlation between the overexpression of selected genes and poor survival in LUAD patients who smoke. Figure 1A–D shows the results of the study on KIF18B: OS (HR = 1.55, p = 7.5e-13), OS + LUAD (HR = 1.88, p = 7.3e-13), OS + LUAD + Smoker (HR = 1.47, p = 0.00021), OS + LUAD + Smoker + Males (HR = 1.73, p = 0.002), and OS + LUAD + Smoker + Females (HR = 2.07, p = 0.00033). Figure 1F–J illustrates the findings regarding KIF20A: OS (HR = 1.57, p = 1.5e–13), OS + LUAD (HR = 1.84, p = 5e-12), OS + LUAD + Smoker (HR = 1.62, p = 4.9e-06), OS + LUAD + Smoker + Males (HR = 1.89, p = 0.00038), and OS + LUAD + Smoker + Females (HR = 1.54, p = 0.031). Figure 1K–O presents the results of the study on KIF2C: OS (HR = 1.64, p = 3.7e-16), OS + LUAD (HR = 1.85, p = 3.2e–12), OS + LUAD + Smoker (HR = 1.67, p = 1.2e-06), OS + LUAD + Smoker + Males (HR = 1.71, p = 0.0024), and OS + LUAD + Smoker + Females (HR = 1.9, p = 0.0015). Figure 1P–T outlines the results on KIF4A: OS (HR = 1.6, p = 7.1e–15), OS + LUAD (HR = 1.84, p = 4.4e–12), OS + LUAD + Smoker (HR = 1.6, p = 7.2e–06), OS + LUAD + Smoker + Males (HR = 1.97, p = 0.00013), and OS + LUAD + Smoker + Females (HR = 2.15, p = 0.00019). Finally, Figure 1U–Y shows the results of the study on KIFC1: OS (HR = 1.91, p = 1.6e–13), OS + LUAD (HR = 1.91, p = 1.6e–13), OS + LUAD + Smoker (HR = 1.9, p = 1.7e–06), OS + LUAD + Smoker + Males (HR = 2.12, p = 2.5e–05), and OS + LUAD + Smoker + Females (HR = 1.78, p = 0.0042). Therefore, based on the findings, we can use KIF18B, KIF20A, KIF2C, KIF4A, and KIFC1 as prognostic markers for LUAD cases.


### Differential expression and transcription factor analysis
  Furthermore, we compared the differential gene expression of these genes in normal versus LUAD samples using multiple databases, including UALCAN, OncoDB, GEPIA2, and ENCORI, with UALCAN retrieving its information from the The Cancer Genomic Atlas Portal (TCGA) repository. It indicated that the levels of KIF18B (p = 1.6e–12), KIF20A (p ≤ 1e–12), KIF2C (p ≤ 1e-12), KIF4A (p ≤ 1e–12), and KIFC1 (p ≤ 1e–12) were significantly higher in LUAD samples compared to normal, as illustrated in Figures 2A–E. Similarly, Figure 2F–O presents box plots from OncoDB and GEPIA2, indicating significant upregulation of these genes in tumor samples. We then employed the ENCORI database to determine the fold change values of these genes in cancerous conditions compared to normal; Figure 2P–T shows a 14.76-fold change in KIF18B, 9.76 in KIF20A, 13.11 in KIF2C, 15.48 in KIF4A, and 7.46 in KIFC1 under LUAD conditions. Additionally, using The Cancer Genome Altas Analyzer (TCGAnalyzeR), we conducted a high-throughput transcriptome-based analysis to study the expression of various genes at a single-cell level based on RNA sequencing data. We observed the magnitude of gene expression change as log fold change values, which demonstrated significant upregulation of KIF18B (log FC = 3.29), KIF20A (log FC = 3.16), KIF2C (log FC = 3.24), KIF4A (log FC = 3.70), and KIFC1 (log FC = 2.33) in the cases of LUAD cells, as shown in Figures 2U–Y. Furthermore, utilizing the UALCAN database, a clinico-pathological parameter-based analysis revealed significant upregulation of these genes in advanced stages, male patients, and smokers (Supplementary File — Fig. S1A–O).

  Next, the study analyzed the key regulators of these genes by examining the transcription factor that plays a crucial role in controlling KIF gene expression. We entered all five KIF genes into the Enrichr database and utilized the Transcriptional Regulatory Relationships Unraveled by Sentence-based Text mining (TRRUST) Transcription Factors dataset to identify the associated transcription factors. As listed in Supplementary File — Tab. S5, E2F1 and specificity protein 1 (SP1) TFs exhibited significant associations with the genes. A strong link between the E2F1 transcription factor and KIF18B (R = 0.795), KIF20A (R = 0.678), KIF2C (R = 0.753), KIF4A (R = 0.713), and KIFC1 (R = 0.806) was observed from the ENCORI database (see Supplementary File — Tab. S6 and Fig. S3A–E). We further validated the correlation values using the OncoDB and GEPIA2 databases, as illustrated in Figure 3F–O. Additionally, we determined the importance of the E2F1 TF for prognosis by analyzing the survival rates, OS (HR = 1.45, CI = 1.28–1.63, p = 1.1e-09), OS + LUAD (HR = 1.53, CI = 1.29–1.81, p = 1.1e–06), and OS + LUAD + smoker (HR = 1.63, CI = 1.25–2.12, p = 0.00026), using the KM Plotter, as shown in Figure 3P, R. We further analyzed the differential expression of E2F1 in normal versus tumor conditions, cancer stages, gender, and smoking history of patients using the UALCAN database. The box plots in Figure 3S and Supplementary File Figure S1P, R demonstrate that overexpression of E2F1 is associated with LUAD, advanced stages, male patients, and smokers.


### The competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) regulatory network analysis
  The ceRNA network, a complex interplay of mRNAs and lncRNAs, regulates gene expression by controlling miRNA interactions with target mRNA pools. lncRNAs, acting as molecular scaffolds, can enhance mRNA stability through various mechanisms. We analyzed the Enrichr database to determine the top 10 lncRNAs associated with selected KIF genes (Supplementary File — Tab. S7). We found further differential lncRNA expression using the UALCAN database (Supplementary File — Tab. S8) and considered only upregulated lncRNAs. Additionally, we analyzed the correlation between the KIFs and lncRNA using the ENCORI database (Supplementary File — Tab. S9). Next, Figures 4A–F show that TMPO-AS1 is a strongly positively related lncRNA, demonstrating significant correlation with KIF18B (R = 0.674), KIF20A (R = 0.607), KIF2C (R = 0.644), KIF4A (R = 0.646), KIFC1 (R = 0.641), and E2F1 (R = 0.581). We also used the KM Plotter database to identify the impact of TMPO-AS1 expression on patient survival. We found that high TMPO-AS1 expression is significantly associated with poor prognosis in the cases of OS+LUAD (HR = 2.16) and OS + LUAD + Smokers (HR = 2.34), as shown in Figure 4G, H. We further conducted differential lncRNA expression analysis in normal vs. LUAD samples using the ENCORI and UALCAN databases, respectively, and observed a significant upregulation (p = 8.0e–26 and p = 6.3e–53, respectively) in TMPO-AS1 expression under tumor conditions, as shown in Figure 4I, J. Examining clinicopathological status based on cancer stage, gender, and smoking history revealed that the level of upregulation was higher in patients with advanced stages of cancer, and a strong link was found between male patients and smokers, as shown in Figure 4K–M.

  The study next aimed to identify shared miRNAs between KIF genes and lncRNA (TMPO-AS1) by using the miRNet database to create a network among KIF18B, KIF20A, KIF2C, KIF4A, KIFC1, TMPO-AS1, and miRNAs (Supplementary File — Fig. S1S). Two miRNAs, hsa-let-7b-5p and hsa-let-7g-5p, were found to be correlated with TMPO-AS1, KIF18B, KIF20A, KIF2C, and KIFC1, while KIF4A was not. The KM Plotter database revealed that downregulation of hsa-let-7b-5p (p = 0.021) was significantly associated with a poor LUAD survival status, whereas hsa-let-7g-5p was insignificant (p = 0.073), as shown in Figure 5A, B. Differential miRNA expression using the CancerMIRNome, UALCAN, and ENCORI databases revealed hsa-let-7b-5p downregulation in LUAD samples compared to normal, as shown in Figure 5C–E. Smokers are at a higher risk for LUAD, with downregulation of hsa-let-7b-5p observed in LUAD samples and advanced stages in male and smoker patients, as shown in Figure 5F–H. Furthermore, Figure 5I–N demonstrates a strong negative correlation between hsa-let-7b-5p and KIF genes, specifically KIF18B (R = −0.462), KIF20A (R = −0.341), KIF2C (R = −0.395), KIFC1 (R = −0.377), E2F1 (R = −0.365), and TMPO-AS1 (R = −0.277), indicating a potential feedback loop that may contribute to the progression of LUAD in smokers. To explore the regulatory effect of hsa-let-7b-5p on selected KIFs, we analyzed the binding energy and heteroduplex formation using miRWalk and RNA22v2 prediction tools, respectively. The analysis revealed strong miRNA-mRNA interactions with KIF18B (NM_001265577.2), KIF20A (NM_005733.3), KIF2C (NM_006845.4), and KIFC1 (NM_002263.4), with predicted binding energies of hsa-let-7b vs. KIF18B (−21.4 and −18.4 kcal/mol), KIF20A (−21.8 and −15.2 kcal/mol), KIF2C (−19.3 and −15.2 kcal/mol), and KIFC1 (−25.1 and −18.2 kcal/mol), as mentioned in Table 1. Moreover, the heteroduplex formation patterns indicated stable Watson-Crick base pairing, with variations in mismatches and bulges across the target sites, which are essential in determining whether miRNA binding leads to mRNA degradation or translational repression. Therefore, these findings suggest that hsa-let-7b-5p may regulate KIF transcripts post-transcriptionally, potentially leading to their degradation or translational repression via RISC-mediated silencing, which could significantly impact mitotic progression and cancer cell proliferation.


### Binding affinity analysis of KIF proteins with natural compounds, chemotherapeutic agents, and carcinogens
  To understand the molecular interactions of KIF proteins with potential therapeutic inhibitors and carcinogenic inducers, a binding affinity analysis was conducted to assess their binding strengths using molecular docking tools. Table 2 presents the binding affinities (in kcal/mol) of various molecules — hesperidin, quercetin, paclitaxel, docetaxel, nicotine, and NNK—with KIF proteins KIF2C, KIF20A, KIFC1, and KIF18B, where lower (more negative) values indicate stronger interactions. Among all molecules, Hesperidin exhibits the strongest binding across all kinesins, having the highest affinity for KIF18B (−9.1 kcal/mol), followed closely by KIFC1 (−9.0 kcal/mol), KIF20A (−8.7 kcal/mol), and KIF2C (−8.4 kcal/mol) in comparison to chemotherapeutic drugs. Quercetin also shows significant binding, with affinities ranging from −7.9 kcal/mol (KIF18B) to −6.6 kcal/mol (KIF2C), suggesting it as a potential modulator of these proteins. The chemotherapeutic agents paclitaxel and docetaxel display substantial interactions, particularly with KIF18B (−7.6 kcal/mol and −7.8 kcal/mol, respectively) and KIFC1 (−7.8 kcal/mol and −7.5 kcal/mol), reinforcing their role in disrupting microtubule associated proteins. KIF20A also binds relatively well with Paclitaxel (−7.5 kcal/mol) and Docetaxel (−8.3 kcal/mol). Additionally, the binding affinities of carcinogenic inducers Nicotine with KIF2C, KIF20A, KIFC1, and KIF18B were −4.8 kcal/mol, −4.3 kcal/mol, −4.7 kcal/mol, and −5.4 kcal/mol, respectively, while NNK exhibited affinities of −5.3 kcal/mol for KIF2C, −5.0 kcal/mol for KIFC1, −5.2 kcal/mol for KIF20A, and −5.8 kcal/mol for KIF18B. Furthermore, we visualized the 2D interactions of KIF proteins (KIF2C, KIF20A, KIFC1, and KIF18B) with Hesperidin and Paclitaxel using Discovery Studio, as shown in Figure 6A–H. The interaction revealed that Hesperidin forms a dense network of hydrogen bonds with key amino acid residues across all KIF proteins, contributing to its stable binding within the active site. Additionally, van der Waals interactions further enhance the ligand’s stability, ensuring a strong molecular association. The presence of multiple hydrogen bonds suggests a higher degree of specificity in the interaction between Hesperidin and KIF proteins, which may contribute to its superior inhibitory potential. In contrast, the lower frequency of strong hydrogen bonds in paclitaxel’s interactions indicates relatively weaker stabilization within the protein binding pockets. These findings reinforce Hesperidin’s potential as a more effective inhibitor of KIF proteins, providing a molecular basis for its superior efficiency over paclitaxel.


### Enrichment analysis and cell cycle regulators
  The study analyzed the interplay between four KIF genes and various biological, cellular, and molecular processes using the GENI database. There was a strong link between the increased levels of mRNA, TF, and lncRNA and several cell cycle processes, including deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) replication, sister chromatid segregation, nuclear division, and the transition from one cell cycle phase to the next (Supplementary File — Fig. S2). Other parts of the study examined how some KIF genes, E2F1, TMPO-AS1, and hsa-let-7b-5p, interact with cell cycle regulators, cyclins, and cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs). These complexes work together to ensure the proper functioning of the cell cycle. Cyclin D with CDK 4 and 6 is involved during the G1/early S-phase; cyclin E with CDK 2 is involved during the late G1/S-phase; cyclin A with CDK 2 participates during the late S/G2-phase; and Cyclin B with CDK 1 is involved during the late G2/M phase. We determined the correlation between CDK and cyclin molecules with KIF18B, KIF20A, KIF2C, KIFC1, E2F1, TMPO-AS1, and hsa-let-7b-5p genes using the ENCORI database. The results indicate a positive correlation between the selected KIF genes, E2F1, and TMPO-AS1 with CDK2/cyclin E and CDK1/cyclin B complexes, and a negative correlation with hsa-let-7b-5p (Supplementary File — Tab. S10). This suggests that restoring let-7b-5p expression could be a promising strategy for halting LUAD progression.


## Discussion
  Lung adenocarcinoma is a challenging cancer with a poor five-year survival rate of only 20% (American Cancer Society). The mortality rate varies depending on the stage at the time of diagnosis, treatment options, and individual patient factors. In non-smokers, early-stage detection increases the survival rate, while late-stage detection lowers it. In smokers, early-stage detection tends to have lower survival rates, typically 40–60%, and later- stage detection significantly shows lower survival rates, often 5–15% [27, 28]. Previous studies in LUAD have focused on identifying molecular markers and pathways critical to its progression. Mutations in EGFR, KRAS, and ALK are well-documented drivers of LUAD and have been targets for therapies such as tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) [29]. Immune checkpoint inhibitors [e.g., anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (anti-PD-1) and anti-programmed cell death ligand 1 (anti-PD-L1) therapies] have also been highlighted in improving survival in advanced LUAD cases [30]. The KIF family of genes, which encode proteins for various processes, has been found to be dysregulated in various types of cancer. These genes have been shown to have prognostic and diagnostic capabilities. KIF3A expression is higher in breast cancer tumor tissues than healthy adjacent tissues, and an increased KIF2A expression is associated with poorer clinical outcomes in patients with LUAD and diffuse large B cell lymphoma [31, 32]. KIF4A expression is strongly associated with poorer prognosis in patients with BC, prostate cancer, and hepatocellular carcinoma [33–35]. KIF11 has similar prognostic values in oral cancer and BC [36]. KIF14 expression is upregulated in tumor tissues of various cancers and inhibiting it has been shown to inhibit the growth of PCa and LUAD cell lines [37], suppress the proliferation of medulloblastoma and NSCLC, decrease cancer cell migration and induce apoptosis in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), and inhibit tumor metastasis in gastric cancer (GC), prostate cancer (PCa), and LUAD [37, 38]. KIF18B expression was also upregulated in LUAD tumor tissues, suggesting its utility as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker in patients with LUAD [39].

  This study aimed to identify novel prognostic biomarkers and potential therapeutic targets for LUAD smoker patients by focusing on the KIF genes and their regulatory network. The KIF genes and their regulatory network were identified as potential therapeutic targets for LUAD smokers. Five KIF genes were identified, with KIFC1 and KIF20A protein significantly overexpressed in LUAD patients compared to the normal (https://www.proteinatlas.org/). These findings highlight the importance of understanding the KIF genes in predicting survival in LUAD patients. These genes were consistently overexpressed in LUAD samples compared to normal lung tissues, with their expression levels correlating with advanced cancer stages, males, and smokers. The E2F1 transcription factor, a key regulator, showed strong positive correlations with the expression of the identified KIF genes, suggesting that E2F1 may play a crucial role in driving the overexpression of these KIFs in LUAD, potentially contributing to uncontrolled cell division and tumor growth. Additionally, a regulatory network involving KIF genes, E2F1, lncRNA, and miRNA was identified, with lncRNA, TMPO-AS1 showing upregulation in tumor samples and strong positive correlations with the KIF genes and E2F1, whereas miRNA-hsa-let-7b-5p shows downregulation in tumor samples and strong negative correlations with the KIF genes and E2F1. These findings may have important implications, as the identified KIF genes, along with their TF-E2F1 and ceRNA network (TMPO-AS1 and hsa-let-7b-5p), could serve as a panel of biomarkers for early detection in LUAD. Inhibiting TMPO-AS1 or restoring hsa-let-7b-5p expression levels could potentially downregulate the expression of the KIF genes, improving LUAD smoker survival. However, experimental validation is necessary to confirm the precise mechanism by which the KIF genes and regulatory molecules contribute to LUAD progression. This knowledge could lead to the development of targeted therapies that enhance patient outcomes and offer new avenues for treatment in LUAD. By exploring the interactions of KIF genes and their regulatory networks, researchers can uncover critical insights into how these elements influence cancer development. Such understanding is essential for designing effective therapeutic strategies tailored to individual patient needs in LUAD.

  Hesperidin, a citrus bioflavonoid, has been reported to exhibit anti-proliferative, pro-apoptotic, and anti-inflammatory properties in various cancer types, including lung cancer [40]. It has been shown to modulate key signaling pathways, including phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/AKT) and nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), thereby suppressing tumor growth and enhancing chemosensitivity [41]. Emerging studies suggest that hesperidin may downregulate oncogenic pathways and influence the expression of genes associated with tumor progression. Given its bioavailability and minimal toxicity, hesperidin represents a potential complementary approach in LUAD management, particularly in targeting dysregulated pathways such as those involving KIF genes. Further investigations are necessary to explore its role in combination therapies and its impact on KIF gene expression in LUAD.

  While our findings provide novel insights into the TMPO-AS1/hsa-let-7b-5p/KIF axis in LUAD, several limitations must be acknowledged, such as that the results are primarily derived from in silico multi-database analyses, and thus require experimental validation in cell lines, animal models, and large, independent patient cohorts to confirm the biological and clinical significance of the identified axis. Although our findings highlight potential diagnostic and therapeutic applications, their translation into clinical settings involves ethical considerations (e.g., patient data privacy, informed consent, and potential off-target effects) and logistical challenges (e.g., cost-effectiveness, regulatory approvals, and integration into existing workflows). Addressing these limitations in future studies will enhance the robustness and clinical applicability of our findings.


## Conclusions
  LUAD remains a challenging cancer with low survival rates, particularly among smokers and patients in advanced stages of the disease. This study emphasizes the significance of KIF genes as important prognostic and diagnostic biomarkers, with KIFC1, KIF18B, KIF2C, and KIF20A showing notable overexpression in LUAD tissues. The research also presents a regulatory network involving the E2F1 transcription factor, lncRNA TMPO-AS1, and miRNA hsa-let-7b-5p, suggesting their potential roles in promoting LUAD progression. These findings highlight the importance of KIF genes and their regulatory elements as potential targets for early detection and therapeutic intervention. Targeting KIF genes could include the development of small molecule inhibitors that disrupt their function, thereby inhibiting cancer cell proliferation. Additionally, RNA-based therapies, such as siRNA or antisense oligonucleotides, could be employed to reduce the expression of overexpressed KIF genes. Combining these targeted therapies with existing treatments, like chemotherapy and immunotherapy, may enhance effectiveness and improve patient outcomes. Moreover, Hesperidin has been reported to induce apoptosis, inhibit cell proliferation, and regulate oxidative stress, making it a promising complementary therapeutic agent compared to current chemotherapeutic drugs. Future research should investigate Hesperidin’s role in addressing KIF gene dysregulation and its potential synergistic effects with existing treatment strategies for LUAD.
