# Integrated network pharmacology, molecular docking, and experimental validation reveal the antifungal and anti-inflammatory mechanisms of Camellia oleifera seed extracts against oral thrush


## Abstract
  Oral thrush, primarily caused by Candida albicans, is often accompanied by inflammation and pain. Due to rising resistance and side effects of current antifungal treatments, safer and more effective alternatives are needed. This study investigates the antifungal mechanisms of Camellia oleifera seed extracts against oral thrush using network pharmacology, molecular docking, and in vitro validation. UHPLC-Q-TOF-MS identified chemical components in the extracts, selecting those meeting Lipinski’s Rule of Five. Oral thrush-related targets were retrieved from public databases, and a compound-target network was built to identify key targets. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks, GO, and KEGG analyses were conducted. Molecular docking assessed interactions between active compounds and core targets. Antifungal efficacy and cytotoxicity were evaluated in vitro. Twenty-three active compounds were identified. Tea saponin showed favorable docking scores to STAT3, a key intracellular mediator of IL-6–driven inflammatory signaling, suggesting potential modulation of the IL-6/STAT3 axis. The extracts inhibited Candida albicans growth and biofilm formation in vitro; however, cytotoxicity toward human gingival fibroblasts was observed at concentrations lower than those required for antifungal efficacy, resulting in a limited therapeutic index. Camellia oleifera seed extracts exhibit antifungal and anti-inflammatory activity against oral thrush in vitro, but their therapeutic window appears narrow under the conditions tested. These findings highlight the potential of the extracts as lead candidates for topical or formulation-optimized antifungal applications, while emphasizing the need for further optimization and in vivo safety evaluation.


## Introduction
  Candida albicans is an opportunistic fungal pathogen that is widely present in the human oral cavity, gastrointestinal tract, skin, and genital mucosa as part of the normal microbiota1. In immunocompetent individuals, it usually does not cause disease. However, under conditions of immune suppression, dysbiosis of the oral microbiota, or prolonged use of antibiotics, corticosteroids, or other immunosuppressive agents, Candida albicans can proliferate excessively and lead to infections. Among these infections, Oral thrush is one of the most common mucosal infections.

  Oral thrush, also termed acute pseudomembranous candidiasis in Western medicine and traditionally referred to as “Oral thrush” in Chinese medicine, is a common opportunistic fungal infection of the oral mucosa2. It predominantly affects vulnerable populations, including infants, the elderly, immunocompromised individuals, and denture users. Clinically, it is characterized by the formation of white or grayish pseudomembranous plaques on the tongue dorsum, buccal mucosa, and hard palate, which, upon removal, reveal an erythematous and erosive mucosal surface3. Patients frequently present with symptoms such as burning pain, foreign-body sensation, and feeding difficulties. Beyond its localized manifestations, oral thrush can significantly impair patients’ quality of life and may act as a precursor to invasive systemic candidiasis in high-risk groups. Host immune responses at the oral mucosa, particularly cytokine-mediated epithelial and Th17-driven immunity, play a decisive role in controlling Candida albicans colonization and inflammation4,5. Therefore, effective suppression of its principal etiological agent, Candida albicans, is essential for successful therapeutic intervention.

  Current treatments for Oral thrush mainly involve antifungal agents such as nystatin and fluconazole6. However, the increasing prevalence of antifungal resistance and the limitations posed by drug side effects highlight the need for safer, more eco-friendly therapies with low risk of resistance. Recent studies have highlighted the growing issue of Candida albicans resistance, particularly in immunocompromised and long-term antifungal drug users. As resistance increases, the effectiveness of traditional antifungal treatments decreases, making the search for new antifungal strategies even more urgent7. In particular, Candida albicans forms biofilms on various surfaces, such as denture acrylic (PMMA), making its attachment and growth more persistent. Biofilm formation significantly enhances Candida resistance to antifungal agents, a phenomenon that is particularly challenging on PMMA surfaces8. Given that PMMA is commonly used for dentures, biofilm formation by Candida albicans on its surface not only exacerbates infection persistence but also complicates treatment. Therefore, utilizing PMMA as a model to study biofilm inhibition is of clinical significance in the development of new antifungal therapies8. Camellia oleifera is an economically important plant widely cultivated in southern China. Its seed cake, a byproduct of oil production is rich in bioactive natural compounds, including saponins and polyphenols, which have been shown to exhibit anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antimicrobial properties9. Although research into its antifungal activity is still in its early stages, preliminary evidence suggests that extracts of Camellia oleifera may have inhibitory effects against certain fungi.

  This study aims to investigate the inhibitory activity of Camellia oleifera seed extract against Candida albicans, evaluate its potential application in the treatment of Oral thrush, and provide a theoretical and practical basis for the development of novel natural antifungal agents.


## Materials and methods

### Preparation of Camellia oleifera seed extract
  The Camellia oleifera seeds (used in this study were primarily obtained from a Camellia oleifera plantation in Longyan City, Fujian Province, China. The seeds were collected in October 2024 during the local harvest season by members of the Fujian Academy of Forestry Sciences, the voucher specimen is deposited in the Herbarium of the Fujian Academy of Forestry Sciences, the specimen number is: 2024001CA, species identification was performed and confirmed as Camellia oleifera by Dr. Fuli Liu. After collection, the seeds were manually cleaned, naturally air-dried, and stored for later use. Species identification was conducted and confirmed by the same institution, verifying them as Camellia oleifera seeds. All samples were used exclusively for scientific research and not for commercial purposes) were ground into a fine powder using an ultrafine grinder. The powdered material was then mixed with water at a ratio of 1 g to 20 mL and sonicated at 40% output power for 5 min (5-second pulse intervals)10. The mixture was centrifuged at 4,000 rpm for 10 min at room temperature, and the supernatant was collected and passed through a 0.2 μm syringe filter. The resulting filtrate was designated as the filtered water-soluble Camellia oleifera seed extract (FSCO), with a final concentration of 50 mg/mL.

  For the second preparation, following sonication, the mixture was autoclaved at 121.3 °C for 20 min, centrifuged at 4,000 rpm for 10 min at room temperature, and the supernatant was collected. This solution was designated as the autoclaved water-soluble Camellia oleifera seed extract (ASCO), also at a final concentration of 50 mg/mL11.


### Component screening and identification of Camellia oleifera seed extract

An ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (1.8 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm, Waters) was used for separation. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (solvent A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (solvent B). The column temperature was set at 40 °C, the flow rate was 0.4 mL/min, and an injection volume of 4 µL was used. The gradient elution was performed according to the MS conditions listed in Table 1. The mobile phase started with 95% A, decreased to 35% A at 5 min, then dropped to 1% A at 6 min and was held for 1.5 min. At 7.6 min, the mobile phase returned to 95% A and was held until the end of the run at 10 min (Table 2).

  The retention times of the detected compounds in the Camellia oleifera seed extract were aligned and corrected, followed by metabolite identification using integrated public databases, predictive libraries, and the metDNA approach. Compound identification confidence levels were assigned according to MSI guidelines: MS¹ only: Identification level 2 (putative identification) and MS/MS library match: Identification level 1 (high confidence identification). For compounds identified using MS/MS library matching, we considered the match score and evaluated it based on the criteria outlined in MSI guidelines. The SMILES identifiers for the active compounds were retrieved from the PubChem database12(https://www.https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Preliminary screening was performed using the SwissADME database13(https://www.swissadme.ch/), based on the following criteria: iLogP ≤ 5, nOHNH ≤ 5, nOH ≤ 10. Compounds that did not satisfy these requirements, including Lipinski’s Rule of Five14, were excluded. Final screening was performed based on high gastrointestinal absorption and lead-likeness as defined in medicinal chemistry, yielding the confirmed active compounds in the Camellia oleifera seeds.


### Validation of disease targets for oral thrush
  Genes associated with Oral thrush were retrieved from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/)15 and DisGeNET database (https://www.disgenet.org/. The disease-related targets were searched using the keywords “Oral thrush” and “acute pseudomembranous candidiasis” and the target names were standardized based on the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/). To ensure data reliability, only genes with a high relevance score (≥ 15.0) were selected for further analysis.


### Protein–protein interaction network construction and analysis
  The genes targeted by the Camellia oleifera seed extract were overlapped with the disease-associated genes for Oral thrush to identify potential therapeutic targets. These candidate genes were combined to obtain a consolidated set of potential targets for the treatment of Oral thrush using the Camellia oleifera seed extract. The resulting target set was then submitted to the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/)16 for protein–protein interaction (PPI) network construction. Homo sapiens was selected as the target species, and a high-confidence interaction score of ≥ 0.9 was applied. Disconnected nodes were removed, and the network was adjusted accordingly. The resulting PPI network was visualized using Cytoscape v3.10.0, and the built-in plugin was used to compute the degree values of all connected target genes, allowing the identification of core targets associated with the therapeutic effects of the Camellia oleifera seed extract against Oral thrush.


### Gene ontology (GO) functional enrichment and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis
  The potential therapeutic targets of Camellia oleifera seed extract for Oral thrush were submitted to the Metascape database17(https://metascape.org/) and the DAVID database (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/)18 for Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses. These analyses were performed to obtain biological information associated with the potential targets and to explore their possible mechanisms of action. The GO enrichment analysis was classified into three categories: Biological Processes (BP), Cellular Components (CC), and Molecular Functions (MF)19.


### Molecular docking
  Molecular docking was performed to predict and obtain the binding energies of protein–ligand interactions. The SDF files of the compounds were retrieved from the PubChem database, and the PDB files of the core target structures were obtained from the RCSB PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org/)20(https://www.rcsb.org/). The following protein structures were used for docking: STAT3 (PDB ID: 1BG1), a central intracellular signaling molecule downstream of IL-6–mediated inflammatory responses. STAT3 was selected as a representative druggable target within the IL-6/STAT3 signaling axis. These structures were downloaded from the PDB database. Missing residues were handled by using the PyMOL 2.5.2 software, which also removed water molecules, ions, and organic molecules. Protein structure optimization was carried out using Chimera 1.1.9. After optimization, the PDB files were prepared in AutodockTools 1.5.6, where hydrogen atoms were added, charges were calculated, and atom types were set to AD4 types. The protein files were saved in pdbqt format. For ligand preparation, Tea saponin (CO19) was downloaded from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) in SDF format. The ligand was minimized using ChemBio3D Ultra 14.0, with a Minimum RMS Gradient of 0.001. The optimized ligand was then imported into AutodockTools 1.5.6, where hydrogen atoms were added, charges were assigned, and chemical bonds were set before saving the ligand in pdbqt format.


### Microbial and cellular preparation
  The standard strain of Candida albicans (ATCC90028, Shanghai Ruichubio Co., Ltd., China) was resuscitated and purified, then inoculated and cultured in yeast peptone dextrose (YPD) medium. The fungal suspension was diluted with YPD, and its concentration was determined using a UV spectrophotometer by measuring the absorbance at 600 nm (OD600). Primary HGFs were mechanically isolated from 1 to 2 mm fragments of gingival tissue collected from a 30-year-old healthy patient undergoing tooth extraction surgery, with informed consent. After enzymatic dispersion, primary cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and antibiotics/antimycotics (penicillin, streptomycin, amphotericin B; Gibco/BRL). Cells between passages 3 and 6 were used for experiments.

### Cytotoxicity and staining evaluation
  HGFs (100 µL, 1 × 10⁵ cells/mL) were seeded into 96-well plates. After 24 h, different concentrations of FSCO and ASCO were added. Following a 48-hour incubation, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and absorbance at 450 nm was measured after 1 h. In another assay, PMMA specimens were immersed in various solutions for 24 h (PBS as control), washed three times with PBS, and then incubated in cell culture medium for another 24 h. Subsequently, 100 µL of the PMMA-conditioned medium was added to HGF cultures. After 48 h, cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 reagent.

  PMMA specimens were immersed in PBS, 100 mg/mL FSCO, 200 mg/mL ASCO, or 100 mg/mL tea saponin solution for 12 h. After rinsing three times with PBS, they were soaked in artificial saliva for another 12 h. On the 7th day, the PMMA specimens were observed under a stereomicroscope (10 × 1.25) to evaluate whether any Camellia oleifera extract residues adhered to the PMMA resin surface25.


## Results

### Identification of chemical constituents and bioactive components in Camellia oleifera seed extract
  Based on UHPLC-Q-TOF-MS metabolomic analysis, a total of 23 compounds in Camellia seed extract were identified that conform to Lipinski’s Rule of Five. Among them, only 19 compounds were found to be potentially related to the treatment of Oral thrush (acute pseudomembranous candidiasis) (Tables 2, 3). Using the keywords “Oral thrush” and “acute pseudomembranous candidiasis”, disease-related genes were retrieved and screened based on relevance, and Oral thrush–related target genes were integrated from multiple databases. After removing duplicates and applying screening criteria, a total of 266 disease targets were identified. By intersecting these disease-related targets with the predicted targets of the active components in Camellia oleifera seed extract, 20 overlapping targets were obtained.

  A PPI (protein–protein interaction) network for the 19 active compounds against Oral thrush was constructed using the STRING database, with the interaction confidence level set to the highest threshold (0.9) for optimization (Fig. 1). The resulting network contains 20 nodes, 57 edges, an average node degree of 5.70, and an average clustering coefficient of 0.62. Among the 20 intersecting targets for Camellia oleifera seed extract in the treatment of Oral thrush, the top 10 core targets with the highest degree of connectivity were: IL6 (14), STAT3 (12), HSP90AA1 (11), LCK (8), KIT (8), JAK3 (8), ESR1 (8), PIK3CD (7), JAK1 (7), and TYK2 (6).

### Gene ontology (GO) functional enrichment and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis
  Biological function analysis was conducted on the potential targets of the 19 active compounds identified from Camellia oleifera seed extract. Based on p-values, the top 10 terms in each category of Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment were visualized, as shown in Fig. 2. In the Molecular Function (MF) category (Fig. 2(a)), the targets were primarily enriched in: Phosphotransferase activity, alcohol group as acceptor (p = 3.47 × 10− 9), Nitric-oxide synthase regulator activity (p = 5.62 × 10− 6), Protein domain specific binding (p = 3.80 × 10− 5).   The results of KEGG pathway enrichment analysis are shown in Fig. 2(d), indicating that the potential mechanisms of action of the active components in Camellia oleifera seed extract mainly involve Th17 cell differentiation (p = 3.16 × 10− 13), Epstein-Barr virus infection (p = 2.19 × 10− 9), Acute myeloid leukemia (p = 1.02 × 10− 5). ### The solutions of the extract of Camellia oleifera seeds inhibits the growth of Candida albicans plankton
  Firstly, we explored the effect of Extract of Camellia oleifera seeds on planktonic Candida albicans. The results are presented as means ± SD of n = 3 biological replicates and n = 3 technical replicates. At 0.6 mg/mL, FSCO showed a 37.43% inhibition rate (mean ± SD = 37.43 ± 1.2%) (Fig. 4(a)(b)), and at 0.8 mg/mL, the inhibition rate was 84.9% (mean ± SD = 84.9 ± 2.3%). ASCO showed a 49.37% inhibition rate (mean ± SD = 49.37 ± 1.5%) at 0.6 mg/mL (Fig. 5(a)(b)), and a 85.3% inhibition rate (mean ± SD = 85.3 ± 2.1%) at 0.8 mg/mL. When concentration was higher than 0.5 mg/ml, the inhibition of FSCO on the growth of Candida albicans was statistically significant compared with the control group (without FSCO). When the ASCO concentration was higher than 0.6 mg/ml, the inhibitory effect of Candida albicans was also statistically significant compared with that of the control group. The MIC for FSCO was found to be 0.8 mg/mL, and the MFC was 1.0 mg/mL. CFU counts confirmed the OD600 growth curve data, showing a significant reduction in colony counts at concentrations above the MIC.

  We acknowledge that the inclusion of standard antifungal controls (e.g., fluconazole or nystatin) would have helped contextualize the potency of Camellia oleifera seed extracts in comparison to established antifungal agents. However, the primary goal of this study was to demonstrate the antifungal potential of Camellia oleifera seed extracts, not to directly compare its effectiveness with that of traditional antifungal agents. Due to time constraints, we were unable to include the standard antifungal controls in this study. We plan to include these controls in future experiments to further compare the antifungal activity of Camellia oleifera seed extracts with that of established antifungal drugs.

### Analysis of cytotoxicity and residual toxicity
  In cytotoxicity tests, FSCO concentrations higher than 0.175 mg/ml significantly reduced HGF cell viability (P < 0.05), with a corresponding 95% CI of [12.6%, 16.3%] reduction in cell viability (Fig. 7(a)). When concentrations were lower than 0.15 mg/ml, cell viability was similar to that of the control group, with no statistical significance between the two groups (P > 0.05) (Fig. 7(b)). As for ASCO, when the concentration was higher than 0.25 mg/ml, results showed obvious cytotoxicity (P < 0.05)(Fig. 7(c)(d)). But after the PMMA specimens were immersed in the FSCO or ASCO solutions for 24 h, and then washed with PBS for 3 times, there was not significant residual toxicity to the HGF cells (P > 0.05)(Fig. 8(a)(b)).

  Fig. 8Residual cytotoxicity of PMMA specimens soaking in Camellia oleifera cake solutions on human gingival fibroblasts. (a) Representative images of cells after 48 h treatment; (b) The results of CCK-8 assay.

  HGF cytotoxicity was observed at concentrations above ~ 0.175–0.25 mg/mL, while antifungal and biofilm inhibition effects were reported at higher concentrations in some assays. To quantify the therapeutic index (TI) and assess practical safety margins for oral applications, we calculated the therapeutic index (TI) using the CC50/MIC ratio. The CC50 (concentration causing 50% cytotoxicity) was determined as 0.25 mg/mL for HGF cells, and the MIC (minimum inhibitory concentration) for FSCO was 0.8 mg/mL. Therefore, the therapeutic index (TI) for FSCO is calculated as CC50/MIC = 0.25 mg/mL/0.8 mg/mL = 0.31. This suggests that the concentration required for effective antifungal and biofilm inhibition is higher than the concentration that induces significant cytotoxicity in HGF cells, which should be carefully considered in future clinical applications.


### Dyeing test
  To explore whether the solutions were safe to the PMMA specimens, we immersed the specimens in the FSCO or ASCO solutions for 12 h, rinsed with PBS for three times and soaked in artificial saliva for 12 h. After 7 days we washed the specimens, placed them in a pose microscope (10*1.25) to check the color. Compared with the effect of standard denture cleaner like polident, there were spots on PMMA that was soak in Extract of Camellia oleifera seeds solution. Since the leaching solutions of the Extract of Camellia oleifera seeds is a colored liquid, materials adhering to the PMMA surface discolored the base after soaked for 7days (Fig. 9).

### The antifungal activity and biological safety of tea saponin
  Due to the discoloration in PMMA caused by substances in FSCO and ASCO, we attempted to study the antifungal effect of the main components of Extract of Camellia oleifera seeds and it was reported that the main ingredient of Extract of Camellia oleifera seeds is tea saponin. Tea saponin showed a concentration dependence on the inhibition of planktonic Candida albicans (Fig. 10(a)(b)). When the concentration of tea saponin was 0.2 mg/ml, the inhibition rate of Candida albicans was 76.5%, and once the concentration rose to 0.25 mg/ml, no colony formation could be observed on the plate. According to the automatic growth curve analyzer, the lag phase of Candida albicans at 0.1875 mg/ml tea saponin had 10 hours’ delay, but when the concentration was 0.25 mg/ml, the lag phase was significantly delayed for 30 h. No fungal growth was observed in the analyzer at 0.3125 mg/mL (Fig. 10(c)).

## Discussion

### Multi-target and multi-pathway mechanisms of Camellia oleifera seeds extracts in treating oral thrush
  This study systematically explores the potential molecular mechanisms of Camellia oleifera seeds extracts in treating Oral thrush through network pharmacology and molecular docking. The results indicate that the active components of Camellia oleifera seeds extracts exhibit extensive interactions with Oral thrush-related targets, rather than reflecting systemic inflammatory or oncogenic signaling, the identified targets predominantly represent host mucosal immune responses to Candida albicans, including epithelial cytokine production, Th17 cell differentiation, and regulation of inflammatory damage at the oral mucosal surface26. Among the identified targets, STAT3 is well-recognized mediators of mucosal immune responses and epithelial inflammation during Candida albicans infection27. In oral candidiasis, STAT3 is primarily activated in epithelial and local immune cells following fungal adhesion and invasion, functioning as regulators of antifungal immunity, rather than drivers of systemic inflammation. rather than being exclusively associated with systemic inflammatory or oncogenic processes28. Increasing evidence indicates that activation of the IL-6/STAT3 axis in oral epithelial cells and local immune cells is a hallmark of oral candidiasis and correlates with disease severity. Gene Ontology enrichment showed that these targets participate in protein modification, cytokine signaling, immune cell differentiation, and intercellular adhesion29. Moreover, KEGG pathway enrichment revealed that these targets were mainly associated with Th17 cell differentiation, the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, and epithelial barrier regulation- all closely linked to mucosal immunity and inflammation in Oral thrush. Overall, these findings underscore a multi-target, multi-pathway mechanism of Camellia oleifera seeds extracts in managing Oral thrush, providing a strong theoretical basis for future preclinical and clinical applications and further expanding the scope of traditional Chinese medicine in oral infectious diseases.


### Alleviation of oral thrush-related inflammation by Camellia oleifera seeds extracts via the IL-6/STAT3 pathway
  IL-6 production and subsequent STAT3 activation in oral epithelial cells and mucosal immune cells have been shown to be essential for initiating Th17-mediated antifungal responses against Candida albicans30,31. Accumulating evidence demonstrates that the IL-6/STAT3 signaling pathway plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of oral candidiasis by mediating local mucosal inflammation rather than systemic immune activation32. Upon Candida albicans adhesion and invasion, oral epithelial cells rapidly produce IL-6, leading to STAT3 activation in both epithelial and immune cells. This signaling cascade promotes Th17 differentiation and IL-17 production, which is essential for antifungal defense but, when excessive, contributes to mucosal inflammation, epithelial damage, and clinical symptoms of oral thrush33,34.

  Therefore, modulation of IL-6/STAT3 signaling represents a host-directed therapeutic strategy aimed at balancing antifungal immunity and inflammatory tissue injury, rather than targeting fungal growth alone. Given that IL-6 is a soluble cytokine without a classical small-molecule binding pocket, future studies should focus on experimentally validating the effects of tea saponin on STAT3 phosphorylation and downstream cytokine production in oral epithelial and immune cells.


## Conclusion
  This study systematically explored the antifungal activity and potential host-directed anti-inflammatory mechanisms of Camellia oleifera seed extracts in the context of oral thrush using network pharmacology, molecular docking, and in vitro experiments. Multiple active components, particularly tea saponins, were predicted to modulate STAT3-mediated inflammatory signaling and exhibited inhibitory effects against Candida albicans growth and biofilm formation.

  Importantly, in vitro cytotoxicity assays revealed that effective antifungal concentrations overlapped with, or exceeded, cytotoxic thresholds in human gingival fibroblasts, resulting in a limited therapeutic index (TI = 0.31). Nevertheless, the observed antifungal efficacy, combined with reversible residual toxicity following material rinsing and the activity of isolated tea saponins, suggests that Camellia oleifera seed extracts may serve as promising lead compounds for the development of topical, short-contact, or formulation-optimized antifungal strategies. Future studies should focus on dose optimization, targeted delivery, and in vivo safety evaluation to better define their clinical applicability in oral fungal infections.
