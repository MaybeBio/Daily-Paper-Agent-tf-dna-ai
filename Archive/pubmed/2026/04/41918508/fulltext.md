# Computational Investigation of Flavonoid-Associated Molecular
Pathways in Astrogliosis Modulation


## Abstract
  Astrogliosis is characterized by an abnormal increase in the number of astrocytes in the brain due to damage, trauma, infection, ischemia, stroke, autoimmune responses, or neurodegenerative disorders. Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein (GFAP) is a marker for astrocyte development and astrogliosis. Flavonoids have unclear anti-neuroinflammatory effects in astrogliosis. This computational analysis was the first to investigate the potential interaction between flavonoids and the transcription factors involved in GFAP expression. Using AutoDock Vina, 60 flavonoids with known anti-inflammatory properties were docked to 26 proteins involved in GFAP expression. Toxicities of the flavonoids were predicted using the ProtoxII server, and drug-likeness and pharmacokinetic properties were assessed using the DruLiTo and pkCSM software, respectively. BIOVIA Discovery Studio software was used to evaluate the interactions between flavonoids and target proteins. Among the studied flavonoids, biochanin A, bavachin, apigenin, epicatechin, wogonin, kaempferol, hispidulin, genistein, farrerol, diosmetin, and daidzein displayed drug-like properties, no toxicity, favorable pharmacokinetic properties, and better docking scores for Janus Kinase 1 (JAK1), Janus Kinase 2 (JAK2), I Kappa B Kinase (I-κKB), Serine/Threonine Kinase (AKT), and Histone Acetyltransferase (P300). This in silico study revealed that predictive associations between flavonoids and JAK1, JAK2, I-κKB, AKT, and P300 might affect both GFAP expression and the astrogliosis process via the Nuclear Factor Kappa-Light-Chain-Enhancer of Activated B Cells (NF-κB) and Janus Kinase/Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription (JAK/STAT) pathway. The interactions between flavonoid(s) and predicted binding partner(s) were confirmed based on previous studies. These predictive associations are extremely valuable for developing nutritional guidelines and for improving flavonoid-based therapeutic strategies for neurological disorders driven by astrogliosis. This research provides insight for further in vitro and in vivo research.


## Introduction
  Astrocytes, the most abundant glial cells in the central nervous system (CNS), play an essential role in maintaining homeostasis and function. They interact closely with neurons and other glial cells, such as microglia and oligodendrocytes, to control ionic homeostasis, neurotransmitter levels, and energy metabolism and participate in synaptic transmission and plasticity [1]. Astrogliosis, reactive astrocytes, is characterized by remarkable molecular, cellular, and functional alterations in astrocytes in response to CNS damage or disease [2,3]. It can result in loss or disrupted astrocyte function and affect the support of neurons and synapses. Neuronal damage or death can occur because neurons themselves are incapable of maintaining tissue homeostasis [4].

  GFAP is a marker for astrocyte development and astrogliosis [2,3]. Reactive astrocytes are identified by an increase in GFAP expression and in the number, thickness, and length of cellular processes [5]. GFAP expression was detected in mice, rats, and humans during the early developmental stages. It is also present in radial glial cells, which give rise to neurons, oligodendrocytes, and astrocytes. Transcription factors Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 3 (STAT3), NF-κB, Activating Protein-1 (AP1), Specificity Protein 1 (SP1), and DNA-Binding Transcription Factor (CSL) are mostly effective in the transcription of the GFAP gene. During development, neuroepithelial cells (NECs) differentiate into astrocytes by synthesizing GFAP via STAT3. The binding of cytokines such as Ciliary Neurotrophic Factor (CNTF), Leukemia Inhibitory Factor (LIF), or Oncostatin M (OSM) to Glycoprotein 130 (GP130) leads to the activation of JAK, which activates STAT3 through phosphorylation. pSTAT is methylated by Protein Arginine N-Methyltransferase 1 (PRMT1). pSTAT3(me), Mothers Against Decapentaplegic Homolog 1 (Smad1), and the CBP/Histone Acetyltransferase (P300) complex bind to the GFAP promoter in the nucleus to stimulate GFAP expression. In addition, Serine/Threonine Kinase (AKT) phosphorylated by the PI3K pathway helps release the transcriptional repressor N-CoR from the promoter region [2].

  The heterogeneity of reactive astrocytes is complex and dynamic [5]. Astrocytes are triggered into two polarization states: a neurotoxic or pro-inflammatory phenotype (A1) and a neuroprotective or anti-inflammatory phenotype (A2) [3]. A1 astrocytes are thought to be toxic and to stimulate the secretion of pro-inflammatory substances and neurotoxins that induce neuronal death. A2 astrocytes exhibit neuroprotective characteristics by upregulating anti-inflammatory substances and neurotrophic factors, which induce survival, growth, and repair of neurons [6,7].

  Flavonoids are polyphenolic compounds with remarkable bioactivity that are extensively present in the plant kingdom [8]. Studies have revealed that foods high in flavonoids include a wide range of neuroprotective, anti-proliferative, anti-cancer, anti-angiogenic, anti-microbial, anti-viral, anti-inflammatory, and analgesic effects [8,9]. The role of flavonoids in reducing multiple proinflammatory mediators in brain-related diseases has been determined both in vitro and in vivo. For example, biochanin A treatment decreased the levels of pro-inflammatory mediators by inhibiting TLR4/MYD88/ NF-κB, PI3K/AKT, and ERK pathways and ROS in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-stimulated BV2 microglia [10].

  Astrocytes could be the common therapeutic target for many CNS disorders. Understanding and decreasing disease-associated loss of function in astrocytes are assumed to be a major therapeutic goal. However, no astrocyte-based therapies or drugs have been determined [4]. Computer-aided drug design (CADD) is utilized not only in the development and design of drugs but also in the understanding of the molecular mechanisms of drugs. CADD techniques are valuable for reducing the cost and time associated with drug discovery by directing experimental research towards promising compounds in a more streamlined manner. Molecular docking is a CADD approach that uses structural information to predict the binding modes and affinities of ligands and receptors at the molecular level by simulating their interactions. Several pharmaceutical products such as imatinib, zanamivir, nelfinavir, and erdafitinib have been identified or optimized using CADD [11,12]. This study hypothesized that if flavonoids have an anti-neuroinflammatory effect, they should affect the synthesis of GFAP to combat astrogliosis, so we focused on pathways involved in GFAP expression. Sixty anti-inflammatory flavonoids were analyzed via molecular docking to predict potential targets and candidates for astrocyte-based therapies.


## Materials and Methods

### Proteins
  The crystal structures of the proteins were retrieved from the Research Collaboratory for Structural Bioinformatics (RCSB) Protein Databank (PDB) (https://www.rcsb.org/) in .pdb file format. Protein structures were processed by using BIOVIA Discovery Studio (Discovery Studio Visualizer v21.1.0.20298). Crystallographic water molecules and ligands/inhibitors were removed from the protein crystal structures if present. Polar hydrogen atoms were also added to proteins. The proteins utilized in this study and their corresponding PDB codes are listed in Appendix A: Supplementary Table 1, along with their roles in astrogliosis.


### Ligands
  The flavonoids used in this study were selected from previous studies that have demonstrated anti-inflammatory effects (Appendix A: Supplementary Table 2) and were used as ligands in molecular docking studies. In addition, 44 commercially available drugs (Appendix A: Supplementary Table 3) influence JAK1 [13], JAK2 [13], AKT1 [14], I-κKB [15-17] and P300 [18-20] proteins were selected and used for comparison. The 3D structures of the selected compounds were downloaded from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) in .sdf format. Before the docking process, the 3D structures of the compounds were energy-minimized, and files were saved as pdbqt using OpenBabel (version 3.1.1) in the PyRx virtual screening tool package [21].


### Molecular Docking
  Protein ligand docking was performed using AutoDock Vina [22] in the PyRx virtual screening tool package [21]. The grid box was adjusted to cover the active sites of proteins. Proteins were used as rigid molecules, and ligands as flexible molecules.

  The molecular docking method was validated using a redocking procedure with co-crystallized ligands of selected proteins (AKT1, I-κKB, JAK1, JAK2, and P300), to which flavonoids had strong binding affinities. However, among these proteins, I-κKB was excluded from the validation protocol because it lacks a co-crystallized ligand or inhibitor in its crystal structure. The crystal structures of proteins were retrieved from the PDB in .pdb file format. Native ligands/inhibitors were extracted from the protein structure by using Pymol (version 3.1.6.1). Proteins and ligands were prepared separately for redocking by using AutoDockTools (MGLTools 1.5.7). Water molecules were removed, and polar hydrogens were added to the proteins. Hydrogens were added to the ligands, and Gasteiger charges were assigned to both proteins and ligands before conversion into a .pdbqt file format. Redocking was performed using the same docking tool, AutoDock Vina, for consistency. The grid box was adjusted to cover the native ligands. The heavy-atom root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) between the redocked pose and native ligand conformation was calculated using PyMOL to evaluate docking accuracy.


### Drug-likeness, Toxicity, and Pharmacokinetic Properties
  The drug-likeness of the ligands, including flavonoids and drugs, was assessed based on the Lipinski rule of 5 (Ro5) using DruLiTo software. Toxicity assessments were performed on the ProtoxII online server. Oral toxicity, hepatotoxicity, carcinogenicity, immunotoxicity, mutagenicity, and cytotoxicity of the compounds were evaluated in silico. The pharmacokinetic properties (absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME)) of the drugs and flavonoids were evaluated using the pkCSM web server (http://structure.bioc.cam.ac.uk/pkcsm) [23,24].


### Protein-Ligand Interactions
  The BIOVIA Discovery Studio software evaluated the interactions between the selected flavonoids or drugs and the target protein.


## Results

### Molecular Docking
  To elucidate the mechanisms of action of flavonoids, their affinities to proteins were estimated using AutoDock Vina. Although there is no exact threshold for binding energies in molecular docking studies, in the literature, binding energies lower than -7 kcal/mol are commonly accepted as “strong binding” [25-28]. However, in the current study, we defined the threshold as -9 kcal/mol to reduce false-positive results and prioritize candidates for further analysis. The flavonoids were filtered according to their binding energies, drug-likeness, and toxicity (Table 1). All the binding energies were provided in Appendix A: Supplementary Table 4.

  Flavonoids bound to GFAP with a binding energy of -4.1 – -7.6 kcal/mol. STAT3, NF-κB, SP1, and CSL are the most effective transcription factors for GFAP. The flavonoids bound to transcription factors with a binding energy varying between -5.3 – -7.4 kcal/mol, -5.5 – -7.9 kcal/mol, -5.4 – -6.9 kcal/mol, and -4.9 – -8.6 kcal/mol, respectively. Cytokines stimulate GFAP synthesis by recruiting STAT3 and AKT. Binding energies of flavonoids to cytokines varied between -6.4 – -8.6 kcal/mol for CNTF, between -2.5 – -6.7 kcal/mol for OSM, and between -5.2 – -7.5 kcal/mol for LIF. The flavonoids bound to AKT1, AKT2 and I-κKB with a binding energy varying between -5.7 – -12.2 kcal/mol, -3.4 – -9.2 kcal/mol, and -5.4 – -10.5 kcal/mol, respectively. The JAK/STAT pathway differentiates NECs into astrocytes and astrocytes into A1 and A2 states. The binding energies of flavonoids to the JAK/STAT pathway protein were as follows: GP130 (-4.5 – -7.1 kcal/mol), JAK1 (-5.0 – -11.0 kcal/mol), JAK2 (-5.2 – -11.0 kcal/mol), PRMT1 (-5.7 – -8.6 kcal/mol), to P300 (-8.2 – -10.8 kcal/mol), CBP (-6.7 – -10.2 kcal/mol), Smad1 (-3.5 – -5.8 kcal/mol), to Bone Morphogenetic Protein-2 (BMP2) (-4.8 – -7.0 kcal/mol), and SWI/SNF Related BAF Chromatin Remodeling Complex Subunit ATPase 4 (BRG1) (-5.4 – -9.6 kcal/mol). The binding energies of flavonoids to inhibitory proteins of GFAP expression were as follows: TAB2 (-1.0 – -7.7 kcal/mol), Methyl CpG Binding Protein 2 (MECP2) (-4.1 – -7.5 kcal/mol), Recombination Signal Binding Protein for Immunoglobulin Kappa J Region (RBPJ (-4.9 – -7.7 kcal/mol), Paired Amphipathic Helix Protein (SIN3A) (-5.1 – -7.7 kcal/mol), and Receptor Tyrosine-Protein Kinase (ERB4) (-5.3 – -7.4 kcal/mol). Flavonoids bound to Tyrosine-Protein Kinase (Lyn) with a binding energy of -5.1 – -6.9 kcal/mol.

  The results showed that flavonoids may play a role in combating neuroinflammation via the AKT, NF-κB, and JAK/STAT signaling pathways. Therefore, we studied the binding energies of commercially available drugs to JAK1, JAK2, AKT1, I-κKB, and P300, which play a role in astrogliosis and are mostly targeted by flavonoids. The threshold was set at -9 kcal/mol. Drugs bound to JAK1 with a binding energy varying between -7.0 – -11.2 kcal/mol, and 13 of 24 drugs had binding energies ≤ -9.0 kcal/mol, while to JAK2 with a binding energy varying between -6.8 – -11.5 kcal/mol, and 14 of 24 drugs had binding energies ≤ -9.0 kcal/mol. Drugs bound to AKT1 with binding energies varying between -9.6 – -12.4 kcal/mol, and all drugs had binding energies ≤ -9.0 kcal/mol. Drugs bound to I-κKB with a binding energy varying between -7.0 – -9.8 kcal/mol, and three of seven drugs had binding energies ≤ -9.0 kcal/mol. Drugs bound to P300 with binding energy varying between -9.2 – -11.7 kcal/mol, and all three drugs had binding energies ≤ -9.0 kcal/mol. All the binding energies of the drugs to specific proteins were provided in Appendix A: Supplementary Table 5.

  To validate the docking method, redocking with native (co-crystallized) ligands to selected proteins (JAK1, JAK2, AKT1, and P300) and RMSD calculations were performed. Native ligands were redocked in the same binding pocket as in the crystallized structures (Appendix B: Supplementary Figure 1(a) – (d)). The best redocking pose of the native ligands of the selected proteins showed binding affinities of -5.179, -8.074, -14.36, and-13.02 kcal/mol for JAK1, JAK2, AKT1, and P300, respectively. In addition, the full-atom RMSD values were calculated as 2.316 Å for JAK1, 0.974 Å for JAK2, 0.340 Å for AKT1, and 0.384 Å for P300. The binding energies and RMSD values of the redocking procedure were provided in Appendix A: Supplementary Table 6.


### Drug-likeness
  Ro5 was used to evaluate the drug-likeness of the flavonoids and drugs. According to Lipinski’s criteria, if a compound possesses a molecular weight ≤ 500 Da, number of hydrogen bond donors (HBD) ≤ 5, number of hydrogen bond acceptors (HBA) ≤ 10, and calculated logP ≤ 5, it is anticipated to be well absorbed and exhibit high permeability [29]. Of the 60 flavonoids assessed, 34 met all Ro5 criteria. Additionally, five phenolic acids had one violation, 11 had two violations, and eight had three violations (Appendix A: Supplementary Table 7). The drug-likeness of the flavonoids based on their binding energies and toxicity properties is listed in Table 2 and Figure 1. In contrast, only stattic fulfilled the Ro5 rule among commercially utilized drugs. Furthermore, 30 drugs had one violation, nine drugs had two violations, and four drugs had three violations in Ro5 (Appendix A: Supplementary Table 8).


### Toxicity
  The predicted toxicities of the flavonoids and drugs were evaluated. Biochanin A, bavachin, apigenin, agathisflavanone, epicatechin, wogonin, trilobatin, tangeretin, phloretin, luteoloside, kaempferol, hispidulin, genistein, farrerol, epigallocatechin-3-O-gallate, diosmetin, and daidzein showed no predicted toxicity (Appendix A: Supplementary Table 9). The toxicities of the flavonoids were listed in Figure 1. In addition, nine of the 44 commercially available drugs, namely ON-044580, momelotinib, ruxolitinib, AG490, XL019, Upadacitinib, Tofacitinib, AT7867, and L002, used in this study did not show any predicted toxicity (Appendix A: Supplementary Table 10).


### Pharmacokinetic Properties
  Absorption (Water solubility (logS), Caco2 permeability, intestinal absorption, and whether being P-glycoprotein substrate and inhibitor), distribution (volume of distribution (VDss), fraction of unbound ligands to serum proteins (fraction unbound, Fu), and blood-brain barrier (BBB) and central nervous system (CNS)), metabolism (whether being a cytochrome P450 substrate and inhibitor), and excretion (total clearance, and whether the renal organic cation transporter (OCT2) substrate) were predicted to evaluate pharmacokinetic properties of flavonoids and drugs.

  Absorption: All flavonoids were found to be soluble in water according to the logS scale: insoluble < -10 < poorly soluble < -6 < moderately soluble < -4 < soluble < -2 < very soluble < 0 < highly soluble [23]. Apigenin, wogonin, genistein, farrerol, and daidzein showed high intestinal permeability (CaCo2 permeability > 0.90), and all flavonoids had high intestinal absorption (> 30 %) varying between 74.29 % - 94.839 %. In addition, all flavonoids were P-glycoprotein substrates, but not inhibitors (Appendix A: Supplementary Table 11). In contrast, all drugs were found to be soluble in water, except for TG101209, fedratinib, GC-DC-0068, AT7867, and AS-602868, which were moderately soluble in water. Half of the drugs showed high intestinal permeability (CaCo2 permeability > 0.90), and all drugs had high intestinal absorption (> 30 %), varying between 70.15 % and 100 %. Seven of the drugs were P-glycoprotein substrates, half of which were P-glycoprotein I inhibitors, whereas 20 of the 44 drugs were P-glycoprotein II inhibitors (Appendix A: Supplementary Table 12).

  Distribution: Bavachin, apigenin, epicatechin, kaempferol, farrerol, and diosmetin showed high VDss (> 0.45); wogonin, hispidulin, and genistein showed moderate VDss; and biochanin A and daidzein showed low VDss (< − 0.15). Apigenin, epicatechin, wogonin, kaempferol, farrerol, and daidzein had high Fu values, whereas other compounds had low Fu values. All flavonoids were poorly distributed in the brain, except daidzein, which was moderately distributed. The CNS was easily penetrated by bavachin and daidzein, but not by epicatechin. Other flavonoids had a moderate ability to penetrate the CNS (Appendix A: Supplementary Table 11). Almost half of the drugs showed high VDss, 17 of them showed moderate VDss, and seven of the drugs showed low VDss. Most drugs (36 of 44) had high Fu values, whereas the others had low Fu values. Nearly all drugs were poorly distributed in the brain, except BAY1125976 and TAS-114, which were moderately distributed in the brain. Moreover, AT7867, VGX-1027, and IKK 16 readily cross the BBB. Seven of the drugs could penetrate the CNS, whereas 11 were unable to penetrate the CNS (Appendix A: Supplementary Table 12).

  Metabolism: None of the flavonoids were CYP2D6 substrates. Biochanin A, bavachin, wogonin, and hispidulin were the substrates of CYP3A4, while the rest of the flavonoids were not. All flavonoids were inhibitors of CYP1A2, except epicatechin and inhibitors of CYP2C19, except epicatechin and kaempferol. Moreover, flavonoids were inhibitors of CYP2C9, except for apigenin, epicatechin, kaempferol, and genistein. None of the flavonoids were CYP2CD6 inhibitors. Finally, none of the flavonoids were inhibitors of CYP3A4, except for bavachin and wogonin (Appendix A: Supplementary Table 11). Most drugs were not substrates of CYP2D6, except peficitinib and AT7867. A total of 29 drugs were substrates of CYP3A4, whereas the remaining drugs were not. A total of 24 drugs were CYP1A2 inhibitors, 17 were CYP2C19 inhibitors, 16 were CYP2C9 inhibitors, four were CYP2CD6 inhibitors, and 24 were CYP3A4 inhibitors (Appendix A: Supplementary Table 12).

  Excretion: The total flavonoid clearance varied between 0.07 – 0.598 ml/min/kg. None of the flavonoids were substrates of renal OCT2 (Appendix A: Supplementary Table 11). The total clearance of the drugs varied between -31.452 and 1.034 ml/min/kg. Most of the drugs were not substrates of renal OCT2, except for gandotinib, BAY1125976, TAS-114, GSK141795, BMS-345541, and CHS-828 (Appendix A: Supplementary Table 12).


### Protein-Ligand Interactions
  Hydrogen bonds and van der Waals interactions were mainly responsible for flavonoid–protein/drug-protein interactions (Table 3). Other interactions (eg, Pi-sigma, alkyl, and carbon-hydrogen bonds) also contributed to protein-ligand interactions. All interactions were shown in 2D in Appendix B: Supplementary Figure 2, and selected flavonoid–protein interactions were shown in 3D in Figure 2A and B as examples.


## Discussion
  Multiple signaling pathways and transcription factors control astrocyte responses under neurodegenerative diseases condition. NF-κB, JAK/STAT3, and NFAT pathways are the main regulators of astrogliosis [30]. This study focused on the pathways involved in the expression of GFAP to predict the role of flavonoid (s) in astrogliosis to improve nutritional guidelines and therapeutic strategies for brain-related disorders. To this end, 60 flavonoids with known anti-inflammatory effects were studied to identify their potential associations. Surprisingly, our results showed that AKT1, I-κKB, JAK1, JAK2, and P300, which are key regulatory elements of NF-κB and JAK/STAT3 pathways, are the most predictive binding partners of the flavonoids.


### Molecular Docking
  The binding energies of flavonoids to proteins involved in GFAP synthesis were determined using a molecular docking approach to provide insight into the mechanisms of action of flavonoids. Based on the aforementioned criteria (affinity to proteins, predicted toxicity, and Ro5), 11 flavonoids, including biochanin A, bavachin, apigenin, epicatechin, wogonin, kaempferol, hispidulin, genistein, farrerol, diosmetin, and daidzein, were predicted as the most promising and effective candidates (Figure 3). These flavonoids were discussed below to elucidate their predicted mechanisms of action.

  Our results demonstrated that the binding energies of flavonoids to AKT1, I-κKB, JAK1, JAK2, and P300 were mostly smaller than -9.0 kcal/mol (Table 1). AKT, an upstream molecule of NF-κB, plays a crucial role in NF-κB activation [31]. Under normal conditions, NF-κB and IκB complexes are present in the cytoplasm. Upon receiving an external stimulus, IKK is activated, promoting IκB degradation. Subsequently, NF-κB translocates to the nucleus, where it regulates the expression of proinflammatory genes [32]. Astrogliosis and astrogliogenesis of neural stem cells (NSCs) and neural progenitor cells (NPCs) are regulated by the JAK/STAT pathway. JAK1 is mainly associated with astrocytic differentiation, whereas JAK2 is essential for NSC proliferation. Cytokines like IL6 trigger JAK activation at the cell membrane. Activated JAK phosphorylates STAT3, which translocates to the nucleus [33]. Furthermore, acetylation of STAT3 by P300 promotes STAT3 dimerization and transcriptional activation [34].

  LYN is highly expressed in glial cells, including microglia and oligodendrocytes. However, its deficiency did not affect GFAP expression [35]. Therefore, it was used as a negative control. As expected, binding energies of flavonoids to inhibitory proteins of GFAP expression (TAB2, MeCP2, RBPJ, SIN3A, and ERB4) and LYN were higher than the threshold (Appendix A: Supplementary Table 4).

  Moreover, the docking protocol was validated by redocking the co-crystallized (native) ligands and RMSD calculations. Native ligands were reintroduced into the identical binding pocket, as observed in the crystallized structures (Appendix B: Supplementary Figure 1(a) – (d)). The RMSD values of the redocked poses of the selected proteins were calculated to validate the docking protocol. RMSD values smaller than 2 Å are commonly accepted as the threshold for accurate pose prediction and docking method validation [36,37]. In the current study, the redocked poses had RMSD values below 2 Å for JAK2, AKT1, and P300, indicating accurate pose predictions. However, the re-evaluated JAK1 conformation had an RMSD of 2.316 Å, slightly above the acceptance criteria. In contrast, the native ligand redocked into the same binding pocket of JAK1, which showed that the redocked ligand was correctly positioned within the binding pocket. Considering the redocked positions and RMSD values of the selected four proteins, the validation results support the reliability and validity of the docking protocol for visual screening.


### Limitations of Molecular Docking Approach
  In the current study, molecular docking was performed using the AutoDock Vina platform, which is one of the most popular docking platforms. The platform is open-source, user-friendly, and relatively fast. Therefore, it is a widely used docking platform [38]. However, there are several limitations of molecular docking platforms, including AutoDock Vina. First, it is noted that docking scoring functions rely on empirical approximations rather than absolute thermodynamic free energy. Therefore, predicted binding affinities should be evaluated as relative rankings, not for quantitative free energy estimations [39]. Moreover, each docking platform uses different algorithms, scoring functions, and energy models. Hence, using a single docking platform may affect hit selection [39,40]. In addition, solvent effects, entropic effects, and receptor flexibility remain as other major challenges [41]. Despite these limitations, molecular docking studies remain powerful, cost- and time-effective tools for drug discovery research.

  In the current research, to reduce the limitations of using a single docking platform, (i) a strict binding energy threshold (-9 kcal/mol) was applied to eliminate false positive hits, (ii) the docking protocol was validated with redocking of native ligands to their selected proteins, and (iii) RMSD values were calculated. However, it should be noted that the present study relies on in silico approaches and should be experimentally validated.


### Validation of the Role of Flavonoids on Molecular Pathways Based on the
Previous Research
  The effects of flavonoids on AKT1, I-κKB, JAK1, JAK2, and P300 have been demonstrated in various in vitro and in vivo studies, such as inflammation, neuroinflammation, neurodegenerative diseases, infectious diseases, cerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury, aging, and cancer. In this section, the function of flavonoids on molecular pathways was examined based on the previous research to determine the relevance of the predicted affinities in a physiological context.

  Biochanin A: The role of biochanin A in inflammation was evaluated in vitro. Biochanin A treatment decreased the levels of pro-inflammatory mediators by inhibiting the TLR4/MYD88/NF-κB, PI3K/AKT, and ERK pathways and ROS in LPS-stimulated BV2 microglia [10]. These results were consistent with the findings of the current study. Biochanin A exhibited a binding affinity of -9.8 kcal/mol to AKT1 (Table 1 and Figure 3). AKT, an upstream molecule of NF-κB, plays a critical role in NF-κB activation [31]. Inhibition of the NF-κB pathway might depend on inhibition of the AKT pathway. Moreover, all studied drugs were specific to the AKT1 pathway and had lower binding energies than biochanin A, except GSK2141795 (Appendix A: Supplementary Table 5).

  Hispidulin: Hispidulin treatment of LPS-stimulated BV2 microglia inhibited the AKT/STAT3/ NF-κB pathway by decreasing phospho-IκB, phospho-NF-κB, phospho-AKT, and phospho-STAT3 [42]. These results were consistent with the findings of the current study. Hispidulin had a high binding affinity for AKT1 (-9.4 kcal/mol), I-κKB (-9.4 kcal/mol), and P300 (-9.0 kcal/mol) (Table 1 and Figure 3). All the drugs targeting AKT1 and P300 had greater affinity compared to hispidulin. In contrast, MLN-0415 and IKK 16 had higher affinity than hispidulin for I-κKB (Appendix A: Supplementary Table 5).

  Apigenin: Apigenin has been shown to play a role in inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway. This is achieved by inhibiting I-κKB activation and preventing IκBα phosphorylation, which is a proteasomal inhibitor of NF-κB [43]. The neuroprotective role of apigenin via the suppression of the NF-κB and NRF2 pathways has also been demonstrated in vivo in rats with LPS-induced Parkinson’s disease [32]. It reduces LPS-induced pro-inflammatory mediator production in inflammatory skin diseases by decreasing the activation of the AKT, Mechanistic Target of Rapamycin Kinase (MTOR), and NF-κB pathways [44]. In cancer-related research, apigenin has been shown to prevent phosphorylation of AKT and JAK, thus preventing downstream activation of transcription factors [45]. These results correlated with the findings in the current study, where the binding energies of apigenin were -9.6 kcal/mol for AKT1 and I-κKB, -9.0 kcal/mol for JAK1/2, and -9.1 kcal/mol for P300 (Table 1 and Figure 3). Although all drugs targeting AKT1 had a higher affinity than apigenin, apigenin showed a higher affinity than the drugs targeting I-κKB, except for IKK 16 and MLN-0415, which showed equal and higher affinity, respectively. Moreover, almost half of the reference drugs (ON-044580, AZD1480, Ruxolitinib, Stattic, Barcitinib, AG490, Peficitinib, Upadacitinib, Decernotinib, Itacitinib, PF-04965842, and Tofacitinib) showed lower affinity to JAK1 or 2 than the mentioned flavonoid (Appendix A: Supplementary Table 5).

  Wogonin: Wogonin treatment can reduce inflammatory reactions by targeting the NF-κB and STAT3 pathways [46,47]. However, the binding partners of wogonin in the NF-κB and STAT3 pathways remain unknown. According to our results, these partners could be either I-κB or JAK1/2, as wogonin exhibited the strongest binding affinity to I-κKB at -9.4 kcal/mol. Also, the binding energy of wogonin is -9.3 kcal/mol for AKT1 and -9.0 kcal/mol for JAK1/2 (Table 1 and Figure 3). Wogonin possessed a lower affinity to AKT1 than its reference drugs but had a higher affinity for I-κB and JAK1/2 than almost half of their reference drugs (Appendix A: Supplementary Table 5). Nevertheless, it is essential to assess the possible effects of wogonin on AKT1, I-κB, and JAK1/2 in both in vitro and in vivo experiments.

  Farrerol: Farrerol treatment reduced the production of pro-inflammatory mediators like TNF-α, IL6, IL1β, inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), and COX-2. It also inhibited the phosphorylation of AKT, NF-κB, ERK1/2, and p38 in the mammary glands of an LPS-induced mouse mastitis model [48]. Similarly, phosphorylation of AKT, NF-κB, ERK1/2, and JNK1/2 was inhibited in TNBS-induced colonic inflammation mice [31]. However, the binding partners of farrerol and the inhibition mechanism(s) of AKT, NF-κB, ERK1/2, JNK1/2, and p38 phosphorylation are unknown. Based on the findings of the current study, farrerol exhibited a strong affinity for AKT1, comparable to that of reference drugs, with a binding energy of -10.0 kcal/mol. Furthermore, farrerol demonstrated a higher affinity for I-κKB (-10.3 kcal/mol) than all the reference drugs (Table 1, Figure 3, Appendix A: Supplementary Table 5). Thus, AKT1 and I-κKB might act as binding partners in the AKT and NF-κB pathways. Moreover, the binding energy of farrerol to JAK2 and P300 is smaller than -9.0 kcal/mol. To the best of our knowledge, there is no information on the interaction between farrerol and JAK2, or between farrerol and P300. This interaction must be validated in both in vitro and in vivo conditions in future studies.

  Daidzein: Daidzein treatment inactivated NF-κB and suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokine production both in vitro and in vivo in lung cancer [49]. This treatment significantly decreased phosphorylated MAPK, GSK-3β, and NF-κB, except for pAKT in LPS-induced BV2 microglia. Inhibition of the activation of MAPK, GSK-3β, and NF-κB reduced inflammation [50]. In our study, daidzein had a high binding affinity for AKT 1 and I-κKB, similar to most of the studied reference drugs (Table 1, Figure 3, and Appendix A: Supplementary Table 5). Daidzein-induced NF-κB inactivation might be related to the predictive association of I-κKB.

  Diosmetin: In a rat model of pneumococcal meningitis, diosmetin reduced neuroinflammation and neuronal apoptosis by modulating the PI3K/AKT/ NF-κB signaling pathway. Diosmetin treatment decreased the levels of proinflammatory cytokines NF-κB and pAKT but didn’t affect AKT levels [51]. The reduction in pAKT and NF-κB could be related to the high affinity binding of diosmetin to AKT1 and I-κKB. Our study showed that disometin had a lower binding energy to AKT1 (-9.5 kcal/mol) than the reference drugs, but it had a higher binding energy to I-κKB (-9.7 kcal/mol) than the reference drugs, except MLN-0415 (Table 1, Figure 3, and Appendix A: Supplementary Table 5). In addition, diosmetin treatment of IL4-stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophages reduced phosphorylation of p-JAK1, p-JAK3, and p-STAT6, whereas JAK1, JAK3, and STAT6 were not affected [52]. In the current study, we found that diosmetin binds to JAK1 and JAK2 with high affinity (-9.2 kcal/mol). The reduction in p-JAK1 and p-STAT6 following diosmetin treatment could be associated with greater binding affinity between diosmetin and JAK1. This association might affect JAK1 phosphorylation directly and STAT6 phosphorylation indirectly.

  Genistein: Genistein treatment showed an anti-inflammatory effect by increasing I-kBα levels and decreasing NF-κB and I-κKα/β levels in LPS-treated RAW 264.7 macrophages [53]. Similar results were detected in LPS-stimulated BV2 microglia [54]. It reduced the secretion of IL1B, IL6, and IL8 by TNF-α-stimulated MH7A cells in a dose-dependent manner. It also prevented TNFα-induced inflammation by inhibiting the phosphorylation of I-κKα/β, NF-κB, IκBα, and AKT [55]. A different study showed that it inhibited AKT phosphorylation and reduced the expression of downstream targets [56]. To the best of our knowledge, no inflammation-based study has shown a relationship between genistein and P300. However, an increase in P300 levels was detected after genistein treatment in lung cancer cells [56]. In the current study, it was demonstrated that genistein might associate with AKT1, I-κKB, and P300 with a low binding energy (-9.2 kcal/mol, -9.5 kcal/mol, and -9.1 kcal/mol, respectively) like reference drugs (Table 1, Figure 3, and Appendix A: Supplementary Table 5). The inhibition of the NF-κB and AKT pathways after genistein treatment might be related to the high binding affinity of genistein for AKT and I-κKB.

  Kaempferol: Kaempferol treatment improved neurological impairments by reducing NF-κB phosphorylation and inhibiting the expression of pro-inflammatory proteins in cerebral ischemia/reperfusion rats [57]. It decreased astrocyte activation, NF-κB expression, and proinflammatory IL1α, TNF-α, and Complement Component 1q (C1q) production in an NPA-induced animal model of Huntington’s disease [58]. NF-κB pathway-dependent inhibition of astrocytes might be related to the high affinity of kaempferol to I-κKB (-9.2 kcal/mol). The binding energy of kaempferol to I-κKB was lower than that of reference drugs except for MLN-0415, AS-602868, and I-KKB 16 (Table 1, Figure 3, and Appendix A: Supplementary Table 5).

  Kaempferol suppresses the proliferation, migration, and invasion of various cancer cell lines and mice by inhibiting the PI3K/AKT pathway. In these studies, total AKT levels did not change, whereas phosphorylated AKT levels decreased [59,60]. The reduction in phosphorylated AKT levels might be related to the distribution of phosphorylation sites on AKT based on kaempferol AKT association. Our results also indicated that kaempferol could bind to AKT1 with high affinity (Table 1).

  A molecular docking study was conducted with an in vivo study, and a dose-dependent inhibitory effect of kaempferol on the JAK/STAT pathway was revealed. It bound JAK1/2 in silico and in vivo [61]. These results increased the reliability of our data because the association energy of kaempferol to JAK2 was -9.1 kcal/mol in our study.

  In this study, the affinity of kaempferol to P300 was the highest among all the studied proteins, with a binding energy of -9.8 kcal/mol, while the reference drugs had higher binding energy except A485 (Table 1 and Appendix A: Supplementary Table 5). To the best of our knowledge, no study has demonstrated an interaction between P300 and kaempferol. Therefore, the relationship between kaempferol and P300 requires further investigation.

  Epicatechin: The role of epicatechin in the AKT, NF-κB, and P300 signaling pathways has been studied in the literature. Epicatechin treatment reduced doxorubicin-induced astrocyte levels and TNF-α, NF-κB, and iNOS mRNA levels in the brain [62]. It inhibited microglial and astrocyte activation by suppressing NF-κB protein expression and stimulating AKT and GSK-3β phosphorylation. It reduced levels of pro-inflammatory markers such as Cyclooxygenase-2 (COX2), TNFα, NF-κB, IFN-γ, IL1β, IL5, and IL6, and promoted the secretion of the anti-inflammatory cytokines IL10 and IL11 in the hippocampi of aging mice [63]. It also suppressed LPS-induced inflammatory responses by reducing proinflammatory cytokine levels such as IL6, IL1β, and TNF-α in BV2 microglia [64]. The literature findings correlated with our data. Molecular docking results showed that epicatechin could bind to AKT1 and I-κKB with high affinity, even though its binding energy was higher than that of the reference drugs (Table 1, Figure 3, and Appendix A: Supplementary Table 5). Epicatechin also had affinity for the P300. However, to the best of our knowledge, no study has established a relationship between epicatechin and P300.

  Bavachin: Bavachin treatment reduced pro-inflammatory mediators and increased anti-inflammatory mediators by attenuating the NF-κB activity of the NLRP3 inflammasome in LPS-induced macrophages and microglia cells [65,66]. It inhibited STAT3 activation and controlled the proliferation of multiple myeloma cells without cytotoxicity in a cancer-based study [67,68]. It also inhibited the activation and phosphorylation of STAT3 to treat inflammatory diseases [68]. To the best of our knowledge, no study has demonstrated an interaction between bavachin and AKT1, AKT2, or P300 expression. The inhibitory effect on the NF-κB and STAT3 pathways might be related to of bavachin’s high binding affinity for JAK2 and I-κKB. Moreover, most reference drugs bind to their specific proteins with a higher affinity than bavachin (Table 1, Figure 3, and Appendix A: Supplementary Table 5).


### Prediction of the Role of Flavonoids on Astrocytes Maturation and
Polarization
  The anti-inflammatory effects of flavonoids have been identified in vitro and in vivo; however, the potential binding partners involved in astrocyte polarization and astrogliosis were unclear. The BBB is a natural protective barrier that prevents the CNS from exposure to toxins and pathogens. It can also limit the entry of most chemical drugs and biopharmaceuticals, resulting in inadequate drug delivery to the brain and low therapeutic efficacy for CNS disorders during pharmacotherapy [69]. However, flavonoids can cross the BBB, making them crucial for their therapeutic effects in neurological diseases [70]. BBB permeability was determined by logPS values obtained from pkCSM. It was observed that despite differences in permeability, nearly all top eleven flavonoids might cross the BBB (Appendix A: Supplementary Table 11). Therefore, they could be used to study astrogliosis in vitro and in vivo. The predictive effect of flavonoid(s) in astrocytes was examined below based on the regulatory role of AKT1, I-κKB, JAK1, JAK2, and P300 on maturation and polarization processes.

  Astrocytes could be activated into two polarization states: A1 and A2 astrocytes [3]. Current studies have suggested that inhibition of A1 and promotion of A2 astrocytes could be potential targets for the treatment of neurodegenerative diseases [7]. A1 astrocytes are thought to be induced by NF-κB signaling, and A2 astrocytes are induced by STAT3-mediated signaling [6]. TNF-α, IL1α, and C1q, secreted by pro-inflammatory microglia, push resting astrocytes into A1 astrocytes. In contrast, IL1β, IL6, and Nuclear Factor I A (NFIA) secretion push resting astrocytes into A2 astrocytes [7].

  The basal level of NF-κB in glia is important for brain health. However, excessive amount of NF-κB is neurotoxic and promotes neurodegeneration [71]. Inhibition of the NF-κB pathway might be a therapeutic strategy for neurodegenerative and inflammatory diseases, and NF-κB or I-κKB inhibitors could be potential drug candidates [72]. For example, Minocycline and Berberine reduce oxidative stress and neuroinflammation in Alzheimer’s disease by inhibiting the NF-κB signaling activation [73,74]. In recent decades, there has been growing interest in the potential of plant-derived natural substances as alternative inhibitors of the NF-κB pathway [64]. Our findings indicated that biochanin A, bavachin, apigenin, epicatechin, wogonin, kaempferol, hispidulin, genistein, farrerol, diosmetin, and daidzein are promising ligands of AKT1 and I-κKB. These interactions might be related to the inhibition of A1 astrocytes to decrease neuroinflammation.

  The JAK/STAT3 pathway is important for A2 astrocytes activation, early brain development, astrogliogenesis, and astrocyte maturation [75]. The A2 astrocyte is present during glial scar formation. Newly proliferating and elongated astrocytes use STAT3 to build their margins [6]. Pharmacological inhibition and genetic invalidation of STAT3 prevent or reduce GFAP mRNA and/or protein levels in astrocytes [34]. Our findings predicted that diosmetin and wogonin had strong affinities for both JAK1 and JAK2, whereas farrerol, kaempferol, apigenin, and bavachin had affinities for JAK2. In addition, bavachin, apigenin, epicatechin, kaempferol, hispidulin, genistein, farrerol, and diosmetin might be associated with P300. Inhibition of the JAK/STAT3 pathway was used as a therapeutic target. For example, AZD1480 (JAK1/JAK2 inhibitor) in Parkinson’s disease [76] and SOCS3 (JAK2 inhibitor) in Alzheimer’s disease [77] reversed the disease phenotype by decreasing neuroinflammation. Similarly, predicted binding of the flavonoids to JAK1, JAK2, and P300 proteins could be a therapeutic target to reduce neuroinflammation. Moreover, JAK1, JAK2, and P300 expression levels increase during astrocyte maturation; these predicted associations might inhibit the formation of new astrocytes during astrogliosis.

  Cell line and animal model-based studies on neurodegenerative disorders showed that flavonoids were beneficial in lowering the risk of disease development and in treating neurological diseases. However, the number of clinical studies on humans is limited due to insufficient bioavailability information and a lack of knowledge about toxicity, safe dose recommendations, and possible side effects of high-dose usage, etc. The application of flavonoids as drugs for the treatment requires further investigation [70]. Our results predicted molecular targets of flavonoids in silico. These results are crucial for researchers to comprehend the cellular mechanisms of flavonoids and speed up the use of flavonoids in clinical research.


### Drug-likeness and Pharmacokinetic Properties of Flavonoids and Drugs
  Although most of the drugs exhibited a higher binding affinity to the target proteins than flavonoids, flavonoids demonstrated better drug-likeness and pharmacokinetic properties as well as lower toxicity. While the selected flavonoids met the criteria of Lipinski Ro5 and had no predicted toxicity (Table 2 and Figure 1), all drugs displayed at least one violation of Lipinski Ro5, except stattic, and most drugs were predicted to cause at least one of the studied toxicities (Appendix A: Supplementary Tables 8 and 10). These results suggest that flavonoids may serve as promising alternatives to traditional medications for targeting specific proteins, potentially leading to more efficacious and less harmful treatments. The enhanced drug-likeness characteristics of flavonoids, along with their reduced toxicity, may lead to better patient outcomes and fewer adverse effects in clinical settings.

  Furthermore, flavonoids and drugs exhibited similar absorption characteristics, including high water solubility and intestinal absorption. However, some flavonoids and drugs had low CaCo2 permeability and were found to be P-glycoprotein substrates, indicating decreased absorption of the compound or drug distribution of the flavonoids, which was predicted to be similar to that of the reference drugs. Some flavonoids and drugs possessed low VDss and Fu values, which suggests limited distribution of the drug to tissues and moderate BBB and CNS permeability (Appendix A: Supplementary Tables 11 and 12). These characteristics may restrict their use in the treatment of brain-related disorders. In addition, an in-silico study was conducted to assess possible drug metabolism and drug-drug interactions involving cytochrome P450 isoforms. The predominant route of metabolism for most drugs is primarily facilitated by CYP3A4 and CYP2D6 [78]. None of the flavonoids were metabolized by CYP2D6, and only biochanin A, bavachin, wogonin, and hispidulin were predicted to be primarily metabolized by CYP3A4. Similar to flavonoids, none of the reference drugs were metabolized by CYP2D6, except for peficitinib and AT7867, but some of them were metabolized by CYP3A4. In addition, bavachin and some of the drugs were found to be inhibitors of CYP3A4, which is the major isoform that may affect the co-administration of drug metabolism. The importance of total clearance in determining drug bioavailability and dosing regimes cannot be overlooked when studying the pharmacokinetics of drug candidates. Unfortunately, the total clearance of flavonoids and reference drugs was low or moderate, and it was also determined that, with a few exceptions, they were not renal OCT2 substrates, which are major transporters in renal clearance. Although flavonoids and reference drugs may be eliminated from the body through other transporters, such as organic anion transporters and multidrug and toxic compound extrusions, it is important to note that they may not be completely cleared and could potentially accumulate, leading to toxic effects.


### Protein-Ligand Interactions
  The prediction of protein-ligand interactions revealed mainly hydrogen bonds and van der Waals interactions between flavonoids or drugs and various amino acid residues of the target proteins (Table 3 and Appendix B: Supplementary Figures 2-7). Hydrogen bonds are important for protein folding, catalytic reactions, and protein-ligand binding and are considered protein-ligand binding facilitators [79]. The stabilization of protein-ligand complexes is significantly influenced by hydrogen-bonding interactions [80]. In the current study, it was predicted that amino acid residues Ser205 and Lys268 in the active site of Akt 1, Lys181 and Lys268 in the binding site of Akt 2, Lys44 in the binding site of I-κKB, Leu 959, Arg 1007, and Gly1020 in the binding site of JAK1, Tyr931 and Arg938 in the binding site of JAK2, and Ser1400 and Gln 1455 were involved in hydrogen binding. The other main interactions were van der Waals interactions between flavonoids or drugs and protein binding, which are weak and non-specific interactions but may still contribute to ligand-protein stability and enhance the overall binding affinity through close contact. Many amino acid residues of the target proteins interacted with flavonoids and drugs to form van der Waals interactions, which may stabilize the ligand in the protein-binding site. Moreover, the contribution of other weak interactions, such as Pi-sigma, Pi-alkyl, and Pi-Pi interactions, was observed between flavonoids, drugs, and proteins. These weak interactions play a crucial role in determining essential chemical and biochemical characteristics. These interactions are instrumental in stabilizing protein structures and can substantially enhance binding affinity. This enhancement may lead to an increase in the overall stability of the complex by facilitating optimal ligand positioning within the binding site [81].


## Conclusion
  Previous studies demonstrated the role of flavonoids in reducing neuroinflammation. However, molecular targeting of flavonoids in astrogliosis has not yet been identified. This study aimed to provide insights into the function of flavonoids in neuroinflammation by using a computational approach. In silico methods are becoming increasingly popular worldwide because of their quick speed and low cost. In the present study, flavonoids were assessed according to Lipinski’s rule of 5, predicted toxicity, pharmacokinetic properties, and docking scores by comparing them to well-known medicines. They were further validated according to in vitro and in vivo research published in the literature. Although most of the drugs exhibited a higher binding affinity to the target proteins than flavonoids, flavonoids demonstrated better drug-likeness and pharmacokinetic properties, as well as lower toxicity. Our findings predicted that biochanin A, bavachin, apigenin, epicatechin, wogonin, kaempferol, hispidulin, genistein, farrerol, diosmetin, and daidzein have higher binding affinities to AKT1 and I-κKB. Diosmetin and wogonin displayed higher affinities for both JAK1 and JAK2, whereas farrerol, kaempferol, apigenin, and bavachin have higher affinities specifically for JAK2. Furthermore, bavachin, apigenin, epicatechin, kaempferol, hispidulin, genistein, farrerol, and diosmetin were predicted to have strong binding ability to P300. The Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion, and Toxicity (ADMET) properties of flavonoids were comparable to those of the existing drugs. This study adds to ongoing investigations into the design of flavonoid-based therapies for astrogliosis. It was shown that bavachin and farrerol are the most effective candidates for future studies due to their anti-inflammatory effects and the lowest binding energies. Notably, this research outlines a method for discovering new potential treatments with benefits for the design of flavonoid-based therapies for astrogliosis.
