# CD55-expressing myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) drive cancer immunoevasion


## Background
  Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) play a pivotal role in shaping the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, thereby driving tumor progression and contributing to resistance to therapy. The surface molecules expressed on MDSCs serve not only as identification markers but also as critical regulators of their immunosuppressive functions. Identifying surface markers that are both widely expressed and functionally significant on MDSCs is essential for the development of effective immunomodulatory therapies.


## Methods
  A comprehensive analysis of publicly available single-cell RNA-seq datasets across eight cancer types was conducted to identify conserved surface markers on MDSCs. CD55 was identified as a promising candidate and subsequently validated in both human patient samples and murine tumor models. Mechanistic investigations employed CD55 knockout and myeloid-specific knockout mice, bone marrow transplantation, DNA pulldown assays, chromatin immunoprecipitation, luciferase reporter assays, co-immunoprecipitation, immunoblotting, immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry, and RNA sequencing. To evaluate the therapeutic potential of targeting CD55, molecular docking analyses, small-molecule inhibitor screening, and surface plasmon resonance imaging-based competitive binding assays were conducted.


## Results
  CD55 was consistently overexpressed on MDSCs across multiple cancer types, with its expression correlating with poor patient prognosis. Genetic depletion of CD55 significantly inhibited tumor growth and enhanced antitumor immunity by reducing MDSCs infiltration and boosting CD8+ T cell activity. Mechanistically, CD55 was found to promote the phosphorylation of glycogen phosphorylase (PYGL), thereby driving glycolysis and maintaining the immunosuppressive phenotype of MDSCs. Pharmacological inhibition of the CD55-PYGL axis with tomatidine effectively disrupted this interaction, alleviated MDSCs-mediated immunosuppression, and markedly improved the efficacy of anti-programmed cell death protein-1 immunotherapy. Furthermore, CD55 expression in MDSCs was found to be transcriptionally regulated by granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor and interleukin-6 through activation of the transcription factor C/EBPβ.


## Conclusion
  This study identified CD55 as a key regulator of MDSCs-mediated tumor immunosuppression via metabolic reprogramming. Targeting CD55 on MDSCs offers a promising therapeutic approach to overcoming immunosuppression and enhancing the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy.


## Introduction
  The immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) represents a major obstacle to the success of cancer immunotherapy.1 Immunosuppressive cell populations—including myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), regulatory T cells (Tregs), and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs)—play central roles in establishing and maintaining this suppressive microenvironment.24 Among these, MDSCs are widely regarded as dominant drivers of sustained immunosuppression.5 In tumors, the chronic release of inflammatory mediators and growth factors, together with hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, acidosis, and endoplasmic reticulum stress, skews bone marrow (BM) myelopoiesis and further activates MDSCs within the tumor niche.6 7 This results in a persistent spatiotemporal “supply” of MDSCs and reinforces their immunosuppressive phenotype. Mechanistically, MDSCs blunt antitumor immunity through multiple parallel pathways,812 including: (1) arginase-1 (Arg1)-mediated and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS)-mediated metabolic reprogramming, accompanied by the generation of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (eg, ROS and NO), which can impair T cell function; (2) engagement of immune checkpoint pathways such as programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1)/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), leading to T cell exhaustion; (3) secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines, including interleukin (IL)-10 and TGF-β, which can suppress T cell activity; (4) induction and expansion of Tregs; (5) inhibition of dendritic cell maturation and antigen presentation; and (6) differentiating into TAMs and promoting their polarization. Collectively, these interconnected mechanisms establish a self-reinforcing, myeloid-centered suppressive network within the TME that sustains immune evasion and tumor progression. Clinically, MDSC accumulation correlates with tumor burden, metastasis, and poor prognosis, and has been implicated in both primary and acquired resistance to anticancer therapies, including immunotherapy.1315 These underscore that MDSCs are key orchestrators of the immunosuppressive microenvironment. Accordingly, targeting MDSCs is considered a promising strategy to remodel the immunosuppressive TME and improve therapeutic outcomes. However, the development of MDSC-targeted therapies remains challenging due to phenotypic overlap with beneficial myeloid populations and substantial heterogeneity across tumor types.

  Surface markers on MDSCs serve not only as biomarkers for their identification but also as critical regulators of their immunosuppressive functions. A recent study by Luo et al used in vivo CRISPR-Cas9 screening to identify CD300ld, a surface receptor uniquely expressed on polymorphonuclear MDSCs (PMN-MDSCs).16 CD300ld regulates both the recruitment and immunosuppressive functions of MDSCs through the STAT3-S100A8/A9 signaling pathway, and its blockade significantly reduces tumor infiltration and reverses immunosuppressive activity, demonstrating synergy when combined with anti-PD-1 therapy. Other surface molecules, including LOX-1,17 CD84,18 and HCAR1,19 have also been implicated in the functional regulation of MDSCs, further highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting MDSC surface markers to overcome TME-mediated immunosuppression. However, these molecules are primarily expressed on the PMN-MDSC subset rather than on the broader MDSC population, making the identification of key regulatory surface molecules on total MDSCs a critical area for further exploration.

  In this study, through comprehensive analysis of publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets across multiple cancer types, CD55, a complement regulatory protein, was identified as a key surface marker of tumor-associated MDSCs. Initially characterized as a decay-accelerating factor (DAF) involved in complement regulation, CD55 has since been recognized for its broader roles in immune modulation.20 Upregulation of CD55 has been shown to promote tumor cell survival, enhance metastatic potential, and increase invasiveness in various malignancies.2123 CD55 sustains the immunosuppressive function of MDSCs by activating glycogen phosphorylase (PYGL), which promotes glycolytic reprogramming and enhances their suppressive capacity. This study not only advances our understanding of MDSC biology but also positions CD55 as a promising therapeutic target for mitigating immunosuppression within the TME.


## Materials and methods

### Reagents and antibodies
  Reagents and antibodies used in this research are detailed in online supplemental table S1.


### Acquisition of scRNA-seq data and screening of key surface membrane markers of MDSCs
  ScRNA-seq datasets from eight tumor types—colorectal cancer, gastric cancer, liver cancer, lung cancer, prostate cancer, breast cancer, cervical cancer, and ovarian cancer—were obtained from publicly available sources (online supplemental table S2).

  The raw sequencing data underwent standard preprocessing, including quality control, normalization, and batch correction. Dimensionality reduction was performed using principal component analysis, followed by uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP). Cell populations were annotated based on the expression of canonical marker genes (online supplemental table 3).

  Due to the heterogeneity of MDSCs and the lack of a universally accepted method for identifying MDSCs in single-cell sequencing, we employed two approaches—scoring and classic markers—to identify MDSCs within myeloid cells.

  Method 1: We used a previously published MDSCs gene signature (IL1B, CCR1, CXCL2, GRINA, IER3)24 to calculate MDSCs scores for myeloid cells across different cancer types using the AddModuleScore function in the Seurat package. Among the myeloid populations, membrane protein-coding genes (from GO:0016020) were screened by evaluating their correlation with the MDSCs score. Genes showing a significant positive correlation (adj. p<0.05) were considered MDSCs-associated membrane proteins.

  Method 2: We performed dimensionality reduction on the myeloid cells and used markers HLA-DRlow, ITGAM, CD33, S100A8, and S100A9 to identify MDSC clusters within the myeloid population. The FindAllMarkers algorithm was used to identify membrane proteins that were significantly upregulated in MDSC clusters (adj. p<0.05, avg_log2|FC|>0.25).

  To identify conserved surface membrane markers across cancer types, a Venn analysis was respectively performed on the positively correlated genes or significantly upregulated genes from each cancer type.


### Prognostic analysis
  To assess the prognostic significance of the identified MDSC-associated surface markers, survival analysis was performed using the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) online platform (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/), which integrates RNA sequencing expression data and clinical information from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA).

  For each gene, patients from the eight cancer types were stratified into high-expression and low-expression groups based on median expression levels. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated, and overall survival differences were analyzed using the log-rank test.


### Patient population
  Patient samples were obtained from treatment-naive individuals diagnosed with colorectal cancer, gastric cancer, liver cancer, lung cancer, prostate cancer, breast cancer, cervical cancer, or ovarian cancer at the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine, China, between December 2023 and July 2025. Clinical characteristics of the enrolled patients are summarized in online supplemental table S5.

  Peripheral blood samples (2 mL) were collected from each participant. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells were isolated from fresh blood using Ficoll-Hypaque density gradient centrifugation and subsequently analyzed by flow cytometry.

  Human colorectal cancer tissues and matched adjacent non-tumorous tissues (online supplemental table S6) were obtained from patients undergoing colonoscopic biopsy or surgical resection. The tissues were fixed and processed for immunofluorescence staining.


### Animal studies
  Wild-type (WT) C57BL/6J mice, Cd55 knockout (Cd55−/−) mice, and Cd55fl/fl mice were obtained from Cyagen Biosciences. To generate mice with conditional deletion of the Cd55 gene in myeloid cells, Cd55fl/fl mice were crossed with Lyz2-cre mice to produce Cd55fl/flLyz2-cre mice.

  MC38 and ID8 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco). Once the cells reached optimal viability (>90%), they were trypsinized, resuspended, and mixed 1:1 with Matrigel (Corning) to a final concentration of 1×106 cells/mL. 6–8 weeks old C57BL/6 mice were subcutaneously injected with 100 µL of the cell suspension (containing 1×10⁵ cells) into the right flank. Tumor growth was monitored every 3 days using digital calipers, with tumor volume calculated as V = (length × width2)/2.

  Tomatidine (TargetMol) was administered via intraperitoneal injection. For the toxicity assessment, tomatidine was given to tumor-bearing mice for 21 days at three different doses: low dose (12.5 mg/kg), medium dose (25 mg/kg), and high dose (50 mg/kg). In the combination experiment with anti-PD-1, a dose of 25 mg/kg was administered once daily for 21 days. Tomatidine was dissolved in corn oil, and control mice received an equivalent volume of vehicle (placebo).

  Anti-Gr1 antibody (Bio X Cell) was administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 200 µg per mouse every 3 days. Isotype control IgG (rat IgG2b, Bio X Cell) was used at the same dose and schedule. Anti-PD-1 antibody (Bio X Cell) was diluted in 200 µL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (Biosharp) and administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 200 µg per injection every 3 days, with control groups receiving an equivalent volume of isotype IgG (rat IgG2a, Bio X Cell).

  All mice were housed in the institutional animal facility under controlled environmental conditions: a 12-hour light/dark cycle, ambient temperature of 22.5°C, and relative humidity of 55%. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine (Approval No. 20250606005).


### Bone Marrow Transplantation
  Two distinct BM transplantation (BMT) strategies were employed to investigate the role of CD55 in tumor progression.


#### Single donor BMT
  BM cells were isolated from the femurs and tibias of WT or Cd55−/− mice. Recipient WT mice (CD45.1 background) were lethally irradiated with a total dose of 9.5 Gy (MultiRad 225, Faxitron), delivered in two fractions 3 hours apart to minimize gastrointestinal toxicity. Within 24 hours of irradiation, the mice were reconstituted by intravenous injection of 5×106 donor BM cells through the tail vein. Post-transplantation, mice were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions and given antibiotic-containing drinking water (containing 10 mg/mL trimethoprim and sulfamethoxazole, Sigma) for 2 weeks. 6 weeks after transplantation, successful hematopoietic reconstitution was confirmed by flow cytometry using CD45.1 and CD45.2 markers. Tumor challenge was then performed via subcutaneous inoculation of MC38 or ID8 cells.


#### Mixed chimera BMT
  To generate mixed BM chimeric mice, BM cells were isolated from WT (CD45.1) and Cd55−/− (CD45.2) donor mice, counted, and mixed at a 1:1 ratio. Recipient mice (CD45.2 background) were lethally irradiated with a total dose of 9.5 Gy (split into two doses, MultiRad 225, Faxitron) and intravenously injected with 5×106 mixed BM cells suspended in sterile PBS (Biosharp). Mice were maintained under SPF conditions and received antibiotic-supplemented water (containing 10 mg/mL trimethoprim and sulfamethoxazole, Sigma) for 2 weeks. 6 weeks post-transplantation, chimerism and engraftment efficiency were evaluated by flow cytometry using CD45.1 and CD45.2 markers. Tumor challenge was then conducted by subcutaneous injection of MC38 or ID8 cells.


### Preparation of mouse spleen, tumor, and bone marrow single-cell suspensions
  Spleens were harvested and mechanically dissociated by passing them through a 70 µm cell strainer (Biosharp). Following centrifugation, the splenic cell suspension was treated with red blood cell lysis buffer (Solarbio) to obtain a single-cell suspension.

  Tumor tissues were sectioned and digested in 2 mL of RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco) containing 1 mg/mL Collagenase IV (Biosharp) and 0.1 mg/mL DNase I (Sigma) at 37 °C for 3 hours. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 70 µm cell strainer (Biosharp), subjected to red blood cell lysis, and processed into a single-cell suspension.

  BM was harvested from the tibiae and femurs of mice, flushed with ice-cold RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 2% FBS (Gibco). After red blood cell lysis, the suspension was filtered through a 70 µm cell strainer (Biosharp) and processed into a single-cell suspension.


### Induction of bone marrow cells into MDSCs
  BM cells were isolated from the tibias and femurs of C57BL/6 mice under sterile conditions. The BM cavities were flushed with PBS (Biosharp), and the collected cells were centrifuged. Red blood cells were lysed using ACK lysis buffer (Solarbio). Subsequently, 2×106 BM cells were seeded into 1 mL of complete RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco). Two common cytokine combinations are used for the in vitro induction of MDSCs: granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF)+IL-6 and GM-CSF+G CSF. Previous studies have reported that the GM-CSF+IL-6 combination yields relatively balanced proportions of PMN-MDSCs and monocytic MDSC (M-MDSC) subsets, whereas induction with GM-CSF+G CSF predominantly generates PMN-MDSCs.25 Since the present study focused on total MDSCs, including both PMN-MDSCs and M-MDSCs, and considering that MDSCs induced by GM-CSF+IL-6 exhibit stronger immunosuppressive activity compared with those generated by GM-CSF+G CSF,25 26 the GM-CSF+IL-6 system was adopted for in vitro induction. Recombinant murine GM-CSF and IL-6 (both from PeproTech, 40 ng/mL each) were added to the culture medium, and the cells were incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ for 96 hours. Thereafter, experimental treatments were applied according to the study design.


### Lentiviral transduction of BM-derived MDSCs
  To generate C/EBPβ-knockdown MDSCs, short hairpin RNA (shRNA) sequences targeting the Cebpb gene were cloned into the pLKO.1-puro vector. The sequences used were as follows: shRNA-1: CTGACGCAACACACGTGTAACCTCGAGGTTACACGTGTGTTGCGTCAG; shRNA-2: CACCCTGCGGAACTTGTTCAACTCGAGTTGAACAAGTTCCGCAGGGTG; Control shRNA: CCTAAGGTTAAGTCGCCCTCGCTCGAGCGAGGGCGACTTAACCTTAGG The pLKO.1-puro plasmid was linearized using AgeI and EcoRI restriction enzymes and purified via 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. The target sequences and linearized plasmids were then ligated using T4 DNA ligase according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Sangon Biotech).

  Lentiviruses were generated by cotransfecting HEK293T cells with the transfer vector, packaging plasmid psPAX2, and envelope plasmid pMD2.G using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Viral supernatants were collected at 48 and 72 hours post-transfection, filtered through a 0.45 µm filter, and concentrated using PEG6000 precipitation followed by differential centrifugation.

  For transduction, BM-MDSCs were exposed to lentiviral particles containing the mouse Cebpb shRNA for 48 hours. Postinfection, cells were harvested, and C/EBPβ-knockdown efficiency was assessed by flow cytometry.


### CD8+ T cell suppression assay
  CD8+ T cells were isolated from the spleens of naïve mice using the EasySep Mouse CD8+ T Cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies) and labeled with 5 µM carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE) (BioLegend) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Labeled CD8+ T cells (2×10⁵ cells per well) were cocultured with either WT or Cd55−/− MDSCs at different ratios (CD8+ T cells:MDSCs=2:1, 4:1, or 8:1) in 96-well round-bottom plates. Cells were stimulated with plate-bound anti-CD3 (2 µg/mL, BioLegend) and soluble anti-CD28 (1 µg/mL, BioLegend) antibodies. After 72 hours of incubation at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere, CD8+ T cell proliferation (measured by CFSE dilution) and intracellular expression of interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) were analyzed by flow cytometry. Data were gated on CD8+ T cells to assess both proliferative capacity and effector cytokine production.


### Immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry
  Immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS) was performed to identify CD55-interacting proteins. BM-derived MDSCs were lysed in IP lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche). Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 14,000×g for 15 min at 4°C, and total protein concentrations were quantified using a BCA protein assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

  Equal amounts of protein were incubated overnight at 4°C with an anti-CD55 antibody (Santa Cruz) or isotype control IgG (Proteintech). Protein-antibody complexes were captured using Protein G magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 2 hours at 4°C. Beads were thoroughly washed with lysis buffer to remove non-specific proteins.

  Immunoprecipitated proteins were eluted, reduced, alkylated, and digested on-bead with trypsin (Promega). The resulting peptides were desalted and analyzed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) using the UltiMate 3000 RSLCnano system coupled to a Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

  Raw MS data were processed using MaxQuant software (V.2.2.0.0, Max Planck Institute), with peptide and protein identification carried out via the built-in Andromeda search engine. Search results were filtered at a 1% false discovery rate at both the peptide and protein levels. Differential protein enrichment between CD55 IP and IgG control samples was assessed statistically. Proteins with a log2 |fold change|>1 and an adjusted p value<0.05 were considered significantly enriched.


### RNA-seq analysis
  Total RNA was extracted from MDSCs using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) and quantified with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. RNA integrity was assessed using a Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent), and samples with an RNA integrity number (RIN) >7.0 were selected for library preparation. Poly(A) messenger RNA (mRNA) was enriched using Dynabeads Oligo(dT)25 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), fragmented at 94°C for 5–7 min, and reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using SuperScript II (Invitrogen). Second-strand synthesis incorporated dUTP, followed by adapter ligation and size selection with AMPure XP beads. After uracil-DNA glycosylase (UDG) treatment, libraries were PCR-amplified and sequenced (2×150 bp) on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (LC-Bio). Clean reads were filtered using fastp and mapped to the Mus musculus (GRCm38) with HISAT2. Transcript assembly and quantification were performed using StringTie, and differential expression analysis was conducted with edgeR. Genes with a log2 |fold change|>1 and adjusted p<0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed.


### Screening of small-molecule inhibitors
  The amino acid sequences of murine CD55 and PYGL were retrieved from the UniProt database and submitted to the AlphaFold3 online platform for protein-protein complex prediction using the multimer mode. The resulting CD55-PYGL complex structure was visualized and analyzed in PyMOL (V.2.5.2, Schrödinger, LLC), focusing on the spatial arrangement at the interface and key molecular interactions, including hydrogen bonds and electrostatic forces. Potential core binding residues were identified based on the structural analysis. To assess druggability, SiteMap (Schrödinger, LLC) was used to evaluate the interaction interface and identify potential ligand-binding pockets within the CD55-PYGL complex. For small-molecule inhibitors capable of disrupting the CD55-PYGL interaction, structure-based virtual screening was conducted using AutoDock Vina (V.1.2.3, The Scripps Research Institute). A diverse compound library of approximately 25,031 molecules was compiled from bioactive compound libraries (TargetMol), Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved drug libraries, and natural product collections. A multistage molecular docking strategy was employed, including high-throughput screening (HTS) for initial filtering, standard precision (SP) docking for intermediate refinement, and extra precision (XP) docking for final selection and pose optimization. Based on docking scores, binding poses, and structural diversity, 10 candidate compounds were selected for further biological evaluation.


### Surface Plasmon Resonance imaging-based competitive binding assay
  Experiments were performed on a Plexera HT system (Plexera LLC). Purified murine CD55 protein (TargetMol) at various concentrations (320, 160, 80, 40, 20, 10, and 5 nM) was preincubated with each test compound (10 µM, dissolved in DMSO, Sigma) and then injected over a sensor chip surface on which murine PYGL protein had been immobilized using standard amine coupling procedures. PBS (pH 7.4, Biosharp) containing 1% DMSO (Sigma) was used as the running buffer for all immobilization and washing steps. Each mixture was injected at a flow rate of 20 µL/min at 25°C, and the association phase was recorded for 100 s. Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) responses were measured in real time and background-corrected using reference areas on the chip. Experimental data were analyzed using Plexera Data Analysis software (Plexera LLC) to assess compound-mediated inhibition of the CD55-PYGL interaction.


### DNA pulldown
  The Cd55 promoter sequence was PCR-amplified and biotin-labeled, then incubated with Pierce Streptavidin Magnetic Beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The beads were subsequently incubated with nuclear protein extracts isolated from either fresh murine BM cells or BM-derived MDSCs at 4°C overnight with gentle agitation. After magnetic separation and extensive washing, specifically bound proteins were eluted, separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), and subjected to silver staining and MS (LC-MS/MS) analysis.


### Chromatin immunoprecipitation assay
  Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) was performed on BM-derived MDSCs following a standard protocol. Cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde (Sigma), lysed, and the chromatin was sheared by sonication (3 s ON/9 s OFF, for 20 cycles) to generate DNA fragments of appropriate size. The lysates were incubated overnight at 4°C with an anti-C/EBPβ antibody (Abcam) for the experimental group, or with control IgG (Abcam) for the negative control. Immune complexes were captured using Protein G agarose beads (Millipore), followed by sequential washing, elution, and reversal of cross-links. DNA was purified using a standard phenol-chloroform or spin-column-based method. Purified DNA was analyzed by quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) using primers specific for predicted C/EBPβ-binding sites within the CD55 promoter region (−1525 to −1515 bp, −1092 to −1081 bp, and −856 to −845 bp). Primer sequences are provided in online supplemental table S1.


### Luciferase assay
  To assess CD55 promoter activity and the regulatory effect of C/EBPβ, a luciferase reporter assay was performed in BM-derived MDSCs. The WT CD55 promoter region and three mutant constructs (mut1, mut2, and mut3, each containing site-specific mutations in predicted C/EBPβ binding motifs) were cloned into the pGL4.10[luc2] vector (Promega) upstream of the firefly luciferase gene. BM-derived MDSCs were transfected with the indicated CD55 promoter-luciferase constructs along with either C/EBPβ expression plasmid or empty vector control (2 µg). A Renilla luciferase plasmid (pRL-TK) (100 ng) was cotransfected as an internal control to normalize transfection efficiency. 36 hours post-transfection, cells were lysed and luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) on a luminometer. Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity, and the data were expressed as relative promoter activity.


### Co-immunoprecipitation and immunoblotting
  MDSCs were lysed using IP lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche). Cell lysates were centrifuged at 14,000×g for 15 min at 4°C to remove debris, and protein concentrations were quantified using the BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

  For co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP), equal amounts of total protein (500 µg) were incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-CD55 antibody (Santa Cruz), anti-PYGL antibody (Abcam), or control IgG (isotype-matched negative control, Proteintech). The mixture was then incubated with Protein A/G magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 2 hours at 4°C with gentle rotation. The beads were thoroughly washed with lysis buffer and resuspended in SDS loading buffer by boiling for 5 min.

  Eluted proteins and input lysates were subjected to SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk in Tris Buffered Saline with Tween 20 (TBST) and incubated with primary antibodies against CD55 and PYGL overnight at 4°C, followed by horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (CST). Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents and imaged using a chemiluminescence detection system (Bio-Rad). All antibodies used in Co-IP are listed in online supplemental table S1.


### Immunofluorescence staining
  Human colorectal cancer tissues, adjacent normal tissues, and mouse tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Solarbio), embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at a thickness of 4 µm. For immunofluorescence staining, paraffin sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval, followed by blocking of non-specific binding sites. Sections were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. The following day, HRP-CST was applied and incubated at room temperature for 50 min, followed by tyramide signal amplification for multiplex fluorescent labeling. Between each round of staining, antibody stripping and repeated antigen retrieval were performed before applying the next primary antibody. Cell nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and slides were mounted using an anti-fade mounting medium. Fluorescent images were captured using an Olympus FV1000 confocal laser-scanning microscope and analyzed with ImageJ software.


### H&E staining
  The hearts, livers, spleens, lungs, and kidneys from mice were collected and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin. After fixation, the tissues were dehydrated through a graded series of alcohol solutions. Following dehydration, the tissues were embedded in paraffin and sectioned into 4-μm-thick slices. These sections were stained with H&E and subsequently examined under a microscope for histological evaluation.


### Seahorse
  Glycolytic function was assessed using the Seahorse XF Analyzer (Agilent Technologies) by measuring the proton efflux rate (PER), which reflects extracellular acidification. After receiving the indicated treatments, 1.5×10⁵ MDSCs were seeded into XF cell culture microplates per well. Prior to the assay, the growth medium was replaced with Seahorse XF assay medium (pH 7.4) supplemented with 2 mM glutamine, and cells were incubated in a CO2-free incubator at 37°C for 1 hour. PER was measured under basal conditions and following sequential injections of 0.5 µM rotenone/antimycin A (R/A) and 50 mM 2-deoxyglucose to assess basal glycolysis and compensatory glycolysis (in response to R/A).


### Western blotting
  Protein samples were lysed and extracted using RIPA buffer (Beyotime) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche). Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein were mixed with 5×loading buffer, denatured at 100°C for 5 min, and separated by SDS-PAGE. Proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore), which were blocked with QuickBlock blocking buffer (Beyotime) for 1 hour at room temperature. Membranes were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature, followed by additional TBST washes. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL detection kit (4ABiotech) and imaged with a high-sensitivity chemiluminescence imaging system (Bio-Rad). Densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ software. All antibodies used in Western blot (WB) analysis are listed in online supplemental table S1.


### Quantitative real-time PCR
  Total RNA was extracted from cells using the EZ-press RNA Purification Kit (EZBioscience) according to the manufacturer’s protocols, followed by reverse transcription into cDNA using 4×Reverse Transcription Master Mix (EZBioscience). qPCR was then performed with the synthesized cDNA using 2×SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (EZBioscience).


### ELISA
  The concentrations of complement components C3a and C5a in tumor tissues were measured using the Mouse C3a ELISA Kit (Novus Biologicals) and Mouse C5a ELISA Kit (MyBioSource), following the manufacturer’s instructions.


### Flow cytometry analysis
  For myeloid and lymphoid cell analysis, all samples were incubated with anti-CD16/32 (BD Pharmingen) at 4°C for 30 min to block Fc receptors and then stained with antibodies against cell surface antigens. Gating strategies were as follows: human samples: MDSCs: HLA-DR−/lowCD11b+CD33+, PMN-MDSCs: HLA-DR−/lowCD11b+CD33+CD14−CD15+, M-MDSCs: HLA-DR−/lowCD11b+CD33+CD14+CD15−; mouse samples: macrophages: CD45+F4/80+CD11b+, MDSCs: CD45+CD11b+Gr-1+, M-MDSCs/monocytes: CD45+CD11b+Ly6G−Ly6C+, PMN-MDSCs/neutrophils: CD45+CD11b+Ly6G+Ly6Clo, DCs: CD45+MHC-II+CD11c+, CD3+ T cells: CD45+CD3+, CD4+ T cells: CD45+CD3+CD4+, CD8+ T cells: CD45+CD3+CD8+, B cells: CD45+CD3−CD19+, natural killer (NK) cells: CD45+CD3−CD49b+.

  To detect intracellular TNF-α and IFN-γ, cells were treated with a cell stimulation cocktail (supplemented with the protein transport inhibitor, TONBO). The cells were processed using a fixation and permeabilization kit (eBioscience) and stained with antibodies specific for intracellular antigens. For intracellular C/EBPβ staining, cells were processed using the same fixation and permeabilization kit (eBioscience) and stained with corresponding antibodies.

  For hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell analysis, BM was harvested from tibiae and femurs of mice and flushed with ice-cold RPMI 1640 + 2% FBS to generate a single-cell suspension. After red blood cell lysis with ACK buffer and filtration through a 300-mesh nylon net, cells were resuspended in fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) buffer (PBS+2% FBS), counted by trypan blue exclusion, and adjusted to 1×10⁸ cells/mL. For staining, 100 µL (1×10⁷ cells) was used per flow cytometry tube.

  Hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) were identified by multiparameter flow cytometry using a 9-color antibody panel. BM mononuclear cells were first stained with Zombie Yellow viability dye to exclude dead cells, followed by Fc receptor blocking with anti-CD16/32. Cells were then incubated with a cocktail of fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies targeting the following surface markers: Lineage (Lin) markers (CD3, CD45R/B220, CD11b, TER-119, Gr-1), Sca-1, c-Kit, Flt3/CD135, CD34, CD127/IL-7Rα, and CD27.

  Gating strategies were applied to define various HSPC populations. The enriched HSPC population was identified as LSK cells (Lin−Sca-1+c-Kit+). Within the LSK compartment, long-term hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) were defined as Lin−Sca-1+c-Kit+CD135−CD34−, short-term HSCs as Lin−Sca-1+c-Kit+CD135−CD34+, and multipotent progenitors as Lin−Sca-1+c-Kit+CD135+CD34+. Myeloid progenitors were identified within the Lin−Sca-1−c-Kit+ fraction, with common myeloid progenitors defined as FcγRˡᵒCD34+, granulocyte-macrophage progenitors as FcγRʰⁱCD34+, and megakaryocyte-erythroid progenitors as FcγR−CD34−. Common lymphoid progenitors were defined as Lin−Sca-1ˡᵒc-KitˡᵒCD135+CD127+.

  All flow cytometry antibodies used in this study are listed in online supplemental table S1. Samples were analyzed on a BD LSRFortessa (BD Biosciences) using BD FACSDiva software (V.9.0, BD Biosciences). Data analysis was performed using FlowJo V.10 software (Tree Star).


### Statistical analysis
  Statistical analysis and graphical visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism V.9.0. Differences between two groups were assessed using either Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test, depending on the distribution of the data. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s post hoc test or the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc test was used, based on the distribution and variance characteristics of the dataset. Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation analysis was conducted to evaluate statistical correlations, depending on whether the data met the assumptions of normality. Survival analyses were performed using the log-rank test. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significance levels are indicated in the figures as follows: *p<0.05, **p<0.01, ***p<0.005, ****p<0.0001, and ns for non-significant differences. All data are presented as mean±SEM.


## Results

### CD55 is consistently upregulated on MDSCs across multiple cancer types and correlates with poor prognosis
  To identify key surface molecules on MDSCs in cancer, scRNA-seq datasets from eight tumor types—colorectal, gastric, liver, lung, prostate, breast, cervical, and ovarian cancers—were collected and subjected to dimensionality reduction and cell-type annotation. This analysis enabled the identification of major cellular populations within each cancer type, including myeloid cells (online supplemental figure S1A–H). Given the inherent challenges in accurately clustering MDSCs, two complementary strategies were employed to identify MDSCs within the myeloid compartment: (1) scoring cells based on established MDSCs gene signatures (figure 1A–H) and (2) defining MDSCs using canonical marker genes (online supplemental figure S2A–H and online supplemental table S3). Based on these two identification approaches, membrane surface proteins that were both positively correlated with MDSC scores and significantly upregulated in MDSCs were screened. CD55 was consistently identified by both methods (online supplemental figure S3A). UMAP visualization of myeloid cells across all eight cancer types revealed a strong overlap between CD55 expression and high MDSC scores (figure 1A–H). Violin plots further demonstrated that CD55 was expressed at higher levels in MDSCs compared with other myeloid cell populations (online supplemental figure S3B). Analysis of integrated TCGA datasets from these eight cancers showed that high CD55 expression was associated with poor patient prognosis (online supplemental figure S3C). Accordingly, CD55 was selected for further investigation. Peripheral blood samples from patients with these cancers were collected to assess the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of CD55 on MDSCs, as well as the proportion of CD55-positive MDSCs and their subsets (online supplemental figure S4). CD55 expression was markedly upregulated on MDSCs and their subpopulations across all cancer types examined, with the most pronounced increase observed in colorectal cancer, followed by ovarian cancer (figure 1I–K; online supplemental figure S5). Consistently, the proportion of CD55-positive MDSCs and their subsets exhibited a similar pattern (figure 1I–K; online supplemental figure S5). Previous studies have reported that progesterone and estradiol can upregulate CD55 expression in endometrial-related cells.2729 Accordingly, we compared the MFI of CD55 on MDSCs, as well as the proportions of CD55-positive MDSCs and their subpopulations, between male and female individuals among healthy donors and patients with colon and rectal, gastric, liver, and lung cancers. No significant sex-associated differences were observed (online supplemental figure S6A,B). Immunofluorescence analysis of colorectal cancer tissues and matched adjacent normal tissues revealed a significant increase in CD33+CD55+ cells within tumor tissues (figure 1L, online supplemental table S6). Although CD55 expression has been reported on B cells,3032 no significant difference in the proportion of CD19+CD55+ cells was detected between colorectal cancer tissues and adjacent normal tissues (online supplemental figure S7). Collectively, these findings indicate that CD55 is consistently upregulated on MDSCs in patients with cancer.


### CD55 deficiency suppresses tumor progression and alleviates the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment in an MDSCs-dependent manner
  To determine which cell types predominantly express CD55 during tumor progression, we first evaluated changes in CD55 expression across multiple immune cell populations under tumor-bearing conditions (online supplemental figure S8A). CD55 expression was markedly upregulated on MDSCs in both the spleens and tumor tissues of MC38-bearing and ID8-bearing mice (online supplemental figure S8B,C). Moreover, as tumors enlarged, CD55 expression in the TME progressively increased on MDSCs and their subsets (online supplemental figure S9A–C). For B cells, compared with naïve spleens, CD55 expression was increased in the spleens of tumor-bearing mice whereas no marked elevation was detected in tumor tissues (online supplemental figure S8B,C). Notably, within the TME, B-cell CD55 expression showed only a slight upward trend as tumors grew (online supplemental figure S9A–C). Collectively, these findings indicate that CD55 is primarily upregulated on MDSCs during tumor progression.

  Next, to investigate how CD55 deficiency affects tumor development and the immune microenvironment, Cd55−/− mice were generated (online supplemental figure S10A–C). Before tumor inoculation, we examined immune cell homeostasis under normal physiological conditions. The proportions of HSCs and progenitor cells in Cd55−/− mice were comparable to those in WT mice (online supplemental figure S10D), suggesting that CD55 deletion does not impair HSCs or progenitor compartments. In Cd55−/− mice, the frequencies of CD3+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and neutrophils were increased in both the spleen and BM, whereas B-cell proportions were reduced in the BM (online supplemental figure S10D,E). Under tumor-bearing conditions, CD55 deficiency significantly inhibited tumor progression, as reflected by reduced tumor growth and tumor weight in MC38-bearing and ID8-bearing Cd55−/− mice relative to WT controls (figure 2A–C; online supplemental figure S11A–C). Flow cytometric analysis revealed a marked reduction in MDSCs and their subsets in CD55-knockout mice, accompanied by increased proportions of CD3+, CD4+, and CD8+ T cells, as well as NK cells, in both the spleen and tumors, compared with WT controls (figure 2D,E; online supplemental figure S11D, E). Given the remarkable capacity of MDSCs to suppress T-cell responses, immunofluorescence analysis was performed to further assess their alterations within tumors. Cd55−/− tumors exhibited significantly fewer Gr-1+ cells (an MDSC marker) and markedly increased infiltration of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the TME (figure 2F; online supplemental figure S11F). These results indicate that CD55 deletion not only suppresses tumor growth but also alleviates the immunosuppressive TME, which is likely mediated predominantly by MDSCs.

  To determine whether MDSCs are responsible for the tumor-suppressive and immunomodulatory effects of CD55 deficiency, we performed BMT to reconstitute recipient immune systems, followed by tumor inoculation. Tumor growth in MC38 and ID8-bearing mice was significantly reduced in those reconstituted with Cd55−/− BM compared with mice receiving WT BM (figure 3A, online supplemental figure S12A). In parallel, tumor-bearing mice were treated with anti-Gr-1 antibodies to deplete MDSCs. MDSC depletion strongly suppressed tumor growth in WT tumor-bearing mice compared with isotype controls, but produced no significant additional effect in Cd55−/− mice (figure 3B, online supplemental figure S12B), suggesting that the antitumor phenotype in Cd55−/− mice is largely MDSC-dependent. Furthermore, we generated Cd55fl/flLyz2-cre mice, in which CD55 is specifically deleted in myeloid cells (online supplemental figure S13A,B), to directly assess the contribution of myeloid CD55 to tumor progression. Tumor volumes were significantly reduced in Cd55fl/flLyz2-cre mice compared with controls (figure 3C, online supplemental figure S12C). Flow cytometry and immunofluorescence analyses showed that myeloid-specific CD55 deletion markedly reduced MDSC infiltration (figure 3D–F, online supplemental figure S12D–F) and was accompanied by enhanced accumulation of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells within the TME.

  Taken together, these data demonstrate that the tumor-inhibitory and immune-reprogramming effects of CD55 deficiency are mediated through MDSCs.


### CD55 deficiency attenuates the formation and immunosuppressive activity of MDSCs
  As previously reported, CD55 accelerates complement decay.33 Therefore, we examined whether CD55 deficiency affects complement levels. Systemic CD55 knockout led to a significant upregulation of complement levels in the TME (online supplemental figure S14A–D). However, this increase was not observed in the myeloid cell-specific CD55-knockout tumor-bearing model (online supplemental figure S14E–H), indicating that the antitumor effects of CD55 inhibition in MDSCs may be mediated through direct modulation of MDSCs themselves. To further investigate this possibility, an adoptive transfer assay was performed in which BM from CD45.1 WT and CD45.2 Cd55−/− mice was transferred into CD45.2 WT recipient mice, followed by MC38 or ID8 tumor implantation (figure 4A, online supplemental figure S15A). In vivo CD55 deficiency resulted in a significant reduction in the frequency of MDSCs and their subsets (figure 4B, online supplemental figure S15B).

  Consistently, in vitro CD55 knockout in BM-derived MDSCs decreased the overall proportion of MDSCs and their subpopulations (figure 4C,D). We next assessed whether CD55 deficiency also influences the immunosuppressive function of MDSCs. Notably, CD55 loss significantly reduced the expression of key immunosuppressive mediators, including ARG1, S100A8, S100A9, IL-10, and TGF-β1 (figure 4E). Given the pronounced changes in MDSCs and CD8+ T cells observed in CD55-deficient tumor-bearing mice, we co-cultured MDSCs with CD8+ T cells. Cd55−/− MDSCs displayed impaired suppressive capacity, as demonstrated by enhanced CD8+ T-cell proliferation (figure 4F) and increased production of IFN-γ and TNF-α by co-cultured CD8+ T cells (figure 4G).

  Collectively, these results suggest that CD55 plays a pivotal role in regulating both the formation and immunosuppressive function of MDSCs within the TME.


### CD55 drives STAT3-mediated immunosuppressive function of MDSCs by promoting PYGL phosphorylation and glycolysis
  To elucidate the mechanism by which CD55 regulates the function of MDSCs, IP-MS was employed to identify CD55-binding proteins, revealing 27 interacting proteins (figure 5A, online supplemental figure S16A). Simultaneously, RNA sequencing was performed on MDSCs induced in vitro from the BM of both Cd55−/− and WT mice. Differential expression analysis identified 1962 genes significantly upregulated in the WT group (figure 5B, online supplemental figure S16B). By integrating the proteomic and transcriptomic data through gene-protein mapping and Venn diagram analysis, PYGL emerged as a CD55-interacting protein, with its mRNA expression significantly elevated in WT MDSCs (with relatively high CD55 expression) compared with Cd55−/− MDSCs (with low CD55 expression) (figure 5C). These results suggest that PYGL acts as a key downstream effector of CD55 signaling in MDSCs.

  The interaction between CD55 and PYGL was further validated by Co-IP (figure 5D). PYGL has been reported to catalyze glycogen breakdown,34 35 releasing glucose-1-phosphate, which enters the glycolytic pathway or the pentose phosphate pathway after conversion to glucose-6-phosphate.36 Accumulating evidence indicates that PYGL plays a critical role in supporting glycolysis3739 and can be activated through phosphorylation.40 41 WB analysis showed that, in MDSCs, CD55 deletion reduced both PYGL protein levels and its phosphorylation ratio, whereas no such changes were observed in freshly isolated BM cells (figure 5E). Given that the immunosuppressive activity of MDSCs is highly dependent on glycolysis,42 and considering the role of PYGL in glycogen catabolism and glycolytic support as well as its downregulation on CD55 loss, we hypothesized that CD55 modulates MDSC function by regulating glycolytic metabolism. Seahorse analysis confirmed that CD55 knockout indeed suppressed glycolysis in MDSCs, while glycolytic activity in fresh BM cells remained unaffected (figure 5F). It has been reported that the glycolytic metabolite lactate can activate STAT3, a key transcription factor for MDSCs immunosuppressive molecules, through multiple mechanisms, including mTOR/HIF-1α signaling,43 METTL3 lactylation-enhanced m6A regulation,44 histone lactylation-dependent upregulation of TET2,45 and so on. Together with our earlier observation that CD55 deficiency in MDSCs markedly reduced the expression of immunosuppressive molecules (figure 4E), we next examined STAT3 and its phosphorylation status. Notably, phosphorylated STAT3 (p-STAT3) was downregulated in Cd55−/− MDSCs, whereas no such change was observed in freshly isolated BM cells (figure 5G).

  To determine whether the glycolysis-regulatory and immunosuppressive effects of CD55 are dependent on PYGL, PYGL was knocked down in both WT and Cd55−/− MDSCs. In Cd55−/− MDSCs, transduction with sh-PYGL lentivirus did not further reduce PYGL expression or its phosphorylation levels (figure 5H). Seahorse analysis showed that both CD55 deficiency and PYGL knockdown suppressed glycolysis in MDSCs; however, PYGL knockdown did not lead to any additional reduction in glycolytic activity in Cd55−/− MDSCs (figure 5I). Consistently, similar patterns were observed for p-STAT3 and its downstream transcriptional targets (figure 5J,K).

  Collectively, these results suggest that CD55 interacts with PYGL to promote glycolysis, thereby activating STAT3 and sustaining the immunosuppressive program of MDSCs.


### Targeting the CD55-PYGL interaction with tomatidine inhibits glycolysis and STAT3-mediated immunosuppressive functions in MDSCs
  Given the regulatory role of CD55 in PYGL, AlphaFold3 was employed to predict the three-dimensional structure of the PYGL-CD55 interaction complex. The resulting CD55-PYGL complex was visualized and analyzed using PyMOL. The interaction interface between CD55 and PYGL was examined for spatial proximity and the types of molecular contacts, including hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions. A comprehensive druggability pocket analysis was conducted using SiteMap to identify potential binding sites within the PYGL-CD55 interface. A magnified view of the key amino acids revealed their distinct spatial distribution (figure 6A). A closer examination of the interface region showed that the ASP168 residue (highlighted in red) of CD55 made significant spatial contact with PYGL, positioned at the core of the interaction between the two proteins (figure 6A). ASP168 participated in multiple interactions within the model, acting as a bridging residue and playing a critical role in stabilizing the CD55-PYGL complex. In contrast, other interface residues, such as LYS142, ARG134, and TYR368 (marked in purple), were located farther from the interface or contributed less to direct interactions (figure 6A), further emphasizing the pivotal role of ASP168 in the stability of the complex. Thus, ASP168 was identified as the key binding site and serves as the central focus for subsequent screening of protein–protein interaction inhibitors, providing valuable structural insights for compound identification.

  To identify potential inhibitors of the CD55-PYGL interaction, AutoDock Vina was used for structure-based virtual screening (online supplemental figure S17A). A diverse virtual library of approximately 25,031 small molecules was constructed from bioactive compound libraries, FDA-approved libraries, and natural product libraries. A multistage molecular docking approach was implemented: (1) HTS for initial broad compound elimination, (2) SP docking for intermediate refinement, and (3) XP docking for final selection and pose optimization of compounds. After evaluating docking scores, binding conformations, and structural diversity, 10 compounds—Cipargamin, ZINC03129319, Arcyriaflavina, Tomatidine, IHMT-PI3Kdelta-372, 3-benzyl-7,8-dihydroxy-4-methyl-2H-chromen-2-one, Epirosmanol, GSK2606414, 4-(di(1H-indol-3-yl)methyl)phenol, and Hydroxysanguinarine—were identified as having the most favorable binding characteristics at the ASP168 pocket. Due to the poor solubility of hydroxysanguinarine, the remaining nine compounds were selected for the SPR imaging (SPRi)-based competitive binding assay.

  The direct PYGL-CD55 protein-protein interaction was investigated to establish baseline binding kinetics, including the association rate constant (ka), dissociation rate constant (kd), and equilibrium dissociation constant (KD). Serial dilution experiments (320, 160, 80, 40, 20, 10, 5 nM) were performed for comprehensive kinetic analysis, and standard SPR analysis software was used to calculate the kinetic parameters (online supplemental figure S17B–K, online supplemental table S7). The competitive inhibition of PYGL-CD55 by the selected compounds was assessed by comparing the binding responses and kinetic parameters between control and inhibitor-treated samples. Among the compounds tested, tomatidine exhibited the most significant inhibitory effect. Specifically, it caused a 2.1-fold reduction in the association rate (ka=3.12E+04 vs 6.54E+04), a 1.6-fold increase in the dissociation rate (kd=8.15E-02 vs 5.15E-02), and a 3.3-fold increase in the KD value (2.61E-06 vs 7.87E-07), as evidenced by a marked decrease in the binding response on the SPR sensorgram, indicating effective competitive inhibition. IHMT-PI3Kdelta-372 demonstrated moderate but notable inhibition, with a 1.5-fold decrease in ka (4.22E+04 vs 6.54E+04), a 1.3-fold increase in kd (6.78E-02 vs 5.15E-02), and a 2.0-fold increase in KD (1.61E-06 vs 7.87E-07). The remaining seven compounds showed minimal deviation from the control baseline, with changes of less than 15%. In a head-to-head comparison, under identical experimental conditions (CD55 320 nM+inhibitor 10 µM), direct SPR response comparison revealed clear differentiation among inhibitors (figure 6B). The sensorgram overlay demonstrated that tomatidine achieved the lowest binding response, at less than 50% of the control, confirming its superior inhibitory efficacy.

  Subsequently, BM-derived MDSCs were treated with varying concentrations (0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25 µM) of tomatidine, which showed a concentration-dependent inhibition of PYGL phosphorylation (figure 6C). Additionally, tomatidine reduced the formation of MDSCs and their subtypes (figure 6D). Seahorse experiments revealed a similar concentration-dependent suppression of glycolysis in MDSCs (figure 6E). Furthermore, tomatidine decreased the phosphorylation levels of STAT3 and reduced the expression of its downstream immunosuppressive molecules (figure 6F,G). These results indicate that tomatidine inhibits CD55-mediated PYGL phosphorylation, thereby suppressing both glycolysis and immunosuppressive functions in MDSCs.


### Disrupting CD55 either pharmacologically with tomatidine or through myeloid-specific deletion enhances the therapeutic efficacy of αPD-1 therapy on tumor
  PD-1 is a key regulator of T-cell exhaustion,46 and αPD-1 therapy is widely used clinically for cancer treatment.47 However, therapeutic resistance remains a significant challenge. Accumulating evidence suggests that elevated levels of MDSCs are closely associated with poor responses to PD-1 blockade,4850 as MDSCs themselves express PD-L1,51 52 and the immunosuppressive molecules produced by MDSCs can directly or indirectly enhance PD-1 signaling in CD8+ T cells.53 54 This suggests that reducing MDSC abundance and inhibiting their immunosuppressive functions may help overcome resistance to αPD-1 therapy. Based on our findings that CD55 regulates both MDSC levels and the expression of immunosuppressive molecules, we investigated whether targeting CD55 in MDSCs—either pharmacologically with tomatidine or genetically through myeloid-specific CD55 deletion—could enhance the antitumor efficacy of αPD-1 therapy.

  First, MC38-bearing or ID8-bearing mice were treated with tomatidine at different doses for 21 days (low dose, 12.5 mg/kg; medium dose, 25 mg/kg; high dose, 50 mg/kg) to evaluate the potential organ toxicity of tomatidine. Histopathological examination revealed no significant alterations in major organs, indicating that tomatidine did not induce evident organ toxicity under the tested conditions (online supplemental figure S18).

  Then, MC38-bearing or ID8-bearing mice were treated with tomatidine and αPD-1 either separately or in combination. The combination of tomatidine and αPD-1 significantly reduced tumor volume and weight compared with placebo or either treatment alone, indicating a synergistic antitumor effect (figure 7A–C, online supplemental figure S19A–C). Tomatidine administration resulted in a reduction in the frequency of MDSCs, including the subtypes M-MDSCs and PMN-MDSCs (figure 7D–F, online supplemental figure S19D–F). Additionally, tomatidine supplementation significantly increased the frequency of infiltrating CD8+ T cells (figure 7G, online supplemental figure S19G) and enhanced their production of effector cytokines, such as IFN-γ and TNF-α (figure 7H, online supplemental figure S19H), within the TME.

  Next, CD55 was conditionally deleted in myeloid cells by crossing Cd55fl/fl with Lyz2-cre mice to generate Cd55fl/flLyz2-cre mice, which were then treated with the anti-PD-1 antibody. Specific knockout of CD55 in myeloid cells enhanced the effectiveness of αPD-1 therapy, as evidenced by a significant reduction in both tumor volume and weight in the Cd55fl/flLyz2-cre + αPD-1 group compared with the other groups (online supplemental figure S20A–C and S21A–C). Similar to the effects observed with tomatidine, this enhancement was accompanied by a reduction in the frequency of MDSCs, including both M-MDSCs and PMN-MDSCs (online supplemental figure S20D–F and S21D–F). Furthermore, Cd55fl/flLyz2-cre + αPD-1 mice also exhibited an increase in CD8+ T cells (online supplemental figure S20G and S21G) and a higher frequency of IFN-γ and TNF-α-producing CD8+ T cells (online supplemental figure S20H and S21H).

  These results collectively demonstrate that pharmacological inhibition of CD55 with tomatidine or myeloid-specific deletion of CD55 enhances the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy.


### C/EBPβ is identified as the key transcription factor mediating GM-CSF/IL-6-induced CD55 expression in MDSCs
  To identify upstream regulatory factors controlling CD55 expression in MDSCs, BM-derived MDSCs were first generated using GM-CSF and IL-6. Flow cytometry analysis confirmed a significant increase in CD55 expression in BM-derived MDSCs compared with freshly isolated BM cells (figure 8A). A DNA pulldown assay targeting the CD55 promoter was then performed, followed by MS analysis (figure 8B). Silver staining confirmed successful protein enrichment from both fresh BM and BM-derived MDSC samples (online supplemental figure S22A,B). Comparative analysis of the pulldown-enriched proteins identified 190 proteins unique to fresh BM and 89 specific to MDSCs. Venn analysis revealed 80 MDSC-specific proteins potentially involved in pathological regulation (online supplemental figure S22C). By intersecting these proteins with transcription factor databases (UCSC, Remap), C/EBPβ was identified as a potential upstream transcriptional regulator of CD55 in MDSCs (figure 8B).

  To determine if C/EBPβ directly regulates Cd55 transcription, the Cd55 promoter region was analyzed for potential C/EBPβ binding sites (online supplemental table S8), and a luciferase reporter assay was performed. The Cd55 promoter contains three putative C/EBPβ binding sites (Sites 1–3) (figure 8C). Mutation analysis of these sites in the luciferase reporter showed that only mutations at Site 1 significantly reduced promoter activity in C/EBPβ overexpressing cells (figure 8D), indicating that Site 1 is critical for C/EBPβ-mediated activation of the Cd55 promoter. ChIP-qPCR results demonstrated significant enrichment of C/EBPβ at Site 1 of the Cd55 promoter compared with the IgG control (figure 8E.F), further confirming Site 1 as the binding site for C/EBPβ. Additionally, C/EBPβ knockdown in MDSCs significantly decreased CD55 expression on GM-CSF and IL-6 treatment (figure 8G,H) and reduced the frequency of MDSCs (figure 8I). Collectively, these results suggest that GM-CSF and IL-6 induce CD55 expression on MDSCs via C/EBPβ signaling.


## Discussion
  MDSCs significantly expand within the TME, exerting immunosuppressive effects. The surface molecules on MDSCs serve not only as biomarkers for identification but also as critical regulators of their immunosuppressive functions. Recently, several novel molecules—such as CD300ld,16 LOX-1,17 CD84,18 and HCAR119—have been identified as potential functional regulators of MDSCs, offering promising avenues for functional characterization. However, these surface molecules are predominantly expressed on the PMN-MDSC subset, rather than across the entire MDSC population. Thus, identifying essential surface molecules that are broadly expressed on total MDSCs in cancer remains crucial for discovering new therapeutic targets.

  In this study, CD55 was identified as a critical surface molecule highly expressed on MDSCs in patients with cancer and associated with poor prognosis. CD55 is a membrane-bound complement regulatory protein that protects host cells from complement-mediated lysis by inhibiting the formation and accelerating the decay of C3/C5 convertases.33 Dysregulation of CD55 has been linked to various non-malignant conditions, including autoimmune diseases such as arthritis,55 as well as viral infections such as HIV, SARS-CoV-2, and parainfluenza virus 5.5658 CD55 overexpression has been reported in multiple cancer types,2223 5962 including lung, colorectal, gastric, ovarian, breast, and neurological malignancies, where it is often associated with poor prognosis. Mechanistically, CD55 contributes to tumor progression by modulating immune responses, promoting cancer stemness, and facilitating drug resistance. For example, in ovarian cancer, CD55 binds chromatin and interacts with the epigenetic regulators ZMYND8 and the PRC2 complex, enhancing H3K27 trimethylation to drive cisplatin resistance and stemness.63 In EGFR-mutant non-small cell lung cancer, EGFR signaling upregulates CD55 (along with CD59), suppressing complement activation and impairing CD8+ T cell function via the β-catenin-LINC00973-miR-216b/miR-150 axis.23 In colorectal cancer, CD55-Smad4 activation leads to apoptosis through caspase signaling.64 Furthermore, in MYCN-amplified neuroblastoma, CD55 acts as a MYCN-regulated driver of tumor stemness and proliferation.65 However, all of these findings pertain to CD55 expression in tumor cells.

  In this study, we discovered a novel role for CD55 in regulating MDSCs. CD55 knockout in MDSCs significantly inhibited tumor progression and alleviated immunosuppression within the TME. Specifically, CD55 deficiency in MDSCs reduced their formation and immunosuppressive activity. To investigate the underlying regulatory mechanism of CD55 in MDSCs, we used IP-MS, RNA sequencing, and Co-IP analyses, which identified PYGL as a key CD55-interacting protein. PYGL catalyzes glycogen breakdown34 35 and has been shown to play a critical role in supporting glycolysis,3639 consistent with our Seahorse analysis showing that CD55 promotes glycolysis in MDSCs through PYGL. Previous studies have demonstrated that the immunosuppressive function of MDSCs is highly dependent on glycolysis,42 with glycolytic byproducts such as lactate activating STAT3 through multiple pathways,4345 a key transcription factor for immunosuppressive molecules in MDSCs. This suggests that CD55 may enhance glycolysis through PYGL, thereby activating STAT3 and its downstream immunosuppressive targets, sustaining the immune-suppressive function of MDSCs. Further validation through CD55 knockout and sh-PYGL transfection experiments in MDSCs confirmed these findings. Notably, CD55 is a glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored protein66 and is generally considered to lack an intracellular domain, making it difficult to directly exert effects on intracellular signaling. However, a recent study in platinum-resistant ovarian cancer reported that surface CD55 can enter the cytoplasm via its serine/threonine-rich region and translocate to the nucleus, where it interacts with the epigenetic regulator ZMYND8 and the PRC2 complex to increase H3K27 trimethylation, thereby promoting cisplatin resistance and stemness.63 Importantly, several proteins identified in the CD55 IP-MS interactome from platinum-resistant ovarian cancer overlap with those in our CD55 IP–MS interactome in MDSCs (including SAFB, SRSF9, FBLL1, and COPA; predominantly nuclear/cytoplasmic proteins), further supporting a model in which CD55 internalization and nuclear translocation contribute to downstream signaling.

  To disrupt the CD55-PYGL interaction, we performed virtual screening followed by SPRi analysis and identified tomatidine as an effective inhibitor. Tomatidine efficiently blocked the CD55-PYGL interaction, inhibited glycolysis, and thereby suppressed STAT3 activation and the expression of immunosuppressive molecules, ultimately attenuating the immunosuppressive function of MDSCs. Additionally, targeting CD55—either pharmacologically with tomatidine or via MDSC-specific deletion—enhanced the therapeutic efficacy of αPD-1 therapy in tumor-bearing mice. These findings position CD55 not only as a tumor-intrinsic factor but also as a key regulator of immune suppression through MDSCs, offering a potential new strategy for cancer immunotherapy.

  It has been reported that anti-CD55 monoclonal antibodies have entered clinical evaluation.67 However, safety concerns regarding their systemic application cannot be ignored. CD55 is a cell surface complement regulatory protein whose primary function is to accelerate the dissociation of C3/C5 convertases, thereby inhibiting the amplification of the complement cascade on the surface of self-cells and protecting them from being attacked by the body’s own complement system.68 CD55 is expressed on most cells exposed to serum (such as blood cells, endothelial cells, epithelial cells),69 70 meaning that any systemic intervention targeting its function could have widespread effects. For example, CHAPLE (Complement Hyperactivation, Angiopathic Thrombosis, and Protein-Losing Enteropathy) is a condition caused by a complete functional loss of both alleles of the CD55 gene, leading to complement dysregulation.71 This mutation results in excessive activation of the complement system, which in turn attacks self-cells. Clinical manifestations typically include protein-losing enteropathy, intestinal lymphangiectasia, hypoalbuminemia, edema, malnutrition, and growth retardation. Paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria is a classic disease characterized by complement regulatory protein deficiency leading to self-blood cells being susceptible to complement attack. The mechanism involves acquired mutations in HSCs leading to the loss of GPI-anchored proteins, including CD55 and CD59.72 As a result, blood cells become sensitive to complement activation, leading to sustained hemolysis and frequent thrombosis. Additionally, in the MRL/lpr (systemic lupus erythematosus-like) mouse model, deletion of DAF/CD55 exacerbates the autoimmune disease progression.73 Recent immunological studies have also discussed the link between CD55 deficiency and B cell responses, TLR9 signaling, and autoimmunity.74 In summary, systemic intervention of CD55 may lead to side effects such as protein loss in the intestines, infections, low immunoglobulin levels, thrombosis, and hemolysis. Therefore, in cancer immunotherapy, selectively targeting CD55 on MDSCs offers a more precise and safer therapeutic strategy.

  GM-CSF and IL-6 upregulate CD55 expression on MDSCs through the activation of the C/EBPβ signaling pathway. C/EBPβ plays a pivotal role in MDSCs pathobiology,75 acting as a key transcriptional hub that responds to inflammatory signals in the TME, such as GM-CSF and IL-6. This regulatory function enables C/EBPβ to govern MDSC development and expansion from BM progenitors, contributing to their aberrant accumulation at tumor sites.2 Notably, C/EBPβ directly induces the transcription of essential immunosuppressive molecules in MDSCs, including Arg1, iNOS, and ROS,76 often in coordination with STAT3 to amplify these suppressive pathways.26 The transcriptional network orchestrated by C/EBPβ equips MDSCs with potent immunoregulatory functions, allowing them to effectively suppress T cell activation and proliferation, thereby facilitating tumor immune evasion. This study further identifies C/EBPβ as a key upstream transcription factor regulating CD55 expression, contributing to the CD55-dependent immunosuppressive activity of MDSCs. These findings underscore the pivotal role of C/EBPβ in modulating MDSC function and highlight its potential as a target for disrupting tumor-induced immunosuppression.

  Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, although we found that CD55 is upregulated in MDSCs and their subsets in patients with cancer, the effects of gender and disease stage on CD55 expression have not yet been clearly defined due to the limited sample size. While we compared CD55 expression in MDSCs between male and female patients and observed no significant differences, a potential influence of gender cannot be excluded, as prior studies have reported that progesterone and estradiol can upregulate CD55 expression in endometrial-related cells.2729 Future studies with larger cohorts are warranted to more comprehensively evaluate the impact of gender and disease stage on CD55 expression. In addition, we observed a trend toward increased CD55 expression in B cells under tumor-bearing conditions, and systemic CD55 knockout resulted in enhanced complement activation. Previous studies have shown that CD55+ memory B cells can secrete TGF-β and exert immunosuppressive effects in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma.77 Accordingly, we speculate that CD55 in B cells may contribute to tumorigenesis by attenuating complement activity while promoting the secretion of immunosuppressive molecules, thereby suppressing antitumor immunity. However, the present study primarily focused on the function of CD55 on MDSCs; further studies are needed to elucidate the role of CD55 in B cells during tumor development.

  This study identified CD55 as a novel surface molecule on MDSCs, expanding the understanding of its function by uncovering a previously unrecognized role in orchestrating the development and immunosuppressive activity of tumor-associated MDSCs. While CD55 has traditionally been studied in the context of complement regulation and tumor cell-intrinsic functions, these findings reveal a novel MDSCs-centered mechanism by which CD55 facilitates tumor immune evasion. This work not only highlights a key immunoregulatory axis in cancer biology but also opens new avenues for the development of targeted therapies aimed at modulating the TME.
