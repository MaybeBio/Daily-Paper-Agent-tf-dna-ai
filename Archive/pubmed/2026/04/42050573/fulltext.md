# Integrative transcriptomic profiling reveals NK cell exhaustion–associated prognostic genes and identifies CSF1 as a key immunoregulatory target in hepatocellular carcinoma


## Background
  Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) features a complex tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) where natural killer cell exhaustion (NKEX) facilitates immune evasion. Its regulatory networks and prognostic value remain insufficiently understood.


## Methods
  We integrated single-cell (GSE149614) and bulk RNA-seq (TCGA-LIHC) data. Following Seurat-based clustering, we utilized CellChat and pySCENIC for communication and transcription factor analysis. NKEX-associated modules were identified via gene set variation analysis and weighted gene co-expression network analysis. A prognostic signature was developed using LASSO-Cox regression and validated in an external cohort (ICGC). CSF1 was prioritized for validation through clinical immunohistochemistry, siRNA knockdown, and Western Blotting. Candidate compounds identified via reverse network pharmacology were validated through CCK-8 assays, docking, western blotting and quantitative PCR.


## Results
  Single-cell analysis revealed pronounced NKEX and disrupted communication in HCC. A four-gene prognostic signature (AKR1B1, SMS, CSF1, CFL1) demonstrated robust predictive performance in both TCGA (1-year AUC: 0.759) and external validation cohorts. High-risk patients showed significantly poorer survival. CSF1 was markedly upregulated in HCC tissues; its silencing inhibited Huh7 cell migration and invasion while upregulating CXCL10 in Huh7 cells and CXCR3 in NK92 cells. Molecular docking and CCK-8 assays justified the dosage and identified punicalagin and evoden as potent inhibitors that significantly suppressed CSF1 expression at both mRNA and protein levels.


## Conclusion
  Through integrated multi-omics and experimental validation, we characterized NKEX in HCC and established a robust prognostic signature. CSF1 emerged as a key immunomodulatory target. Punicalagin and evoden were identified as potential lead compounds to modulate the CSF1, offering a promising strategy to restore antitumor immunity in HCC.

## Introduction
  Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) represents a leading cause of cancer-related mortality around the world, characterized by a high degree of heterogeneity and aggressiveness, as well as a poor prognosis [1, 2]. The absence of symptoms in patients with HCC during the early stages results in poor overall survival rates despite significant advances in diagnostic and therapeutic techniques, such as PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors [3]. A core factor contributing to this phenomenon is the inherent molecular heterogeneity of HCC itself. Most conventional tumor studies rely on bulk tumor tissue samples, which are often limited in clinical quantity and fail to accurately capture the characteristics and molecular expression profiles of different tumor subpopulations and various cell types within the tumor microenvironment [4, 5]. Such a constraint restricts comprehensive insights into tumorigenic mechanisms and hampers the formulation of targeted therapeutic approaches.

  Advances in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) technologies have provided new directions for cancer research, particularly in exploring the heterogeneity within tumors [6]. The scRNA-seq can analyze the gene expression profiles of different cell populations [7]. Thus clarifying the functional differences between cells and their roles in tumorigenesis, progression and drug resistance [8, 9]. This, in turn, provides potential new targets for the early diagnosis and treatment of HCC. Complementarily, bulk RNA-Seq is capable of not only comprehensively capturing the expression changes of all transcripts in the tumor tissues of HCC patients, but also identifying key genes and pathways that are closely related to the biological behavior of tumors [10].

  Growing awareness of the critical function of the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) in cancer development and treatment outcomes has highlighted the necessity of identifying immune-related signatures and characterizing immune features across diverse cancer types [11]. The anti-tumor function of natural killer (NK) cells in HCC is particularly significant [12]. As crucial components of the innate immune defense, NK cells can identify and destroy tumor cells directly, independent of antigen presentation. Nevertheless, previous studies have revealed that in the HCC immune milieu, NK cells progressively enter a state of exhaustion due to prolonged exposure to inhibitory factors secreted by tumor cells (e.g., TGF-β and IL-10) [13, 14]. This leads to a notable weakening of their anti-tumor effects, consequently allowing cancer cells to escape immune detection. Moreover, growing evidence suggests that NK cell exhaustion (NKEX) is a dynamic, multi-stage process, with each stage characterized by unique molecular features and functional impairments [15–17]. Therefore, how to regulate NK cells, identify key targets, and discover potential drugs may represent important research directions in oncology.

  This study established an integrated analytical framework using scRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq data to systematically investigate NKEX-associated prognostic biomarkers in HCC. By bridging single-cell resolution with clinical sample cohorts, we delineated the NKEX regulatory network and evaluated its prognostic significance. Our analysis identified CSF1 as a key prognostic gene. Subsequently, through reverse network pharmacology, potential CSF1-targeting compounds were identified and validated via molecular docking and in vitro assays. This work provides novel molecular evidence and therapeutic strategies for precision medicine in HCC (Fig. 1).

## Materials and methods

### The acquisition and pre-processing of 10×scRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq data
  Transcriptomic profiles along with associated clinical data from 328 hepatocellular carcinoma patients were obtained from the Cancer Genome Atlas Program (TCGA, https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) repository for this study, while integrating a publicly available scRNA-seq dataset (GSE149614) from the Gene Expression Omnibus database (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The single-cell data were processed and preprocessed using the Seurat (V5.1.0) R package, which is currently one of the most widely used tools for single-cell transcriptome analysis, providing comprehensive modules for quality control, data normalization, batch effect correction, and cell type annotation [18]. During preprocessing, cells of low quality were removed according to predefined quality control standards: specifically, cells expressing less than 300 or over 10,000 genes, as well as those with mitochondrial transcripts exceeding 10%, were removed from further analysis. The remaining high-fidelity cells were then employed for clustering and downstream analyzes. For the TCGA transcriptome dataset, raw expression measurements were initially reported in Fragments Per Kilobase per Million mapped reads (FPKM) format. To standardize data across all samples, these values were subsequently converted to Transcripts Per Million (TPM) prior to further investigation. Corresponding clinical information, including patient age, sex, tumor stage, and follow-up records, was incorporated for prognostic evaluation and model development.


### Dimensionality reduction and clustering analysis
  The data underwent preprocessing prior to normalization with the Seurat (V5.1.0) package. The gene expression of each cell was initially log-normalized, and the top 2000 highly variable genes exhibiting the greatest expression changes were identified. An initial dimensionality reduction was then conducted through principal component analysis, with significant principal components identified based on the first 20 principal components. Subsequently, the Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection algorithm was applied to conduct additional nonlinear dimensional reduction of cellular data. The SingleR package was employed for the automatic and manual annotation of cells into distinct subpopulations [19].


### Differential expression genes (DEGs) analysis
  In this study, multiple differential expression analysis methods were employed to analyze bulk RNA-seq data from TCGA-HCC, including DESeq2 (http://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/vignettes/DESeq2/inst/doc/DESeq2.html), edgeR (http://bioinf.wehi.edu.au/edgeR/), limma (https://bioconductor.org/packages/limma), and Wilcoxon rank-sum test [20]. The screening criteria for DEGs were set as |log2 fold change| > 1 with an adjusted P-value < 0.05. The intersection of results from these four methods was taken as the final DEG set. The screening results were visualized using volcano plots and heatmaps. Following identification of DEGs, functional enrichment analyzes, including Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway evaluations, the ClusterProfiler package was employed to investigate the involvement of these genes in various biological functions and molecular signaling pathways.


### Cell communication analysis
  To investigate the intercellular interactions among distinct cell populations, this study employed the CellChat (V1.1.3) software package (https://github.com/sqjin/CellChat) for cell-cell communication analysis. CellChat integrates a systematic receptor-ligand database and probabilistic models, which are applicable to single-cell transcriptomic data across diverse tissue types. This tool enables quantitative identification of active signaling pathways and facilitates comparative analysis of communication patterns between different experimental groups [21]. Using the standard CellChat workflow, overexpressed interactions and pathways were identified, and communication probabilities between cell subsets were computed. The netAnalysis_signalingRole_scatter function quantified and displayed the strength of outgoing and incoming signals, while the netVisual_circle function generated circular plots illustrating interaction networks and communication intensity for each subset. For overall network visualization, the top 10% of interactions were highlighted, and the compareInteractions function calculated the total inferred cell-cell interactions per dataset after merging the CellChat object.


### Transcription factors (TFs) regulatory network analysis
  To construct cell population-specific TFs regulatory networks, this study employed the pySCENIC (V0.12.1) analysis pipeline [22]. First, a preliminary gene co-expression network was established using the GRNBoost2 algorithm based on single-cell expression matrices to identify associations between TFs and their potential target genes. Subsequently, motif enrichment analysis was integrated to retain only TF-target gene pairs with supported regulatory binding sites, thereby constructing high-confidence regulatory modules (regulons). Next, the activity of each regulatory module in individual cells was scored using the AUCell algorithm, and TFs exhibiting significant activity in specific cell populations were identified. Finally, UMAP and heatmap visualizations were implemented to display TFs activity patterns and target gene distributions, untangling the cell-type-specific expression characteristics of key regulatory factors. This integrative approach, combining co-expression and motif information, enables effective dissection of regulatory mechanisms underlying cell state transitions and immune exhaustion.


### Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) analysis
  GSVA enables the computation of enrichment scores for individual samples across specific gene sets without the need for preassigned group labels, making it well-suited for functional comparisons at the individual sample level or in the context of continuous variables. We referenced Beatriz Cózar’s study on NKEX [23], NKEX-related signaling pathways and corresponding gene sets from the Molecular Signaling Database (MSigDB, version 7.2) were used to calculate NKEX pathway activity scores for individual patients via the GSVA R package [24].


### Weighted gene co-expression network (WGCNA) analysis
  The WGCNA is a systems biology method used to construct and analyze gene co-expression networks, aiming to untangle complex relationships among genes and identify highly synergistic gene sets. WGCNA was conducted in R using average FPKM values and the WGCNA package. Initially, outlier samples were identified and removed through clustering analysis. To select the soft-thresholding power, a scale-free topology criterion was applied based on an unsigned Pearson correlation matrix, with the β parameter chosen where the fit index first reached 0.9. The network was then constructed in a stepwise manner, the adjacency matrix was transformed into a topological overlap matrix, after which hierarchical clustering was conducted. Gene modules were subsequently detected using a dynamic tree-cutting method.


### One-way Cox regression analysis
  To investigate the relationship between NKEX-associated genes and patient outcomes, univariate Cox proportional hazards regression was performed with the R survival package to determine genes significantly linked to overall survival (OS). A significance threshold of P < 0.05 was applied as the screening standard. The results were presented in a forest plot, which allows for the visualization of the risk ratio (HR, Hazard Ratio) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) for each gene.


### Lasso analysis and multifactor Cox regression analysis
  The genes screened by one-way Cox regression, the penalty parameters were introduced by Lasso regression, and the best λ value was selected by cross-validation using the “glmnet” R package, and then the genes with significant differences were screened out, and subjected to multifactorial Cox regression analysis. A prognostic model based on NKEX-associated gene expression was established, and individual patient risk scores were computed according to the formula below:

  The regression coefficient for each gene obtained from the multivariate Cox model was applied to estimate the individual risk score. ### The reverse network pharmacology assay
  To systematically elucidate the potential mechanisms of action of the studied subject in disease initiation and progression, a reverse network pharmacology strategy was applied. First, disease-related transcriptomic data were obtained from public databases, including TCGA and GEO, and disease-associated gene sets were identified through differential expression analysis. Combined with clinical information, survival analysis or correlation analysis was further performed to screen key candidate genes closely associated with disease progression. Subsequently, based on the identified key candidate genes, potential drugs and active compounds related to the targets were collected and integrated from the TCMSP, HERB, and ETCM databases. The candidate genes were defined as core targets to construct the interaction network, followed by network topological analysis. Furthermore, molecular docking analysis was conducted to evaluate the binding affinity between core targets and potential active compounds, thereby identifying key bioactive components that may exert biological effects.


### Molecular docking assay
  The three-dimensional crystal structure of CSF1 was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/) database and saved in PDB format. The two-dimensional structures of the candidate small-molecule compounds punicalagin and Evoden were downloaded from the PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) database and saved in SDF format. The protein and ligand structures were then imported into the CB.dock2 (http://183.56.231.194:8001/cb-dock2/index.php) online platform for molecular docking analysis. The docking pose with the lowest binding energy was selected as the optimal conformation, and LigPlot+ software (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/thornton-srv/software/LIGPLOT/) was used to visualize and analyze the interactions between the ligands and CSF1.


### Differential expression analysis of CSF1 in HCC using UALCAN based on TCGA
  The differential expression of CSF1 was analyzed using the UALCAN (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/index.html) website [25], which provides a tumor gene expression analysis tool based on TCGA data. In the “Cancer Type” section, “Liver Hepatocellular Carcinoma” was selected, and “CSF1” was entered in the “Gene Expression” module for query submission. The expression levels of CSF1 in normal liver tissues and HCC tissues were compared by selecting the “Normal” and “Tumor” groups, and statistical significance was assessed using a t-test (P < 0.05). This approach provided reliable data support for subsequent experimental validation.


### External validation of the prognostic model
  To validate the generalizability of the prognostic model, an independent liver cancer cohort from the ICGC database was employed for external validation. The expression matrix was normalized using Z-score transformation to eliminate platform-specific biases between the training and validation datasets. Prior to validation, multicollinearity diagnostics were performed, confirming the statistical independence of the four genes with all Variance Inflation Factors (VIF) below 5. The risk score for each patient was calculated using the pre-determined coefficients from the training set. Subsequently, patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups based on the optimal cutoff determined by the surv_cutpoint algorithm. The model’s predictive performance was evaluated through Kaplan-Meier survival analysis with log-rank tests and time-dependent ROC curves for 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival.


### Experimental verification

#### Cell culture
  Huh7 cells were purchased from the China Center for Type Culture Collection (GDC027, CCTCC, China). The cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM medium (BL301A, Biosharp, China) containing 10% FBS (FSD500, Excell, China) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (BL505A, Biosharp, China) at a temperature of 37 ℃ and under 5% CO2 conditions. The human NK cell line NK-92 was purchased from Cellcook (CC1624, Cellcook, China) and cultured in NK-92-specific medium (CC1624S, Cellcook, China). The NK-92 cells were maintained at 37 ℃ in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.


#### siRNA transfection assay
  CSF1-siRNA and control siRNA were obtained from Huayu Gene (Wuhan, China). The transfection mixture was first prepared following the manufacturer’s protocol. siRNAs were introduced into Huh7 cells using the Rfect V2 siRNA Transfection Reagent (11041, Baidai, China). For each reaction, 5 µL of siRNA was combined with 100 µL of transfection buffer to prepare mixture A, while 5 µL of the reagent was diluted with 100 µL of the same buffer to form mixture B. These two preparations were gently mixed and maintained at ambient temperature for 30 min to facilitate complex formation. Subsequently, cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded, and the transfection complex was uniformly added once the cell confluence reached approximately 50%. After 24 h of incubation, the transfection efficiency was evaluated using real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR). The siRNA sequences employed are as follows:

  CSF1-1 siRNA:5’-GAUCCAGUGUGCUACCUUATT-3’;CSF1-2 siRNA:5’-GCUGCUUCACCAAGGAUUATT-3’;CSF1-3 siRNA:5’-CGAGCGAGCGAGCGAGCGATT-3’.


#### Reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) assay
  After transfection, total RNA was extracted using a Total RNA Extraction Kit (R1200, Solarbio, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The concentration and integrity of RNA were determined based on the absorbance ratio at 260/280 nm using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Subsequently, 1 µg of RNA was converted into complementary DNA (cDNA) with the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (RR037A, Takara, Japan) in accordance with the supplier’s recommendations. The reverse transcription reaction (20 µL system) was performed under the following temperature program: 42 °C for 2 min, 37 °C for 10 min, and 85 °C for 5 s, followed by storage of the synthesized cDNA at − 20 °C. Quantitative PCR was conducted on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, USA) using the PrimeScript™ FAST RT Master Mix containing gDNA Eraser (RR092A, Takara, Japan). Each 25 µL amplification system included 12.5 µL 2× SYBR Green Master Mix, 1 µL of each primer (10 µM), 2 µL cDNA, and 8.5 µL RNase-free water. Thermal cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 10 s. Melting curve analysis (65–95 °C) was performed to verify amplification specificity. Relative mRNA levels were normalized to GAPDH using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

  CSF1 Forward: 5’-AGCATCTTCTCCACTCCCTG-3’,CSF1 Reverse: 5’-GATGAGATCCTGGGAGAGCC-3’;GAPDH Forward: 5’-AATCCCATCACCATCTTCCA-3’,GAPDH Reverse: 5’-TGGACTCCACGACGTACTCA-3’.


#### Western blotting assay
  Following 24 h of exposure to siRNA, Punicalagin (HY-N0063, MCE, USA), or Evoden (T20419, TargetMol, USA), Huh7 cells were disrupted using RIPA lysis buffer (R0010, Solarbio, China) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Servicebio, China) to isolate total cellular proteins. Cell lysates were subsequently centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 ℃, and the clarified supernatants were harvested. Protein levels were determined using a BCA assay kit (BL521A, Biosharp, China). Based on the measured concentrations, samples were mixed with 5× SDS loading buffer and heated at 100 ℃ for 10 min to denature proteins. Equal protein amounts were separated by SDS-PAGE and electroblotted onto PVDF membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore, USA) via a wet-transfer method. The membranes were blocked with 5% skimmed milk for 1 h to prevent nonspecific interactions and then incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with primary antibodies against CXCR3 (26756-1-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech, China), CSF1 (A10754, ABclonal, China) and GAPDH (AC036, 1:50000, ABclonal, China). After TBST rinsing, membranes were exposed for 1 h at room temperature to HRP-linked secondary antibodies (SA00001-2, goat anti-rabbit IgG, 1:5000, Proteintech, China). Finally, protein signals were visualized using an ECL chemiluminescent detection kit (BL520A, Biosharp, China), and images were captured on a Bio-Rad imaging platform (USA).


#### Immunohistochemistry (IHC)
  Six HCC clinical tissue samples were obtained from the Affiliated Hospital of Inner Mongolia Medical University. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Inner Mongolia Medical University (Ethics Approval No. YKD202403111) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant ethical guidelines. Paraffin-embedded human HCC tissue sections were incubated at 60 °C for 30–60 min to ensure proper adhesion, subsequently rehydrated through two changes of xylene (5 min each) and a graded ethanol series (100%, 95%, 80%, and 70%; 3 min each), ending with distilled water. Antigen retrieval was conducted by heating the sections in Tris-EDTA buffer in a microwave oven for 20 min. After cooling to room temperature, the sections were washed three times with PBS (5 min each). Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min at room temperature, followed by blocking with 5% normal goat serum for 30 min at room temperature. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber with anti-CSF1 primary antibody (dilution 1:400). After three washes with PBS, HRP-conjugated secondary antibody was applied and incubated for 30–60 min at room temperature. The color reaction was developed using DAB for 1–5 min and stopped by rinsing with running tap water. Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin for 1 min, then rinsed, dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and mounted with coverslips. Images were acquired using a light microscope, and the relative expression of CSF1 in HCC tissues was quantified with Saiviewer software (V2.1.1). (see Supplementary Material 2 for further details on experimental procedures)


### Data visualization and statistical analysis
  Statistical and visual analysis of bioinformatics-related data were performed using R software (V4.4.0). For analyzes involving multiple hypothesis testing, correction was applied using either the Bonferroni or Benjamini-Hochberg method. Validation experiment data were analyzed statistically with SPSS (V 17.0). All experiments were conducted independently at least in triplicate. Results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. To compare two groups, an independent-samples t-test was applied, while one-way ANOVA was used to evaluate differences across multiple groups, followed by post hoc pairwise comparisons using the least significant difference method. A P-value < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.


## Results

### The transcriptomic profile of HCC cells revealed by scRNA-seq data
  To investigate the compositional characteristics of immune cells in the HCC TIME, we analyzed the scRNA-seq dataset GSE149614 from the GEO database. The top 2000 most variable genes were selected, and HCC sample cells were reclustered using Seurat and UMAP dimensionality reduction algorithms, identifying 45 major cell populations (Fig. 2A). According to the characteristic expression profiles of established marker genes, these cell clusters were identified as B lymphocytes, endothelial cells, hepatocytes, fibroblasts, myeloid populations, T cells, and natural killer cells. The accuracy of cell annotation was validated by expression heatmaps of cluster-specific marker genes (Fig. 2B-D). When tumor and adjacent non-tumor samples from GSE149614 were separately subjected to dimensionality reduction and clustering, tumor tissues exhibited a marked decrease in NK cell proportion (Fig. 2E). To further explore the heterogeneity of NK cells, we extracted the NK cell cluster and performed reclustering, ultimately identifying 27 NK cell subpopulations (Fig. 2F). Based on their gene expression profiles, these subpopulations were categorized into six functionally distinct subsets (Fig. 2G), among which the sub-1 subset was found to be predominant in tumor tissues (Fig. 2H).

### Construction and overall characterization of communication networks between NK cell subpopulations
  To elucidate the potential intercellular communication mechanisms underlying NKEX in HCC, we systematically analyzed ligand–receptor interactions between NK cells and six other types of tumor-infiltrating cells, as well as among six distinct NK cell subclusters, using the CellChat package. The results revealed extensive and complex communication networks between NK cells and hepatocytes, myeloid cells, T cells, endothelial cells, B cells, and fibroblasts (Fig. 3A, B). Subcluster analysis further demonstrated marked heterogeneity in NK cell communication patterns, with NK cell subcluster 1 (sub-1) acting as a major interaction hub (Fig. 3C, D). As a principal signal sender, sub-1 exhibited high communication probabilities across multiple signaling pathways, particularly occupying a central regulatory position in major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I), MHC-II, CD99, C-type lectin (CLEC), and macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) signaling pathways (Fig. 3E, F). Specifically, the MHC-I signaling pathway showed the highest interaction probability between sub-1 and sub-2 (0.21). In contrast, MHC-II signaling was predominantly enhanced among subclusters 3, 5, and 6, with communication probabilities consistently exceeding 0.15, and appeared to be largely independent of sub-1 involvement. In addition, CLEC signaling was mainly enriched between sub-1 and sub-2, whereas the CD99 pathway formed a distinctive closed-loop interaction among sub-1, sub-4, and sub-5, suggesting a potential role in regulating NK cell migration and intercellular adhesion. Notably, MIF signaling was broadly activated across all NK cell subclusters, with sub-1 participating in multiple signaling connections, highlighting its potential key role in establishing an immunosuppressive and exhaustion-prone tumor microenvironment (Fig. 3G). Similar signaling distributions were also validated by circos plot analysis (Fig. 3H), further supporting the robustness and reliability of these findings.

  Overall, these results indicate that sub-1 establishes extensive and tight interaction networks with other NK cell subclusters through multiple immune-related signaling pathways, exerting a dominant role in MHC-, CLEC-, CD99- and MIF-associated signal transduction. These interactions may contribute to NK cell functional exhaustion and tumor immune evasion by modulating cell migration, immune recognition, and immunosuppressive mechanisms.


### Reconstruction and identification of TFs regulatory networks in NK cells
  To elucidate the transcriptional regulatory mechanisms underlying NK cell heterogeneity, we systematically reconstructed TFs regulatory networks in NK cells using the pySCENIC framework. The results revealed pronounced transcriptional regulatory heterogeneity among different NK cell subclusters, with sub-1 accounting for the largest proportion among all NK cell subsets (Fig. 4A). Based on the regulon specificity score (RSS), TF activities were ranked across subclusters, identifying EOMES, ELF1, CREM, IRF1, and FOSL2 as exhibiting the most significant differences in regulatory activity (RSS > 0.01, P < 0.05) (Fig. 4B). Further analysis demonstrated that EOMES and CREM were expressed at relatively low levels in NK cells, suggesting that their regulatory signals may originate from other immune cell types within the TIME. In contrast, ELF1, IRF1, and FOSL2 showed high expression levels in NK cells, indicating that they may serve as key transcriptional regulators mediating NKEX (Fig. 4C-G). Based on TF regulatory databases, a total of 8,208 potential target genes were identified (Fig. 4H). Consistent with previous studies, these highly active transcription factors have been widely reported to be closely associated with tumor proliferation, invasion, and drug resistance in HCC and other cancer types [26–30], supporting the potential clinical relevance of our findings.

### Identification and characterization of NKEX-related regulatory genes in HCC
  In the present research, RNA-sequencing profiles together with related clinical data from 378 HCC cases were retrieved from the TCGA cohort. To improve the reliability of subsequent analyses, differential gene expression analysis comparing tumor versus adjacent normal tissue samples was performed by applying four established methods: Wilcoxon, limma, edgeR, and DESeq2. Through intersection analysis, 1,117 consistently DEGs were identified (Fig. 5A). A heatmap was constructed to display the top 50 genes exhibiting the most pronounced upregulation and downregulation (Fig. 5B). To investigate the potential functional roles of these DEGs in NKEX-related pathways, a GSVA enrichment score was calculated for each patient using four predefined NKEX-related gene sets extracted from the relevant literature [31, 32]. This yielded pathway activity scores for all samples. Subsequently, the GSVA scores were incorporated as trait data, combined with the DEG expression matrix, for WGCNA. In the WGCNA process, the soft-thresholding parameter was set to β = 10, resulting in a scale-free topology fit index of approximately 0.9, thereby satisfying the requirements for network establishment (Fig. 5C). Using dynamic tree cutting, seven co-expression modules were detected, with each module corresponding to a cluster of genes under coordinated regulation (Fig. 5D). The correlation analysis between modules and traits indicated that the yellow module showed the highest positive correlation with NKEX pathway activity (Fig. 5E), thus being designated as the key module. To further investigate the functional characteristics within this module, a PPI network was constructed. By applying a screening threshold of degree > 10, a highly interconnected subnetwork containing several core nodes was identified (Fig. 5F). Among these nodes, genes such as CSF1, TREM2, and MS4A6A exhibited the highest connectivity, suggesting their potential role as hub regulators mediating NK cell exhaustion in the HCC microenvironment.

### Risk prognostic modeling using NKEX marker genes
  To identify potential regulatory genes within this module, genome-wide TF-target genes were predicted using the online tool TF Target Finder (https://jingle.shinyapps.io/TF_Target_Finder/), yielding 8,208 putative TF-target interactions. Intersection analysis between these targets and the yellow module’s eigengenes ultimately identified 73 candidate key genes highly associated with NKEX (Fig. 6A). To enhance the prognostic predictive performance of NKEX-related candidate genes identified by WGCNA and to reduce the risk of model overfitting, univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed on 73 candidate genes using survival data from the TCGA-LIHC cohort. The results identified 13 genes that were significantly associated with OS (Fig. 6B). These genes were subsequently subjected to LASSO regression analysis with ten-fold cross-validation. Based on the minimum criterion, the optimal λ value was determined, and four genes with non-zero regression coefficients—AKR1B1, SMS, CSF1, and CFL1—were ultimately retained (Fig. 6C, D). These genes were defined as NKEX-related prognostic signature genes.Patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups according to the median risk score. Heatmap analysis demonstrated that the expression levels of these four signature genes were significantly higher in the high-risk group than in the low-risk group (Fig. 6E).

  A risk score model was then constructed based on the expression levels of the four genes and their corresponding regression coefficients. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed that patients in the high-risk group had significantly shorter OS compared with those in the low-risk group (Fig. 7A). To facilitate individualized prognostic assessment, a comprehensive nomogram integrating the risk score and clinical characteristics (age, sex, and tumor stage) was established (Fig. 7B). Calibration curves indicated good agreement between predicted and observed survival probabilities at 1, 3, and 5 years, closely aligning with the ideal 45° reference line (Fig. 7C). The ROC curve analysis was further conducted to evaluate the predictive performance of the model. The AUC for the risk score alone was 0.633 (Fig. 7D). Time-dependent ROC analysis revealed that the AUC values for predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS were 0.759, 0.617, and 0.625, respectively (Fig. 7E). Notably, the nomogram model integrating clinical factors and the risk score exhibited superior predictive performance, with an AUC of 0.723, outperforming traditional clinical indicators such as TNM staging (Fig. 7F). External validation using the ICGC cohort confirmed the robustness of the model. Multicollinearity diagnostics showed that the VIF for all four genes was below 2.0 (Fig. 7G). Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed that high-risk patients had significantly poorer overall survival than low-risk patients (Fig. 7H). Furthermore, the AUC values for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival were 0.652, 0.653, and 0.744, respectively (Fig. 7I), demonstrating the stable and accurate predictive performance of the signature.

  To further explore the biological basis underlying prognostic stratification, somatic mutation profiles of TCGA-LIHC patients were analyzed. The high-risk group exhibited higher mutation frequencies in TP53 (32.0%), CTNNB1 (28.7%), and TTN (27.1%), which are consistent with aggressive tumor phenotypes (Fig. 7J). In addition, Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) analysis further demonstrated that the high-risk group displayed a more pronounced immunosuppressive state and a lower predicted response rate to immunotherapy (Fig. 7K), providing additional support for the biological relevance of the NKEX-related prognostic model.


### The NKEX signature gene CSF1 drives HCC malignancy and might regulate NK cell activity by modulating the CXCL10/CXCR3 axis
  To validate the functional relevance of the screened NKEX-associated marker genes, CSF1 was prioritized as a representative marker for subsequent experimental verification based on relevant literature [33]. Notably, the expression level of CSF1 in HCC tissues was significantly higher than in normal liver tissues (Fig. 8A). Consistently, IHC analysis of human HCC revealed strong cytoplasmic and membranous staining of CSF1 in tumor cells, whereas weak or negligible staining was observed in adjacent non-tumorous tissue (Fig. 8B, C). These findings collectively confirm that CSF1 is markedly upregulated in HCC and may play a pivotal role in tumor progression.

  To further investigate the biological role of CSF1 in HCC, three independent siRNA sequences targeting CSF1 were transfected into Huh7 cells. qRT-PCR analysis revealed that si-CSF1-2 exhibited the highest knockdown efficiency at the mRNA level (Fig. 8D) and was thus selected for subsequent experiments. We then performed single-gene enrichment analysis of CSF1. GO analysis showed that CSF1 was significantly enriched in immune-related biological processes, including monocyte differentiation and activation, macrophage proliferation, differentiation and chemotaxis. At the cellular component level, the associated genes were predominantly localized to the endoplasmic reticulum lumen. At the molecular function level, CSF1 was significantly enriched in cytokine activity, cytokine receptor binding, and receptor ligand activity, suggesting an important role in intercellular signal transduction (Fig. 8E). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further demonstrated that CSF1 was significantly involved in multiple immune- and inflammation-related signaling pathways, including cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, TNF signaling pathway, and MAPK signaling pathway, indicating that CSF1 may participate in immune regulation, inflammatory responses, and tumor-associated signal transduction processes (Fig. 8F).

  To evaluate the biological effects of CSF1 silencing, AO/EB double staining was first performed to assess cell viability and apoptosis. Silencing of CSF1 significantly increased the proportion of apoptotic cells compared to the si-NC control, indicating impaired cell survival (Fig. 8G). Furthermore, Transwell migration assay and wound healing assay demonstrated that CSF1 knockdown robustly inhibited Huh7 cell migration, which was confirmed by quantitative analysis (Fig. 8H, I). We then explored the mechanistic link between CSF1 and NK cell regulation by focusing on the CXCL10/CXCR3 axis, a critical pathway involved in NK cell recruitment and activation. ELISA showed that CSF1 silencing elevated CXCL10 secretion in Huh7 cells (Fig. 8J). Correspondingly, Western blot analysis revealed that co-culture with CSF1-silenced Huh7 cells up-regulated CXCR3 protein expression in NK cells (Fig. 8K, see Supplementary Material 1 for the original figures of the results). Furthermore, flow cytometric analysis indicated that inhibition of CSF1 expression increased the proportion of NK cells expressing NKG2D+ (an activating receptor) while decreasing the proportion of NK cells expressing TIGIT+ (an inhibitory receptor) (Fig. 8L). Consistently, ELISA demonstrated a significant increase in tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) secretion in the co-culture supernatant of the CSF1-siRNA-2 group (Fig. 8M). In summary, these results suggest that CSF1 promotes HCC cell survival and migration and may suppress NK cell activity via the CXCL10/CXCR3 signaling axis.


### Identification of CSF-targeting compounds and the FOSL2-CSF1 transcriptional regulatory axis
  Reverse network pharmacology analysis identified five candidate small-molecule compounds with the potential to modulate CSF1 expression: crotonaldehyde, evoden, punicalagin, hexose, and arecoline (Fig. 9A). To evaluate the potential interactions between these candidates and the CSF1 protein, molecular docking simulations were performed. The results suggested that punicalagin and evoden could stably occupy the predicted active binding pocket of CSF1, yielding minimum binding energies of -11.7 kcal/mol and − 8.5 kcal/mol, respectively (Fig. 9B). Detailed interaction modeling indicated that both compounds formed multiple hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions with key amino acid residues (Fig. 9C, D), which may contribute to the stability of the ligand-protein complexes. While these computational findings suggest a high binding affinity, the definitive regulatory roles of these compounds require further experimental validation.

  To evaluate the regulatory effects of candidate compounds on CSF1 expression, the IC50 were first determined using CCK-8 assays. The IC50 values for Punicalagin and Evoden were 64.78 µM and 13.56 µM, respectively (Fig. 9E, F). Accordingly, treatment concentrations of 50 µM for Punicalagin and 10 µM for Evoden were selected for subsequent functional validations. RT-qPCR analysis revealed that both drugs significantly reduced the mRNA levels of CSF1 compared to the control group (Fig. 9G, H). Consistently, Western blot analysis further confirmed that both compounds markedly downregulated CSF1 expression at the protein level (Fig. 9I, see Supplementary Material 1 for the original figures of the results). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Punicalagin and Evoden are potent inhibitors of CSF1 expression.

  To elucidate the upstream mechanism underlying CSF1 inhibition, its transcriptional regulation was investigated. Previous single-cell analysis identified five transcription factors (EOMES, ELF1, CREM, IRF1, and FOSL2) associated with NK cell exhaustion (Fig. 4). Among these, FOSL2, a core component of the AP-1 transcription factor complex, was particularly prominent and emerged as a key candidate regulator of CSF1. A review of the literature revealed that existing studies have confirmed that inflammation-related transcription factors, including FOSL2, can regulate CSF1 within the TIME [34]. Furthermore, predictive analysis identified a potential AP-1 binding site at approximately − 507 bp within the CSF1 promoter region (Fig. 9J), suggesting a direct transcriptional regulatory role for FOSL2. This conclusion was further corroborated by a significant positive correlation between FOSL2 and CSF1 expression levels in HCC samples (Fig. 9K). Taken together, these findings suggest the existence of a FOSL2-CSF1 regulatory axis in HCC, providing a theoretical basis for the mechanism underlying NK cell exhaustion and pointing to a clear direction for future research.


## Discussion
  HCC remains a highly aggressive malignancy with a poor prognosis, largely due to the fact that most patients are diagnosed at advanced stages, where treatment options are limited [35]. In recent years, the TIME has been recognized as a key factor driving HCC progression and therapeutic resistance. An immunosuppressive TIME facilitates tumor immune evasion and directly impacts the efficacy of targeted therapies and immunotherapies [36]. However, the profound heterogeneity and complex cellular crosstalk within the TIME contribute to the overall modest benefits of immunotherapy in solid tumors such as HCC [37, 38].

  To characterize the dysfunctional immune landscape of the HCC TIME, we analyzed the GSE149614 scRNA-seq dataset. Dimensionality reduction and clustering revealed a pronounced state of NKEX, marked by a significant reduction in NK cell abundance in HCC tissues compared to adjacent non-tumorous counterparts. Subclustering further delineated six distinct NK cell subpopulations. Cell-cell communication analysis identified MHC-I, MHC-II, CLEC, CD99, and MIF as the dominant signaling pathways mediating interactions among these subsets. Our data suggests that dysregulation within these pathways fundamental to NK cell education and cytotoxicity may underpin the exhausted phenotype. Specifically, MHC-I interactions, critical for classical NK cell education and functional maturation. Of these receptors, Ly49 in mice and KIR in humans have been the most thoroughly studied. NK cell subsets deficient in inhibitory receptors recognizing MHC-I generally display a “hyporesponsive” phenotype, a process known as classical NK cell education, which is essential for their differentiation, maturation, and maintenance of self-tolerance [39]. The CLEC network primarily comprises CLEC2C/CLEC2B/CLEC2D-KLRB1 receptor-ligand pairs along with other members of the C-type lectin superfamily, which are largely expressed in non-malignant cells. These genes exhibit elevated expression in NK cells and are crucial for modulating NK cell function and cytotoxic capacity [40]. Diminished CLEC pathway activity may result in a reduction of NK and NKT cell populations. Notably, the study by Z. Husak demonstrated that CD99 functions as a strong ligand to potentiate NK cell cytotoxicity, primarily through the upregulation of HSP70 in leukemia cells [41]. Moreover, MIF derived from macrophages exhibits broad expression across multiple cell types and tissues. It rapidly activates downstream MAPK and PI3K signaling pathways in response to inflammation by binding to heterodimeric complexes like CXCR2, CXCR4, and ACKR3 through independent interactions with CD74. This mechanism is not only closely related to tumorigenesis and growth but may also promote tumor metastasis and dissemination [42].

  The dysregulation of these signaling pathways strongly suggests a state of functional impairment within the NK cell compartment. Specifically, subclustering identified the sub-1 subpopulation as the predominant subset, exhibiting the most pronounced exhaustion signature. Transcriptional regulatory network analysis of this subset identified five key exhaustion-related TFs EOMES, ELF, CREM, IRF1, and FOSL2 providing mechanistic insights into the establishment and maintenance of the exhausted state in HCC. Among these TFs, EOMES is a master regulator of NK cell maturation and is closely associated with terminal differentiation and exhausted phenotypes [26]; ELF family members participate in lymphocyte development and immune tolerance [43]; CREM acts as a transcriptional repressor in chronic immune activation and has been linked to T and NK cell dysfunction [44]; IRF1 plays a critical role in interferon signaling and immune stress responses [45]; while FOSL2, a component of the AP-1 complex, is involved in inflammatory signaling and immune suppression within the tumor microenvironment [46].

  To translate these mechanistic insights into a clinically applicable prognostic tool and identify central effector genes, we sought to define the downstream targets of this NKEX-associated TF network. By using TF target prediction tools, a total of 8,208 potential target genes were predicted. By integrating TCGA-LIHC data with the GSVA algorithm, we identified gene modules significantly linked to NKEX. Through intersection analysis with target genes of the aforementioned TFs, a total of 73 candidate genes were initially screened, and subsequent multivariate Cox and Lasso regression analyzes revealed four statistically significant key genes: AKR1B1, SMS, CSF1, and CFL1. Based on these four genes, a prognostic model for HCC was constructed, which effectively stratified patients according to their different overall survival outcomes. The predictive accuracy of the model was further validated by ROC curve and calibration curve analyzes. A nomogram integrating the NKEX risk score with clinical parameters was also developed, providing a visual tool for comprehensive survival prediction.

  All four identified genes have established roles in HCC progression and immune modulation. AKR1B1 promotes the Warburg effect and metastasis via the Akt/STAT3/c-Myc axis [47, 48]. while the hypoxia-responsive gene CFL1 facilitates HCC progression through PLD1/AKT signaling and regulates NK cell infiltration via actin remodeling [49–51]. High SMS expression is associated with M2 macrophage infiltration and CD8 + T cell exhaustion, suggesting a broad role in immune dysfunction [52–54]. Crucially, CSF1 emerged as the most significant candidate due to its paramount prognostic value and direct role in shaping the immunosuppressive TIME. CSF1 accelerates cancer progression through the CSF1/CSF1R axis and suppresses anti-tumor immunity by inducing M2 macrophage polarization [55–57]. Moreover, CSF1 enhances the expression of inhibitory receptors (e.g., TIGIT, NKG2A) on NK cells via TAM-mediated suppression, directly driving functional exhaustion [58]. Given its central role in exacerbating NKEX and its superior prognostic power in our cohorts, CSF1 was selected as the focal point for subsequent bioinformatics analysis and experimental validation.

  Building on the selection of CSF1 as the core target, we proceeded to characterize its expression pattern, and functional impact in HCC. Validation using the TCGA dataset and clinical immunohistochemical staining confirmed significant overexpression of CSF1 in HCC tissues compared to adjacent non-tumor tissues. Functional assays demonstrated that CSF1 silencing in Huh7 cells markedly impaired cell viability and migration while promoting apoptosis, underscoring its cell-intrinsic oncogenic role. Beyond these effects, single-gene enrichment and mechanistic analyses suggested that CSF1 modulates the TIME via the CXCL10/CXCR3 signaling axis, potentially governing immune cell recruitment and activation. To screen for therapeutics targeting this signaling axis, this study employed reverse network pharmacology and molecular docking techniques. Among the five candidate compounds, subsequent molecular docking analysis showed that punicalagin had the lowest binding energy to evoden and significantly inhibited CSF1 mRNA expression in Huh7 cells. Punicalagin is a hydrolyzable tannin widely distributed in pomegranate leaves and peels and exhibits diverse biological activities, including antioxidant, antitumor, and anti-inflammatory effects. Previous studies have shown that punicalagin can induce apoptosis by inhibiting the ERK and NF-κB signaling pathways and suppress the invasive capacity of gastric cancer cells [59]. In addition, in breast cancer models, treatments containing punicalagin have been reported to induce reactive oxygen species-mediated apoptosis [60]. According to the Herb database, Evoden, also known as raloxifene, is a clinically approved selective estrogen receptor modulator primarily used for the treatment of postmenopausal osteoporosis. In pancreatic cancer, evoden has been reported to inhibit tumor growth by interfering with the ERβ and IL-6/gp130/STAT3 signaling axis [61]. Moreover, in glioblastoma, evoden suppresses tumor cell proliferation and migration by targeting PKN1 and enhances the sensitivity of GBM cells to the chemotherapeutic agent temozolomide [62].

  In addition, to understand the upstream regulation of CSF1, we performed transcription factor analysis, which identified FOSL2 as the transfactor most strongly associated with CSF1. Correlation analysis further confirmed a significant positive correlation between FOSL2 and CSF1 expression in HCC samples, strongly suggesting that CSF1 may be transcriptionally regulated by FOSL2. Intriguingly, both punicalagin and evoden are known to modulate AP-1 activity, of which FOSL2 is a core component. Punicalagin activates the PDK1/p90RSK pathway, leading to AP-1 activation in macrophages [63]. Similarly, Evoden regulates gene expression via AP-1 tethering mechanisms involving ERα and JNK. We therefore hypothesize that these compounds may suppress CSF1 by interfering with the AP-1 complex (likely via FOSL2) at the CSF1 promoter, thereby repressing its transcription. This model logically connects their known mechanisms of action to our newly defined FOSL2-CSF1 regulatory axis. Taken together, punicalagin and evoden emerge as promising therapeutic candidates for HCC, potentially acting through targeting the CSF1-related pathway and modulating the TIME. However, it should be noted that the proposed regulatory interaction between FOSL2 and CSF1 is currently inferred from bioinformatic analysis and transcriptional regulatory networks. The absence of direct binding assays, such as ChIP-qPCR, remains a limitation of the present study. Future experimental validation is required to definitively establish the biochemical basis of this regulatory axis.

  Despite these insights, several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, while we identified candidate therapeutic agents such as punicalagin and evoden through multi-omics integration, their efficacy and safety within the HCC TIME remain to be validated in vivo. Due to our current reliance on bioinformatic predictions and single-cell line (Huh7) experiments, future research utilizing a broader panel of HCC models and established animal studies is essential to characterize the systemic immunomodulatory roles and translational potential of these compounds. Second, although the prognostic significance of the four-gene signature (AKR1B1, SMS, CSF1, and CFL1) was confirmed, the precise molecular cascades through which these genes drive NK cell dysfunction yond the role of CSF1 remain largely unexplored. Subsequent investigations should prioritize elucidating the downstream pathways of these genes to provide a more robust theoretical foundation for therapeutic strategies aimed at reversing NK cell exhaustion.

  In conclusion, by integrating single-cell and bulk transcriptomic analyses, this study not only deepens our understanding of NKEX in the HCC microenvironment but also identifies CSF1 as a promising immunomodulatory target and potential therapeutic candidates. The findings and frameworks presented here pave the way for future research aimed at restoring anti-tumor immunity and improving clinical outcomes for HCC patients.
