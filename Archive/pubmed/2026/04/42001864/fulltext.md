# Recombinant IL-38 Alleviates Temporomandibular Joint Synovial Inflammation via IL-1R1-NF-κB-IL1β Pathway


## Introduction and aims
  Interleukin (IL)-38 has been identified as an anti-inflammatory cytokine; however, its specific role in temporomandibular joint (TMJ) synovial inflammation and the identity of its functional receptors remain elusive. This study aimed to investigate the therapeutic efficacy of IL-38 in TMJ synovial inflammation and to elucidate its potential receptor mechanisms.


## Methods
  A rat model of temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis (TMJOA) was established via bilateral TMJ injection of mono-iodoacetate (MIA). Fifteen Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats were randomly allocated into three groups: sham (PBS), model (MIA), and treatment (MIA +human recombinant IL-38, hIL-38). Protein BLAST analysis was utilized to predict IL-38 function and receptor affinity. Synovial histopathology was assessed using HE staining. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was employed to quantify inflammatory gene expression, while immunohistochemistry (IHC) was utilized to detect inflammatory proteins and IL-1 receptor type 1 (IL-1R1) expression. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network and protein-protein docking analyses were performed to predict the interaction between IL-38 and IL-1R1.


## Results
  IL-38 exhibited 43% identity and 55% positivity with interleukin-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1Ra), its precursor, and its isoforms X1 and X2. HE staining demonstrated that hIL-38 significantly reduced synovial inflammatory cell infiltration. IHC staining revealed that hIL-38 inhibited macrophage infiltration (CD68⁺) and suppressed the expression of iNOS and COX-2, thereby attenuating synovial inflammation. Docking and PPI analyses corroborated a direct interaction between IL-38 and IL-1R1. Furthermore, IHC staining indicated that IL-38 down-regulated the protein levels of IL-1R1, NF-κB p65, and IL-1β.


## Conclusions
  IL-38 functions as a novel anti-inflammatory cytokine that alleviates TMJ synovial inflammation, potentially by antagonizing IL-1R1 and blocking the NF-κB signalling cascade.


## Clinical Relevance
  IL-38 may offer a novel therapeutic strategy for alleviating synovial inflammation in TMJOA.


## Introduction
  The temporomandibular joint (TMJ) is a critical synovial articulation indispensable for mastication, respiration, communication, and facial expression.1 Temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis (TMJOA) is a prevalent and debilitating disorder characterized by synovitis, cartilage degeneration, and bone erosion, significantly diminishing quality of life.2 Synovial inflammation serves as a key pathogenic driver of TMJOA, with early manifestations including inflammatory cell infiltration, synovial hyperplasia, and vascularization. Inflammatory cytokines released by the synovial tissue drive cartilage destruction and joint degeneration.3 Despite its clinical significance, the molecular mechanisms underlying synovitis in TMJOA remain incompletely understood.

  Interleukin-38 (IL-38), a novel anti-inflammatory cytokine, has been demonstrated to modulate inflammatory responses in various diseases, including myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury4 and rheumatoid arthritis.5 In our previous study, we demonstrated that IL-38 exerts an anti-inflammatory effect by down-regulating GLUT1 in complete Freund’s adjuvant (CFA)-induced TMJ synovitis.6 However, the receptors through which IL-38 mediates these effects in TMJOA have not been identified, and the identity of the physiological IL-38 receptor remains controversial. Therefore, the present study investigated the role of IL-38 in mono-iodoacetate (MIA)-induced TMJ synovitis and explored its underlying mechanisms.

  Previous investigations have posited IL-36R, IL-1R1, and IL-1RAPL1 as potential receptors for IL-38, though conclusive evidence remains limited.7 Among these candidates, IL-1R1 warrants particular attention due to its well-established role in mediating IL-1α and IL-1β signalling- pathways, which are central to the initiation and amplification of TMJ inflammatory cascades.8 Notably, IL-1R1 is a cell-surface receptor of the interleukin-1 receptor family, tightly regulated by IL-1Ra. Upon ligand binding, IL-1R1 recruits the co-receptor IL-1RAcP (IL-1 receptor accessory protein) to form a heterotrimeric complex, initiating downstream signalling.9,10 Research has demonstrated an association between IL-1R1 and temporomandibular joint disorders in humans11 and IL-1Ra has shown durable clinical benefit in human OA.12 Recent studies also suggest that IL-38 may interact with IL-1R1 to play a pivotal role in central nervous system diseases.13 Nevertheless, the precise mechanism of action of IL-38 and its receptor specificity in the TMJ remain unknown.

  In this study, comprehensive sequence alignment revealed that IL-38 shares higher primary-structure identity with IL-1Ra than with any other IL-1 family member. Given the structural and functional homology between IL-38 and IL-1Ra, we hypothesized that IL-38 interacts with IL-1R1 to down-regulate the inflammatory cascade, ameliorating synovitis in TMJOA.


## Methods

### TMJOA model establishment
  Fifteen 8-week-old male SD rats were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Xi'an Jiaotong University. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care Committee of Xi'an Jiaotong University (XJTUAE2024-2351). Animals were selected using simple random sampling and provided with standard laboratory chow ad libitum. The rats were randomly allocated into three groups (n = 5): PBS group, MIA group, and MIA + human recombinant IL-38 (MIA + hIL-38) group. To induce TMJOA, rats received bilateral intra-articular injections of MIA (2 mg in 50 μL PBS; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) or an equivalent volume of PBS. In the treatment group, bilateral intra-articular injections of recombinant human IL-38 (hIL-38, 25 μg/mL, 50 μL; Sino Biological, Beijing, China) were administered 24 h prior to MIA injection, and subsequently on days 3, 6, 9, and 12. The control and MIA groups received equivalent volumes of PBS following the same schedule. No adverse events were observed in any animals. On 15 days following the initial MIA injection, animals were sacrificed and bilateral TMJ synovial tissues were collected. All animal experiments were conducted in strict adherence to the ARRIVE guidelines.


### Histological and immunohistochemical staining
  Synovial histopathology was evaluated using HE Staining (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). For IHC, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-CD68 (Servicebio, Wuhan, China), anti-iNOS (Servicebio, Wuhan, China), anti-COX-2 (Proteintech, Wuhan, China), anti-IL-1R1 (SAB, Nanjing, China), anti-NF-κB p65 (Bioss, Beijing, China), and anti-IL-1β (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). All procedures were performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.


### Sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis of IL-38
  The amino acid sequences of IL-1F10 (IL-38) from various species were retrieved from NCBI. Sequence alignment was performed using MEGA11, and the results were visualized with ESPript 3.2. For phylogenetic analysis, protein–protein BLAST (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) was employed to identify homologous sequences among the interleukin family members, and a phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Fast Minimum Evolution method.


### Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) analysis
  Tissues were harvested at 15 days. Total RNA was extracted using RNAiso (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). qPCR was performed on a PCR system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, USA) utilizing Cham Q Universal SYBR Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), strictly following the manufacturer’s instructions. Specific primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1.


### Protein-protein molecular docking analysis
  The 3D structures of IL1F10 (Uniprot ID: D4A9I5) and IL-1R1 (Uniprot ID: Q02955) were retrieved from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (https://www.uniprot.org/). Molecular docking was performed using the H-dock online docking server (http://hdock.phys.hust.edu.cn/) and analysed by PLIP (https://plip-tool.biotec.tu-dresden.de/plip-web/plip/index).14


### Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network construction
  To further evaluate the functional interactions between IL-1R1 and IL1F10, a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using the STRING tool (http://www.string-db.org).


### Statistical analysis
  All data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v9.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). Normality of residuals was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test for all variables across the three experimental groups. Homogeneity of variances was verified using the Brown–Forsythe test (P > .05). Differences among multiple groups were evaluated using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. For body weight data, two-way repeated measures ANOVA was conducted, followed by one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test at individual time points. A two-tailed P value <.05 was considered statistically significant: *P < .05, **P < .01, ***P < .001, ****P < .0001.

## Discussion
  TMJOA is a pervasive degenerative disease characterized by chronic synovial inflammation and progressive structural deterioration.15 While IL-38 is recognized for its anti-inflammatory properties, its specific physiological binding partner remains a subject of controversy. Although IL-36R, IL-1R1, and IL-1RAPL1 have been proposed as potential receptors,16 no studies have yet investigated these interactions within the context of TMJOA. Phylogenetic analysis conducted in this study positioned IL-38 in close proximity to interleukin-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1Ra) proteins (Figure 2B), suggesting that IL-38 may utilize the IL-1 receptor system to exert its anti-inflammatory effects. Consequently, we hypothesised that IL-38 antagonises IL-1 signalling by interacting with IL-1R1 during TMJ synovitis.

  To test this hypothesis, we established a rat TMJOA model via intra-articular injection of MIA. Histological examination confirmed pronounced synovial hyperplasia, consistent with previous studies validating the efficacy of the MIA-induced TMJOA model.17 IL-38, a member of the interleukin-1 family, has been implicated in diverse inflammatory responses.18,19 Given the high degree of sequence homology (>82%) between human and rat IL-38 amino acid sequences (Figure 1A), human recombinant IL-38 was selected for intra-articular administration in the rat model. To predict the functional properties and potential receptor binding of IL-38, conserved sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis were performed. The results situated IL-38 in closer phylogenetic proximity to the interleukin-1 receptor antagonist proteins (IL-1Ra) than to other family members, such as interleukin-36 alpha, interleukin-36 beta, and interleukin-36 receptor antagonist. This evolutionary proximity suggests a shared functional lineage between IL-38 and IL-1Ra. Notably, IL-1Ra has been well-documented to ameliorate inflammation and improve outcomes in knee osteoarthritis.12 Previous research indicates that IL-38 and interleukin-1 receptor antagonist proteins share key conserved domains, likely reflecting their roles in the interleukin-1 signalling pathway.20 Based on these structural and evolutionary parallels, we hypothesized that IL-38 may utilize the IL-1 receptor system to antagonize IL-1 signalling, thereby exerting anti-inflammatory effects in TMJ synovial inflammation. Consequently, our experimental investigation focused on IL-1 receptor 1 (IL-1R1) for further experimental investigation.

  In our model, IL-38 administration significantly reduced synovial thickening, inflammatory-cell infiltration, and macrophage accumulation. Both qPCR and IHC demonstrated that IL-38 down-regulated key pro-inflammatory mediators, specifically iNOS and COX-2. These results align with previous findings regarding the anti-inflammatory efficacy of IL-38.21 To validate the binding affinity between IL-38 and IL-1R1, molecular docking and PLIP analysis were performed. The results indicated a stable interaction characterized by favourable binding free energy and docking score. Furthermore, IHC staining revealed that IL-38 down-regulated IL-1R1 expression. As IL-1R1 is the primary receptor for IL-1α and IL-1β, and prior studies have implicated IL-1R1 as a binding partner for IL-38.22 As a previous study shows, IL-38 binds IL-1R1 in the central nervous system and insulin resistance.13,23 Finally, IHC staining showed that IL-38 suppressed the expression of NF-κB and IL-1β. The IL-1R1–NF-κB–IL-1β cascade is a well characterized pro-inflammatory axis24 strongly correlated with TMJ disorders11,25; its suppression by IL-38 provides a plausible mechanism for the observed anti-inflammatory effects.

  This study provides meaningful insights while acknowledging certain considerations that can guide subsequent research. First, the investigation was confined to an in vivo rat model, and corresponding in vitro validation remains to be performed. Second, while the direct IL-38-IL-1R1 interaction is supported by STRING network, docking analysis, and IHC staining of IL-1R1, additional direct biological evidence would help consolidate this finding. Third, the absence of an IL-38-only group limits the assessment of basal or off-target effects of the recombinant protein; future safety studies may include this control to rule out low-level toxicity or homeostatic modulation in naive TMJs.

  In summary, our study provides evidence that IL-38 exerts a protective effect in TMJOA by inhibiting the IL-1R1-NF-κB-IL-1β axis and reducing synovial inflammation (Figure 6). These findings suggest that IL-38 may serve as a potential therapeutic target for TMJOA.
