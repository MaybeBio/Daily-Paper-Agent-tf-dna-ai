# β-sitosterol suppresses malignant biological behaviors and glycolysis via modulating YBX1-hnRNP K interaction in non-small cell lung cancer


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50434-0.


## Introduction
  Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is the most prevalent form of lung malignancy, characterized by heterogeneous histological subtypes and distinct molecular profiles1. Major risk factors include tobacco smoking, environmental pollutants (e.g., PM2.5), and genetic predispositions2,3. Pathologically, NSCLC is characterized by uncontrolled proliferation, evasion of apoptosis, and metastatic potential, driven by aberrant activation of oncogenic pathways and inactivation of tumor suppressors4. Contemporary approaches to treating NSCLC include surgical intervention, radiation therapy, chemotherapeutic regimens, targeted drug therapies, and immunotherapeutic strategies5. However, therapeutic outcomes are often limited by drug resistance and tumor heterogeneity, underscoring the demand for novel treatment agents. Bioactive compounds sourced from traditional Chinese medicine are increasingly investigated for their oncotherapeutic potential, offering the advantages of multi-target activity and low toxicity6.

  β-sitosterol, a widely occurring phytosterol present in various plant-based foods and botanical formulations, shares structural homology with cholesterol and is known for its cholesterol-lowering effects7. In addition to modulating lipid metabolism, accumulating studies indicate that this compound also exhibits anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antitumor properties, suggesting broad functions for various pathologies7,8. Previous investigations have further reported the promising anticancer activity of β-sitosterol across multiple types of malignancies9. Through these regulatory effects, β-sitosterol suppresses tumor cell proliferation, triggers apoptotic cell death, and restricts metastatic progression10,11. Recent research highlights its role in regulating metabolic reprogramming, particularly glycolysis, a hallmark of cancer cells12. In NSCLC, elevated glycolytic activity fuels tumor growth and confers therapeutic resistance13. Nevertheless, the specific mechanisms through which β-sitosterol influences glycolytic pathways in NSCLC cells remain incompletely understood.

  Y-box binding protein 1 (YBX1) is a gene that gives rise to a multifunctional oncoprotein participating in transcriptional activities, translational processes, and the maintenance of mRNA stability14. Demonstrating elevated expression in NSCLC, YBX1 drives tumor advancement by boosting cellular proliferative capacity, invasive potential, and resistance to chemotherapeutic agents15,16. Additionally, it exerts control over glycolytic metabolism through the stabilization of mRNA transcripts encoding key glycolytic enzymes17. Similarly, heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein K (hnRNP K), an RNA-binding protein, has been implicated in oncogenesis through its regulatory roles in transcriptional and post-transcriptional processes, thereby promoting tumor progression and metabolic adaptation18.

  While both YBX1 and hnRNP K represent promising therapeutic targets for lung cancer interventions, their specific roles in the β-sitosterol-induced suppression of NSCLC have yet to be explored19. Several lines of evidence prompted us to focus specifically on the YBX1–hnRNP K axis. First, both proteins are RNA-binding proteins that have been independently implicated in regulating glycolysis—YBX1 stabilizes mRNAs encoding glycolytic enzymes20, while hnRNP K participates in metabolic gene regulation21. Second, a previous study by Fu et al.22 demonstrated that the YBX1–hnRNP K complex directly regulates CDC25a expression and contributes to lung cancer metastasis, providing a precedent for their cooperative oncogenic function. Therefore, we aimed to test the specific hypothesis that disruption of the YBX1–hnRNP K interaction contributes to the anti-tumor effects of β-sitosterol in NSCLC. As an initial cell-based exploration, we aimed to test the specific hypothesis that the anti-tumor effects of β-sitosterol in NSCLC are correlated with the disruption of the YBX1–hnRNP K interaction.


## Methods

### Cell culture and treatment
  The A549 and H1299 cells, sourced from SUNNCELL Biotech Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China), were cultured in manufacturer-provided medium (SUNNCELL) containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin under 37 °C and 5% CO₂.

  For β-sitosterol treatment, cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 5 × 10⁵ cells per well and allowed to adhere for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were treated with β-sitosterol (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA; purity ≥ 96%) at 0, 1, 5, or 10 µM for 48 h. Vehicle control groups received 0.1% DMSO only. All experiments were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.


### Cell transfection
  Cells were transfected at 70–80% confluence using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Transfections included 2 µg of either HNRNP K-directed shRNA (sh-hnRNP K), a YBX1 overexpression plasmid, or corresponding non-targeting controls (sh-NC or empty vector) obtained from Genescript Biotech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). After 6 h, the transfection mixture was aspirated and replaced with standard growth medium. Following an additional 48 h of culture, RT-qPCR was performed to quantify hnRNP K and YBX1 levels.

  To investigate the functional importance of specific residues in YBX1–hnRNP K complex formation, cells were transfected with plasmids encoding wild-type or mutant versions of both proteins. The mutant constructs were generated by site-directed mutagenesis using wild-type plasmids as templates: YBX1 carrying an aspartic acid-to-alanine substitution at position 105 (Asp-105, MUT-YBX1) and hnRNP K carrying a lysine-to-alanine substitution at position 34 (Lys-34, MUT-hnRNP K). All mutations were confirmed by DNA sequencing. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 10⁵ cells per well and cultured for 24 h to reach 70–80% confluence prior to transfection. For each well, 2 µg of plasmid DNA (WT-YBX1, MUT-YBX1, WT-hnRNP K, MUT-hnRNP K) was diluted in Opti-MEM medium and mixed with Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection for subsequent experiments.


### Bioinformatics analysis
  To predict the interaction between YBX1 and hnRNP K, we first utilized the STRING database (v11.5, https://string-db.org/) with a minimum required interaction score of 0.4 (medium confidence) to retrieve known and predicted protein-protein associations. For structural prediction of the YBX1–hnRNP K complex, we employed the DMFold web server (Deep Multiple Sequence Alignment Based Protein Folding, v1.0, https://www.dmfold.com/), which integrates deep learning-based folding algorithms. Default parameters were applied, including the use of multiple sequence alignments generated by HHblits (v3.3) against the UniRef30 database, with three iterative search rounds. The predicted complex models were ranked by predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT) scores, and the top-ranked model was selected for visualization and downstream analysis.


### 5-Ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay
  H1299 cells cultured in 24-well plates were incubated with 10 µM EdU (Thermo Fisher) for 2 h. After fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilization with 0.5% Triton X-100, samples were stained with Alexa Fluor 488-azide under light-protected conditions. Nuclei were labeled with Hoechst 33,342 (5 µg/mL). Images from five randomly selected fields per well were acquired under a 20× objective microscope. The proportion of EdU-positive cells was quantified using ImageJ.


### Cell migration and invasion measurement
  For migration, A549 and H1299 cells in serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chamber, while the lower compartment contained medium with 10% FBS as chemoattractant. After 24 h, non-migrated cells were removed from the upper surface with a swab. Migrated cells on the lower side were fixed with 4% PFA, stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Thermo Fisher), and imaged (5 random fields per insert). For invasion assays, membranes were pre-coated with Matrigel (1:8 dilution) and incubated for 4 h before cell seeding; other steps matched the migration protocol except for a 48 h incubation.


### Flow cytometry
  Apoptosis in A549 and H1299 cells was evaluated using an Annexin V-FITC/PI detection kit (BD Biosciences). Following collection and washing with cold PBS, cells were resuspended in binding buffer and stained with 5 µL Annexin V-FITC and 5 µL PI for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Analysis was performed on a BD FACSCanto™ flow cytometer with 488 nm excitation. Fluorescence signals were collected at 530/30 nm (FITC, FL1) and 585/42 nm (PI, FL2). A total of 10,000 events were recorded per sample, and data were analyzed with FlowJo (v10.8.1).


### Glucose consumption and lactate production
  After 24 h of culture in 6-well plates, A549 and H1299 cell culture supernatant samples were collected and clarified by centrifugation. Glucose consumption and lactate production in A549 and H1299 cell culture supernatants were quantified using commercial kits (Sigma-Aldrich). Standard curves were used for quantification. All values were normalized to total protein content measured by BCA assay.


### Extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) measurement
  ECAR was measured in A549 and H1299 cells using the Seahorse XF Glycolysis Stress Test Kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Cells were seeded at 8,000 cells/well in XF96 plates and equilibrated for 45 min at 37 °C in bicarbonate-free DMEM, pH 7.4. After baseline measurement in a Seahorse XFe96 Analyzer (Agilent), cells were sequentially exposed to 10 mM glucose, 1 µM oligomycin, and 50 mM 2-deoxyglucose. Each injection was followed by three cycles of measurements (3 min mix/3 min read). ECAR values (mpH/min) were normalized to cellular protein content and analyzed using Wave Software.


### RT-qPCR
  Total RNA was extracted from A549 and H1299 cells with TRIzol reagent (Sigma). cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg RNA using the PrimeScript RT kit (Takara, Tokyo, Japan). Quantitative PCR was performed with SYBR Green Master Mix (Sigma) on a QuantStudio 5 system. The used primers were listed in Table 1. Gene expression was normalized to β-actin and analyzed via the 2–ΔΔCt method23.

### Western blot
  Total protein was extracted from A549 and H1299 cells with RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay. Samples (30 µg) were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies obtained from Thermo Fisher against CDC25a (1:1500, #PA5-99521), YBX1 (1:3000, #PA5-78094), hnRNP K (1:3000, #PA5-27522), and β-actin (1:10,000, #MA1-140). Following TBST washes, membranes were treated with a secondary antibody (1:10,000, Thermo Fisher, #31402) for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were developed with ECL substrate and quantified using Image Lab (v6.1), with β-actin serving as the loading control.


### Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)
  To examine the interaction between YBX1 and hnRNP K, Co-IP was performed in treated or transfected A549 and H1299 cells. Cells were lysed on ice in RIPA buffer with protease inhibitors for 30 min, and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. Cleared lysates were pre-incubated with Protein A/G beads (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) for 1 h, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-YBX1 antibody (2 µg/mg lysate; Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA,; #A303-230 A) or control IgG (Santa Cruz, #sc-2025). Complexes were captured with Protein A/G beads for 2 h, washed extensively, and eluted in Laemmli buffer at 95 °C. Eluates were analyzed by Western blot. Reciprocal Co-IP was carried out using anti-hnRNP K antibody (Abcam, #A300-678 A) and probing for YBX1.


### Molecular docking
  To further investigate the YBX1–hnRNP K interaction interface, molecular docking was performed using HADDOCK (v2.4, https://haddock.science.uu.nl/), an information-driven docking protocol. Structural models of YBX1 (PDB ID: 1H95, chain A) and hnRNP K (PDB ID: 2BZJ, chain A) were retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank. Missing residues were modeled using MODELLER (v9.23) based on sequence homology. Prior to docking, both structures were prepared by removing water molecules, adding polar hydrogens, and assigning Gasteiger charges using PDB2PQR (v2.1.1) with the CHARMM force field. Active residues were defined based on previously reported RNA-binding domains and our mutation analysis: YBX1 residues 50–100 (including Asp-105) and hnRNP K residues 200–250 (including Lys-34) were designated as “active” (directly involved in the interaction), while surface-exposed neighboring residues were defined as “passive” automatically by HADDOCK. Docking simulations were performed under default parameters with 1,000 structures generated for rigid-body docking (it0), followed by flexible refinement (it1) and explicit solvent refinement (itw) for the top 200 structures. The resulting docking poses were clustered using a backbone RMSD cutoff of 2.5 Å, and clusters were ranked by the HADDOCK score, a weighted sum of van der Waals, electrostatic, desolvation, and restraint violation energies. The best model was selected based on the lowest HADDOCK score and favorable interface properties (buried surface area ≥ 1,000 Å², ΔG ≤ -10 kcal/mol). Visualization and analysis of intermolecular contacts were performed using PyMOL (v2.5.0, Schrödinger, LLC).


### Statistical analysis
  Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS (v21.0) and GraphPad Prism (v8.0.1). Data are presented as mean ± SD. Differences among groups were assessed by one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc analysis, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.


## Results

### β-sitosterol inhibited malignant biological behaviors and glycolysis in A549 and H1299 cells in a concentration-dependent manner
  To investigate the functional impact of β-sitosterol on NSCLC cells, we first assessed its effects on key malignant behaviors. A549 and H1299 cells were treated with increasing concentrations of β-sitosterol (0, 1, 5, and 10 µM). As shown in Fig. 1A–C, β-sitosterol treatment led to a dose-dependent reduction in EdU-positive cells, as well as in the number of migratory and invasive cells. Concurrently, flow cytometry analysis revealed a progressive increase in late apoptotic cell death with higher doses of β-sitosterol (Fig. 1D). Given that metabolic reprogramming, particularly enhanced glycolysis, is closely linked to tumor proliferation and survival, we next examined whether β-sitosterol affects glycolytic activity in NSCLC cells. Indeed, β-sitosterol treatment significantly reduced glucose consumption, lactate production, and ECAR in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 1E–G). In addition, β-sitosterol treatment significantly decreased the LDHA (Fig. 1H) and HK2 (Fig. 1I) mRNA levels. Collectively, these results demonstrate that β-sitosterol suppresses both malignant phenotypes and glycolytic metabolism in NSCLC cells, suggesting that its antitumor effects may be partly mediated through inhibition of glycolysis.

### Reduced YBX1-hnRNP K interaction is observed following β-sitosterol treatment in A549 and H1299 cells
  According to a previous report22, the association between YBX1 and hnRNP K contributes to lung cancer metastasis by modulating CDC25a expression. To determine whether β-sitosterol influences this interaction in NSCLC, we first evaluated the protein abundance of YBX1 and hnRNP K in A549 and H1299 cells following treatment with increasing concentrations of β-sitosterol. Western blot analysis revealed that neither protein exhibited significant changes in expression (Fig. 2A), indicating that β-sitosterol does not affect their steady-state levels. In addition, the CDC25a protein levels were decreased following treatment with increasing concentrations of β-sitosterol (Fig. 2B). Co-IP assays demonstrated a progressive attenuation in the endogenous binding of YBX1 to hnRNP K with higher doses of β-sitosterol (Fig. 2C). These findings indicated that β-sitosterol treatment is associated with a reduction in the physical interaction between YBX1 and hnRNP K, without altering their individual expression. Whether this reduction results from direct competition for binding sites, allosteric effects, or indirect modulation of upstream regulators remains to be determined.

### YBX1 at Asp-105 site or hnRNP K at Lys-34 site weakened their interaction in A549 and H1299 cells
  To gain structural insight into the YBX1–hnRNP K interaction and to identify specific residues that might be critical for complex formation, we performed in silico structural predictions. These analyses were designed to characterize the binding interface between the two proteins, not to model β-sitosterol binding. First, we used the DMFold web server to predict the three-dimensional structure of the YBX1–hnRNP K complex (Fig. 3A). The predicted model revealed a complementary binding interface with extensive surface contacts. Molecular docking using HADDOCK further refined this interaction. The binding interface of YBX1 comprises the following amino acid residues: TYR-281, TYR-99, TYR-148, ASP-105, ARG-101, TYR-138, and ARG-69. The binding interface of hnRNP K comprises the following amino acid residues: GLY-36, ASN-149, THR-39, LYS-34, GLU-145, ARG-37, and GLU-191. Multiple intermolecular interactions were identified between contacting residues of YBX1 and hnRNP K, including hydrogen bonds (TYR-281:GLY-36, TYR-99:ASN-149, TYR-148:THR-39, ARG-101:GLU-145, TYR-138:ARG-37) and salt bridges (Asp-105:Lys-34, ARG-69:GLU-191). These interactions collectively contribute to the stability of the YBX1–hnRNP K protein complex. Furthermore, analysis of the protein binding surface revealed favorable surface complementarity between YBX1 and hnRNP K, which facilitates the formation of a stable binding interface (Fig. 3B–D). Analysis of the docked structure highlighted several residues at the interface, including Asp-105 of YBX1 and Lys-34 of hnRNP K, which appeared to form critical electrostatic interactions. To experimentally validate the importance of these residues, we generated point mutants and assessed their binding by co-immunoprecipitation. As shown in Fig. 3E, mutation of YBX1 (Asp-105) or hnRNP K (Lys-34) significantly reduced their interaction in both A549 and H1299 cells. These findings confirm that these specific residues are functionally important for YBX1–hnRNP K complex formation and provide a structural framework for understanding how the interaction might be disrupted.

### hnRNP K suppression reversed the promoted proliferation, migration, invasion, and glycolysis induced by YBX1 overexpression in A549 and H1299 cells
  To elucidate the functional contributions of YBX1 and hnRNP K in NSCLC development, gain- and loss-of-function models were established in A549 and H1299 cells via transfection with YBX1 overexpression and hnRNP K knockdown constructs. Efficient upregulation of YBX1 (Fig. 4A, B) and downregulation of hnRNP K (Fig. 4C, D) were confirmed by qPCR and Western blot. Functional characterization demonstrated that YBX1 overexpression enhanced proliferative (Fig. 4E), migratory (Fig. 4F), and invasive (Fig. 4G) capabilities, and suppressed apoptosis (Fig. 4H). In contrast, hnRNP K silencing led to inhibition of proliferation (Fig. 4E), migration (Fig. 4F), and invasion (Fig. 4G), alongside increased late apoptotic rates (Fig. 4H). At the metabolic level, YBX1 overexpression significantly elevated glycolytic flux, as indicated by increases in glucose consumption (Fig. 4I), lactate production (Fig. 4J), and ECAR (Fig. 4L). Conversely, hnRNP K knockdown markedly suppressed these glycolytic parameters. Furthermore, YBX1 overexpression increased the CDC25a protein levels (Fig. 4K), LDHA (Fig. 4M) and HK2 (Fig. 4N) mRNA levels, hnRNP K knockdown decreased CDC25a protein levels (Fig. 4K), LDHA (Fig. 4M) and HK2 (Fig. 4N) mRNA levels. Given that enhanced glycolysis is a hallmark of cancer that fuels rapid proliferation, supports metastatic dissemination, and confers resistance to apoptosis, these metabolic changes provide a mechanistic basis for the observed phenotypic effects. Collectively, these results suggest that YBX1 promotes NSCLC malignancy at least in part through hnRNP K-dependent enhancement of glycolytic metabolism, and that disruption of this axis—as observed with β-sitosterol treatment—may suppress tumor progression by reversing this metabolic advantage.

### β-sitosterol inhibited malignant biological behaviors and glycolysis in A549 and H1299 cells through YBX1–hnRNP K axis.
  Finally, the β-sitosterol treated NSCLC cells were transfected with WT-YBX1, MUT-YBX1 (Asp-105) and sh-hnRNP K. WT-YBX1 significantly increased the proliferation (Fig. 5A), migration (Fig. 5B), and invasion (Fig. 5C), alongside decreased late apoptotic rates (Fig. 5D) of β-sitosterol treated A549 and H1299 cells, while MUT-YBX1 showed no effect. hnRNP K knockdown reversed the effects of WT-YBX1 on proliferation (Fig. 5A), migration (Fig. 5B), invasion (Fig. 5C), and late apoptotic rates (Fig. 5D) of β-sitosterol treated A549 and H1299 cells. ## Discussion
  Our results demonstrate that β-sitosterol suppresses malignant biological behaviors and glycolytic activity in NSCLC cells, which were consistent with previous studies identifying β-sitosterol as a multifaceted anticancer compound, capable of modulating tumor progression in several cancer types such as cervical, colon, gastric, breast, lung, and pancreatic malignancies7. Supporting our observations, Zhao et al.12 show that β-sitosterol—an active constituent of Ganoderma—modulates aerobic glycolysis and promotes apoptosis in lung adenocarcinoma. Similarly, a β-sitosterol-enriched fraction from Strobilanthes crispus was found to reduce glucose uptake and lactate levels in triple-negative breast cancer models24. In NSCLC, β-sitosterol has also been reported by Wang et al.25 to mitigate anlotinib resistance through pathway regulation. Broader evidence further indicates the role of β-sitosterol in non-neoplastic pulmonary conditions, including acute lung injury, infection, and inflammatory processes26–28. However, it is essential to emphasize that all data presented here are derived from in vitro experiments using established cell lines. Therefore, while our observations provide mechanistic insights into how β-sitosterol may affect NSCLC cells at the cellular level, they cannot be directly extrapolated to predict therapeutic efficacy, safety, or pharmacokinetic behavior in living organisms.

  In this study, we observed that β-sitosterol treatment is associated with reduced interaction between YBX1 and hnRNP K. This is consistent with the findings of Fu et al.22, who identified a regulatory role of the YBX1–hnRNP K complex in CDC25a expression and its contribution to lung cancer metastasis. Similarly, Shnyreva et al.29 confirm a direct interaction between hnRNP K and YBX1 through co-immunoprecipitation assays. Interestingly, Li et al.30 report that hnRNP K also binds to MAP 1B-LC1 to facilitate epithelial–mesenchymal transition in lung cancer. Additionally, hnRNP K has been shown to interact with lncRNA00460 to enhance migratory and invasive behaviors in lung cancer cells.

  In addition, we observed that β-sitosterol disrupts the interaction between YBX1 and hnRNP K in NSCLC cells without altering their protein levels. This raises an important mechanistic question: whether this disruption occurs through direct binding of β-sitosterol to either protein or via indirect modulation of upstream signaling pathways. Our molecular docking and structural analyses offer preliminary insights into this issue. The YBX1–hnRNP K interaction interface involves critical residues such as Asp-105 in YBX1 and Lys-34 in hnRNP K, which we confirmed by mutagenesis to be essential for complex formation. Given the hydrophobic and amphipathic nature of β-sitosterol, it is plausible that this phytosterol may insert into the binding cleft and interfere with key contacts between these residues. Although direct evidence is lacking, one speculative hypothesis is that β-sitosterol may act as a small-molecule disruptor of the YBX1–hnRNP K complex by occupying or allosterically modulating their interaction interface. Future studies employing techniques such as surface plasmon resonance, fluorescence polarization, or cellular thermal shift assays are warranted to validate this hypothesis and to identify the precise binding site. Nevertheless, our findings provide a foundational basis for considering β-sitosterol as a potential modulator of protein–protein interactions involved in NSCLC progression.

  Our functional experiments further revealed that knockdown of hnRNP K counteracts the enhancing effects of YBX1 overexpression on proliferation, migration, invasion, and glycolysis in A549 and H1299 cells. These results are in line with reports by Su et al.31, in which YBX1 silencing suppresses NSCLC progression and triggered apoptosis, and with studies showing that lncRNA HOXC-AS3 facilitates NSCLC development by upregulating YBX132. Moreover, Li et al.33 demonstrate that hnRNP K depletion attenuates lung cancer proliferation, migration, and cell cycle progression through modulation of p53-dependent signaling.

  The metabolic reprogramming observed upon YBX1 overexpression and hnRNP K knockdown provides important insights into the mechanism of β-sitosterol action. Enhanced glycolysis, a key feature of the Warburg effect, enables cancer cells to meet the high energetic and biosynthetic demands associated with uncontrolled proliferation, invasion, and survival under stressful conditions. Our findings demonstrate that YBX1 overexpression increases glycolytic flux, which likely contributes to the enhanced proliferation, migration, and resistance to apoptosis observed in these cells. Conversely, hnRNP K silencing reverses these metabolic changes, suggesting that the YBX1–hnRNP K complex plays a pivotal role in regulating glycolytic metabolism in NSCLC. This is consistent with previous reports showing that YBX1 stabilizes mRNAs encoding glycolytic enzymes, and that hnRNP K participates in metabolic gene regulation20,21. Therefore, the disruption of the YBX1–hnRNP K interaction by β-sitosterol may impair the expression or function of key glycolytic enzymes, thereby reducing the metabolic fitness of tumor cells. This metabolic vulnerability could, in turn, sensitize NSCLC cells to apoptosis and limit their invasive potential. Together, these findings position the YBX1–hnRNP K axis as a critical metabolic checkpoint in NSCLC and highlight β-sitosterol as a potential metabolic intervention strategy.

  In addition, our results demonstrate that β-sitosterol suppresses malignant biological behaviors and glycolytic activity in NSCLC cells, consistent with previous studies identifying β-sitosterol as a multifaceted anticancer compound34. It is important to emphasize that β-sitosterol is known to modulate multiple signaling pathways, including PI3K/AKT, MAPK, NF-κB, and various apoptosis regulators35–37. Therefore, the anti-tumor effects observed in our study are likely attributable to a combination of mechanisms, rather than a single molecular event. Our investigation focused specifically on the YBX1–hnRNP K protein-protein interaction, based on literature evidence linking both proteins to lung cancer progression and our preliminary observation that β-sitosterol affects their binding without altering expression. However, this focused approach does not imply that other pathways are unaffected or less important. Rather, our study should be interpreted as an exploration of one potential contributing mechanism among many. Future studies employing unbiased approaches such as RNA-seq, phosphoproteomics, or pathway enrichment analysis will be essential to comprehensively map the molecular landscape of β-sitosterol-treated NSCLC cells and to determine the relative contribution of the YBX1–hnRNP K axis compared to other known targets.

  In summary, this in vitro study demonstrates that β-sitosterol suppresses malignant behaviors and glycolysis in NSCLC cells, and that these effects are associated with reduced interaction between YBX1 and hnRNP K. Importantly, this reduction occurs without changes in the expression levels of either protein, highlighting a novel mode of regulation. While the current study does not establish whether this effect is direct or indirect, it provides the first evidence that β-sitosterol is associated with the modulation of a key protein-protein interaction involved in lung cancer progression. Nevertheless, certain limitations of this study must be considered. First, while our study focused on the YBX1–hnRNP K axis, other signaling pathways or molecular targets of β-sitosterol in NSCLC remain unexplored. Given the pleiotropic nature of phytosterols, it is likely that β-sitosterol exerts its effects through multiple mechanisms, and the YBX1–hnRNP K axis may represent one component of a broader network. Second, without unbiased screening approaches such as proteomics or transcriptomics, we cannot determine whether this axis is a primary mediator or a downstream consequence of other molecular events. Third, all experiments were conducted using cultured NSCLC cell lines (A549 and H1299) under controlled in vitro conditions. While cell-based models are valuable for initial mechanistic exploration, they cannot recapitulate the complexity of the tumor microenvironment in vivo, including interactions with stromal cells, immune cells, extracellular matrix, and three-dimensional tissue architecture. Therefore, no conclusions regarding the therapeutic relevance, efficacy, safety, or clinical applicability of β-sitosterol can be drawn from this study. Fourth, the clinical applicability of β-sitosterol as a monotherapy or adjuvant agent needs further investigation, considering potential pharmacokinetic challenges and combinatorial effects with existing therapies. Addressing these gaps through comprehensive mechanistic studies and in vivo validation will strengthen the rationale for targeting this axis in future therapeutic strategies.
