# Integrated computational screening of FDA-approved anticancer drugs as novel HPV-16 E6 inhibitors in cervical cancer


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42656-z.


## Introduction
  Human papillomavirus (HPV) is a double-stranded DNA virus with strong tropism for epithelial tissues, encompassing more than 200 identified genotypes. Among these, high-risk strains—particularly HPV16 and HPV18—are implicated in nearly 70% of cervical cancer cases worldwide, making this malignancy one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality among women1. Recent global epidemiological estimates underscore the disease burden, reporting approximately 604,000 new cases and 342,000 deaths in 2020, with the highest incidence occurring in low- and middle-income countries where access to vaccination and screening services remains limited2,3. The molecular pathogenesis of cervical cancer is tightly linked to the activity of the viral oncoproteins E6 and E7, which disrupt host tumor suppressor pathways. The E6 protein plays a particularly central role by binding to the E6-associated protein (E6AP), a ubiquitin ligase, forming a trimeric complex with p534,5. This interaction triggers the proteasomal degradation of p53, a key regulator of apoptosis and genomic stability, ultimately promoting unchecked cellular proliferation and carcinogenesis6,7. Despite the availability of several treatment modalities—such as surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy—current therapeutic options show limited efficacy in advanced or metastatic disease8,9. These treatments often cause substantial toxicity, reduced quality of life, and significant financial burden, particularly in resource-limited settings10,11. Importantly, none of the conventional therapies directly target the viral oncoproteins that drive HPV-mediated malignancy. These approaches exhibit limited long-term efficacy in HPV-driven cervical cancer because they do not directly target the viral oncoproteins E6 and E7—the primary molecular drivers of malignancy. Persistent expression of E6 and E7 continuously disables key tumor-suppressor pathways, particularly those involving p53 and Rb, allowing cancer cells to survive and proliferate even after cytotoxic therapy12. Moreover, conventional treatments are associated with considerable toxicity, treatment resistance, and poor outcomes in advanced or recurrent disease, underscoring the urgent need for therapeutic strategies capable of directly inhibiting HPV oncoproteins. Consequently, the development of therapeutic agents capable of selectively inhibiting E6 represents a promising and much-needed strategy for restoring p53 activity, halting cancer progression, and minimizing adverse effects13–15. In recent years, the search for small-molecule inhibitors of E6 has gained substantial momentum, supported by several new studies describing druggable structural interfaces within E6 and emphasizing the therapeutic significance of targeting persistent viral oncoproteins. Recent analyses, including a 2025 comprehensive review by Ranasinghe and McMillan, have highlighted E6 and E7 as ideal therapeutic targets due to their continuous expression in HPV-infected cells and their central role in promoting cervical carcinogenesis16,17. In parallel, innovative strategies such as PROTAC-mediated E6 degradation and nanobody-based E6 antagonists have demonstrated significant antitumor activity in preclinical HPV-positive cancer models, providing strong evidence that direct targeting of E6 can suppress tumor growth and restore host immune responses18. Together, these advancements reinforce the growing scientific interest in developing E6-directed therapies and provide a strong rationale for computational approaches aimed at identifying novel E6 inhibitors. Advances in computational drug discovery—especially the integration of machine learning (ML) techniques—have transformed this field by enabling the rapid and accurate analysis of large chemical libraries19,20. Recent studies have also highlighted the importance of overcoming challenges such as class imbalance and model overfitting in drug-discovery datasets, demonstrating that ML- and AutoML-based balancing strategies can significantly enhance prediction robustness and overall screening performance in computational repurposing pipelines21,22. Furthermore, state-of-the-art ML frameworks have increasingly been incorporated throughout the preclinical drug-discovery process, facilitating accelerated hit identification, mechanism-of-action elucidation, and optimization of drug-like physicochemical properties23,24. High-impact reports published in recent years underscore that ML-integrated pipelines are poised to play a pivotal role in the next generation of computational drug-development strategies.

  ML-driven drug repurposing platforms can efficiently identify new therapeutic applications for existing drugs, providing a cost-effective and time-saving alternative to traditional drug development pipelines25,26. When combined with complementary computational approaches such as quantum mechanical calculations, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics simulations, these tools allow for precise prediction and optimization of drug–target interactions27–31. In this study, we present a comprehensive computational framework aimed at repurposing FDA-approved anticancer drugs to identify novel inhibitors of the HPV-16 E6 protein32. Our workflow integrates ADMET profiling and physicochemical descriptor analysis with robust ML models33,34, including Random Forest35, Naïve Bayes36, Support Vector Machine (SVM)37, and Gradient Boosting38, to predict inhibitory potential. Compounds identified as highly promising were subsequently refined using quantum mechanical calculations and evaluated through molecular docking to assess their binding affinity and structural stability within the E6 binding site. This multifaceted approach aims to pinpoint potent therapeutic candidates and provide a foundation for future preclinical validation, contributing to the development of more effective and targeted therapies for HPV-associated cervical cancer.


## Methods

### Dataset collection and curation
  In this study, we utilized a curated dataset of inhibitors targeting the HPV-16 E6 oncoprotein, obtained from well-established scientific repositories and peer-reviewed publications25,39,40.

  The dataset encompassed diverse chemical and biological properties necessary for conducting comprehensive computational analyses. To ensure data reliability, an initial filtering stage was performed based on reported inhibitory potency from both in vitro and in silico studies. This process removed redundant and incomplete entries, resulting in a consistent and high-quality dataset.

  Following this curation process, a total of 35 compounds were selected, representing various chemical classes such as flavonoids, polyphenols, plant-derived metabolites, and other antiviral agents (Table S1). The structures of all selected compounds were retrieved from the ZINC database (https://zinc20.docking.org/), a widely recognized platform for chemical informatics and virtual screening. The refined dataset was subsequently optimized for downstream computational tasks, including molecular docking and ML-based predictive modeling, to ensure suitability for mechanistic evaluation of E6 inhibition.

  In parallel, we curated a comprehensive library of 100 FDA-approved anticancer drugs from the Selleckchem database (https://www.selleckchem.com). This collection was assembled to support drug-repurposing analyses by incorporating compounds with established therapeutic relevance and clinical safety. Selection criteria included FDA approval status, documented anticancer activity in preclinical or clinical studies, availability of complete structural and physicochemical information, drug-like characteristics such as solubility and stability, and accessible toxicity or safety profiles. These criteria ensured that the resulting library was both pharmacologically meaningful and computationally robust.

### Selection of molecular descriptors
  Feature selection plays a crucial role in developing machine learning (ML) models for predictive drug discovery. This process involves identifying and extracting molecular characteristics that are most relevant to determining a compound’s behavior in biological systems. For this study, both physicochemical descriptors and ADMET (Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion, and Toxicity) parameters were prioritized. These descriptors are essential in predicting drug efficacy and safety, which are critical factors in early-stage drug development. The integration of ADMET parameters ensures that ML models can provide robust and precise predictions, enhancing their reliability in screening potential drug candidates41. The structural representations of all compounds were obtained in SDF format from the PubChem database, ensuring compatibility with advanced descriptor calculation tools. Molecular descriptors were generated using a combination of the ADMETlab 3 platform (https://admetmesh.scbdd.com/)42 and ProTox server (https://tox-new.charite.de/protox_II/)43, which are widely recognized for their comprehensive analyses of pharmacokinetic and toxicity properties. These tools provided a broad spectrum of descriptors, ranging from physicochemical properties (e.g., molecular weight, log P, hydrogen bond donors/acceptors) to toxicity predictions and drug-likeness metrics. To optimize the dataset for ML modeling, a systematic preprocessing step was performed. Variables with zero values or those exhibiting minimal variance were removed to reduce noise and enhance the accuracy and stability of the models. This preprocessing step not only minimized potential errors during model training but also ensured that only the most relevant and informative features were retained for downstream analyses. The incorporation of carefully selected molecular descriptors into the predictive framework lays the foundation for robust ML models capable of accurately predicting drug properties. This approach ensures a streamlined, data-driven pathway for identifying promising therapeutic candidates with desirable efficacy and safety profiles.


### Generating machine learning models
  To explore the potential antiviral properties of a dataset comprising 100 anticancer drugs, this study utilized supervised classification algorithms. The dataset was methodically divided into two categories: antiviral and non-antiviral compounds. FDA-approved antibiotics were included as negative controls to define the non-antiviral class. The rationale behind this choice lies in the analogous nature of viruses and bacteria as external agents affecting the host system, providing a logical reference point for differentiating antiviral from non-antiviral activities. Antibiotics were included in a proportion equivalent to 80% of the total antiviral compounds, ensuring a balanced dataset and reducing the risk of bias in model training. To establish clear boundaries between the two categories, the dataset underwent a rigorous curation process, creating a robust foundation for machine learning (ML) analysis. Furthermore, a stratified validation strategy was employed, wherein 10% of the data from each class was reserved as the test set. This ensured that both antiviral and non-antiviral categories were adequately represented during model evaluation, leading to reliable and generalizable predictive outcomes. Four widely recognized ML algorithms were selected for this study:

  Random Forest (RF): Known for its ensemble-based approach and robust performance in handling complex datasets.Naïve Bayes (NB): A probabilistic model well-suited for binary classification tasks with smaller datasets.Support Vector Machines (SVM): Capable of generating precise decision boundaries for high-dimensional data.Gradient Boosting (GB): Renowned for its ability to optimize model accuracy through iterative refinement of weak learners.

  These algorithms were implemented and optimized using Orange software, an open-source platform chosen for its user-friendly interface, reproducible workflow design, and advanced machine-learning capabilities44. Orange’s visual programming framework facilitated seamless integration of data preprocessing, model building, and performance evaluation, ensuring consistency and transparency throughout the analysis. The selection of algorithms was informed by their demonstrated success in chemical compound classification tasks, particularly in pharmacological studies. Each model was fine-tuned to maximize predictive accuracy, with performance metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, and area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) being used to evaluate and compare their efficacy. By leveraging these advanced tools and methodologies, the study provided a robust framework for analyzing the antiviral potential of anticancer compounds, paving the way for novel drug discovery opportunities.


### Validating and selecting the best models
  The model validation framework employed in this study combined k-fold cross-validation and stratified random sampling to ensure robustness and reliability. In the 5-fold cross-validation approach, the training dataset was divided into five exclusive subsets. During each iteration, four subsets were used for training, and the remaining subset was utilized for validation, ensuring that each subset served as the validation set once. This systematic process helped mitigate overfitting and provided a balanced evaluation. To further enhance robustness, random sampling was conducted using 50% of the training data across 10 independent replicates. This complementary validation step ensured unbiased performance assessment and improved the reliability of the results. A comprehensive suite of metrics was employed to evaluate the models, including accuracy, which was the primary measure of overall performance. The precision-recall relationship assessed the reliability of positive predictions, while the F1-score harmonized precision and recall into a single metric45. The Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) was particularly emphasized for its ability to evaluate performance even with imbalanced datasets, incorporating all confusion matrix categories: True Positives (TP), True Negatives (TN), False Positives (FP), and False Negatives (FN)46. The discriminative capability of the models was assessed using ROC curve analysis and AUC values, while False Positive Rate (FPR) quantified the likelihood of Type I errors by analyzing the fraction of negative instances incorrectly classified as positive. ### ML prediction
  To enhance prediction accuracy and minimize error rates, a two-stage classification approach was employed following the selection of the optimal machine learning model. This hierarchical strategy allowed for systematic filtering and refinement of potential HPV-16 E6 protein inhibitors by incorporating complementary molecular characteristics at each stage. In the first stage, the training and test datasets were screened using ADME descriptors and physicochemical properties, establishing a broad filter to identify compounds with general drug-like characteristics. This stage ensured that only compounds meeting the fundamental criteria for drug-likeness and potential E6 inhibitory activity were retained for further analysis. In the second stage, the preliminary predictions were refined using organ toxicity-related descriptors and toxicity endpoints, enabling a more targeted assessment of the compounds’ similarity to known E6 protein inhibitors. By integrating toxicity data, this stage provided critical insights into the safety profiles of the compounds, which are essential for identifying viable therapeutic candidates. The rationale behind this two-stage classification approach lies in its ability to progressively narrow the pool of candidates while maintaining high predictive accuracy. The first stage acted as a broad molecular filter, prioritizing general drug-like attributes, whereas the second stage focused on toxicological refinement, ensuring the exclusion of compounds with undesirable safety profiles. This sequential consideration of distinct yet interconnected molecular features enabled a comprehensive evaluation of the dataset, leading to the identification of high-potential inhibitors with both efficacy and safety considerations.


### DFT calculation
  To gain a deeper understanding of the electronic and structural properties of the compounds identified through machine learning, comprehensive quantum chemical calculations were performed. The molecular geometries of the selected compounds were optimized using density functional theory (DFT) at the B3LYP level of theory with the 6-311G basis set47,48. This computational framework was chosen due to its established accuracy and reliability in predicting molecular properties and electronic structures of chemical compounds. The electronic structure analysis primarily focused on molecular orbital calculations, particularly the energy levels of the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO). These calculations were conducted within the same theoretical framework (B3LYP/6-311G) to ensure consistency and reproducibility across the computational protocol. The HOMO-LUMO energy gap (ΔE), a critical parameter derived from this analysis, provided valuable insights into the compounds’ chemical reactivity, stability, and electronic transitions49. Key electronic descriptors, including hardness, softness, chemical potential, and electronegativity, were also derived from the HOMO and LUMO energies. These descriptors are pivotal in evaluating the compounds’ reactivity profiles and potential interaction mechanisms with the HPV-16 E6 protein. By providing detailed insights into the electronic properties and structural features of the identified compounds, this approach enabled a more comprehensive evaluation of their potential as HPV-16 E6 protein inhibitors.


### Molecular docking
  Molecular docking simulations were performed to investigate the interactions between machine learning-identified compounds and the HPV-16 E6 protein50. The crystal structure of the HPV-16 E6 protein was obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 4XR8). The original structure, containing the E6/E6AP/p53 ternary complex, was processed to isolate the F chain of the E6 protein for detailed docking studies. The preparation of the protein structure involved several steps to optimize it for docking simulations, including the removal of crystallographic water molecules and non-essential ions, followed by the addition of hydrogen atoms to ensure structural stability. The atomic dipole tensor (ADT) method was employed for Coleman partial atomic charge calculations, which further refined the protein structure for accurate interaction studies. For ligand preparation, the identified compounds underwent a rigorous preprocessing protocol. This included the addition of hydrogen atoms under physiologically relevant pH conditions, ensuring that the ligands adopted the correct ionization state. The ADT method was also applied to evaluate the ligands’ rotational and torsional degrees of freedom, optimizing their conformational flexibility. The docking simulations were executed with carefully defined parameters. A grid map with fixed dimensions of 68 Å × 60 Å × 64 Å was used for all docking simulations. The grid box was centered on the centroid of each ligand (ligand-centered docking) to ensure complete sampling of the binding region. For reproducibility, the exact grid box center coordinates (X, Y, Z) for all ligands are provided in Table S2. A total of 300 docking runs were performed for each ligand, employing the Lamarckian Genetic Algorithm (LGA) to explore diverse binding conformations. This iterative algorithm is well-suited for molecular docking as it balances computational efficiency with a thorough exploration of conformational space51. The docking simulations provided detailed insights into the binding interactions between the compounds and the E6 protein. Key outcomes included the identification of hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions contributing to binding stability. A quantitative assessment of binding energies and structural complementarity was performed to rank the identified compounds based on their interaction potential. This comprehensive molecular docking approach ensured a robust evaluation of potential binding modes, offering valuable insights into the mechanistic basis of protein-ligand interactions. By integrating these findings with machine learning and quantum chemical analyses, the study provided a thorough exploration of the compounds’ potential as HPV-16 E6 protein inhibitors. The evaluation framework incorporated five-fold cross-validation and stratified random sampling, ensuring a robust and unbiased assessment of model performance.

  In the first stage, models were evaluated based on their ability to classify compounds using ADME descriptors. The results demonstrated exceptional performance across most algorithms (Table 1). Gradient Boosting and Random Forest achieved perfect classification accuracy, with MCC (1.0), CA (1.0), and AUC (1.0) values. SVM exhibited nearly equivalent performance, with MCC = 0.98, CA = 0.98, and AUC = 1.0, reflecting its strong predictive capabilities. Conversely, Naïve Bayes, while still reliable, demonstrated slightly lower performance metrics (MCC = 0.93, CA = 0.93, and AUC = 0.98) due to the simpler nature of its probabilistic model.

### Prediction results
  The two-stage virtual screening framework employed in this study successfully identified potential antiviral compounds from a library of 100 FDA-approved anticancer drugs. In the initial screening stage, compounds were evaluated using ADME descriptors, resulting in the identification of 41 potential antiviral candidates, corresponding to a 41% success rate. In the second stage, these 41 candidates underwent further refinement using toxicity descriptors, which provided additional insights into their safety profiles and pharmacological viability. This step reduced the pool to 21 compounds with promising antiviral properties, representing 51.2% of the candidates from the first stage. A comprehensive analysis of these 21 selected compounds highlighted their strong antiviral potential. These compounds demonstrated significant structural similarity to the training set of known HPV-16 E6 protein inhibitors, reinforcing their likelihood of effective interaction with the E6 protein. The high similarity metrics indicated that these compounds possess molecular features critical for binding to the E6 protein and potentially disrupting its function. ### DFT results
  Molecular orbital analysis was conducted using Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations at the B3LYP/6-311G level of theory. The electronic properties, particularly the frontier molecular orbital energies—HOMO (highest occupied molecular orbital) and LUMO (lowest unoccupied molecular orbital)—and their corresponding energy gaps (ΔE), were systematically analyzed and are visualized in Figure S1. The HOMO-LUMO energy gap (ΔE) is a critical parameter that provides insights into molecular stability and chemical reactivity. This gap represents the minimum energy required to excite an electron from the HOMO to the LUMO. Generally, a larger ΔE correlates with enhanced molecular stability and reduced chemical reactivity, indicating chemical hardness, whereas a smaller ΔE suggests higher reactivity, chemical softness, and a greater likelihood of participating in chemical interactions. Among the analyzed compounds, Fluorouracil exhibited the largest energy gap (6.32 eV), indicating substantial molecular stability and low chemical reactivity. Other compounds with relatively large energy gaps included Decitabine (5.63 eV), 2-Methoxyestradiol (5.62 eV), and Diethylstilbestrol (5.41 eV), all of which demonstrated significant chemical hardness. Conversely, compounds such as Mitoxantrone (2.21 eV) and Topotecan (3.56 eV) exhibited notably smaller energy gaps, suggesting higher chemical reactivity and molecular softness. These findings highlight their potential as active compounds capable of interacting with the HPV-16 E6 protein. In addition to the energy gap analysis, various electronic parameters were derived, including chemical hardness, softness, chemical potential, and electronegativity, providing a comprehensive profile of the compounds’ electronic behaviors. These parameters are summarized in Table 3 and offer valuable insights into the reactivity patterns and potential interaction mechanisms of the studied molecules. For instance, chemical softness and lower chemical potential are indicative of compounds that may more readily participate in electron exchange during binding interactions. This computational analysis underscores the importance of DFT-derived electronic properties in identifying promising antiviral candidates, providing a robust framework for understanding molecular stability, reactivity, and potential mechanisms of action.

### Molecular docking
  The molecular docking analysis evaluated the interaction parameters between the selected compounds and the HPV-16 E6 protein. Binding energies, inhibition constants, and intermolecular energy parameters were calculated across 300 docking simulations for each compound. This analysis identified five top-performing compounds with enhanced inhibitory potential: axitinib (-8.13 kcal/mol), cabozantinib (-9.24 kcal/mol), tivozanib (-8.38 kcal/mol), deflazacort (-8.23 kcal/mol), and cilostazol (-8.87 kcal/mol). These compounds exhibited significantly lower binding energies compared to other tested molecules, indicating stronger and more stable interactions with the E6 protein (Table 3). Their superior binding profiles suggest enhanced inhibitory potential against the HPV-16 E6 protein, positioning them as leading candidates for further investigation.


### Structural analysis of protein-ligand interactions
  A comprehensive structural analysis was performed to characterize the interaction mechanisms of the top five ligands within the HPV-16 E6 binding pocket. Three-dimensional surface mapping and two-dimensional interaction diagrams collectively revealed that these compounds occupy the core region of the hydrophobic cavity implicated in E6–E6AP–p53 recognition (Figure S2). All five ligands demonstrated well-defined binding orientations, stabilized through a combination of hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic contacts, and π–π stacking interactions. Key residues—including Tyr32, Cys51, Tyr70, Ser74, and Arg131—were recurrently involved across the ligand set, consistent with previously identified functional hotspots essential for E6’s oncogenic activity. The 3D surface visualizations clearly showed deep insertion of the ligands into the active pocket, while the 2D interaction diagrams highlighted residue-level contacts responsible for anchoring the ligand within the site. Collectively, these structural features confirm that the predicted inhibitors are not only geometrically compatible with the E6 active site but also establish interaction patterns indicative of strong inhibitory potential.

  Axitinib exhibited a well-defined binding mode characterized by two critical hydrogen bonds with Cys51(F) and Gly130(F) (2.91 Å and 2.86 Å, respectively). Additional hydrophobic interactions were observed with residues Phe45(F), Leu67(F), Val62(F), Leu50(F), Trp132(F), Arg131(F), Leu100(F), and Arg102(F). These interactions suggest a binding mechanism that effectively stabilizes the compound within the E6 binding pocket, potentially disrupting the protein’s function (Fig. 6A).

#### Cabozantinib interactions
  Cabozantinib demonstrated a robust binding profile with five hydrogen bonds involving residues Ser71(F), Gln107(F), Arg102(F), Tyr32(F), and Trp132(F), with bond distances of 2.94 Å, 2.65 Å, 2.50 Å, 2.74 Å, and 3.08 Å, respectively (Fig. 6B). These strong interactions indicate cabozantinib’s potential as a potent inhibitor of the E6 protein.


#### Tivozanib interactions
  Tivozanib formed three strategic hydrogen bonds with residues Cys51(F) (3.26 Å), Tyr70(F) (2.67 Å), and Tyr32(F) (2.84 Å). Hydrophobic interactions were observed with residues Val31(F), Phe45(F), Leu50(F), Val53(F), Ala61(F), Val62(F), Leu67(F), Ser71(F), Ser74(F), and Gln107(F) (Fig. 6C). This combination of hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions underscores tivozanib’s stable binding and strong inhibitory potential.


#### Deflazacort interactions
  Deflazacort established three hydrogen bonds, one with Cys51(F) (3.07 Å) and two with Arg102(F) (2.76 Å, 2.74 Å). Hydrophobic interactions involved residues Val31(F), Tyr32(F), Val53(F), Leu67(F), Val62(F), Leu50(F), Gln107(F), Arg131(F), and Leu100(F). These interactions suggest moderate but significant inhibitory potential for deflazacort against the E6 protein (Fig. 6D).


#### Cilostazol interactions
  Cilostazol displayed four hydrogen bonds, one with Trp132(F) (2.91 Å) and three with Arg129(F) (2.67 Å, 3.06 Å, and 2.79 Å). Hydrophobic interactions were observed with residues Leu100(F), Arg102(F), Gly130(F), and Arg131(F), contributing to the compound’s binding stability within the active site (Fig. 6E).

  Previous studies have identified residues such as Tyr32(F), Cys51(F), Tyr70(F), Ser74(F), and Arg131(F) as crucial for the stability and function of the E6 protein30. The interactions of the five identified compounds with these key residues suggest their potential to disrupt E6 protein activity. By targeting these critical residues, these compounds may effectively interfere with the HPV-16 E6 protein’s oncogenic functions, making them strong candidates for further computational and experimental investigations. To further validate the reliability of the docking protocol, redocking of all ligands was performed, and the corresponding RMSD values between docked and redocked conformations were calculated. ## Discussion
  The present study established a multi-layered computational framework for identifying potential inhibitors of the HPV-16 E6 oncoprotein through the repurposing of FDA-approved anticancer drugs. Because E6 promotes cervical carcinogenesis via ubiquitin-mediated degradation of p53, the disruption of the E6–E6AP–p53 interface has become a central objective in HPV-targeted therapy. The integrated methodology employed here—consisting of two-stage machine learning (ML) prioritization, quantum chemical analysis, and structure-based molecular docking—produced a coherent set of candidate inhibitors whose mechanistic relevance is supported by convergent computational evidence.

  The two-stage ML pipeline demonstrated high discriminative performance, with Gradient Boosting achieving perfect MCC, CA, and AUC values. This hierarchical design parallels recent trends in ML-driven preclinical discovery, where sequential filtering using ADME descriptors and toxicity refinement increases hit-enrichment efficiency and reduces false-positive rates. Notably, ML-predicted active compounds also showed favorable quantum descriptors and strong docking performance, demonstrating internal consistency among the computational layers and strengthening confidence in the selected candidates.

  Quantum-chemical calculations revealed that compounds with intermediate HOMO–LUMO energy gaps exhibited superior binding characteristics. This observation aligns with established SAR principles and is consistent with prior E6-focused studies, including Kumar et al. (2019)53, which demonstrated that ligands with balanced stability-reactivity properties most effectively engage the E6AP binding pocket. The correlation between electronic parameters and docking behavior underscores the value of combining QC descriptors with structural modeling for mechanistically informed inhibitor prediction.

  Docking analysis identified cabozantinib, tivozanib, axitinib, cilostazol, and deflazacort as the most promising E6 inhibitors. These ligands formed stable hydrogen-bonding and hydrophobic interactions with key functional residues—Tyr32, Cys51, Tyr70, Ser74, and Arg131—residues repeatedly highlighted as essential to E6–E6AP–p53 interactions in crystallographic and computational studies. These residue-level correspondences closely mirror interaction patterns reported by Kumar et al.53 and are further supported by the mechanistic insights provided in recent reviews, including Ranasinghe & McMillan (2025)16 and Soumia et al. (2022)12 overview of HPV E6/E7 therapeutic targeting.

  An additional mechanistic dimension emerged from the predominance of kinase inhibitors among the top-ranked compounds. Prior literature, including the comprehensive review by Gomes et al. (2021) on HPV E6/E7 inhibitors, has reported similar observations, noting that several kinase inhibitors exhibit dual antiviral and anticancer activity by modulating host signaling pathways exploited by viruses. The strong docking interactions observed here, combined with previously reported antiviral effects of agents such as cabozantinib and cilostazol, suggest that their therapeutic potential may extend to HPV by interfering with E6-mediated p53 degradation. To contextualize these computational hits within current clinical practice, the binding affinities and ADMET characteristics of the top candidates were compared with standard cervical-cancer therapeutics including cisplatin, paclitaxel, topotecan, carboplatin, and Vorinostat. The repurposed compounds demonstrated superior or comparable docking scores and more favorable ADMET predictions, such as improved oral bioavailability, reduced hepatotoxicity potential, and enhanced drug-likeness. Considering the dose-limiting toxicity, poor bioavailability, and lack of viral specificity associated with conventional chemotherapeutics, these computationally identified inhibitors present a potentially advantageous therapeutic profile.

  Despite the strength of this integrated framework, certain limitations remain. Computational methods cannot fully recapitulate the dynamic conformational landscape of E6, compound bioavailability in biological systems, or off-target interactions. Therefore, experimental validation—including biochemical assays, cell-based functional studies, and in vivo evaluation—is essential for confirming the inhibitory potential of the identified compounds. Nevertheless, the synergy between ML filtering, quantum-chemical assessment, and docking predictions provides a multi-validated and mechanistically coherent foundation for further investigation. Future work will advance these findings through long-timescale molecular dynamics simulations, free-energy calculations, and subsequent in vitro and in vivo validation.


## Conclusion
  This study presents a pioneering computational framework that advances the field of drug repurposing for HPV-16 E6 inhibition through a sophisticated multi-stage screening approach. By integrating machine learning algorithms, quantum chemical calculations, and molecular docking analyses, our methodology has successfully identified five FDA-approved anticancer compounds with strong E6 inhibitory potential. These compounds, leveraging their pre-established safety profiles, represent a promising therapeutic strategy by combining known anticancer properties with novel E6 inhibitory effects. This dual-action mechanism provides an efficient approach for the treatment of HPV-positive cancers. This work underscores the potential of integrated computational approaches, particularly the application of machine learning, in accelerating drug discovery. While our computational predictions are highly encouraging, further biochemical assays and cell-based studies are essential to validate the predicted inhibitory effects and confirm their clinical applicability. These findings not only highlight a significant advancement in therapeutic strategies for HPV-associated malignancies but also provide a robust foundation for future research in this rapidly evolving field.
