# BayesPI-FLY: a Bayesian neural network approach for inferring feature weighted TF–DNA interaction


## Abstract
  Understanding how transcription factors (TFs) recognize DNA motifs is central to deciphering gene regulation. However, integrating multi-omics data, particularly DNA methylation, which can variably influence TF binding, remains a significant challenge. To address this, we developed BayesPI-Feature Learning Yard (BayesPI-FLY), a Bayesian neural network for de novo motif discovery that integrates DNA sequence information with DNA methylation status data. Building upon the classical biophysical model of TF–DNA interactions, BayesPI-FLY employs a two-layer inference architecture to jointly estimate model parameters and hyperparameters within a Bayesian framework. The core algorithms are implemented in C and parallelized through Python, ensuring computational efficiency. BayesPI-FLY quantitatively characterizes methylation effects at both single-nucleotide and motif levels, and generates position weight matrices and sequence logos to facilitate motif interpretation. Validation using synthetic and high-throughput sequencing datasets, including whole-genome bisulfite sequencing data, demonstrates that the framework can recapitulate known methylation-associated TF-binding patterns and infer strand-specific associations within the modeling framework. Collectively, BayesPI-FLY offers a versatile and extensible computational platform for characterizing methylation-related TF-DNA binding patterns across complex epigenetic contexts.


## Introduction
  Transcription factors (TFs) selectively bind conserved DNA sequence patterns, binding motifs, typically located in regulatory regions (e.g., enhancers and promoters) to control gene expression. Motifs are commonly represented by position weight matrices (PWMs), which describe nucleotide preferences at each position of a TF-binding site. By recognizing and interacting with these motifs, TFs regulate activation or repression of downstream target genes. In vivo assays such as ChIP-seq [1] directly capture TF–DNA interactions in living cells via chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by high-throughput sequencing (HTS), revealing binding in native chromatin contexts. Although ChIP-seq data are frequently integrated with DNA sequence to predict TF motifs, most motif-discovery algorithms neglect dynamic epigenetic modifications that influence TF binding. DNA methylation is a key epigenetic mark catalyzed by DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs), which transfer a methyl group from S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) to the 5th carbon of cytosine, producing 5-methylcytosine (5mC) [1–3]. Methylation of CpG dinucleotides often impedes TF recognition and binding, leading to gene silencing [4]. For example, methylation of a CpG island at a FoxP3 enhancer reduces CREB binding and suppresses FoxP3 expression in regulatory T cells [5]. Conversely, some TFs recruit DNMTs to promote methylation and repress transcription; Myc, for instance, increases methylation at the p21Cip1 promoter via Dnmt3a, silencing the gene [6, 7]. A subset of TFs preferentially bind methylated CpGs. OCT4, for instance, display selective affinity for methylated DNA during embryonic development and differentiation [1]. Thus, DNA methylation can either hinder or facilitate TF binding, depending on the genomic and cellular context.

  The methylation–TF interplay is central-to-normal cellular processes and is frequently disrupted in cancer [8], contributing to aberrant gene expression and disease progression [9]. Alterations in genes that regulate DNA methylation cause global hypomethylation and focal CpG island hypermethylation, reshaping TF occupancy and gene expression [10, 11]. Nevertheless, how methylation modulates TF binding in a cell type–specific manner remains incompletely understood [12, 13]. Incorporating DNA methylation data (e.g., from whole-genome bisulfite sequencing [WGBS]) into computational prediction of TF-binding motifs is critical because methylation adds complexity to gene regulation [1]. WGBS is a standard method for genome-wide methylation profiling, detecting 5mC at single-base resolution via bisulfite treatment. Integrating ChIP-seq (TF binding) with WGBS (DNA methylation) offers a powerful framework to systematically investigate how DNA methylation regulates TF occupancy. This is particularly important in the context of disease, where alterations in TF binding can lead to significant regulatory consequences [14, 15]. However, identifying methylation-sensitive loci and quantitatively estimating the effects of methylation on TF-binding affinity remain substantial challenges [16]. Several recent studies have quantified the impact of methylation on TF binding. Zuo et al. [14] performed methylation-specific SELEX-seq to compare TF affinity for methylated versus unmethylated DNA and derived energy PWMs (ePWMs) with position- and strand-specific methylation parameters to compute relative TF-binding affinities. Rube et al. [17] introduced ProBound, which models a methylated CpG as a fifth base in the PWM to quantify methylation effects at binding sites. de Mendoza et al. [15] used targeted promoter methylation to show that 5mC effects on gene expression are genomic-context specific (silencing, enhancing, or neutral). Viner et al. [16] expanded the DNA alphabet to include 5mC (M) within MEME Suite, enabling single-nucleotide-resolution motif prediction that accounts for both nucleotide preference and methylation. Other studies, such as MeDeMo [18], integrate intra-motif dependencies, and develop methylation-aware motif models, thereby enhancing genome-wide analyses of TF binding.

  Despite recent advancements, most existing approaches either treat methylation as an extended nucleotide alphabet or rely on in vitro binding assays. However, few methods integrate both sequence information and methylation profiles into a unified framework. In this work, we propose a new biophysical and probabilistic modeling framework that jointly learns DNA sequence feature and methylation profiles. By explicitly modeling both data types, our approach not only enables the quantitative estimation of conditional binding probabilities but also facilitates a systematic analysis of methylation-dependent TF-binding specificity. Based on this framework, we developed BayesPI-FLY (Fig. 1a), a Python package for de novo motif discovery grounded in the biophysical theory of TF–DNA interactions. While BayesPI [19] also features motif prediction, BayesPI-FLY extends the Bayesian evidence model [20] by incorporating both DNA sequence data (e.g., ChIP-seq peaks) and WGBS methylation profiles. This enhancement allows BayesPI-FLY to predict TF motifs and explore the relationship between TF binding and DNA methylation. In BayesPI-FLY, model parameters are trained within a Bayesian neural network framework through a two-level inference process (Fig. 1b): (1) learning TF-binding parameters (PWMs and methylation preferences) via neural networks and (2) tuning hyperparameters for improved generalization using Bayesian evidence approximation [21]. The tool supports parallel computation and includes visualization utilities to accelerate analysis, helping clarify whether a TF favors or disfavors methylation at specific positions. This novel implementation opens up new possibilities for studying methylation effects at both single-nucleotide resolution and across entire TF-binding sites. By treating methylation as an additional genomic feature, this integrated framework can be easily extended to other omics data (e.g., histone modifications, nucleosome density) in future work.

### Evaluation: Assessing BayesPI-FLY by synthetic and real data
  To assess the robustness of Bayesian neural networks for inferring feature weighted TF–DNA interactions, we first created a set of synthetic datasets to evaluate the performance of BayesPI-FLY in predicting both TF-binding motifs and the correlation between these motifs and DNA methylation (e.g. the generation of synthetic datasets is described in the Supplementary Methods and illustrated in the Supplementary Figs S1 and S2). Next, we downloaded WGBS data of DNA methylation from the human K562 cell line, part of the ENCODE project [31], via the GEO database [32] (accession number: GSM2308596). Human TFs that preferentially bind to either methylated (e.g. 5mC) or unmethylated DNA sequences were selected from MethMotif.Org [33]: CEBP-B, CEBP-G, and SP1 are known to bind methylated (5mC) DNA sequences [1], while SP1 is insensitive to DNA methylation on CpGs [34]. In contrast, FOXA1 [35] and MYC [36] preferentially bind to unmethylated DNA sequences [6]. ChIP-seq datasets for these five TFs from the human K562 cell line were also obtained from the ENCODE project. The preprocessing steps for these datasets are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

## Results

### Validation of BayesPI-FLY using synthetic DNA methylation datasets
  In this study, six synthetic datasets were used to evaluate the performance of BayesPI-FLY: 500, 1000, 2000, 4000, 6000, and 8000 synthetic DNA sequences. Each dataset consists of three types of data—“plain,” “with_methy,” and “with_rand”—with 16 samples per type. The inserted motif lengths range from 5 to 15 bp in each dataset. In the first (plain), no genomic features are present in the DNA sequences. In the second (with_methy), genomic features (e.g., DNA methylation) are included at single-nucleotide resolution, and their coupling with the TF-binding motif is known (see Supplementary Fig. S1). In the third dataset (with_rand), only random background genomic features are inserted into the DNA sequence, with no association between the features and the signal. For each type of dataset, the sequence length is 200 bp, the signal contains 20% of random noise, the implanted motif length ranges from 5 to 15 bp and each of them was generated 10 times with 10 different random seeds, respectively. Thus, in each set of synthetic data (e.g., from 500 sequences to 8000 ones), there are a total of 160 samples for plain, with_methy, and with_rand synthetic data, and the prediction accuracy (%) for each type of dataset was calculated as the number of correctly predicted motifs divided by 160. Supplementary Fig. S1 illustrates two examples of simulated 200-bp regions containing inserted motifs of 7 and 8 bp, respectively, demonstrating negative and positive correlations with genomic features (e.g., DNA methylation).

  In Fig. 2, the prediction accuracies of BayesPI-FLY for six synthetic datasets are illustrated, which rang from 500 to 8000 sequences. Here, BayesPI-FLY performed poorly (<50% of accuracy) when the size of training data was small (e.g., 500 synthetic sequences). However, the accuracy improved when the data size increased from 1000 to 4000 sequences (e.g., the accuracies at with_methy data are 60%, 70%, and 80% for 1000, 2000, and 4000 sequences, respectively). After the data size was further increased (e.g., from 6000 to 8000 sequences), BayesPI-FLY only shows a clear improvement in prediction accuracy on some types of data (e.g. with_methy). Though BayesPI-FLY performed poorly on synthetic datasets with small sample size (~500 sequences), its accuracy improved to ~60%–80% when the training data size increased from 1000 to 4000 sequences. Further increasing the training data (e.g. 6000 to 8000 sequences) had a smaller impact, with accuracy reaching around 80%–90% for predicting the correlations between binding motifs and genomic features on the DNA sequences.


### Validation of BayesPI-FLY using HTS data
  To test BayesPI-FLY in real genomic datasets, we obtained both genome-wide DNA methylation data (e.g. WGBS) and six human TF ChIP-seq datasets in the K562 cell line from the ENCODE project. The six selected human TFs are known to bind either CpG methylated (e.g., CEBPG, CEBPB, SP1, and ZBTB33) or unmethylated DNA (e.g. MYC and FOXA1) according to literature information [6, 37]. All the HTS datasets were mapped to the hg38 human reference genome. The bigWig file of the WGBS data was converted to bedGraph format that contains both the CpG methylated position and the percentage of methylation. In each ChIP-seq dataset, there are around 30,000 called peaks with genome-wide fold change over control obtained from MACS2. The signal of a called peak is the average fold change over control within the called peak region, which represents the observed TF-binding occupancy/enrichment in the peak region. For each TF, all called peaks were sorted by peak signal. Subsequently, the top and the bottom 1000 ranked peaks were selected for this work. The DNA methylation in these selected peaks were extracted from the bedGraph file of WGBS data. For the rationale behind selecting the top- and bottom-ranked peaks for motif inference and methylation association studies, please refer to the Supplementary Methods and previous publications [38, 39].

  To evaluate the performance of BayesPI-FLY for predicting associations between the TF binding and the CpG methylation in in vivo experiments, the aforementioned three types of genomic datasets (e.g., DNA sequence, peak signal, and peak methylation profile) from the selected top and bottom 1000 peaks were simultaneously input to BayesPI-FLY, where the parameter settings used in the analysis were: (1) The TF-binding signal and the DNA methylation of selected peaks were log-transformed and converted to Z-scores. (2) A position-dependent methylation model was used to predict the impact of DNA methylation on TF-binding sites. (3) The maximum number of iterations in SCG algorithm and Bayesian evidence estimation were 800 and 3 for the learning model parameters and the model hyperparameters, respectively. (4) The prediction of associations between TF binding and DNA methylation was performed on the forward strand, the reverse strand, and on both strands of the DNA sequences.

  Figures 3, 4, and Supplementary Fig. S3 show sequence logos and enrichments of DNA methylation at TF-binding sites in the K562 cell line as identified by applying BayesPI-FLY on the forward, reverse and both strands of the DNA sequences. The results recovered true contributions of DNA methylation to TF binding for six human TFs in accordance with the literature information: four TFs (CEBPG, CEBPB, SP1, and ZBTB33) are known to bind CpG methylated DNA [6, 37], but two TFs (MYC and FOXA1) prefer to bind unmethylated DNA [6, 33]. BayesPI-FLY is able to predict an association between methylation and TF binding to DNA at single-nucleotide resolution. When comparing the predicted methylation enrichment in a TF-binding site between the forward and the reverse strands (e.g. Fig. 3 vs. Fig. 4), we thus find that there is a strand-specific coupling between DNA methylation and TF binding for some of the TFs: There is a single-nucleotide C with strong positive methylation contribution on the forward strand in the CEBPG-binding site (Fig. 3a), whereas on the reverse strand there are two G nucleotides with a marginal positive methylation contribution in the CEBPG-binding sites (Fig. 4a). A similar discrepancy is also observed at the ZBTB33-binding site: there are three nucleotides, G, G, and T (Fig. 3d) with strong positive methylation contributions on the forward strand, but the T is replaced by C in the reverse strand result and (G, G, C; Fig. 4d) are positively correlated with DNA methylation. These results reveal strand-dependent differences in the inferred methylation–binding associations. While BayesPI-FLY does not directly establish mechanistic strand-specific recognition, the observed asymmetry is consistent with previous reports suggesting that TF–DNA interactions can be influenced by strand context [40].


### Impact of parallel computation and comparison of motif discovery method
  BayesPI-FLY supports parallel computation across four types of motif learning algorithms: the independent model [19], di-nucleotide model [41], DNA shape model [42], and methylation model. This significantly improves performance, such as the real-time prediction speed increases substantially as the number of processes grows (Supplementary Table S1). A comparison of motif discovery performance between BayesPI-FLY and MEME [43] shows that BayesPI-FLY achieves higher prediction accuracy with an increasing number of input sequences, while maintaining algorithmic efficiency. In contrast, MEME is limited by data size, with longer computational times required as the number of input sequences increases, resulting in a significant drop in performance. For further details, please refer to Supplementary Figs S4 and S5.


## Discussion
  In this study, we introduce BayesPI-FLY, a Bayesian neural network–based Python package for de novo motif discovery that integrates DNA sequence features with WGBS methylation data. Using both synthetic and real-world datasets, we show that BayesPI-FLY reproduces previously reported TF–methylation binding preferences, demonstrating its ability to recover established biological patterns from integrated multi-omics datasets. Additionally, the framework reveals strand-dependent methylation–binding associations, which warrant further experimental exploration. By leveraging parallel computation, runtime is reduced by more than 50%, enabling efficient analysis of large-scale multi-omics datasets. Beyond its methodological advances, BayesPI-FLY offers a quantitative framework for exploring the relationship between DNA methylation and TF binding.

  In practice, BayesPI-FLY can be applied to matched ChIP-seq and WGBS datasets from different tissues, cell types, or disease states to quantify context-specific methylation–TF associations [44]. By comparing conditions such as normal and tumor samples, the framework can identify TFs whose inferred methylation sensitivity varies between states, highlighting candidate regulators potentially involved in epigenetically driven regulatory rewiring [45]. Applications across developmental stages or lineage-specific contexts can similarly reveal dynamic shifts in methylation-associated TF-binding patterns, providing a quantitative basis for generating hypotheses about regulatory alterations during cellular differentiation and disease progression [46]. DNA methylation is frequently altered in pathological settings [47], including cancer and hematologic malignancies. In these contexts, BayesPI-FLY can help identify TFs or regulatory elements whose inferred methylation associations differ across disease states or in response to therapeutic intervention [48]. For instance, the framework could be used to investigate methylation-sensitive TF-binding patterns in patients treated with hypomethylating agents or in tumors with mutations in DNA methyltransferases (e.g., DNMT3A/B) or IDH family genes [49]. Such analyses may provide insights into disease-associated regulatory mechanisms and therapeutic response. These applications are hypothesis-generating and experimental validation is required to establish causal or mechanistic relationships.


## Conclusion
  BayesPI-FLY offers a powerful computational framework that bridges statistical modeling and biological insight. By characterizing the methylation dependence of TF binding and enabling the integration of additional epigenetic layers, it provides a versatile platform for exploring how epigenetic regulation shapes transcriptional control. This approach not only supports hypothesis generation but may also guide future experimental and translational research aimed at understanding and targeting epigenetic mechanisms.
