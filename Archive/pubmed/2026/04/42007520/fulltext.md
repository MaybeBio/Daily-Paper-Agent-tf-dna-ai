# NyxBind: enhancing deep neural representations for transcription factor binding site prediction via contrastive learning


## Abstract
  Although pretrained genomic language models effectively capture general DNA sequence patterns through masked language modeling, they often struggle to discriminate subtle yet biologically critical differences among transcription factor binding site (TFBS) motifs. Recent studies suggest that contrastive learning can enhance the discriminative power of embeddings by explicitly modeling inter-instance similarities and differences. Building on this insight, we introduce NyxBind, a TFBS prediction model that applies contrastive learning across multiple TFBS types to enhance regulatory sequence representations. Across 159 TFBS prediction tasks, NyxBind achieves the best performance on all evaluation metrics and improves Matthews Correlation Coefficient by 4.71 percentage points over DNABERT2. NyxBind supports both full-parameter and parameter-efficient fine-tuning while maintaining strong performance. It also enables accurate motif visualization, with results closely matching experimentally validated transcription factor binding profiles.


## Introduction
  Transcription factors (TFs) regulate gene expression by binding to short DNA regions known as transcription factor binding sites (TFBSs), typically 6–20 base pairs in length [1, 2]. Accurate identification of TFBSs is crucial for understanding gene regulation, interpreting noncoding variants, and elucidating cellular processes such as differentiation and disease progression [3, 4]. Each TF recognizes specific sequence motifs, commonly represented by position weight matrices (PWMs) and visualized as sequence logos. These motifs can be inferred from high-throughput assays such as ChIP-seq, SELEX, or PBMs [5], and identified computationally using tools such as MEME and HOMER [6, 7]. However, TF binding specificity is influenced not only by core motifs but also by flanking sequence context and higher order genomic features such as chromatin accessibility and DNA shape [8], thereby necessitating more expressive models.

  Deep learning has significantly advanced TFBS prediction by enabling the automatic learning of complex sequence features. DeepBind [9] pioneered the use of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for this task, learning motif-like patterns directly from one-hot-encoded DNA sequences. This approach was subsequently extended by models such as DeepSEA [10], which employed deeper CNN architectures to predict a broad range of epigenomic features, and DanQ [11], which combined CNNs with recurrent neural networks to capture both short-range and long-range sequence dependencies.

  More recently, inspired by advances in natural language processing, genomic language models (gLMs) have emerged as powerful tools for DNA sequence representation. DNABERT [12] adapts the BERT architecture [13] to DNA by tokenizing sequences into k-mers and pretraining on reference genomes using the masked language modeling (MLM) objective. DNABERT2 [14] builds on this with architectural improvements and more efficient training strategies. Several other gLMs, including Nucleotide Transformer [15], MutBERT [16], and GPN-MSA [17], further expand on this paradigm using diverse pretraining corpora and modeling choices. Additionally, models such as Caduceus [18] and Evo [19] employ alternative architectures for efficient long-sequence modeling.

  Despite their success in capturing general sequence patterns, gLMs pretrained solely with MLM are not inherently optimized for tasks that require distinguishing between closely related sequence classes—such as identifying which TF a given binding site belongs to. Although MLM enables rich contextual representation learning, it lacks an explicit objective to cluster functionally similar sequences together and separate dissimilar ones in the embedding space. As a result, embeddings from MLM pretraining may be suboptimal for classification tasks that demand high discriminative power. To address this limitation, several recent studies have explored augmenting MLM with additional training strategies. For instance, DNABERTS [20] introduces contrastive learning to enhance cross-species generalization by enforcing separation between functionally distinct sequences; however, it yields limited improvement in TFBS identification tasks. Other task-specific adaptations, such as BERT-TFBS [21] and BertSNR [22], combine MLM-pretrained models with convolutional layers, attention mechanisms, or multi-task learning frameworks to enhance TFBS prediction. While these models leverage the strengths of MLM, their performance gains underscore the need for explicit supervision at the representation level to better capture fine-grained regulatory distinctions.

  Here, we introduce NyxBind, a contrastive learning framework that leverages multiple TFBS sequences to further pretrain existing MLM-based genomic models. NyxBind employs an advanced contrastive learning strategy to construct positive and negative sequence pairs across different TFBSs, thereby capturing sequence-level similarities and differences and reorganizing the embedding space to enhance functional separability. This approach enables the model to more effectively distinguish closely related TFBSs, producing embeddings that are both highly discriminative and biologically meaningful, and thus better suited for downstream regulatory tasks. By leveraging TFBS-derived supervision, NyxBind directly addresses this limitation, achieving substantial performance gains in TFBS-specific tasks.

## Materials and methods

### Data
  Contrastive learning was employed to further enhance the pretrained model using 690 human ChIP-seq TFBS datasets from the ENCODE project [23], all mapped to hg19, as in prior work (e.g. DeepSEA). These datasets comprise 160 TFs across 91 human cell types, with detailed information provided in Supplementary Table S1. To facilitate contrastive learning—and because TFBS motifs of the same TF are generally conserved across cell types—sequences from the same TF but different cell types were grouped into a single class, resulting in 160 labeled TFBS categories. Following the protocol of DNABERT2, we extracted 101 bp sequences centered at ChIP-seq peak summits from all chromosomes except chromosomes 11 and 12, which were held out, merged, and then split evenly into validation and test sets to prevent data leakage.

  For TFBS prediction benchmarking, we selected 159 datasets from the original 690 ChIP-seq datasets, covering 33 human cell types and 35 TFs, following the evaluation protocol used in BERT-TFBS. Although the original benchmark included 165 datasets, we excluded six datasets during preprocessing because their test sets contained fewer than 100 samples, which could lead to unreliable performance estimates; consequently, 159 datasets were retained for all subsequent experiments. Positive samples consisted of 101 bp sequences centered at all ChIP-seq peaks, while negative samples were sampled from nonoverlapping regions with GC content filtering to ensure comparable base composition, following the approach described in DNABERT2 [14]. A complete list of the datasets is provided in Supplementary Table S2.

  For motif discovery, 33 TFBS datasets from the JASPAR database [24] were used, which were previously employed in the BertSNR framework and cover a diverse range of TF families. Positive sequences contained a single TFBS randomly positioned within the sequence, and negative samples were generated via dinucleotide-preserving shuffling using uShuffle [25]. This construction strategy was adopted to facilitate direct comparison with BertSNR. To enable independent motif visualization, an additional set of 33 JASPAR TFBS datasets not used during model training was employed. The detailed dataset composition is reported in Supplementary Table S3.


### NyxBind overview
  NyxBind is pretrained through a two-stage process, as illustrated in Fig. 1(a). In the first stage, MLM is used to pretrain a BERT-based model on large-scale genomic sequences. We directly adopt the DNABERT2 pretrained weights for Stage 1, leveraging its robust and generalizable representations learned from multispecies genomic data. In the second stage, full-parameter contrastive learning is employed across TFBS categories, guiding the model to encode shared TFBS semantics while maintaining discriminative representations for different TFs.

  Following contrastive pretraining, NyxBind can be further fine-tuned on task-specific TFBS prediction datasets. We support both full-parameter FT and parameter-efficient adaptation using low-rank adaptation (LoRA) [26], offering flexibility for deployment under varying computational constraints.


#### Model structure
  NyxBind adopts an architecture based on DNABERT2, combining a byte-pair encoding (BPE) tokenizer [27] with an embedding layer that integrates token and positional information. The BPE tokenizer segments DNA sequences into variable-length tokens by iteratively merging frequently co-occurring nucleotide pairs. This approach improves motif representation, reduces input length, minimizes redundancy, and enhances computational efficiency.

  The encoder consists of 12 Transformer layers with multi-head self-attention and several architectural optimizations, producing hidden representations with a dimensionality of 768. These optimizations include Flash Attention [28], an attention implementation that reduces memory access overhead and improves training speed; low-precision layer normalization for improved computational efficiency; and the GEGLU activation function, which offers greater expressiveness compared with ReLU. During contrastive learning, a contrastive head is applied to aggregate token-level representations into a sequence-level embedding via mean pooling.

  For downstream TFBS classification tasks, a task-specific classification head is appended during FT. Both full-parameter and parameter-efficient FT modes are supported, allowing flexible adaptation of the model to various settings and datasets.


#### Contrastive learning
  To enhance the discriminative capacity of the pretrained MLM-based model, NyxBind introduces an additional contrastive learning stage with full-parameter training, employing Symmetric Multiple Negatives Ranking Loss (SMNRL) as the primary optimization objective. As shown in Fig. 1(b), the model is optimized using TFBS pairs, where each pair consists of two TFBSs belonging to the same class. One sequence is treated as the anchor, while the other serves as the positive sample. For each input sequence, the model produces a sequence-level representation by applying mean pooling. SMNRL then constructs a similarity matrix based on these embeddings for all anchors and positive samples within a batch, encouraging each anchor representation to achieve the highest similarity with its corresponding positive counterpart while treating all other samples as implicit negatives. Due to the symmetric formulation of SMNRL, anchors and positive samples are interchangeable, and the similarity computation is performed in both directions, generating two similarity matrices that jointly contribute to the training objective.

  To validate the effectiveness of SMNRL and assess alternative formulations, we conducted ablation experiments comparing five loss functions: SMNRL, MultipleNegativesRankingLoss (MNRL) [29], Information Noise-Contrastive Estimation (InfoNCE) loss [30], GISTEmbedLoss (GEL)[31], and ContrastiveLoss (CL) [32]. Among them, SMNRL consistently demonstrated the best overall discriminative performance, justifying its adoption as the final loss function in NyxBind.

  During contrastive training, we utilized sequences containing TFBSs collected from 690 ChIP-seq datasets, covering 160 distinct TFBS types. For the SMNRL, MNRL, and GEL objectives, 50 000 positive sequence pairs were constructed per TFBS type, resulting in a total of 8 million positive pairs. These objectives were trained without explicitly providing negative pairs, as negatives were implicitly formed within each batch. In contrast, for the CL objective, 25 000 positive pairs and 25 000 negative pairs were generated per TFBS type, also yielding 8 million pairs in total. For validation and testing, we further constructed 500 positive and 500 negative sequence pairs per TFBS from sequences drawn from chromosomes 11 and 12, resulting in 160 000 pairs for the validation set and 160 000 pairs for the test set. These sets were specifically designed to evaluate whether the model can produce distinguishable embeddings for distinct TFBSs.

  To assess the utility of the contrastive pretraining for downstream tasks, we performed FT on 159 ChIP-seq datasets to conduct ablation experiments, enabling a fair comparison of different training objectives and their effects on task performance.

### Evaluation

#### Evaluation metrics
  For contrastive learning, we evaluate the quality of the learned representations using the binary classification evaluator module from the Sentence Transformers framework. This evaluator computes cosine similarity between sequence pairs and reports three metrics: cosine average precision (cosine AP), cosine F1 score, and cosine accuracy. Cosine AP measures the model’s ability to rank positive pairs above negative ones across a range of thresholds, providing a threshold-independent evaluation. Cosine F1 and cosine accuracy are computed using a fixed similarity threshold, capturing performance trade-offs between precision, recall, and overall classification accuracy.

  TFBS prediction is typically formulated as a binary classification task [33]. To evaluate model performance, we consider four widely used classification metrics: accuracy (ACC), ROC-AUC, PR-AUC, and Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC). ACC is a threshold-based metric that measures the proportion of correctly classified samples and provides a basic assessment of overall performance. ROC-AUC, a threshold-free metric, evaluates the model’s ability to discriminate between positive and negative classes across varying decision thresholds. PR-AUC, also threshold-free, focuses on the trade-off between precision and recall and is particularly informative for imbalanced datasets where the positive class is sparse. MCC is a threshold-based metric that incorporates all four confusion matrix components (true/false positives and negatives) into a single score, offering a balanced evaluation even in the presence of class imbalance. Together, these metrics capture both overall and class-specific performance under different evaluation perspectives, providing a comprehensive assessment of TFBS prediction accuracy.


#### Fine-tuning strategies for NyxBind
  NyxBind supports two FT strategies for TFBS prediction: full-parameter FT and parameter-efficient FT based on LoRA. Full FT updates all model parameters, whereas LoRA freezes the pretrained weights and optimizes only lightweight low-rank adapters, which are applied to the query, key, and value projections in the self-attention mechanism and the linear layers of the feed-forward network.

  To compare the effectiveness of these two FT strategies, we performed both full-parameter and LoRA-based FT across 159 TFBS classification tasks. For full-parameter FT, we used a learning rate of 3e-5, a batch size of 32, and five training epochs. For LoRA, we used a learning rate of 7e-4, a batch size of 32, 5 training epochs, a LoRA rank of 8, a LoRA alpha of 16, and a dropout rate of 0.05.


#### Evaluation of NyxBind and alternative models for TFBS prediction
  A diverse set of representative models was selected, spanning both traditional architectures and pretrained transformer-based approaches. DeepBind employs a single-layer CNN with one-hot encoding to capture local sequence motifs. DanQ builds on this architecture by combining CNNs with bidirectional long short-term memory layers to model both local and long-range dependencies. We used the PyTorch implementations provided in DeepSTF [33], with a learning rate of 1e-3, a batch size of 64, and 15 training epochs, following the hyperparameter settings reported in the original paper. The NT series [15] includes several large-scale transformer models pretrained on different genomic datasets. NT-500M-Human was trained on the human reference genome (hg38), NT-2500M-1000G on 3202 human genomes from the 1000 Genomes Project, and NT-2500M-Multi on 850 genomes from multiple species. NT-v2-500M-Multi incorporates architectural refinements including rotary positional embeddings, SwiGLU activations, and the removal of biases and dropout layers. All NT models were fine-tuned using LoRA with a learning rate of 1e-4, a batch size of 32, five training epochs, a LoRA rank of 8, a LoRA alpha of 16, and a dropout rate of 0.05, following the hyperparameter settings used in DNABERT2 [14]. DNABERT2 is a BERT-based gLM pretrained on multi-species genomic sequences. In contrast, DNABERT-S focuses on producing species-aware DNA embeddings, enabling improved cross-species representation learning. BERT-TFBS extends DNABERT2 by integrating CNN layers and a Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM), enabling enhanced motif detection. For DNABERT2, DNABERT-S, and NyxBind, we followed the hyperparameter settings of DNABERT2, using a learning rate of 3e-5, a batch size of 32, except that the number of training epochs was increased to 5. For BERT-TFBS, we followed the hyperparameter settings reported in the original paper, using a learning rate of 1.5e-5, a batch size of 32, and 15 training epochs to ensure convergence given its added complexity. Transformer-based models often serve as embedding backbones in hybrid architectures. To evaluate the generalization ability of NyxBind in this context, we replaced the DNABERT2 encoder in BERT-TFBS with NyxBind and fine-tuned it using the same hyperparameters, yielding a variant referred to as BERT-TFBS_N. This experiment assesses whether the representations learned by NyxBind retain their effectiveness when transferred to a downstream architecture.

  All models were optimized using the AdamW optimizer with a weight decay of 0.01 and 30 warm-up steps. These settings were adopted across all models, as preliminary experiments indicated that variations in these hyperparameters had minimal impact on performance. In addition, early stopping based on validation loss was applied to prevent overfitting and to retain the model with near-optimal validation performance.


### Motif analysis
  To assess whether NyxBind captures biologically meaningful sequence patterns, we developed a motif visualization framework adapted from DNABERT [12]. The objective is to extract TF binding motifs learned by the model and evaluate their alignment with experimentally validated reference motifs.

  Attention scores were extracted from the final Transformer layer of the model, specifically capturing the attention from the [CLS] token to all other tokens. Each token’s attention score was redistributed across its corresponding nucleotide positions, producing a base-resolution attention map. These scores were then standardized using z-score normalization to generate a quantitative attention profile along the input sequence. A sliding window was subsequently applied to this profile to identify high-confidence regions. Windows with aggregate scores exceeding a predefined threshold were selected, and the central regions were extracted as candidate motif instances. These instances were then aligned to produce PWMs and sequence logos, enabling direct comparison with canonical TF motifs.

  To evaluate motif recovery performance, we followed the experimental setup proposed in BertSNR [22]. We first fine-tuned each model on 33 human TFBS datasets from the JASPAR database. To avoid data leakage during evaluation, we then applied motif discovery to a separate set of 33 matched datasets corresponding to the same TFs. This pairing ensured that the evaluation was conducted on held-out sequences not used during training, while maintaining biological relevance through one-to-one TF correspondence.

  A comparative evaluation was conducted between NyxBind and three baseline methods for motif visualization: (1) BertSNR, a BERT-based model that supports single-nucleotide-resolution prediction; (2) DeepSNR [34], a hybrid CNN–deconvolutional model; and (3) D-AEDNet [35], an encoder–decoder architecture that integrates convolutional and attention layers to enhance feature extraction and information flow.

  Motif similarity was assessed using the TOMTOM tool [36], which compares discovered motifs with all human TFBS PWMs in the JASPAR database. ## Results

### Training objective variants
  We conducted an ablation study comparing SMNRL with other contrastive learning methods, including InfoNCE, MNRL, GEL, and CL. As shown in Table 1, SMNRL consistently achieves the highest MCC across all settings.

  Compared with alternative objectives, SMNRL provides more stable gradient signals by symmetrically optimizing anchor-positive interactions in both directions, which mitigates representation collapse and reduces training instability. Furthermore, SMNRL makes more effective use of in-batch samples without introducing auxiliary models or complex sample selection strategies, resulting in improved batch efficiency. In contrast, InfoNCE relies on a larger similarity matrix with relatively sparse positive signals per anchor, which can make optimization more challenging under limited batch sizes.

  To assess NyxBind’s ability to generate informative representations of TFBSs, we evaluated its performance in producing discriminative embeddings for pairs of similar and dissimilar TFBS sequences on the test set, as shown in Table 2. Evaluation was performed using cosine similarity between the representations of TFBS pairs, with metrics including cosine AP, cosine accuracy, and cosine F1 score. NyxBind exhibits substantial improvements over the baseline, with a 26.01 percentage-point increase in cosine AP, a 15.75 percentage-point increase in cosine accuracy, and a 3.50 percentage-point increase in cosine F1. These results highlight the effectiveness of contrastive learning in enhancing the model’s ability to generate consistent embeddings for functionally similar sequences and to differentiate between different TFBS binding patterns.

#### NyxBind improves TFBS prediction
  A large-scale comparison with nine representative methods was carried out on 159 ChIP-seq datasets for TFBS prediction. Detailed results are provided in Supplementary Table S4. As shown in Table 3 and Fig. 3(a), averaged over 159 TFBS ChIP-seq datasets, NyxBind achieves the best performance across all evaluation metrics, surpassing the second-best model, DNABERT2, with absolute gains of 2.33% in ACC, 4.71% in MCC, 1.25% in PR-AUC, and 1.51% in ROC-AUC. As illustrated in the scatter plots of Fig. 3(b), NyxBind exhibits robust and consistent improvements across all four evaluation metrics on the majority of the 159 ChIP-seq datasets. Collectively, these results demonstrate the strong performance and generalization capability of NyxBind in TFBS prediction and its suitability for downstream regulatory genomics tasks.

  We evaluated DNABERTS on TFBS prediction and found its performance largely comparable with DNABERT2 across most datasets. This is consistent with the fact that DNABERT-S performs contrastive learning across multi-species sequences rather than TFBS-specific sequences, making it less specialized for TFBS discrimination.

  To further evaluate the effectiveness of NyxBind as a sequence embedding model, we conducted additional experiments by replacing the DNABERT2 encoder in BERT-TFBS with NyxBind, while keeping all other components unchanged—including the CNN, CBAM, and the output layers. The resulting configuration, referred to as BERT-TFBS_N, was fine-tuned using full-parameter training on 159 ChIP-seq datasets. As shown in Table 3, BERT-TFBS_N consistently outperforms the original BERT-TFBS model across all evaluation metrics. These results demonstrate that NyxBind provides more informative embeddings than a model pretrained solely with MLM and can enhance downstream TFBS prediction when integrated into hybrid architectures.


#### NyxBind performs well with both LoRA and full-parameter fine-tuning
  To assess parameter-efficient FT performance, we evaluated both NyxBind and DNABERT2 on 159 TFBS ChIP-seq datasets using two strategies: full-parameter FT and parameter-efficient FT via LoRA. As shown in Table 4, NyxBind maintains consistently high performance across both tuning strategies, with only marginal differences between FT and LoRA: 0.21% in ACC, 0.47% in MCC, 0.05% in PR-AUC, and 0.13% in ROC-AUC. These results demonstrate NyxBind’s strong adaptability to diverse FT paradigms. In contrast, DNABERT2 exhibits a more pronounced performance drop when switching from FT to LoRA, with declines of 1.69% in ACC, 3.46% in MCC, 1.55% in PR-AUC, and 1.69% in ROC-AUC, indicating lower robustness to parameter-efficient adaptation. ### Ablation study of the two-stage training strategy in NyxBind
  To investigate whether the two-stage training strategy of NyxBind is necessary, we conducted a series of ablation studies by removing each stage independently. Specifically, we considered three variants: (i) wo MLM, where the model is trained with contrastive learning from random initialization without the stage-one MLM pretraining; (ii) wo CL, where the model is initialized from the DNABERT2 MLM-pretrained weights and directly fine-tuned on TFBS prediction without stage-two contrastive learning; and (iii) MLM+CL, corresponding to the full NyxBind framework.

  All models were evaluated on 159 TFBS prediction tasks, and performance was primarily assessed using the average MCC across tasks. As shown in Table 5, removing either MLM or contrastive learning leads to consistent performance degradation across all evaluation metrics. These results demonstrate that both MLM and contrastive learning are essential components of NyxBind, with MLM pretraining providing a particularly critical foundation for effective representation learning.


### NyxBind effectively identifies motifs from sequences

The resulting base-resolution scores were then standardized using z-score normalization and used for visualization of attention-derived motifs.

## Discussion
  In this study, we presented NyxBind, a contrastive learning framework designed to refine sequence representations for TFBS prediction. Through large-scale benchmarking on 159 ChIP-seq datasets, we show that NyxBind consistently outperforms existing models across diverse evaluation settings and generalizes well across TFs, cell types, and binding contexts.

  While existing genomic foundation models are typically pretrained using self-supervised objectives such as MLM or next-token prediction, these objectives are not explicitly designed to discriminate between functionally distinct regulatory sequences. NyxBind addresses this limitation by applying contrastive learning to TFBS-labeled sequences, explicitly pulling together sequences from the same TF and pushing apart those from different TFs. This task-aligned supervision encourages the model to learn more discriminative and functionally meaningful representations, leading to substantial performance improvements in TFBS classification.

  Although NyxBind demonstrates strong performance in TFBS prediction and motif visualization, future work could focus on expanding the training datasets to include additional TFs, integrating cross-species regulatory sequences, and introducing more fine-grained similarity annotations to further improve the model’s robustness and biological generalization.

  In summary, we have demonstrated a contrastive learning approach that enhances the representation of TFBSs. By explicitly modeling similarities and differences between regulatory sequences, NyxBind generates highly discriminative representations, providing a solid foundation for further advances in regulatory genomics.
