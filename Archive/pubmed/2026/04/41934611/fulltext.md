# Transcriptional mechanisms of sex-biased gene expression and their connections to disease-associated variation


## Abstract
  Humans display sexual dimorphism across many traits, but little is known about underlying genetic mechanisms and impacts on disease. We utilized single-cell RNA-seq of 480 lymphoblastoid cell lines (LCLs) to identify 1200 genes with significantly sex-biased expression. While reproducibility was highest among LCL datasets, 71% were found to be sex-biased in at least one GTEx tissue, with a core dataset of 21 genes displaying sex-biased expression across all datasets and tissues examined. While 7.7% of sex-biased genes can be directly explained by differences in the number of sex chromosomes, most sex-biased genes (79%) are targets of transcription factors that display sex-biased expression. FOSL1, ZNF730, ZFX, and ZNF726 appear to make the largest contribution to this based on machine learning and linear modeling approaches, and all four of these transcription factors are regulated by the number of X chromosomes. Further, by testing the difference in genetic effect size (β) of conditionally independent expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) identified in each sex separately, we identified 2,390 sex-biased eQTL (sb-eQTL) across the genome, but evidence of replication in an independent dataset was modest. However, permutation analysis demonstrated that sb-eQTL identified using real sex was more likely to have concordant direction of effect. Further exploratory analysis revealed that these sb-eQTL are enriched in over 100 GWAS phenotypes, including many loci associated with female-biased autoimmune diseases such as multiple sclerosis. Our results demonstrate widespread genetic impacts on sexual dimorphism and identify possible mechanisms and clinical targets for sex differences in diverse diseases.


## INTRODUCTION
  Humans display sexual dimorphism across a variety of traits. Anthropometric and physiological traits (e.g., body height, waist-to-hip ratio, etc.) are obvious examples of differences between males and females, but sex differences are also evident in the incidence, prevalence, severity, and treatment response in human disease. For example, females are more likely to develop autoimmune disease(1, 2) but show greater resistance to many infectious agents than males(3). Sex disparities are also apparent across cancers, with kidney(4), liver(5), skin(6), and laryngeal(7) cancers displaying a male bias, while breast and thyroid cancers(8) are more common in females. Phenotypic sex differences have been attributed to various factors including sex-specific hormone activity, sex chromosome complement, sex and gender influences on behavior, and complex differences in environment. However, despite these widespread differences, little is known about the mechanisms and biological functions that underlie sexual dimorphism in humans.

  Previous studies have shown that gene expression differs by sex throughout the genome(9, 10), and several genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified genetic variants that show sex-specific effects in human disease(11–14). However, while previous studies have identified genes with sex-biased expression (sb-Genes)(10, 15, 16), how well sb-Genes replicate across diverse datasets and the underlying molecular mechanisms of why individual genes display sex-biased expression are not well characterized. Further, recent attempts to link gene expression differences to single genetic variants through sex-biased expression quantitative trait loci (sb-eQTL) discovery have reported few significant associations, with little replication across independent datasets(10, 17–19), leading to uncertainty in whether sb-eQTL contributed meaningfully to sex-biased expression(10). The lack of sb-eQTL previously discovered and replicated may be due to relatively small differences in the genetic control of gene expression by sex, low power in previous studies, or tissue-type specificity of genetic effects.

  Here, we performed single-cell RNA-seq of 240 male and 240 female lymphoblastoid cell lines (LCLs) to identify transcriptomic sex differences and sb-eQTL across the genome. We identified 1,200 sb-Genes, replicated these in multiple datasets across tissues, and categorized their effects by underlying mechanism. Although a two-step method identified 2,390 sb-eQTL, we found that sb-eQTL are not a major factor in sex-biased expression, although likely still contribute to sex-biased traits at individual loci. In contrast, we were able to assign ~97% of all sb-Genes to mechanisms involving sex chromosome copy number or transcription factor expression, revealing a molecular basis for sex-biased gene expression throughout the genome.


## RESULTS

### Sex-biased gene expression is reproducible and a subset is generalizable across tissues
  Previous work in identifying sex-biased gene expression has identified thousands of genes displaying sex-biased expression (sb-Genes)(10, 16). The GTEx study examined conservation of sb-Genes across tissues and found largely tissue-specific effects, although a fraction of sb-Genes enriched for XCI-escapees were consistent across tissues. However, it is important to determine how well the sb-Genes identified in this ground-breaking work replicate across different individuals, particularly using a dataset with diverse genetic ancestry. Therefore, we sought to identify genes with sex-biased expression (sb-Genes) in a single cell type that would allow for testing replication in multiple datasets.

  We previously developed a rapid and scalable pooled scRNA-seq method to uncover transcriptomic differences in a diverse cohort of 96 LCLs called scHi-HOST(20). We expanded this method to 480 LCLs in twelve worldwide populations (Fig. 1A). Through integration of phased whole genome sequencing, we are able to deconvolute pooled transcriptomics to identify gene expression from each individual LCL, allowing for rapid screening of hundreds of cell lines. Importantly for this study, scHi-HOST was conducted with equal numbers of male and female LCLs, as defined by sex chromosome complement. Through this controlled method, we can isolate sex-specific transcriptomic signatures due to genetic differences without the impact of differential hormones and environmental conditions and integrate our results with publicly available data to discover genome-wide sex differences (Fig. 1A). In total, we sequenced 196,338 single cells that mapped to a single individual, with a mean of 409 cells per individual (Table S1; Fig. S1A, B). Single-cell UMAP clustering revealed a homogeneous cell population without notable clustering by individual LCL (Fig. S1C). As expected, we detected highly significant differences of X and Y chromosomal genes that matched the reported sex of these LCLs (Fig. S1D) but also observed significant changes in autosomal genes. In total, we discovered 1,200 genes with significant differential expression by sex (sb-Genes, FDR<0.05; Fig. 1B; Table S2). Also as expected, the effect size of sex-bias was largest for Y chromosomal genes (mean |log2FC|=6.59, 3.56–7.96), followed by X chromosomal genes (mean |log2FC|=0.34, 0.04–7.22), with autosomal genes showing the smallest effect (mean |log2FC|=0.02, 0.02–1.88; Fig. 1C). Overall, the effect size of sb-Genes is small, with only 16 sb-Genes (2 X and 14 Y chromosomal genes) with large effect (|log2FC|≥2). This is consistent with previous work in bulk RNA-seq from 29 tissues(54) that found a median of 36 sex-differential genes with large effect (|log2FC|≥1). In addition, the GTEx consortium bulk RNA-seq of 43 tissues(10) found small effect sizes in autosomal sb-Genes (MASH-adjusted mean |log2FC|=0.09) and larger effects in sex-biased X chromosomal genes (MASH-adjusted mean |log2FC|=0.25) with 22 genes showing a strong (|log2FC|≥2) sex difference. In GTEx LCLs, the X chromosome gene XIST, the lncRNA integral to XCI, was the only detected sb-Gene of large effect (|log2FC|=11.1), also detected in our dataset (|log2FC|=7.2). Y chromosomal genes were excluded from the pan-tissue GTEx results. KEGG enrichment revealed sb-Gene functional enrichment in pathways involving immune responses and basic cellular functions (Fig. S1E). We additionally tested if sb-Gene effect is consistent between the diverse ancestries represented in our dataset. We found that the effect of sb-Genes correlated well between ancestries, supporting that global sex-biased expression is well-maintained in diverse populations (Spearman’s ρ when compared with full dataset: 0.938 in AFR, 0.960 in ASN, 0.870 in AMR, 0.894 in EUR) with sex chromosomal sb-Genes displaying greater correlation (mean Spearman’s ρ=0.962) than autosomal sb-Genes (mean Spearman’s ρ=0.903; Fig. S1F).

  We assessed the level of replication of sb-Genes in an independent LCL dataset of comparable power (MAGE(28)) and across human tissues with the GTEx dataset(10) (Fig. 1D). Autosomal and X chromosomal scHi-HOST sb-genes were tested for enrichment using a Fisher’s Exact Test, restricting analysis only to genes expressed in both scHi-HOST LCLs and the tissue being compared. Enrichment was verified using permutation with matching for expression level in non-sex-biased genes (Fig. 1D). The greatest fold-enrichment was observed with the MAGE and GTEx LCLs, with 331 sb-Genes reproduced in either LCL dataset (Table S2B) and an extremely high-confidence list of 54 sb-Genes that reproduce in both LCL datasets (Table S2C). Whole blood and spleen, tissues with high lymphocyte counts, were also highly enriched for scHi-HOST sb-Genes, but we detected enrichment across nearly all tested tissues. In a parallel analysis, we tested if the effect size of scHi-HOST sb-Genes was consistent across other datasets or tissue types. We found that scHi-HOST sex effects correlated well with diverse tissues (Fig. S2A), particularly in other LCL datasets like MAGE (Spearman’s ρ= 0.793, pval=1.45×10−263) and GTEx LCLs (Spearman’s ρ= 0.561, pval=2.00×10−101) as well as whole blood (Spearman’s ρ= 0.414, pval=6.80×10−50). We then tested if sb-Genes identified using more stringent thresholds (FDR<0.05, <0.01, and <0.001) demonstrated increased levels of replication. We found that enrichment in diverse tissues is stronger at more stringent thresholds, with sb-Genes at FDR<0.001 showing nearly 10-fold enrichment with 65 of 251 sb-Genes replicating with GTEx LCLs, and that more significant sb-Genes replicated in more tissue types (Kruskal-Wallis p=2.8×10−8, Fig. S2B, C, D). Thus, sex biased differences in gene expression are highly reproducible across three LCL datasets and a subset is generalizable across tissues. In total, 849 (71.5%) of tested scHi-HOST sb-Genes were reproduced in at least one other dataset, with a core subset of 21 primarily X chromosomal genes previously identified to escape XCI(10) showing sex-biased expression in all tested tissues. Interestingly, a single autosomal gene, DDX43, also showed conserved sex-biased expression in all tested tissues. This gene has previously been implicated in male fertility and spermatogenesis(55), and displays sex-biased methylation in newborn(56) and adult(57) individuals.


### Partitioning of sb-Genes by mechanism
  The variable effect size of sb-Genes points to different underlying mechanisms of sex-biased gene expression. The largest effects are due to the Y chromosomal genes that are entirely absent in females. We detected 14 Y chromosomal genes expressed in male LCLs. The next largest effect are genes that vary by copy number due to their presence on the X chromosome. In total, 92 X chromosomal genes display significant sex-biased expression, none of which lie in the pseudoautosomal regions shared by the X and Y chromosomes. Of these, 78 have previously been annotated as escaping X chromosome inactivation in at least one tissue(10) and display a larger sex difference in expression (mean |log2FC|=0.38) than both sex-biased autosomal genes (mean |log2FC|=0.02) and sex-biased X chromosomal genes not annotated as XCI-escapees (n=14, mean |log2FC|=0.15). In total, 7.7% of sb-Genes can be explained due to a direct difference in copy number.

  However, the vast majority of sb-Genes occur on the autosomes where they exhibit more modest effect sizes. One step upstream of these differences in gene expression is activation or repression by transcription factors (TFs), which have been previously shown to be involved in sex differences across mammalian lineages(58). Thus, we hypothesized that some fraction of the remaining sb-Genes could be traced to variable expression of upstream TFs that lead to downstream targets exhibiting sex-biased expression. To determine if TF expression is predictive of sex-biased expression, we utilized a deep neural network (DNN) model previously trained and evaluated using >100,000 randomly drawn RNA-seq samples from the ARCHS4 resource(33). This DNN predictor uses the combinatorial effects of TFs to predict the expression of all genes. We assigned TF expression in females as the baseline condition before applying the TF fold-changes determined from the differential expression analyses described above. Through this approach, we discovered that DNN-predicted target expression (based on 1600 TFs) agreed with our empirical dataset (Spearman’s ρ=0.25, p=1.72×10−15). This level of prediction accuracy is similar to other applications of the same scHi-HOST DNN to predict differential expression between normal and diseased states (48 phenotypes from 27 distinct studies aggregated in Magnusson et al, 2022(33)). Average Spearman’s ρ for these predictions was 0.28 (range from −0.17 to 0.69, Table S3), suggesting that while not able to account for all sex-biased expression, the DNN performance is on par with other independent RNA-seq datasets and disease perturbations, and captures a substantial portion of the observed variation in expression patterns. We further hypothesized that only significantly sex-biased TF expression would be sufficient to predict genome-wide sex-biased expression. We thus restricted the DNN prediction to only include effects of sex-biased TFs (n=127, FDR<0.05) and discovered that DNN-predicted expression still correlated with our empirical data (Spearman’s ρ=0.20, p=4.53×10−11). Both results above were generated using all sb-Genes. When we removed the sb-Genes that are likely driven directly by copy number (the 14 Y chromosomal and 79 XCI-escapees), the DNN prediction showed improved accuracy (Spearman’s ρ=0.39, p=1.10×10−34 based on 1600 TFs and Spearman’s ρ=0.28, p=1.67×10−18 based on the 127 sb-TFs), consistent with the hypothesis that transcription factor activity is more impactful on sex-biased expression in these genes.

  We then sought to identify and model the effects of the TFs that could be driving sex-biased expression. TF enrichment analyses through ChEA3 (which ranks likely causal TFs based on RNA-seq co-expression and ChIP-Seq datasets(29)) revealed 608 TFs with significant (FDR<0.05) enrichment for sex-biased targets (Table S4) in at least one dataset. Out of these enriched TFs, 81 display sex-biased expression themselves (sb-TF, FDR<0.05). This number of sb-TFs is about twice as many as we observed from gene expression level matched permutation analysis (drawing 608 expression-matched TFs (10 expression bins) from the pool of TFs not enriched for sex-biased targets; 10,000 permutations, mean = 38; p<0.0001; Fig. S3A). By investigating the targets of enriched TFs, we discovered that the majority (79%) of sb-Genes are targets of at least one enriched sb-TF. An additional 10% of sb-Genes are targets of non-sex-biased TFs that were also enriched for sb-Gene targets. Thus, ~89% of sb-Genes can be classified as possibly secondary to a difference in TF expression or activity (Fig. 2A). This is strikingly similar to previous work utilizing gene network analyses to understand sex-biased expression showing that 87% of sex-biased expression is associated with differential targeting by TFs(54). Previous work has also shown that sex-differential TF activity modeled through gene regulatory networks is associated with sex-biased gene expression in cancer patients(59). Consistent with these observations, deep neural network models that use only transcription factor expression can explain more than 95% of the variance in genome-wide gene expression, indicating that TF activity is sufficient to capture the vast majority of transcriptome-wide expression patterns(33).

  To further investigate the enrichment of sb-Genes as targets of sb-TFs, we performed enrichment analyses. We observed a modest but statistically significant enrichment of sb-Genes among sex-biased TF targets compared to non-sex-biased targets (fold enrichment=1.1, Fisher’s exact test p=4.04×10−6). To assess whether this enrichment could arise by chance, we performed permutation analyses by randomly shuffling sex labels and recalculating differential expression. However, no genes were significantly differentially expressed by permuted sex (FDR<0.05). Thus, instead we conducted a permutation analysis to find if sb-Genes (vs. expression matched non-sex-biased genes) were enriched in sb-TF targets. We found that 950/1105 tested sb-Genes (excluding known XCI-escapees and Y chromosomal genes) are targets of sb-TFs. This overlap is significantly greater than permuted equal size sets of non-sex-biased genes (10,000 permutations, mean = 890 genes; permutation p<0.0001, Fig. S3B). These results support the specificity and biological relevance of the observed regulatory relationship between sb-TFs and sex-biased gene expression.

  To further identify which TFs primarily contribute to sex-biased expression, we integrated enriched TFs with the DNN described above. By removing the effect of a single enriched TF at a time from the model, we were able to determine that the sb-Gene FOSL1 has the largest impact on sex-biased expression (Fig. 2B). FOSL1 encodes the Fos-like 1 (FOSL1, also called FRA1) protein which can act as both a transcriptional activator and repressor(60). ScHi-HOST transcriptomics revealed that FOSL1 displays significant male-biased expression (log2FC=0.37, p=7.39×10−6, Fig. 2C). We also observed that sb-Gene targets of FOSL1 are primarily male-biased, suggesting that FOSL1 is predominately acting as a transcriptional activator and promoting male-biased expression (Fig. 2D).

  Three other sb-TFs (ZNF730, ZFX, ZNF726) notably affected DNN model fit. In each case, we broadly see concordant direction of effect in the sex-biased expression of the TF and the sb-Gene targets (Fig. 2C, D). While little is known about ZNF730 and ZNF726, ZFX is a highly female-biased gene that escapes X chromosome inactivation and has previously been shown to drive X-responsive expression across the genome(34, 35). Thus, for ZFX, the mechanism for female-biased TF expression is higher copy number in females. Previous work has demonstrated that much of the effect of additional copies of the X chromosome are mediated by ZFX and that a homologous TF encoded on the Y, ZFY, activates a similar set of targets, with ZFX having a larger effect(35). To test if autosomal enriched TFs are also impacted by X chromosome copy number, we utilized bulk RNA-seq data from LCLs derived from individuals with 1 to 4 X chromosomes. Linear modeling of TF expression and X chromosome copy number revealed that FOSL1, ZNF730, and ZNF726 are all responsive to X chromosome copy number at a nominal threshold (p<0.05; Fig. 2E; data from(34, 35)). Notably for all 81 enriched sb-TFs that we identified, 49 (60.5%) are also correlated with X chromosome copy number (p<0.05; Table S5). To test if this response to X chromosome copy number is unique to sex-biased TFs, we also investigated the 527 enriched TFs that were not sex-biased. We found that only 27.4% of non-sex-biased TFs were differentially expressed by X chromosome copy number (p<0.05) and that sb-TFs are enriched in genes that respond to X chromosome copy number (fold enrichment=1.9, Fisher’s Exact Test p=1.29×10−8). Thus, sb-TFs were about twice as likely to be correlated with X chromosome copy number than non sb-TFs. While this suggests some false positive sb-TFs correlated with X chromosome, the sb-TFs also exhibited stronger correlation than non-sb-TFs as measured by either the effect size (log2FC mean = 76.9 vs. 1.6; log2FC median = 0.12 vs. 0.09; Wilcoxon p = 0.0017) or significance of correlation (-log(p-value) mean = 5.7 vs. 2.9; median = 3.6 vs. 2.4; Wilcoxon p = 0.0014), which may indicate the correlation with X chromosome for non-sb-TFs may also be relevant, but the effect may not be large enough to cause significantly sex-biased expression of the TF.

  Additionally, 29 sb-TFs (35.8%) are significantly differentially expressed in ZFX CRISPRi knockdown fibroblasts (p<0.05; Table S5), which supports previous work highlighting ZFX as a key transcriptional regulator of genome-wide sex differences(35). However, the top autosomal sb-TFs previously discussed (FOSL1, ZNF730, ZNF726) are not significantly impacted during ZFX knockdown and display only limited correlation in expression (Table S5, Fig. S3C). This suggests that the sex-biased expression of most TFs that appear to mediate the sex-biased expression of autosomal genes may arise secondary to the number of X chromosomes, although this effect may be ZFX-independent.

  Thus, through integration of ChIP-Seq signatures, coexpression datasets, and DNN modelling, we identified sb-TF expression as a likely mechanism for sex-biased expression across the genome and highlight four primary TFs driving these sex differences. Of these four, ZFX has higher expression in females due to females having two copies, while the sex-biased expression of the other three TFs can be traced back to X-chromosome copy number effects independent of ZFX.


### Modeling and testing the effect of a TF on sex-biased expression throughout the genome
  We tested how well sb-Genes could be explained by the effects of sb-TFs through linear modeling and through examining experimental perturbation of sb-TF expression. First, the effects of each of the 81 enriched sb-TFs can be modeled based on two metrics from our dataset: 1) effect size of TF sex difference (log2FC) (see Fig. 2C) and 2) the beta of the regression of TF on downstream target (Fig. 3A). Specifically, the expression level of the target gene can be modeled based on TF log2FC and the linear regression coefficient (β) of the effect of TF expression on target expression within one sex: Targetexpression=β×TFexpression×log2FCTF+α+ε where α and ε represents the regression intercept and residual respectively. The effect of sex-biased expression can then be simply modeled as a set of linear equations for all downstream targets of that TF. Comparing actual target gene expression vs. the modeled expression when the sex difference of the TF is removed reveals an estimate for how much of the difference in target gene expression between LCLs from male vs. female donors is due to the sex-biased expression of the TF.

  We tested this model on the top four sb-TFs highlighted in the DNN models. We discovered that removing the sex difference in TF expression accounts for a statistically significant decrease in the sex-biased expression in targets of FOSL1 (8.9%, p=0.01 from one-sample t-test), ZNF730 (11.5%, p=3.75×10−9), and ZNF726 (11%, p=8.69×10−8) (Fig. 3B, C). The direction is also largely concordant across the target genes for each of these three transcription factors, indicating the log2FC between sexes and the correlation between TF and target are in the same direction and suggesting these three TFs are primarily acting as transcriptional activators. Modeling results for the sb-TF ZFX revealed that ZFX sex-biased expression still accounted for a mean 6.7% of target sex differences, but the model tended to over- or under-correct expression and the mean was not statistically significant (p=0.35) (Fig. 3A, B, C). This is likely due to the influence of ZFY, the Y chromosome homolog of ZFX.

  We then tested the same models in the independent MAGE dataset. We again show that remediating the sex differential expression of FOSL1 reduces the sex-bias in target expression (Fig. S3D), with parallel results also found for ZNF730 (Fig. S3E) and ZNF726 (Fig. S3F).

  Finally, for ZFX and its Y-chromosome homolog ZFY, RNA-seq has been published, defining how gene expression is affected by CRISPRi knockdown in fibroblasts(35). We determined that targets of ZFX that display sex-biased expression in scHi-HOST are enriched in differentially expressed genes (FDR<0.1) during ZFX knockdown in both XX (fold enrichment=1.25, Fisher’s Exact Test p=0.001) and XY (fold enrichment=1.83, Fisher’s Exact Test p=0.0002) fibroblast cells. Similarly, we identified significant enrichment of sex-biased targets in differentially expressed genes (FDR<0.1) following ZFY knockdown in XY fibroblasts (fold enrichment=2.17, Fisher’s Exact Test p=0.004). We further identified a high degree of correlation between scHi-HOST differential expression directionality by sex and effects of ZFX knockdown in XX (Spearman’s ρ=0.41, p-1.1e-5) and XY (Spearman’s ρ=0.6, p-6.6e-5) cells as well as ZFY (Spearman’s ρ=0.6, p=0.02) knockdown (Fig. 3D).

  Thus, our categorization of sb-Genes appears accurate following computational and experimental testing. Modeling the effects of sb-TFs based on our scHi-HOST dataset accurately predicts the expression of downstream target genes in two independent datasets and experimental knockdown supports these findings. The largest sex differences in gene expression are secondary to differences in the number of sex chromosomes, while most sex differences are found in autosomal genes and can be traced back to sex-biased TFs, the majority of which are influenced by copy number of the sex chromosomes.


### Identification of sb-eQTL with a two-step approach
  While the large effects seen with sb-Genes on the X and Y are easily explained by their variation in copy number, and our evidence indicates that many of the smaller autosomal differences are secondary to these differences, some of these smaller differences might also be due to sb-eQTL. However, previous work has identified limited evidence of sb-eQTL through genome-wide interaction models(17, 61) or difference in genetic effect methods(19). These methods support the discovery of large-effect sb-eQTL but are hampered by low power due to high multiple-testing burden. In addition, previous studies that have identified sb-eQTL have sampled populations with primarily European ancestry, further limiting discoveries of functional genetic variation. To identify variants associated with sex-biased expression, we first utilized a genome-wide G×Sex interaction model. In concordance with previous reports(17, 18), we were only able to identify a single sb-eQTL (FDR<0.05; rs1066832-F8A3). We then tested a two-step technique utilized in GTEx, where conditionally independent eQTL were determined in a sex-combined analysis before tested for G×Sex interaction(10). This method is limited to discovering sex-biases in amplification or rare cases where effect in a single sex is sufficient to drive a sex-combined association. Out of 13,911 conditionally independent eQTL, we again identified only a single sb-eQTL (FDR<0.25; rs56412041-C17orf53). Therefore, we utilized a modified two-step pipeline in the diverse scHi-HOST cohort that reduces the multiple-test burden by first restricting analyses to conditionally independent eQTL in males or females separately before testing for a difference in genetic effect size (β) between sexes (Fig. 4A). This exploratory approach utilizes scalable linear regression analyses to prioritize variants with effects on gene expression in at least one sex to identify small sex differences in eQTL effect size, unique sex-specific eQTL, and eQTL with reverse effects in each sex. We first used an iterative linear regression approach to identify conditionally independent eQTL in each sex separately. In conditionally independent eQTL discovery, the lead eQTL effect for a gene is first detected and then regressed out, allowing subsequent regression tests to uncover additional independent association signals. This method promotes the detection of multiple distinct regulatory variants for the same gene while reducing multiple testing burden for downstream analyses.

  We identified 5,502 conditionally independent eQTL in females and 5,740 eQTL in males in 4,599 and 4,718 eGenes (target genes of eQTL) respectively (q<0.05; Table S6). Interestingly, we identified an eQTL (rs202200185) on the Y chromosome for the eGene TTTY14, demonstrating what may be the first example of genome-wide significant eQTL on the human Y chromosome(62) (Fig. S4A). We found that TTTY14 expression is variable by LCL ancestry (Kruskal-wallis p=0.04; Fig. S4B). Additionally, allele frequency of rs202200185 differs by continental ancestry, although the eQTL effect is consistent across populations (Fig. S4C). Combined, there were 10,529 unique eQTL for 6,331 eGenes, with 1,613 female-specific eGenes and 1,732 male-specific eGenes. We then tested all unique eQTL for a difference in genetic effect size (β) between males and females using the z-test method previously described(19, 40), and identified 2,390 sb-eQTL for 2,226 sb-eGenes (q<0.05), including 66 X-chromosomal genes (Table S7). The vast majority (90.2%) of discovered sb-eQTL display a significant effect in only one sex, although differences in amplification (7.4%) as well as rare instances of reverse effect between sexes (2.3%) are also evident (Fig. 4B, C). The majority of sb-eQTL are present in intronic regions, although several are also found in 3’ and 5’ UTRs and other annotated regulatory regions of the genome (Fig. S5A). By comparing the female and male β, we found that across all unique conditionally independent eQTL, there is a highly significant correlation of genetic effect (Pearson’s R=0.83, p<2.2×10−16; Fig. S5B). This correlation is greatly reduced when only considering the variants identified as sb-eQTL (Pearson’s R=0.11, p=5.8×10−8) but improved when only considering non sb-eQTL variants (Pearson’s R=0.95, p<2.2×10−16; Fig. S5B), further supporting that identified sb-eQTL show distinct sex differences in genetic effect size (β). Similar to our result with sb-Genes, we found the sex-biased effect (β) of sb-eQTL from the full dataset correlated strongly in each separate ancestry group, although we saw improved correlation in AFR and ASN groups compared to AMR and EUR (Spearman’s ρ: 0.814 in AFR, 0.822 in ASN, 0.550 in AMR, 0.577 in EUR) (Fig. S5C). We also conduced eQTL discovery with a sex-combined analysis and identified 13,911 conditionally independent eQTL in 8,937 eGenes. Interestingly, we found that only 8.2% (195) of the 2,390 sb-eQTL from the two-step pipeline were identified in this sex-combined analysis. Thus, the sex-combined analysis identified more conditionally independent eQTL (as one might predict from the increased power of twice as many individuals) but did not identify most of the sb-eQTL. Indeed, of the 8.2% identified, a higher proportion (28% of this subset) were amplification sb-eQTL compared to when sb-eQTL were identified using the two-step sex-stratified method (7.4% amplification) and none were reverse effect, suggesting the importance of identifying conditionally independent eQTL separately in males and females to uncover effect and reverse effect sb-eQTL.

  Next, we assessed replication of the primary eQTL effect and the sex-biased interaction with an independent eQTL dataset (MAGE). We identified moderate replication at nominal p<0.05 of the primary eQTL effect of female-biased sb-eQTL (24.8%; Fig. 4D) and male-biased sb-eQTL (26.1%; Fig. 4E) with concordant effects in sex-stratified analyses. This replication of eQTL discovered in each sex supports our eQTL discovery pipeline. However, replication of sb-eQTL (based on z-test p-values) was minor (5.6% at nominal p<0.05). Consistent with this being just slightly better than chance, 61% (56/92) of the replicated sb-eQTL had concordant direction of effect. Further, we did identify significant correlation between scHi-HOST and MAGE sex differences in effect size (β) (Pearson ρ=0.087, p=0.00039; Fig. 4F). This highlights that although replication by p-value is minor, we do observe significant correlation of sex differences in genetic effect sizes (β). We additionally conducted permutation analyses to identify possible biases in our two-step method. We found that permuting the sex prior to the first step of the pipeline led to similar numbers of sex-biased eQTL (Fig. S5D), but that the concordance of effect with MAGE was only found with true sex (p<0.01; Fig. S5E). For comparison, we tested conditionally independent eQTL for sex differences using a linear genotype-by-sex interaction approach. However, this method yielded a lower number of significant sb-eQTL at q<0.05 (n=1,482), lower correlation of sb-eQTL effect in MAGE (Pearson ρ=0.065), and less replication in MAGE through permutation analyses (p=0.04). Thus, although this two-step method revealed greater numbers of significant sb-eQTL than previous reports with significant concordance of effect in an independent dataset, we find that stratified approaches are prone to noise. Therefore, we conclude that at the current power, sb-eQTL cannot be detected at greater significance than observed by chance but that their direction of effect is more likely to be consistent across datasets.

  Importantly, examination of the overlap between sb-Genes (genes with sex-biased expression; see Fig. 1) and sb-eGenes (genes regulated by sb-eQTL) demonstrated no enrichment (1.03-fold enrichment, p=0.69; Fig. 4G). For shared sb-Genes and sb-eGenes, we did observe slight correlation in effects, although this was also not significant (Pearson ρ=0.08, p=0.28; Fig. 4H) Thus, sb-eQTL do not appear to explain sex-biased expression, consistent with previous findings(10, 19).


### sb-eQTL underlie some sex-bias in human traits
  Although most sex-biased gene expression is not due to sb-eQTL, we hypothesized that the cases where it does occur might underlie some of the sex differences seen in human phenotypes and disease risk. This is most pronounced in autoimmunity where diseases such as multiple sclerosis (MS) display severe sex-bias. To identify sb-eQTL associated with human disease risk, we performed an exploratory analysis using the genetic architecture tool iCPAGdb(41). iCPAGdb integrates the NHGRI-EBI GWAS catalog(43) with large datasets of plasma(45) and urine(44) metabolites and cellular host-pathogen phenotypes(46) to determine shared genetic factors. Briefly, iCPAGdb overlaps lead SNPs from the integrated GWAS data with SNPs from a user-submitted list, accounts for linkage between signals, and calculates enrichment. We discovered that 173 phenotypes significantly overlap with our sb-eQTL (FDR<0.05, Table S8) with phenotypes such as body height and body mass index highly enriched (Fig. S6A).

  When results were restricted to disease susceptibility risk, we identified 47 significant enrichments with sex-biased autoimmune diseases (FDR < 0.05, Fig. 5A). Due to the high enrichment of sb-eQTL in MS susceptibility loci (fold-enrichment=33.0, p= 2.89×10−9) and the relevance of lymphoblastoid cells to this disease (as EBV infection of B cells has been demonstrated to play a causal role(63, 64)), we further investigated sb-eQTL effects in MS. MS is a highly female-biased disease with ~75% of cases presenting in female adults(65). In addition, previous work has found that sex differences in gene expression may contribute to the distinct sex-bias of this autoimmune disease(66, 67). We utilized previously published genome-wide summary statistics of MS GWAS from the International Multiple Sclerosis Consortium(49) (47,429 MS cases and 68,374 controls) to identify sb-eQTL associated with MS susceptibility. Through this genome-wide query of sb-eQTL, we identified 14 sb-eQTL (12 sb-eGenes) with genome-wide significant association (p<5×10−8) with MS, and an additional 4 (4 sb-eGenes) with suggestive association (p<1×10−5). We then tested all 18 associated variants for colocalization of GWAS signals with sex-stratified eQTL and identified 6 variants that show strong colocalization (PP4>0.7) in at least one sex (Fig. 5B). These eQTL results may reflect sex-biased effects on known MS-risk genes such as CD58(68), DDX39B(69), GTF2H4(70), and DEXI(71). One variant (rs1335532-CD58) shows strong colocalization with male, female, and all-individual eQTL, indicating a likely causal SNP effect regardless of sex (though the effect size might differ). Interestingly, this variant is an example of an amplification sb-eQTL that is associated with CD58 expression in all individuals (p=1.99×10−15, β=0.38) but more strongly in males (p= 3.29×10−12, β=0.48) than females (p=3.13×10−4, β=0.25).

  We identified a sb-eQTL (rs7117261) on chromosome 11 that is significantly associated with expression of the nearby gene H2AX (formerly H2AFX) in females (p=7.30×10−7, Fig. 5C, D), but not in males (p=0.25). While H2AX was not identified as a globally sex-biased gene (i.e., not a sb-Gene by our metrics), rs7117261 exhibits a female-specific eQTL effect. In fact, in sex-combined eQTL analyses, the only conditionally independent eQTL for H2AX (rs2509050) is not in linkage with rs7117261 (R2=0.0009), highlighting that this sb-eQTL is a highly female-specific effect. Importantly, the minor allele (T) of rs7117261 is associated with increased risk of MS (Fig. 5E, p=7.16×10−9, OR=1.13). To determine if this variant is driving both the MS and H2AX associations, we tested the sex-stratified eQTL and sex-combined MS GWAS for colocalization and found significant colocalization in female individuals (PP4=0.93) and not in male individuals (PP4=0.11) suggesting a sex-specific risk effect of H2AX expression in MS pathogenesis (Fig. 5F, Fig. S6B, C). With sex-combined eQTL, we identified only moderate colocalization (PP4=0.52, Fig. 5F, Fig. S6B, C), highlighting the importance of integrating sex-stratified analyses into genome-wide discoveries and suggesting that this effect is driven by the female eQTL association. To determine whether this sex-biased effect extends beyond MS, we queried a previously published phenome-wide association study (PheWAS) of UKBB sex-combined traits and identified an additional association of rs7117261 with doctor-diagnosed hayfever/allergy risk (p=1.6×10−7)(50). We then examined sex-stratified GWAS summary statistics from the same study(50) and found evidence of sex differences in association (female p=8.0×10−6, male p=0.003). We again tested for colocalization, this time in each sex separately, and identified strong colocalization in female (PP4=0.94) eQTL, but not in male (PP4= 0.01) or sex-combined (PP4=0.56) analyses, mirroring the previous MS result (Fig. 5F, Fig. S6B, D). H2AX encodes the histone variant H2A.X. This histone variant becomes phosphorylated upon DNA double-strand breaks and has previously been primarily linked with multiple cancers including breast cancer(72) and hepatocellular carcinoma(73). However, previous work identified the upregulation of phosphorylated H2A.X in peripheral mononuclear blood cells (PBMCs) from patients with relapsing-remitting MS, the most prevalent form of the disease(74).

  We further explored if transcription factor activity is a possible underlying mechanism for this risk variant. We found that in ENCODE ChIP-seq data of the female ECC-1 cell line (endometrial adenocarcinoma), there is evidence for the glucocorticoid receptor (GR) binding at rs7117261(75, 76). Previous studies have shown that there are distinct sex differences in eQTL effects on GR activity(77) and that genetic regulation of GR activity is associated with treatment efficacy and relapse risk in MS patients(78).


## Discussion
  We identified sex-biased gene expression across the genome with 9% of genes displaying significant sex-bias. The largest sex differences in gene expression are the product of differences in the number of sex chromosomes, but these account for only 3% of sb-Genes in this study. However, the vast majority (79%) of sex-biased genes are targets of at least one sex-biased transcription factor, suggesting a possible mechanism of global sex differences in gene expression. One such enriched transcription factor, ZFX, is known to escape X chromosome inactivation and suggests that sex chromosome copy number is the underlying mechanism leading to sex-biased expression of ZFX targets. In fact, sex chromosome number may also underlie the sex-biased expression of autosomal TFs and drive sex-biased expression of targets. Thus, our data demonstrate that much of the observed sex-biased expression of human genes can be traced directly or indirectly to the number of X chromosomes, although our model cellular data represents cell-autonomous sex differences and does not account for the influence of sex hormones.

  Beyond the effects of the X chromosome, the initial mechanism of sex-bias of autosomal genes may have additional contributing factors, such as differential chromatin accessibility between biological sexes(61). In addition, a small portion of sex-biased genes (10%) are not targets of sex-biased transcription factors. These examples of sex-biased expression may be due to additive effects of multiple transcription factors or a possible amplification of minor sex differences in transcription factor expression. Lastly, not included in this study was the consideration of transcription-associated RNA-binding proteins or large-scale 3D-genome structure which may explain some residual sex differences in gene expression.

  But beyond categorizing sex-biased transcriptional effects, we have modeled the effects of the most important sex-biased TFs on their target genes and experimentally examined the effects of ZFX and ZFY in a loss-of-function transcriptomic dataset. By modeling effects of sex-biased TF expression on their targets, we are able to attribute ~10% of sex-biased expression directly to single TF changes. However, this leaves the majority of sex-difference unexplained. This could be due to several factors including multiple TFs acting in concordance, effect of RNA-binding proteins, or chromatin structure effects. In combining this categorization of sex-biased effects with modeling and empirical testing, we move to the level of molecular detail necessary to understand why individual genes have sex-biased expression and how this might be manipulated to impact human health. Future studies can further refine the impacts of FOSL1, ZNF730, and ZNF726 on sex-biased gene expression. In addition, our study does not interrogate the impact of global sex-differences in expression variance, which may impact how sex-biased expression impacts phenotypes(79, 80).

  While it is well-accepted that sb-Genes are abundant throughout the genome, with many conserved across tissues(10), the existence and relevance of sb-eQTL is much more contentious. Through our analyses, we support previous evidence that traditional methods of discovering interaction eQTL are unable to identify ubiquitous evidence of sb-eQTL across the genome(10, 17, 61). By focusing on conditionally independent eQTL identified in each sex separately, we reduce the multiple-testing burden and have discovered evidence of sex differences in cis-acting genetic regulation that follow differences in amplification, effect in a single sex, and opposite effects in each sex, with similar proportions of female- and male-biased effects in each category. Although we are unable to find strong replication of sb-eQTL, consistent with previous reports(10, 19, 61) and indicative of possibly a high fraction of false positives, we observed that the directionality of observed sb-eQTL was more strongly concordant in an independent dataset than with permuted data. This suggests there are true positive sb-eQTL in the dataset, but that with 480 LCLs, we are still underpowered and additional analyses with increased power are necessary to confirm small differences in sex-biased regulation. Although this approach allows for discovery of single-variant effects on sex-biased expression, we are likely underpowered in identifying large-scale sex differences in amplification, which has previously been identified as a mechanism of genomic sex differences(81). This is likely due to small differences in effect sizes that may not be detected in our method. In addition, recent work has shown that additional eQTL may be discovered by integrating cell-to-cell variability(82), which could improve detection of sb-eQTL in scRNA-seq data in future studies.

  Through our investigation of sb-eQTL, we identified possibly the first instance of a genome-wide significant eQTL on the human Y chromosome (rs202200185-TTTY14). There are several possible explanations for we were able to identify this association. The primary reason is likely the inclusion of the Y chromosome in this analysis. Many well-powered eQTL studies to date including GTEx(37), eQTLGen(83), and MAGE(28) exclude the Y chromosome, even in studies of sex-biased expression(10, 84, 85). Another possibility is that of ancestry-specific allele frequency or TTTY14 expression differences that we captured in our cohort compared to other studies. Additionally, rs202200185 is an indel (T deletion) that may not be annotated in all genomic alignments. In fact, this variant is not genotyped in 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3(86) and is only annotated as of the recent 30X sequencing of these LCLs by the New York Genome Center(87). Our results support further inclusion of the Y chromosome in genome-wide analyses, particularly in studies of sex-biased expression.

  While sb-eQTL contribute little to the large-scale differences in sex-biased gene expression, sb-eQTL provide useful datasets for exploring sex-specific effects in complex traits and diseases and may even reveal mechanisms that explain sex differences in disease risk and severity. Indeed, in concordance with previous work, we find enrichment of sb-eQTL in anthropometric traits such as height and body mass index(19, 81, 88, 89), as well as disease risk in type II diabetes(88) and MS(89). Importantly, we identified a sb-eQTL (rs7117261) that shows colocalization with MS preferentially in one sex. These results appear to contrast with Porcu et al.(19), who previously reported that sex-biased eQTL in whole blood seldom translate into detectable sex-specific trait associations in GWAS studies. They attributed this to limited statistical power, small effect sizes, tissue heterogeneity, and a reliance on sex-combined eQTL discovery (in contrast to the sex-stratified eQTL discovery used here). Our findings are consistent with this interpretation, as we do not contend that sb-eQTL broadly explain sexual dimorphism in human traits. Rather, by leveraging sex-specific eQTL datasets, we demonstrate that a subset of sb-eQTL show stronger trait associations and improved colocalization in a single sex, particularly for traits and diseases with pronounced sex bias. In our analysis, we evaluated all sb-eQTL that overlapped with MS GWAS loci, as MS is highly sex-biased and EBV-transformed B cells are important in MS pathogenesis. Six of 18 loci showed evidence for colocalization in at least one sex (PP4 > 0.7), and we more broadly explored rs7117261, finding sex-specific association of this SNP with hay fever/allergy risk in the UK Biobank. Thus, while the association of most of these MS loci do not appear attributable to sb-eQTL, our work provides a path for finding those that are—through identification of sb-eQTL in a relevant context, finding overlapping GWAS signals (ideally from sex-stratified analysis), and testing for colocalization. Future work integrating sex-stratified GWAS with larger eQTL datasets that leverage context- or tissue-specific regulation(90), that we are unable to capture in this study, will be needed in assessing the generalizability or uniqueness of our findings for the role of sb-eQTL in disease.

  The effect of rs7117261 on MS risk appears to be mediated through H2AX. Although H2AX is not a globally sex-biased gene, our sb-eQTL approach allows us to find sex-specific disease associations. We contend that an eQTL that has an effect only in one sex does not necessarily need to cause an overall change in expression relative to the other sex in order to contribute to disease. Our interpretation is that there is 1) a cell-intrinsic factor in female cells (e.g. expression of specific TFs or epigenetic differences near the H2AX gene) that cause this eQTL to exhibit an effect only in females and 2) this genotype difference in H2AX expression alters MS disease risk.

  This study isolates the contributions of cell-intrinsic factors to sb-eQTL. Future studies can address contributions of sex hormones and age. Indeed, previous work has found that both sex chromosome complement and hormones impact sex-biased expression even early in development(91, 92). Others have found interactions between sex-biased expression and age(93, 94), suggesting that longitudinal sampling is necessary to fully elucidate sex differences in gene expression. Our results provide crucial resolution to sex-differences of cell-intrinsic factors and will support further investigations to uncover how additional factors like sex hormones and aging affect sex-biased expression and disease risk.

  This work highlights the importance of investigating genomic data in a sex-stratified manner and is a call-to-action to continue to grow our efforts to improve the resolution of the effects of sex across the genome. Further stratification of genome-wide discoveries by sex will continue to reveal novel biological mechanisms and support the inclusion of sex as a biological variable.


## MATERIALS AND METHODS

### Lymphoblastoid cell lines
  240 male and 240 female 1000 Genomes LCLs from 12 worldwide populations (Table S1) were purchased from the Coriell Institute. LCLs were maintained at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere and grown in RPMI 1640 media (Invitrogen) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 2 mM glutamine, 100 U/mL penicillin-G, and 100 mg/mL streptomycin. Equal numbers of pooled LCLs are stored in liquid nitrogen vapor phase. Studies in this manuscript using LCLs do not constitute Human Subjects Research (Duke IRB; Pro00044583). These cells and genotype data were generated by the 1000 Genomes Project and are publicly available from Coriell.


### Single-cell transcriptomics
  Equal numbers of LCLs were pooled and added to a 24-well non-tissue culture treated plate in PBS with 0.4% BSA, Mg2+, Ca2+, 100 U/mL penicillin-G, and 100 mg/mL streptomycin. Uninfected controls from our previously described pooled single-cell RNA-seq and cellular GWAS method, scHi-HOST (single-cell high-throughput human in vitro susceptibility testing)(20), were spiked after 3 h with 500 μL of the LCL growth media described above. After 24 h, pooled cells were collected, spun down, and resuspended in PBS with 0.04% BSA for single-cell cDNA library preparation. This process was repeated three times with 48 LCLs used in scHi-HOST Neo (previously scHH-EIK in(20)), 48 additional LCLs used in scHi-HOST Morpheus (previously scHH-LGC in(20)), and 384 additional LCLs used in scHi-HOST Trinity (not previously described; Table S1) for a total of LCLs from 480 donors assayed by scHi-HOST.

  Cell counts and viability were collected on a Guava EasyCyte HT system by 7-AAD staining before dilution to a solution of 1 million cells/mL with intended capture of 10,000 cells/well for scHi-HOST Neo and Morpheus and to 1.25 million cells/mL with intended capture of 20,000 cells/well for scHi-HOST Trinity. The 10x Chromium Single Cell 3’ platform version 3.1 (Pleasanton, CA) was used to generate individual barcoded cDNA libraries for each well following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, the 10x Chromium Controller separates individual cells into nanoliter-scale gel beads in emulsion (GEMS) where cell-specific barcoding and oligo-dT-primed reverse-transcription occurs. For scHi-HOST Neo, 13,675 uninfected droplets were captured in 1 Chromium well. For scHi-HOST Morpheus, 36,716 uninfected droplets were captured across 2 Chromium wells. For scHi-HOST Trinity, 85,558 uninfected droplets were captured across 4 Chromium wells.

  cDNA samples from scHi-HOST Neo were single-indexed and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq system with a target depth of 50,000 reads per barcoded droplets. Reads were sequenced with read 1 length of 150 base pairs (bp) and read 2 length of 150 bp. This resulted in a mean depth per cell of 37,815 reads. scHi-HOST Morpheus samples were dual-indexed and sequenced on one Illumina NovaSeq S4 flow cell with a target depth of 100,000 reads per barcoded droplet. Reads were sequenced with read 1 length of 28 bp and read 2 length of 150 bp resulting in a mean depth per cell of 67,415 reads. Lastly, cDNA samples from scHi-HOST Trinity were dual-indexed and sequenced on a NovaSeq S4 flow cell with a target depth of 50,000 reads per droplet. Reads were sequenced with read 1 length of 28 bp and read 2 length of 150 bp which resulted in a mean depth per cell of 69,933 reads.


### Single-cell RNA-seq alignment and LCL assignment
  As previously described(20), we processed all raw sequencing results using the 10X Genomics CellRanger 7.0.1 with default parameters unless otherwise indicated. Reads from each sample were mapped to GRCh38. For the single-indexed scHi-HOST Neo, we used the 10X Genomics Index-hopping-filter to remove index-hopped reads (https://github.com/10XGenomics/index_hopping_filter) before mapping to the human genome. To remove possible ambient RNA contamination, we used CellBender (21) v.0.3.0 with –fpr 0.01 and –epohcs 150. CellBender uses a deep generative model to learn a background noise profile to delineate cell-containing and empty droplets and provide noise-free gene count quantification.

  We then used Demuxlet (22) to assign each barcoded read from cell-containing droplets to an LCL. Demuxlet utilizes a CellRanger bam file with barcoded sample reads and a VCF containing all autosomal genotypes (obtained from the 1000 Genomes Project, NYGC 30x high-coverage release) to computationally deconvolute each droplet into its corresponding LCL identity. After assigning each droplet to its corresponding LCL, CellBender gene counts were pseudobulked by sum to produce gene counts for each LCL. To reduce any batch effects in expression between the scHi-HOST Neo, Morpheus, and Trinity datasets, we conducted ComBat-seq(23) correction and merged all LCL gene counts into the combined dataset scHi-HOST Matrix which was used for all downstream analyses.

  We additionally used Seurat v5.4.0(24) to perform single-cell analysis of the Cellbender filtered and annotated expression matrices from each screen. LCL identity from Demuxlet. Analysis on feature counts data was performed using standard log-normalization. Droplets with >10% mitochondrial reads and <3000 features were excluded from analysis as well as any cells unable to be assigned as singlets in Demuxlet. Harmony(25) was used to adjust for possible batch effects between the scHi-HOST Neo, Morpheus and Trinity datasets. We generated UMAP plots by scaling and centering features in the dataset before reduction to 15 dimensions (per Seurat:ElbowPlot). K-param nearest neighbors were calculated using Seurat:FindNeighbors with 15 dimensions. These data were projected onto 2 dimensions using Seurat:RunUMAP.


### Sex-biased differential expression
  The R package “DESeq2”(26) was then used to perform all differential expression analyses. Feature counts were normalized according to the DESeq2 default “Median of Ratios” method which accounts for differences in sequencing depth and RNA composition between samples. Genes with at least 5 normalized counts across 10% of individuals were retained for further analyses. We additionally tested for expression of key sex chromosomal genes RPS4Y1 and XIST to confirm sex-biased expression and retained all samples for downstream analyses. Differentially expressed genes by sex were then discovered in DESeq2 with LCL population as an additional covariate to account for possible population effects. We also investigated possible ancestry-specific sex differences by calculating sex-biased expression as described above in each continental ancestry group. KEGG pathway enrichment was conducted using the R package “clusterProfiler”(27).

  A parallel analysis was conducted to discover sb-Genes in the MAGE dataset(28). Raw counts were normalized as described above with LCL population as a covariate. Sb-Genes in GTEx were previously discovered using a linear model with covariates accounting for known sample and donor characteristics, as well as surrogate variables that capture hidden factors of expression variability(10).

  Permutation analysis was conducted by drawing equal number of genes from the indicated set, matching for similar expression of normalized counts of genes divided into 10 bins. Either 1000 or 10,000 permutations were conducted as indicated in the figure legends. Distributions of results were plotted in R and compared to the real value to obtain an empirical p-value.


### Transcription factor enrichment and deep neural network modeling
  Transcription factor enrichment of sb-DEGs was accomplished in ChEA3(29) through the utilization of two co-expression datasets (ARCHS4(30) and GTEx(31)) and two ChIP-Seq datasets (ENCODE(32) and Literature(29)). Briefly, the overlap of sb-DEGs and known TF targets from these distinct datasets was compared and enrichment calculated with a Fisher’s Exact Test. Enriched TFs were then prioritized for additional analyses based on the ChEA3 Mean Rank Score.

  The deep neural network (DNN) model developed by Magnusson, et al.(33) was used to predict gene expression from TF expression. This neural network was trained on the ARCHS4 expression database (30) using two hidden layers with 250 hidden nodes each. Using this model, we predicted sex-biased expression in scHi-HOST Matrix from TF expression and female/male fold-change results from the above DESeq2 analyses. We then compared the predicted values of sb-Genes to observed quantifications using Spearman’s correlation. To determine if sb-TFs are sufficient to predict sb-Gene expression, we retained only those TFs with significant sex-biased expression (n=127) and repeated the model as outlined above. We then tested the DNN prediction of other phenotypes by using TF fold-changes and transcriptomic differences between normal and diseased states from 48 phenotypes in 27 distinct studies, as described previously(33). Briefly, mean TF expression from scHi-HOST Matrix was used in the DNN to predict differential expression in disease states. Spearman’s correlation was used to compare predicted values to observed values. Lastly, to prioritize TFs that highly impact sex-biased expression, we iteratively removed the effect of one TF at the time and evaluated the difference in model performance, as described previously(33).

  To test the impact of X chromosome copy number on enriched TF expression, we utilized bulk RNA-seq data from a previously published datasets of LCLs with natural variation in sex chromosome copy number(34, 35). These 106 LCLs were derived from individuals with one to four X chromosomes and zero to four Y chromosomes. As previously described(34, 35), linear modeling to find X-responsive genes was conducted in DESeq2 with the covariates of Chr X and Chr Y copy number as well as library preparation batch. Output “log2FC” values are equivalent to the log2FC in expression per copy of Chr X.


### Transcription factor linear modeling and enrichment in knockdown datasets
  We utilized linear models to determine the effect of removing the observed sex-bias in TF expression on target genes. We tested all sb-Genes annotated as targets of a particular TF in Chea3 for co-expression by generating a linear model of target expression as a function of TF expression. Target and TF expression matrices were generated as outlined above in the DESeq2 differential expression analyses. We then modeled how the removal of sex differential TF expression would impact expression of those targets with significant (p<0.05) co-expression. We utilized a model based on the effect size of TF sex-biased expression (log2FC) previously calculated in DESeq2 analyses and the linear regression coefficient of the effect of TF expression level on target expression level within one sex (β) while accommodating the regression intercept (α) and residual (ε): Targetexpression=β×TFexpression×log2FCTF+α+ε. To determine which targets were most impacted by sb-TF expression, we compared the modulated log2(M/F) fold changes of each target to the baseline values. For ZFX CRISPRi knockdown, we utilized DESeq2 differential expression results previously published in (35).


### Sex-biased eQTL discovery
  Sex-biased eQTL were discovered through a two-step method executed primarily in tensorQTL(36) in Python v.3.11.4. First, scHi-HOST Matrix counts in each sex separately and sex-combined were normalized through the median of ratios method in DESeq2. Genes with at least 6 unnormalized counts across 20% of individuals were retained for eQTL discovery. Gene counts were then further normalized using rank-based inverse normal transformation as previously described(37). To adjust for population structure, we incorporated the top 5 genotypic principal components (PCs) derived from the autosomal genome as covariates, guided by the elbow point identified in a scree plot of variance explained (Fig. S7A, B, C). In a method previously described to determine optimal PC selection in a diverse set of LCLs(28), we also investigated the correlation of genotypic PCs with continental ancestry and found that only the first 4 PCs correlate (Fig. S7D, E, F). In addition, the top 15 probabilistic estimation of expression residuals (PEER) factors(38) were used to account for hidden confounding variables in the expression matrix, again guided by the elbow point identified in a scree plot of expression variance explained (Fig. S7G, H, I). PEER factors were tested for collinearity with known covariates such as known genotypic PCs and sex (sex-combined analysis only). The adaptive permutation mode (--permute 1000 10000) of tensorQTL was conducted in each sex with the covariates described above in a 1 Mb window from the transcriptional start site (TSS) of each gene to account for multiple tests within each gene. Multiple test correction across the genome was then conducted using the Storey’s Q(39) method on the top eQTL for each gene to determine a significance threshold (q<0.05) for each gene. Conditionally independent cis-eQTL were identified in each sex using an iterative linear regression model implemented in tensorQTL, with a gene-level significance threshold of q<0.05. In this approach, the lead eQTL effect for a gene is first detected and then regressed out, allowing subsequent tests to uncover additional, independent association signals. This iterative procedure reduces the multiple testing burden by focusing on top associations, while retaining sensitivity to detect multiple distinct regulatory variants for the same gene.

  We then tested all unique conditionally independent cis-eQTL for a difference in β (genetic effect size (β)) between females and males with the following z-test method previously described(40):  z=βfemale-βmaleβ_sefemale+β_semale

  Resulting p-values were then corrected using the Storey’s Q method (q<0.05) to determine significant sb-eQTL.

  For comparison with the z-test method, we tested conditionally independent eQTL using a linear genotype-by-sex interaction model executed in tensorQTL using the top 5 genotypic PCs and top 15 PEER factors of all individuals as covariates. Resulting p-values were then corrected using the Storey’s Q method to determine significant sb-eQTL.

  Discovered sb-eQTL were classified into amplification, effect in a single sex, and reverse effect. The classification for amplification was if a sb-eQTL was genome-wide significant in one sex, nominal p<0.05 in the other sex, and the slopes are concordant between both sexes. Reverse effect was categorized as genome-wide significant in one sex, nominal p<0.05 in the other sex, and the slopes are discordant between both sexes. Effect in a single sex was categorized as any sb-eQTL that was genome-wide significant in one sex, with nominal p>0.05 in the other sex.

  We also investigated possible ancestry-specific sex differences in sb-eQTL by calculating nominal eQTL results in tensorQTL as described above in each continental ancestry group separately. We then stratified these nominal results by sb-eQTL discovered in the full dataset and calculated the differences in β by sex using the z-test described to identify possible ancestry-specific effects.


### Phenotype association of sex-biased eQTL
  To investigate possible phenotypic associations of sb-eQTL, we utilized the command-line execution of the interactive Cross-Phenotype Analysis of GWAS database (iCPAGdb(41)). Briefly, iCPAGdb trims an input list of SNPs (here sb-eQTL) to leading variants based on linkage disequilibrium information from 1000 Genomes European populations(42). These variants were then queried against cataloged GWAS variants from the NHGRI-EBI GWAS catalog(43), urine(44) and plasma metabolites(45), and Hi-HOST infectious disease phenotypes(46). Both direct overlap and overlap with SNPs in linkage disequilibrium (r2>0.4) with lead variants were considered. Enrichment of query SNPs within GWAS phenotypes was calculated based on observed vs. expected overlap and significance calculated with Fisher’s Exact Test. Phenotypes with an enrichment FDR<0.05 were considered to share significant genetic architecture with sb-eQTL. Phenotypes were filtered to include only single effect phenotypes and behavioral GWAS were removed due to their likelihood to be biased by misreporting(47).


### Colocalization of phenotype-associated sex-biased eQTL
  We applied Giambartolomei et al.’s colocalization analysis (COLOC), using the R package “coloc”(48) to determine if GWAS and eQTL signals were due to the same causal SNP. COLOC uses a Bayesian framework to calculate the posterior probabilities that two traits are not associated in the locus of interest (PP0), only one trait is associated in the locus (PP1 and PP2), both traits are associated at the locus but with different, independent causal variants (PP3), or both traits are associated with a single causal variant in the locus (PP4). For GWAS summary statistics, we filtered SNPs within a 100 kilobase (kb) window from the SNP of interest. For eQTL datasets, we filtered all eQTL for a candidate eGene and then filtered SNPs within a 100 kb window from the SNP of interest. We ran the COLOC “coloc.abf” function using the default prior parameters, p1=1×10−4, p2=1×10−4, and p12=1×10−5 for all analyses. We conducted colocalization of scHi-HOST Matrix eQTL nominal summary statistics from all, male, and female individuals as previously described. We utilized publicly available GWAS summary statistics for MS and hayfever/allergy susceptibility from the International Multiple Sclerosis Consortium(49) and the UK Biobank(50) respectively. PP4 between 0.700 to 0.900 was interpreted as likely to share a single causal variant, while PP4>0.900 was interpreted as sharing a single causal variant. The PP4/PP3 measured the intensity of the colocalization signal with values >5.00 indicating further support for colocalization and >3.00 suggesting likely colocalization(51, 52). LocusZoom plots were created using “locuszoomr”(53).
