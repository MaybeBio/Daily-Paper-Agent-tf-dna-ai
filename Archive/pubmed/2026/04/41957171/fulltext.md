# Predicting mild familial exudative vitreoretinopathy with autosomal dominant inheritance using deep learning


## Abstract
  Familial exudative vitreoretinopathy (FEVR) is a genetically and phenotypically heterogeneous disorder in which identifying autosomal dominant Norrin/β-catenin (AD-N/BC)–associated disease is often challenging, particularly in mild cases. We investigated whether deep learning could identify color fundus images corresponding to AD-N/BC gene mutation carriers as defined by peripheral FA and individuals with similar fundus features despite lacking confirmed genetic variants. A total of 305 images from 305 eyes of 101 FEVR families were used for training, validation, and testing. Images were labeled as affected or unaffected based on fluorescein angiography findings indicative of AD-N/BC–associated FEVR. A deep learning model was trained using images from eyes with pathogenic AD-N/BC gene variants and evaluated on a test dataset consisting of images without such variants. The main outcome is performance for identifying color fundus features corresponding to AD-N/BC gene mutation carriers. In the test dataset, 12 of 38 affected images and 107 of 111 unaffected images were correctly classified, yielding a sensitivity of 31.6% (95% CI: 0.1818–0.4792), a specificity of 96.4% (95% CI: 0.9273–0.9914), and an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.786 (95% CI: 0.703–0.864). These findings indicate that deep learning applied to ultra-widefield color fundus images may facilitate the identification of AD-N/BC mutation carriers and individuals with similar fundus features despite lacking genetic variants, thereby enabling more precise phenotypic characterization and potentially improving the accuracy of genetic counseling.


## Introduction
  Familial exudative vitreoretinopathy (FEVR) is an inherited retinal disorder characterized by deficient vascular development in the peripheral retina1. FEVR exhibits heterogeneous inheritance patterns: although autosomal dominant (AD) inheritance is most common, autosomal recessive (AR) and X-linked recessive forms have also been reported. Causative genes are diverse, with Norrin/β-catenin (N/BC) signaling-related genes (FZD4, LRP5, TSPAN12, and NDP) accounting for nearly 50% of FEVR cases2.

  The KIF11 gene, which is associated with microcephaly-chorioretinopathy syndrome and has a pathogenic mechanism distinct from the N/BC pathway, accounts for approximately 5% of FEVR cases3. Patients with KIF11 mutations tend to show peripheral retinal phenotypes that differ from those observed in N/BC-related FEVR4. In addition, several other causative genes have been identified in recent years; however, these are rare, and their clinical and genetic characteristics, including penetrance, remain poorly understood5.

  Compared with typical FEVR presentations such as retinal detachment, mild FEVR phenotypes are highly heterogeneous and complex. Heterozygous carriers of AR-FEVR may exhibit subtle retinal changes that appear intermediate between classic FEVR findings and normal retinal features (Fig. 1)6. Moreover, retinal manifestations associated with rare or unidentified genes may differ from those seen in N/BC-related FEVR. As a result, establishing a diagnosis of AD-FEVR remains challenging when retinal abnormalities are mild or confined to limited retinal regions.

  To address this diagnostic challenge, in a previous study, we demonstrated that specific peripheral retinal findings on ultra-widefield fluorescein angiography (FA) serve as biomarkers for AD-FEVR associated with N/BC gene mutations6. Based on these findings, we trained a deep learning model using ultra-widefield color fundus images centered on the posterior pole from patients with genetically confirmed AD-FEVR. The objective was to determine whether the model could identify color fundus images corresponding to AD-N/BC gene mutation carriers as defined by peripheral FA and individuals with similar fundus features despite lacking confirmed genetic variants.


## Results
  In total, 305 images from 173 individuals were included in the analysis.


### Training and validation datasets
  The training and validation datasets consisted of 156 images, including 64 affected images from 39 individuals and 92 unaffected images from 48 individuals; one individual contributed one affected and one unaffected eye. The mean age of the affected group was 27.7 ± 12.6 years (mean ± s.d.), whereas that of the unaffected group was 35.2 ± 12.3 years; the affected group was significantly younger (P < 0.001, Student’s t-test; Table 1). The proportion of male patients was 43.8% (n = 28) in the affected group and 43.5% (n = 40) in the unaffected group, with no significant difference between groups (P = 0.97, chi-square test).

### Test dataset
  The test dataset included 149 images, comprising 38 affected images from 29 individuals and 111 unaffected images from 64 individuals; six individuals contributed one affected and one unaffected eye. The mean age of the affected group was 22.1 ± 11.9 years, compared with 30.6 ± 12.7 years in the unaffected group; the affected group was significantly younger (P < 0.001, Student’s t-test; Table 1). The proportion of male patients was 42.1% (n = 16) in the affected group and 55.9% (n = 62) in the unaffected group, with no significant difference between groups (P = 0.14, chi-square test).

  Of the 38 images labeled as affected, 12 were correctly classified as affected and 26 as unaffected. Of the 111 images labeled as unaffected, 107 were correctly classified as unaffected and 4 as affected. Accordingly, the sensitivity and specificity were 31.6% (95% CI: 0.1818–0.4792) and 96.4% (95% CI: 0.9273–0.9914), respectively, with an AUC of 0.786 (95% CI: 0.703–0.864) and an accuracy of 79.9% (95% CI: 0.7383–0.8658). The precision and F1 score were 75.0% (95% CI: 0.538–0.937) and 0.444 (95% CI: 0.279–0.607), respectively, with a PR-AUC of 0.587 (95% CI: 0.433–0.735) (Fig. 2).

## Discussion
  We previously demonstrated that specific peripheral angiographic signs, such as V-shaped vascular notches and brushy vascular ends, can discriminate AD-N/BC-associated eyes from mildly ambiguous FEVR and normal eyes6. However, FEVR-specific retinal changes are not confined to the peripheral retina. Prior studies have reported central vascular abnormalities in mild FEVR, including increased peripapillary vessels, increased arterial and venous branching, incomplete vascular interdigitation temporal to the macula, and altered temporal vessel angles7–9. These features are often subtle, difficult to quantify, and may also be observed in normal individuals. Although peripheral FA provides the most reliable markers of FEVR pathology, analogous features on central color fundus images may offer greater clinical applicability. We therefore hypothesized that FEVR, particularly AD-N/BC-associated type, could be identified using central color fundus images. However, qualitative and simultaneous assessment of multiple subtle biomarkers is complex, and deep learning may provide an effective approach to evaluate their combined significance.

  In this study, we evaluated a deep learning model using central color fundus images to distinguish AD-N/BC-associated FEVR from normal eyes and other forms of mild FEVR (Fig. 1). The comparison group included heterozygous carriers of AR-FEVR and patients carrying the FZD4 p.H69Y variant, whose pathogenicity remains uncertain. Using a dataset of 156 labeled images for training and validation, the model achieved a sensitivity of 31.6% and a specificity of 96.4% in identifying AD-N/BC-equivalent FEVR images. Grad-CAM heat map analysis further demonstrated that regions of interest were predominantly located on the temporal side of the retina, suggesting that the model captured clinically relevant retinal features8. This approach may contribute to more accurate phenotypic stratification and support genetic counseling in FEVR.

  In FEVR, the genetic cause remains unknown in nearly 50% of patients, and retinal manifestations associated with unidentified genes may differ from those linked to N/BC genes. Although eyes with KIF11 mutations were excluded from this study, they exemplify such heterogeneity, as they tend to show discrepant vascular changes between the posterior and peripheral retina, with subtler peripheral findings than eyes with N/BC gene mutations4. When KIF11-positive images were separately evaluated using the same N/BC gene-based training and validation system, poor performance was observed, with 0% sensitivity and 53.8% specificity (data not shown).

  Another example is the FZD4 p.H69Y variant, which is frequently identified in patients diagnosed with FEVR, while variant-positive family members may lack typical FEVR features, suggesting multigenic inheritance or incomplete penetrance of autosomal dominant inheritance10. Such genotype-phenotype heterogeneity may have influenced the performance of the model in the test dataset. Therefore, caution is required when developing deep learning models that incorporate images from diverse genetic backgrounds into a single system, particularly when exploring novel FEVR-associated genes.

  From a future perspective, the model may be applied to assess phenotypic similarity between fundus images from patients with newly identified FEVR-associated genes and those with established N/BC-related disease. For example, images resembling KIF11-associated phenotypes may yield low similarity scores to N/BC-related patterns. Thus, the model may serve as a tool for phenotypic stratification of FEVR according to underlying genetic etiology.

  This study has several limitations. First, the number of images was small, although the model achieved high specificity. Second, classification as affected or unaffected relied on examiners’ subjective judgment based on previously reported criteria6. Although the identification of these features involves some degree of subjective judgment, it is based on predefined criteria and can be performed with high accuracy. Therefore, it is unlikely to have significantly affected the study results. Third, images containing artifacts such as light reflexes or eyelash reflections may have affected prediction accuracy. This limitation may be addressed in future studies by implementing stricter image exclusion criteria. Fourth, the genetic origins of the test images were unknown. Fifth, in the training/validation dataset, one eye of a single patient (1/40, 2.5%) was labeled as unaffected because of milder phenotypic expression. This highlights a limitation related to variable penetrance, which is an inherent feature of FEVR. A similar phenomenon was more frequently observed in the test dataset, where discrepant labeling between the two eyes occurred in 6 of 29 patients (21%). This finding suggests that patients with genetic causes other than N/BC-related genes may present with unilateral or highly asymmetric FEVR and ambiguous retinal phenotypes. Finally, we did not compare the performance of our deep learning model with other architectures.

  In conclusion, eyes with mild FEVR exhibit diverse retinal characteristics. A deep learning model applied to central color fundus images can help discriminate AD-N/BC-related phenotypes from subtle and ambiguous FEVR-like changes. These findings provide more precise characterization of FEVR and may improve the accuracy of genetic counseling.


## Patients and methods
  This retrospective observational study was conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Occupational and Environmental Health, Japan (CRD24-039). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to genetic testing.


### Participants
  Patients with FEVR and their family members who underwent genetic testing between 2015 and 2023 at the University of Occupational and Environmental Health Hospital were included.

  A total of 180 individuals from 103 families were enrolled, in which at least one member had a diagnosis of FEVR based on peripheral retinal signs on FA obtained using the Optos 200Tx system (Optos PLC; Dunfermline, Scotland, United Kingdom). Forty-one eyes with retinal detachment, corresponding to stage 3 or more severe disease according to the classification of Pendergast and Trese11, were excluded, while the fellow eyes were included (Fig. 4).

  Based on our previous study, AD-FEVR-specific peripheral retinal vascular abnormalities were defined as vascular notching and/or brushy vascular ends6. Eyes showing one or both findings were classified as affected, and all remaining eyes were classified as unaffected. Because classification was performed on an eye-based rather than an individual-based basis, one eye was classified as affected and the fellow eye as unaffected in 7 patients.


### Processes for training and testing
  For deep learning, central color images with a resolution of 3900 × 3072 pixels were used. One image per eye was included. Images of the left eye were horizontally flipped to ensure the same processing conditions as those of the right eye. All fundus images were resized to (300, 300, 3).

  For training and validation, 156 images from 156 eyes of 86 individuals (49 families) were included. The genotypes and clinical characteristics of these cases have been previously reported6,12. As exceptions, 3 FEVR patients carrying N/BC variants but lacking AD inheritance were included and classified as affected because they exhibited clinical findings compatible with AD-FEVR. These included two female family members from a family carrying an NDP variant and one proband with compound heterozygous LRP5 variants consistent with AR-FEVR12,13. In total, 40 FEVR patients carrying variants in N/BC signaling pathway genes were classified as affected. Seven heterozygous carriers of AR-FEVR were included and labeled as unaffected. Six individuals carrying the FZD4 variant NM_012193.3:c.205 C > T (p.H69Y), for which pathogenicity has not been established, were also included and labeled as unaffected12.

  To minimize data leakage related to the familial nature of FEVR, the dataset was divided at the family level into a training set of 125 images from 39 families and a validation set of 31 images from 10 families. The training set contained 51 affected and 74 unaffected images, whereas the validation set contained 13 affected and 18 unaffected images.

  Data augmentation was performed using horizontal and vertical flipping and random rotations within ± 10°. All images were normalized using a mean of [0.485, 0.456, 0.406] and a standard deviation of [0.229, 0.224, 0.225].

  An EfficientNet-B3 model pretrained on ImageNet was used as the backbone network, and the final layer was modified for binary classification (affected vs. unaffected). Model training was performed using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001, and the cross-entropy loss function was minimized over 100 epochs. To ensure reproducibility of data augmentation, the random seed was incremented by one at the end of each epoch. Model performance was evaluated on the validation set over 100 epochs, and the best-performing model (epoch 100) was selected. The classification threshold was set at 0.5.

  Model development and evaluation were implemented in Python (version 3.12.12) on a Google Colab GPU environment using a Tesla T4. PyTorch (version 2.10.0 + cu128) and torchvision (version 0.25.0 + cu128) were used for model development, and scikit-learn (version 1.6.1) was used for performance evaluation. The random seed was fixed at 123.

  For the test set, 94 individuals from 54 families without identified pathogenic variants in N/BC genes were eligible (Fig. 4). Patients with pathogenic KIF11 variants were excluded because they are known to exhibit peripheral FA findings distinct from those associated with N/BC gene variants4. As a result, 149 eyes from 87 individuals were included in the test dataset.

  Images classified as affected were defined as the positive class. Model performance was evaluated using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1-score, and the area under the AUC. A 95% confidence interval was calculated for the AUC. To enhance interpretability, gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM) was applied to visualize image regions contributing to the model’s predictions14.

  Because this was a retrospective study using all eligible images available during the study period, the sample size was determined by data availability. No additional imputation procedure was applied to the final analytic image dataset.
