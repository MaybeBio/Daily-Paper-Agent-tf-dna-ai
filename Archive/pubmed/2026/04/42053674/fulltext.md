# Integrative systems biology and drug repurposing reveal key regulatory hubs and a prognostic signature in gastric cancer


## Background
  Gastric cancer remains one of the most lethal malignancies worldwide, characterized by extensive molecular heterogeneity, late diagnosis, and limited therapeutic options. Identifying key regulatory genes, upstream transcription factors, and clinically meaningful prognostic markers is essential for improving patient management and accelerating targeted drug discovery.


## Methods
  We performed an integrative systems biology analysis using microarray data (GSE146996) comprising 50 tumor and 15 normal gastric tissues. After RMA normalization, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified and used to construct a protein–protein interaction network in STRING, followed by hub-gene prioritization via CytoHubba. Functional annotation was performed using ClueGO (KEGG) and BiNGO (GO). Upstream transcription factors were predicted with iRegulon. To assess clinical relevance, RNA-seq and survival data from the TCGA-STAD cohort were used for univariate Cox regression, Kaplan–Meier analysis, LASSO Cox modeling, and development of a multigene prognostic signature. Finally, network pharmacology, homology modeling, and AutoDock Vina–based molecular docking identified candidate therapeutic compounds targeting key hub proteins.


## Results
  A total of 16,304 DEGs were identified, and a refined gastric-specific PPI network of 1,282 genes revealed FN1, CTNNB1, ACTB, and HSP90AB1 as central hubs. Enrichment analysis emphasized pathways related to PI3K–Akt signaling, focal adhesion, proteoglycans in cancer, and immune regulation. iRegulon identified several transcription factors, including SSX3, ARP1, and RBBP9. Prognostic assessment in TCGA-STAD showed that FN1 and SRSF7 were significantly associated with overall survival. Based on these associations, a simple two-gene Cox-based prognostic model was constructed, showing modest stratification of patients into high- and low-risk groups (log-rank p = 0.047). Docking analysis identified several computationally predicted high-affinity compound–protein interactions, including SNX-5422 for HSP90AB1 (–9.5 kcal/mol), Empesertib and ICG-001 for CTNNB1 (≈ − 9.1 kcal/mol), and Pimozide for ACTB (–9.4 kcal/mol).


## Discussion
  Integrating transcriptomic profiling with network biology, transcription factor prediction, survival modeling, and molecular docking highlights a multilayer regulatory architecture underlying gastric cancer progression. The identification of survival-associated genes and druggable hubs provides biologically relevant, hypothesis-generating insights that help bridge basic systems biology with translational oncology. Although the prognostic model demonstrated moderate predictive power, its stability across time points suggests potential utility as a foundation for further refinement and validation. Likewise, the identified compounds warrant experimental exploration to confirm their therapeutic promise.


## Conclusion
  This comprehensive pipeline identified key molecular drivers, upstream regulators, survival-associated biomarkers, and potential therapeutic compounds in gastric cancer. The findings offer a robust framework for future experimental validation and contribute to the development of personalized therapeutic strategies for improving clinical outcomes in gastric cancer.


## Introduction
  Gastric cancer (GC) ranks among the most common and deadly malignancies worldwide, with high morbidity and mortality rates particularly in East Asia and developing countries [2]. Despite advances in surgical techniques and chemotherapeutic regimens, the prognosis of advanced gastric cancer remains dismal, largely due to late-stage diagnosis, metastasis, and the emergence of drug resistance [3]. This underscores the critical need for identifying reliable molecular biomarkers and novel therapeutic targets to improve early detection, risk stratification, and patient-specific treatment strategies.

  In recent years, the integration of omics technologies and bioinformatics has opened new horizons for cancer research. High-throughput gene expression profiling, particularly microarray datasets from public repositories like the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), provide valuable resources for discovering differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between cancerous and normal tissues [4, 5]. Computational analysis pipelines using robust normalization and statistical methods enable the identification of transcriptional signatures that are consistently altered in tumors, offering insights into the molecular underpinnings of gastric cancer [6–9].

  However, identifying individual DEGs is often insufficient to capture the complexity of cancer biology. Network-based systems biology approaches, such as protein–protein interaction [1] network construction, offer a holistic view of molecular interplay [10]. By analyzing the topological features of these networks, researchers can identify hub genes that occupy central regulatory positions and may act as key drivers of tumorigenesis. Algorithms such as MCC, Degree, MNC, and DMNC are commonly used to prioritize such hub nodes [11].

  To understand the biological relevance of these DEGs, functional enrichment analyses such as Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways are employed [12]. These analyses help categorize DEGs into relevant cellular processes, molecular functions, and biological pathways. In gastric cancer, deregulated pathways often involve inflammation, immune responses, apoptosis, and various cancer-associated signaling cascades [13]. Such insights provide valuable context for understanding disease progression and potential points of therapeutic intervention.

  Beyond functional annotation, deciphering the upstream transcriptional control of DEGs is equally important. Transcription factors (TFs) play pivotal roles in regulating gene expression programs that drive cancer progression [14]. Predictive tools like iRegulon integrate motif discovery and ChIP-seq data to identify potential TFs that regulate clusters of DEGs. Mapping these transcriptional regulators not only enhances our understanding of gene regulation in gastric cancer but also introduces new layers of potential therapeutic targeting [15].

  Additionally, the integration of network pharmacology and molecular docking allows for the rational identification of drug-like compounds that may bind and inhibit hub proteins [16]. By leveraging compound databases such as PubChem, 3D structural modeling of target proteins, and docking platforms like PyRx and AutoDock Vina, researchers can screen and prioritize ligands based on their predicted binding affinity. This computational approach bridges the gap between bioinformatics and pharmacology, paving the way for in silico drug discovery tailored to molecular profiles of cancer [17, 18].

  In this study, we conducted a comprehensive systems biology investigation of gastric cancer using transcriptomic data from GEO (GSE146996). We identified DEGs, constructed PPI networks to extract hub genes, performed GO and KEGG enrichment analyses, predicted upstream transcription factors, and finally conducted molecular docking to identify promising drug candidates targeting key proteins. Our integrative approach aims to unravel the complex regulatory landscape of gastric cancer and highlight novel candidates for future experimental validation and clinical application.

  Methods:


### Data collection and preprocessing
  Gene expression microarray data were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database under accession number GSE146996, which includes 65 CEL files obtained from gastric cancer and normal gastric tissue samples [19]. The raw CEL files were downloaded and extracted locally. Sample metadata indicated 50 tumor and 15 normal control tissues. All raw CEL files were imported and processed using the ‘oligo’ package in R. Robust Multi-Array Average (RMA) normalization was applied to correct background noise, normalize signal intensities, and calculate log2-transformed expression values. Chip annotation was performed using the platform-specific annotation package ‘pd.hta.2.0’. Probe-level expression data were summarized, and a gene expression matrix was generated. Probes were mapped to gene symbols using the ‘hta20transcriptcluster.db’ package, and only probes with valid gene symbol annotations were retained for downstream analysis. To ensure high-confidence results, only genes with adjusted p-values (adj.P.Val) < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed [20]. Additionally, a log2 fold-change (|logFC|) cutoff of ≥ log2(1.1) was applied, corresponding to at least a 1.1-fold up- or down-regulation threshold. In addition to applying statistical filters based on adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |logFC| ≥ log2(1.1), which initially yielded over 9,000 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), a further biological filter was applied to retain only those genes known to be expressed in gastric tissue. This step was performed to enhance the tissue-specific relevance of the analysis, ultimately narrowing the list to approximately 2,000 gastric-expressed DEGs [21, 22].


### Protein–protein interaction network construction and topological analysis
  The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were imported into the STRING database (version 11.5; https://string-db.org/) to construct a protein–protein interaction [1] network. Only interactions with a combined confidence score ≥ 0.4 were considered significant [23–25]. The resulting PPI network was then visualized and analyzed using Cytoscape software (version 3.9.1; https://cytoscape.org/). To identify the hub proteins, topological analysis of the PPI network was performed using the CytoHubba plugin (version 0.1) within Cytoscape [26]. Several centrality algorithms, including Maximal Clique Centrality (MCC), Degree, Maximum Neighborhood Component (MNC), and Density of Maximum Neighborhood Component (DMNC), were applied to rank nodes based on their importance. Proteins consistently ranked among the top positions across multiple algorithms were defined as hub regulators [27, 28].


### Enrichment profiling of differentially expressed genes
  Functional enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was conducted using Cytoscape software (version 3.9.1; https://cytoscape.org/). KEGG pathway analysis was performed with the ClueGO plugin (version 2.5.9; https://apps.cytoscape.org/apps/cluego) [26]. The official HGNC symbols of the DEGs were uploaded, and Homo sapiens (TaxID: 9606) was set as the reference organism. Enrichment was assessed using a two-sided hypergeometric test with Benjamini–Hochberg correction, and pathways with p-value < 0.05 were considered significant. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment was assessed using the BiNGO plugin (https://apps.cytoscape.org/apps/bingo) across Biological Process, Molecular Function, and Cellular Component, applying the hypergeometric test with Benjamini–Hochberg correction; terms with adjusted p-value (FDR) < 0.0001 were considered significant, and the output was visualized as GO networks in Cytoscape [29].


### Transcription factor prediction and motif enrichment analysis
  To identify upstream transcriptional regulators of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs), we employed the iRegulon plugin (version 1.3; http://iregulon.aertslab.org) in Cytoscape. The analysis was performed using Homo sapiens (HGNC symbols) as gene identifiers. The search space was defined as gene-based, and motif discovery was conducted using the 10 K motif collection (9713 position weight matrices, PWMs) and 1120 ChIP-seq tracks (ENCODE raw signals). Putative regulatory regions were scanned within 20 kb centered around transcription start sites (TSS). Both motif and track rankings were calculated using the 20 kb region around TSS (ChIP-seq derived). Recovery thresholds were set as follows: enrichment score threshold = 3.0, ROC threshold for AUC = 0.03, and rank threshold = 5000. For transcription factor (TF) prediction, the maximum false discovery rate (FDR) on motif similarity was set to 0.001, while the minimum identity between orthologous genes was set to 0.0. Results were reported as transcription factors associated with significantly enriched motifs or ChIP-seq tracks, along with their predicted target genes. The methodology follows the original description of iRegulon.


### Prognostic analysis and construction of a risk model in the TCGA-STAD cohort

#### Data acquisition and preprocessing
  RNA-sequencing expression data (FPKM), clinical information, and overall survival (OS) records of patients with stomach adenocarcinoma [30] were downloaded from the UCSC Xena Browser (TCGA-STAD cohort). A total of 448 tumor samples with expression data, 469 samples with survival information, and 511 samples with clinical annotations were obtained.

  FPKM values were log2-transformed [log2(FPKM + 1)]. Ensembl gene identifiers were converted to official gene symbols using the GENCODE v36 annotation file. When multiple Ensembl IDs mapped to the same gene symbol, their average expression value was calculated. Matched samples across expression, survival, and clinical datasets were merged using the TCGA barcode.


### Sample harmonization and construction of the matched TCGA-STAD cohort
  To ensure consistent downstream analysis, RNA-seq expression, survival, and clinical datasets from TCGA-STAD were harmonized by TCGA barcode. First, column names of the expression matrix were set to the original TCGA sample barcodes provided in the FPKM file. Survival (TCGA-STAD.survival.tsv.gz) and clinical (TCGA-STAD.clinical.tsv.gz) tables were then aligned using thesample column. Common barcodes present in all three datasets were identified, yielding a final cohort of 420 patients with complete expression, overall survival, and clinical information. The expression matrix was subset to these 420 samples to obtain the final gene-by-sample expression matrix (expr_tcga_final). Survival (surv_tcga_final) and clinical (clin_tcga_final) data were reordered to match the same sample order as the expression matrix. These three harmonized datasets were used for all subsequent prognostic analyses.


### Univariate cox regression of hub genes
  To evaluate the prognostic effect of hub genes identified in the previous network analysis, a univariate Cox proportional hazards regression was performed using OS time and OS status as survival endpoints. Hazard ratios (HR), 95% confidence intervals, and p-values were calculated. A gene was considered prognostically relevant when p < 0.05. Among all candidate hub genes, FN1 showed a statistically significant association with overall survival, and was therefore selected for further modeling.


### Kaplan–Meier survival analysis
  For genes passing univariate screening, patients were dichotomized into high- and low-expression groups based on the median expression value. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated, and the log-rank test was used to compare survival distributions. FN1 expression demonstrated a significant difference in survival between high and low groups, supporting its prognostic relevance in STAD.


### Construction of a Cox-based two-gene prognostic signature
  A multivariate Cox proportional hazards model was constructed using FN1 and SRSF7, the two hub genes that exhibited significant associations with overall survival in the univariate and Kaplan–Meier analyses. The model was fitted using the “survival” package in R (version 4.5.1), and regression coefficients (β) were used for subsequent calculation of the prognostic risk score.


### LASSO–Cox regularization for model selection
  To evaluate the prognostic contribution of the 16 hub genes identified in the PPI network, a Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) penalized Cox regression model was fitted using the glmnet package in R. The model was trained using 10-fold cross-validation with the parameter family = “cox” and alpha = 1. The optimal penalty parameter (λ_min) was selected based on the minimum partial likelihood deviance. Genes with non-zero coefficients at λ_min were retained as potential prognostic predictors. This procedure yielded a four-gene preliminary model consisting of FN1, ACTB, HNRNPA1, and HNRNPH1. To assess stability and discriminative performance, the LASSO-derived model was compared against a traditional Cox model using concordance index and time-dependent ROC curves.


### Multivariate Cox regression and derivation of the final model
  Because FN1 and SRSF7 showed consistent prognostic significance in the univariate Cox and Kaplan–Meier analyses, a multivariate Cox proportional hazards model was constructed using these two genes. The model was fitted using the survival package in R, and regression coefficients (β) were extracted for risk-score computation. Comparison of model performance revealed that the two-gene Cox model demonstrated superior concordance and interpretability compared with the LASSO-based model; thus, it was selected as the final prognostic signature.

### Survival analysis and performance evaluation
  Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated using the survfit and survminer packages to compare overall survival between the stratified risk groups, and statistical significance was assessed using the log-rank test.

  Predictive performance of the two-gene signature was quantified using Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) and time-dependent ROC curves at 1, 3, and 5 years, calculated with the timeROC package. The two-gene Cox signature achieved improved discrimination compared with the LASSO-derived model and was therefore adopted as the final prognostic classifier. the high-risk group exhibited markedly poorer survival outcomes compared to the low-risk group.


### Statistical analysis
  All statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.5.0). Survival analyses were performed using the “survival” and “survminer” packages. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.


### Network pharmacology and molecular docking
  To explore potential therapeutic interactions between hub proteins and bioactive compounds, an integrative network pharmacology and molecular docking workflow was applied. Each hub protein identified from the PPI network was queried in PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) to retrieve associated compounds and pathway-related interactions [31]. The compound set was filtered to retain only those reported in Homo sapiens. The PubChem compound IDs (CIDs) were collected, and the corresponding 3D structures in SDF format were downloaded. Ligand preparation was performed in UCSF Chimera, where hydrogens and charges were added, followed by energy minimization [32]. Protein structures were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB; https://www.rcsb.org/) whenever available [33]. For proteins lacking crystallographic structures, homology models were generated using SWISS-MODEL (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/) [34]. Structural quality of the models was validated by Ramachandran plot and ERRAT analysis (https://servicesn.mbi.ucla.edu/ERRAT/), and only proteins with acceptable quality scores were selected. In cases where the selected protein structures showed residues located in disallowed regions of the Ramachandran plot or exhibited low ERRAT quality scores, the models were refined using the GalaxyRefine web server to improve stereochemical quality and structural reliability [35]. Proteins were further prepared in Chimera through hydrogen addition, charge assignment, and energy minimization. The clashscore, of all modeled protein structures was assessed using the MolProbity server (http://molprobity.biochem.duke.edu). to ensure structural reliability. Models with acceptable scores were retained for subsequent refinement and docking analyses.

  For FN1 (UniProt ID: P02751), a crystal structure fragment of fibronectin (PDB ID: 3R8Q, resolution 2.4 Å) corresponding to FNIII domains 12–14 (residues 1812–2082, 271 amino acids) was selected as the structural template. This domain was chosen due to its known involvement in extracellular binding interactions. The selected fragment was refined using the GalaxyRefine web server to improve its structural quality. In contrast, for the other three hub proteins—CTNNB1 (P35222, 781 amino acids), ACTB (P60709, 375 amino acids), and HSP90AB1 (P08238, 724 amino acids)—the full-length amino acid sequences were modeled and used for docking analysis. This decision was based on the presence of multiple potential ligand-binding sites distributed throughout the entire protein sequences.Ligands and proteins were then converted into docking-compatible formats using Dock Prep in Chimera. Molecular docking was carried out using PyRx (AutoDock Vina) (https://pyrx.sourceforge.io/), and docking scores were calculated to evaluate binding affinities [36]. Finally, protein–ligand complexes were visualized and analyzed in PyMOL (https://pymol.org/) to illustrate binding conformations and molecular interactions [37].


## Results

### Differential expression profiling and sample classification
  A total of 16,304 transcripts were identified as differentially expressed between gastric cancer and normal tissue samples, based on the thresholds of adjusted P-value < 0.05 and |log₂ fold change| ≥ log₂ (1.1). The list of DEGs was subsequently imported into the STRING database and visualized in Cytoscape to construct the protein–protein interaction (1) network. The initial network comprised 9,949 nodes representing protein-coding genes and their predicted interactions. To enhance the tissue-specific relevance of the analysis, only genes expressed in gastric tissue were retained. After applying this biological filter, a refined gastric-specific PPI network consisting of 1,282 proteins was obtained, representing a focused gastric cancer–relevant interaction network (Fig. 1A).

  In the volcano plot, upregulated and downregulated genes are distributed symmetrically across the fold-change axis, with the majority of significant transcripts clustering around moderate effect sizes, confirming subtle but consistent differences between groups. MA plot analysis further validates this pattern, where significantly deregulated transcripts appear as dispersed clouds at the top and bottom margins, with non-significant genes forming a dense central band (Fig. 1B).

  Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed to assess variance across samples. When stable genes were used (those with minimal fold change), a moderate overlap between cancer and normal samples was observed, indicating biological variability. However, PCA based on DEGs (not shown here) yielded more pronounced separation, supporting the discriminative power of the identified gene set (Fig. 1C).

  Clustering analysis of the top 50 differentially expressed genes, ranked by adjusted P-value, revealed distinct expression patterns between the two groups. The heatmap demonstrates clear segregation between cancer and control samples, with visible gene clusters showing coordinated upregulation or downregulation. Gene symbols were used to annotate rows for interpretability. These expression signatures highlight biologically relevant molecular changes and offer candidate markers for further validation (Fig. 1D).

These results suggest an association of the DEGs with immune-related processes, inflammation, and cancer-associated signaling pathways. ### Prediction of upstream transcription factors potentially regulating DEGs
  To uncover upstream transcriptional regulators of the DEGs, iRegulon analysis was performed in Cytoscape. The analysis identified several significantly enriched transcription factors (TFs) associated with regulatory motifs and ChIP-seq tracks. Among the top-ranked TFs were SSX3 (NES = 4.124, 413 targets), ARP1 (NES = 4.004, 554 targets), and RBBP9 (NES = 3.762, 378 targets), which showed the strongest enrichment scores. Additional regulators included SRF, MYBL2, CEBPB, TCF12, and CAT, each with normalized enrichment scores (NES) above the significance threshold of 3.0. Overall, these TFs were predicted to regulate a broad set of DEGs, with the number of predicted target genes ranging from 115 (SRF) to 554 (ARP1). The results suggest that multiple TFs, particularly SSX3, ARP1, and RBBP9, may represent candidate transcriptional regulators within the studied biological context (Table 2).

### Overview of TCGA-STAD data
  A total of 449 tumor samples with RNA-seq expression data, 469 samples with survival records, and 511 samples with clinical annotations were retrieved from the TCGA-STAD cohort. The expression matrix contained 60,660 Ensembl gene identifiers and 449 TCGA samples, while the survival table provided overall survival time and status for 469 patients. Clinical metadata included demographic variables, disease type, and additional pathological attributes across 511 cases. These raw datasets were successfully imported into R and used as the starting point for downstream preprocessing and prognostic modeling.


### Construction of a matched TCGA-STAD cohort
  After harmonizing the RNA-seq, survival, and clinical datasets by TCGA sample barcode, a total of 420 patients were retained with complete expression, overall survival, and clinical information. These 420 matched cases constituted the final TCGA-STAD cohort used for prognostic evaluation of hub genes and development of the risk model.


### Univariate Cox prognostic analysis of hub genes
  Univariate Cox regression analysis was conducted for the 16 hub genes to determine their association with overall survival in the TCGA-STAD cohort. Among all candidates, only FN1 exhibited a statistically significant prognostic effect (HR = 1.63, 95% CI: 1.08–2.45, p = 0.018), indicating that higher FN1 expression was associated with shorter survival. None of the remaining hub genes showed significant associations with OS (all p > 0.05). Accordingly, FN1 was selected for subsequent survival validation using Kaplan–Meier analysis and inclusion in the LASSO-Cox modeling (Table 3).

## Discussion
  Gastric cancer remains one of the most lethal malignancies worldwide, ranking among the top five cancers in incidence and the top three in cancer-related mortality [39]. Despite considerable advances in diagnostic imaging, surgical resection, and systemic chemotherapy, the overall survival rate of patients with advanced gastric cancer remains dismal [39]. This poor prognosis primarily stems from the tumor’s molecular heterogeneity, complex signaling landscape, and late clinical presentation, which collectively hinder early detection and targeted therapeutic intervention [13]. Given this intricate biological network, the identification of reliable molecular biomarkers and therapeutic targets remains a major challenge in gastric oncology. Traditional single-gene or pathway-focused studies often fail to capture the systemic interactions underlying tumorigenesis. Therefore, integrative multi-omics approaches have emerged as a powerful strategy to uncover the interconnected molecular signatures that drive cancer progression [40].

  In this context, the present study employed a comprehensive bioinformatics and network pharmacology framework to identify candidate genes, regulatory transcription factors, and potentially druggable targets implicated in gastric cancer. By combining differential gene expression profiling with protein–protein interaction [1] mapping, transcriptional network reconstruction, and molecular docking analyses, we sought to provide a systems-level overview of molecular processes potentially involved in gastric cancer. This systems-level approach helps bridge transcriptomic discovery with in silico drug screening, allowing not only the identification of candidate biomarkers but also the prediction of candidate small molecules with potential binding affinity to proteins implicated in cancer-related pathways.

  Among the identified hub genes, FN1, CTNNB1, ACTB, and HSP90AB1 emerged as central nodes within the protein–protein interaction [1] network. These genes are not only structurally interconnected but are also involved in processes such as cytoskeletal organization, cell adhesion, and signal transduction. FN1 (Fibronectin 1 biologically intertwined through critical cellular functions) is overexpressed in gastric cancer tissues and is associated with advanced tumor stage and larger tumor size [41]. Its high expression correlates with poor prognosis and has been associated with tumor proliferation, migration, and the tumor immune microenvironment, particularly macrophage infiltration [41]. CTNNB1 encodes β-catenin and plays a critical role in gastric cancer through the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. Mutations or altered regulation of CTNNB1 lead to increased proliferation, invasion, and metastasis of gastric cancer cells. It is involved in the regulation of stem cell-like properties and epithelial-mesenchymal transition, processes crucial for cancer progression [42]. ACTB (beta-actin) is upregulated in multiple cancers, including gastric cancer, promoting cell invasion and metastasis. Its overexpression is associated with poor survival outcomes and advanced metastatic behavior, likely due to its role in cytoskeletal dynamics and cancer cell motility [43]. HSP90AB1 is a molecular chaperone that is overexpressed in gastric cancer. It stabilizes key oncogenic proteins such as LRP5, thereby being implicated in signaling pathways such as Wnt/β-catenin and AKT, which have been linked to cancer cell proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and chemoresistance. Elevated HSP90AB1 correlates with poor prognosis and aggressive disease stages [44].

  Functional enrichment analyses indicated that the differentially expressed genes were significantly enriched in cancer-related pathways, including proteoglycans in cancer, focal adhesion, PI3K–Akt signaling, platelet activation, and immune-associated pathways, reflecting the interplay between oncogenic signaling and the tumor microenvironment in gastric cancer progression [45]. Consistent with these findings, ClueGO-based KEGG enrichment analysis highlighted similar pathway patterns, which have also been reported in recent clinicopathological studies as being associated with tumor progression and treatment response in advanced gastric cancer [46, 47].

  Furthermore, iRegulon-based prediction highlighted several transcription factors (TFs) (including SSX3, ARP1, and RBBP9) as putative upstream regulators of these hub genes. Notably, SSX3 has been linked to epigenetic regulation and chromatin remodeling, suggesting that altered transcriptional regulation may be associated with the expression patterns observed in this dataset [48–50]. There is limited direct evidence linking SSX3 specifically to gastric cancer in the retrieved literature. SSX3 is a member of the synovial sarcoma X breakpoint protein family and is known as a prognostic marker in other cancers like liver cancer and brain cancer [51]. However, explicit roles or mechanisms involving SSX3 in gastric cancer were not found in the current sources. ARP1 (also known as COUP-TFII): COUP-TFII, a member of the nuclear receptor transcription factor family, is reported to suppress proliferation, migration, and invasion of gastric cancer cells [38, 52]. This suggests ARP1’s potential role as a tumor suppressor in gastric cancer, regulating cancer cell behaviors detrimental to tumor progression [52]. Retinoblastoma Binding Protein 9 (RBBP9) is a retinoblastoma binding protein implicated in the regulation of cell proliferation and differentiation [53]. Though studies on its transcription factor role are limited, RBBP9 has been found to modulate sex steroid hormone receptor activity and may influence cell cycle regulation [54]. Direct studies linking RBBP9’s transcriptional function with gastric cancer are scarce, but its involvement in proliferation control suggests a possible link. CEBPB and MYBL2, also identified among the enriched TFs. CEBPB is frequently overexpressed in gastric cancer and is involved in disrupting the balance between cell proliferation and differentiation in the stomach [55]. It suppresses differentiation markers like TFF1 and interacts with tumor suppressor RUNX1t1, which is often downregulated by promoter hypermethylation in gastric cancer [55]. CEBPB expression is also regulated and activated by CREB under IL1B signaling, which links chronic inflammation (e.g., Helicobacter pylori infection) to gastric carcinogenesis through survival mechanisms in gastric epithelial cells [56]. The OTX2/CEBPB signaling axis has been implicated in the progression of poorly differentiated gastric cancer, especially in young adults, suggesting its role in gastric cancer cell differentiation and progression [57]. CEBPB is activated in gastric cancer tissues and may contribute to metastasis and tumor microenvironment remodeling [58]. MYBL2 is upregulated in gastric cancer tissues and cell lines and has been reported to be involved in promoting gastric cancer cell proliferation while inhibiting apoptosis [59]. MYBL2 has been linked to activation of oncogenic pathways including PI3K/AKT signaling and influences apoptotic regulators such as BCL2, BAX, and cleaved caspase-3 [59]. Elevated MYBL2 expression correlates with poor patient survival, advanced tumor stage, lymph node metastasis, poor differentiation, and may serve as a prognostic biomarker [60]. It functions in synergy with other oncogenes such as CDC20 and is linked to activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, which may contribute to gastric cancer progression [61]. MYBL2 is transcriptionally regulated by E2F1 and E2F7 transcription factors, forming a regulatory network that impacts gastric cancer cell growth through the PI3K/AKT pathway [62].

  TCGA-STAD enhanced the potential translational relevance of our findings. While many bioinformatics studies stop at pathway or network characterization, our prognostic analysis demonstrated that among the identified regulatory genes, FN1 and SRSF7 exhibited significant associations with overall survival, enabling the construction of a proof-of-concept two-gene risk model [63]. This integrative approach highlights the biological importance of these genes in gastric cancer progression and underscores their potential utility in patient stratification and prognostic evaluation [64]. Although the predictive accuracy of the model was modest, its stability across multiple time points suggests that combining systems biology with survival-based modeling may provide a more realistic and clinically meaningful framework. Importantly, this model should be interpreted as a hypothesis-generating tool rather than a clinically definitive prognostic instrument. Future experimental validation and incorporation of additional omics layers may further enhance the robustness of such prognostic signatures [65].

  Molecular docking and network pharmacology analyses provided additional computational insights by identifying potential inhibitors targeting the identified hub proteins [66]. Several bioactive compounds demonstrated strong binding affinities, suggesting promising therapeutic potential.

  For instance, HSP90AB1 showed high affinity for SNX-5422 (–9.5 kcal/mol), a well-characterized HSP90 inhibitor previously investigated in clinical trials for solid tumors [67]. The favorable docking interaction is consistent with previous reports suggesting that HSP90 inhibition may destabilize oncogenic client proteins. Similarly, CTNNB1 exhibited strong binding with Empesertib (–9.1 kcal/mol) and ICG-001 (–8.9 kcal/mol), both known Wnt/β-catenin pathway inhibitors. These results highlight the potential relevance of β-catenin as a therapeutic target in gastric cancer, where Wnt pathway activation is a recurring molecular signature [68].

  The docking affinity of ACTB with Pimozide (–9.4 kcal/mol) and Lazucirnon (–9.2 kcal/mol) suggests an intriguing drug-repurposing opportunity. Although originally developed for neuropsychiatric and anti-inflammatory indications, these compounds have been reported to exhibit cytoskeletal and anti-proliferative effects in cancer cells [69, 70]. Their interactions with ACTB may be relevant to actin-associated processes involved in cell motility, an essential step in tumor invasion. Furthermore, FN1 displayed moderate affinity for Entinostat (–6.4 kcal/mol) and Celastrol (–6.9 kcal/mol), both of which possess anti-fibrotic and epigenetic modulatory properties [71]. These findings support the notion that targeting extracellular matrix–associated proteins could attenuate gastric cancer metastasis. Empesertib is an experimental drug and selective inhibitor of monopolar spindle 1 kinase (MPS1) mainly in clinical trials for breast cancer [72]. Naringin, a flavonoid abundant in citrus fruits, shows broad-spectrum anticancer activity against many cancers, including gastric, breast, prostate, lung, ovarian, colorectal, thyroid, and cervical cancers [73]. It works through mechanisms such as cell cycle arrest, inducing apoptosis (programmed cell death), inhibiting tumor cell invasion and metastasis, and enhancing chemotherapy sensitivity. Naringin regulates important signaling pathways like PI3K/Akt/mTOR, NF-κB, and Wnt/β-catenin, and influences the expression of proteins involved in cell proliferation and apoptosis (e.g., cyclin D1, Bcl-2, Bax, caspases). It also helps reverse drug resistance in tumor cells, making chemotherapy more effective. Additionally, naringin has anti-inflammatory properties and can reduce chemotherapy side effects, all contributing to its anticancer effects [73]. Sesamin, a polyphenolic lignan found in sesame seeds, exhibits anticancer effects particularly through inhibiting tumor growth and metastasis. It has been reported to suppress angiogenesis (formation of new blood vessels that feed tumors) under hypoxic conditions in cancers such as colorectal cancer, which helps limit tumor growth and spread. Sesamin also shows activity against other cancer types but is less extensively documented compared to naringin [74].

  Recent pan-cancer studies have demonstrated that the prognostic value of genes is often shaped by network-level interactions and the tumor immune context, and that interpreting survival-associated genes in isolation may be limiting. These studies emphasize that prognostic gene signatures frequently align with patterns of immune cell infiltration and immune subtypes, achieving greater interpretive power when analyzed within systems-level and network-based frameworks. Accordingly, the findings of the present study should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, adopting a systems-oriented approach that integrates network topology and survival modeling to identify potential prognostic biomarkers, while warranting further experimental validation [75, 76].

  Collectively, these observations indicate that gastric cancer progression is orchestrated by a multifaceted molecular network linking cytoskeletal reorganization, Wnt signaling activation, and proteostasis regulation. The co-upregulation of FN1, CTNNB1, ACTB, and HSP90AB1 suggests a cooperative role in maintaining tumor cell plasticity and adaptive stress response. The identification of upstream TFs such as SSX3 and CEBPB further emphasizes transcriptional plasticity as a potential contributor to tumor evolution. Importantly, the predicted small-molecule ligands not only align with known pathway inhibitors but also introduce previously unreported candidates with strong docking potential, providing a valuable starting point for experimental validation.


### Study limitations and future perspectives
  While this study provides systems-level insights into the molecular landscape of gastric cancer, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the analyses were primarily based on computational and in silico approaches, and the identified hub genes, transcriptional regulators, prognostic model, and predicted compound–protein interactions require experimental validation using in vitro and in vivo models. Second, the prognostic risk model demonstrated modest predictive performance, and therefore should be interpreted as exploratory rather than clinically definitive. Third, the use of a single GEO transcriptomic dataset may not fully capture the molecular heterogeneity of gastric cancer across different populations and disease subtypes. Future studies should integrate additional independent cohorts and multi-omics data, including proteomics and epigenomics, to enhance robustness. Moreover, functional assays, patient-derived organoids, xenograft models, and molecular dynamics simulations will be essential to validate biological relevance, binding stability, and therapeutic potential of the predicted compounds.


## Conclusion
  In conclusion, this integrative transcriptomic and network pharmacology analysis identified FN1, CTNNB1, ACTB, and HSP90AB1 as central molecular regulators in gastric cancer and proposed several bioactive compounds, including SNX-5422, Empesertib, and Pimozide, as promising therapeutic candidates. These findings provide a theoretical foundation for experimental exploration of drug repurposing strategies targeting key oncogenic pathways in gastric cancer. The study not only enhances our molecular understanding of gastric tumorigenesis but also provides a computational framework that may inform future translational investigations.
