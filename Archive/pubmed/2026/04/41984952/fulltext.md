# Cross-species prediction reveals chromatin regions with increased accessibility in humans


## Abstract
  Human-specific genetic variations have shaped unique traits by influencing gene expression. Open chromatin regions (OCRs) are critical regulatory elements associated with gene expression, so investigating the changes in chromatin accessibility is essential for understanding human evolution. Because of the limited availability of epigenetic data from great apes, we developed a convolutional neural network–based approach for cross-species prediction to identify regions of increased chromatin accessibility in humans, which we term “human predicted increased chromatin accessibility regions” (hPICAs). Leveraging the limited available assay for transposase-accessible chromatin sequencing (ATAC-seq) data, we demonstrated that models trained exclusively on human chromatin accessibility data can achieve accurate predictions in other primates. We constructed prediction models based on chromatin accessibility data from 111 human cell types and developed a framework to systematically identify hPICAs. We showed that variants within hPICAs are more likely to affect chromatin accessibility by altering transcription factor binding sites. Last, hPICAs are enriched in regions associated with human-specific traits, offering a previously unexplored perspective for investigating human evolution.


## INTRODUCTION
  Humans have undergone notable changes compared to their closest evolutionary relatives, such as brain size (1), skeletal morphology (2), and body fat (3). Along with these unique traits, humans also exhibit specific susceptibility to diseases, including schizophrenia (4), knee osteoarthritis (5), and obesity (3). Initial genetic studies on human-specific traits primarily focused on protein-coding regions to identify genetic variations influencing phenotypes (6). For example, gene duplications are important drivers of human evolution, with recent studies identifying several gene duplications associated with human cortical development (7–9). However, the coding region sequences between humans and chimpanzees exhibit only a few differences (10, 11). Genome-wide association studies (GWASs) have identified hundreds of thousands of genetic variants associated with complex human diseases and gene expression, most of which are located in noncoding regions (12). These regulatory elements in noncoding regions can exert their functions by modulating the expression of coding genes (13), thus studying genetic variations in noncoding regions can help us better understand human evolutionary history.

  The increasing number of assembled whole genomes from primates and other species has enabled comparative genomic studies of noncoding regions (14, 15). Now, such research primarily relies on sequence conservation and nucleotide evolutionary rates, using methods like PhastCons (16) and PhyloP (17). By identifying conserved genomic regions in humans, studies have revealed that these regions are predominantly located in noncoding sequences and harbor numerous functionally important sites contributing to human diseases and traits (18, 19). Beyond conserved regions, researchers have identified other functional genomic elements, including human accelerated regions (HARs) (20, 21), human-conserved deletions (hCONDELs) (22), and copy number variants (CNVs) (23–26). HARs and hCONDELs seem to function as cis-regulatory elements, whereas CNVs may also influence the transcript level of the duplicated gene. A notable example is HACNS1, a HAR harboring multiple human-specific substitutions that likely alter its transcription factor (TF) binding affinity, enabling its specific activation of GBX2 in humans (27, 28). Recently, a comparative analysis of human and human-chimpanzee ancestral genomes identified 1581 human ancestor quickly evolved regions (HAQERs). In vivo single-cell sequencing demonstrated that sequence variations in HAQERs forged hominin-unique enhancers in the developing cerebral cortex (29). These findings highlight how mutations contribute to gene expression regulation in specific cell types or developmental stages, forming a molecular basis for species evolution.

  Although sequence conservation and evolutionary rates are useful tools for predicting whether noncoding regions are functional, many regulatory elements are not conserved, and sequence alignments alone cannot determine in which cell types or developmental stages these regulatory elements are active. With the large-scale application of functional genomics sequencing technologies such as assay for transposase-accessible chromatin sequencing (ATAC-seq) (30), chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) (31), and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) (32, 33), HARs located within transcriptional regulatory elements have been identified, including HaDHS (34) and ace-DHS (35). An alternative approach involves directly comparing functional genomics data (including chromatin accessibility, gene expression levels, etc.) across species to pinpoint conserved and human-specific regulatory elements (36–38). However, because of the limited availability of tissue samples from most nonhuman primates, current studies are restricted to a few tissues, such as the brain.

  Over the past decade, deep learning models have been widely applied in genomics (39, 40). These models autonomously extract features from DNA sequences to predict TF binding (41–43), histone modifications (44, 45), chromatin accessibility (46, 47), and gene expression (48, 49) with high accuracy. Furthermore, deep learning enables rapid and efficient prediction of the effects of sequence variants on thousands of epigenetic features, thereby facilitating the assessment of their phenotypic impacts (50). Notably, recent studies demonstrate that deep learning can achieve cross-species prediction of epigenetic features, overcoming current limitations imposed by the scarcity of epigenomic data in nonmodel organisms (51–53). This capability establishes an effective framework for investigating human evolution (54).

  In this study, we demonstrate that a convolutional neural network (CNN) trained exclusively on human DNA sequences enables accurate cross-species prediction of chromatin accessibility among primates and that prediction differences across species are statistically associated with experimentally measured differences in chromatin accessibility. Building on this foundation, we designed a framework to identify human open chromatin regions (OCRs) with increased accessibility, which we termed “human predicted increased chromatin accessibility regions” (hPICAs), across 111 cell types. We found that hPICAs are enriched in promoters and enhancers versus other OCRs and are subject to positive selection. Compared to traditionally defined HARs, genetic variants in hPICAs are more likely to affect TF binding sites and consequently alter chromatin accessibility. Furthermore, we found that hPICAs are enriched in regions associated with human-specific traits. Our study illustrates that deep learning-based cross-species prediction provides a powerful approach to uncover functional noncoding variants, providing a distinct framework to study the evolution of human-specific phenotypes.


## RESULTS

### CNN achieved robust cross-species prediction in primates
  To demonstrate the efficacy of CNN for cross-primate prediction, we collected chromatin accessibility datasets (bulk ATAC-seq) from three primate species—humans, chimpanzees, and macaques (see Materials and Methods). The datasets included lymphoblastoid cell lines (Lym) (55), prefrontal cortex neurons (NeuN+), and glial cells (NeuN−) (fig. S1, A and B, and data S1) (56).

  We trained CNNs separately for each species (see fig. S2 for the model architecture) and evaluated their performance in terms of area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) using entire chromosome test sets with extremely class-imbalanced tasks. Subsequently, we used the human-trained model to perform cross-species prediction on chimpanzee and macaque test sets, repeating the procedure above four times to ensure robustness (see Materials and Methods). We found that on the test sets for their respective species, the CNNs predominantly achieved AUROC values above 0.9. The mean AUPRC values for their respective species were 0.478 (Lym), 0.232 (NeuN+), and 0.412 (NeuN−), substantially exceeding the corresponding AUPRC baselines of 0.008 (Lym), 0.004 (NeuN+), and 0.008 (NeuN−) (Fig. 1, A and B, and fig. S3A). Across all test sets, the cross-species performance of human-trained CNNs was comparable to that of independent-species CNNs, with almost no reduction in either AUROC or AUPRC (Fig. 1, A and B; fig. S3A; and data S2). This result suggests that a CNN trained on human data alone can effectively predict chromatin accessibility in the same tissues of other primates.

  To further understand the reason behind the CNN’s robust cross-species predictive performance, we used the interpretability method, called integrated gradients, to compute the contribution of each nucleotide in the input sequence. Using a sliding window approach, we identified the regions with the highest predictive effects for each input sequence, termed salient regions (SRs). We then determined the TF motifs within SRs (see Materials and Methods). In lymphoblastoid cell lines, the shared TF motifs between humans and chimpanzees were 289 (hypergeometric test, P = 8.39 × 10−206), whereas the shared TF motifs between humans and macaques were 324 (hypergeometric test, P = 2.32 × 10−258; Fig. 1C). Similarly, in the prefrontal cortex, CNN identified highly consistent TF motifs between humans and chimpanzees or macaques (Fig. 1D and fig. S3B).

  Furthermore, we ranked the TF motifs identified in each species based on their enrichment P values and computed the correlation between humans and chimpanzees or macaques. In lymphoblastoid cell lines, the correlation coefficient between the motifs shared by humans and chimpanzees was 0.961 (Spearman P = 5.52 × 10−162), and the correlation between humans and macaques was 0.868 (Spearman P = 2.59 × 10−100; Fig. 1E). In the other two datasets, the correlation coefficients were all greater than 0.8 (Fig. 1F and fig. S3C). Overall, the interpretability analysis demonstrates that the CNN’s robust cross-species prediction performance is due to the CNN learned highly similar TF motifs across different species.


### Association between predicted difference and accessibility difference for orthologous sequences
  To assess the association between prediction differences and experimentally measured differences for OCRs, we first identified the orthologous sequences of human OCRs in chimpanzee and macaque genomes. These orthologous sequences were classified into two categories based on sequencing read counts: human-specific peaks and common peaks (fig. S1, C and D; see Materials and Methods). Using the pretrained models, which were trained exclusively on human data, we predicted chromatin accessibility differences between the orthologous sequences, denoted as ΔPr [ΔPr = Pr (human) − Pr (ape)]. In lymphoblastoid, the mean value of ΔPr for human-specific peaks was 0.03 (H-C, human-chimpanzee) and 0.11 (H-M, human-macaque), whereas the mean value of ΔPr for common peaks was 0.004 (H-C) and 0.01 (H-M). The mean value of ΔPr in human-specific peaks was significantly higher than that in common peaks (Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, H-C P = 6.43 × 10−13; H-M P = 6.83 × 10−98), a finding that was also observed in the prefrontal cortex dataset (Fig. 2A and fig. S4A). These results indicate that, although CNN was trained as a binary classifier, the predicted differences are statistically associated with differences in their chromatin accessibility.

  To further validate this relationship, we identified human increased accessibility regions within orthologous sequences by comparing their accessibility levels between species. We then predicted the regions under different ΔPr thresholds (see Materials and Methods; ΔPr = −0.3, −0.2, −0.1, 0, 0.1, 0.2, and 0.3). As the ΔPr threshold increased, the predicted regions exhibited greater enrichment for the experimentally identified regions with increased accessibility in humans (Fig. 2, B and C, and fig. S4B). Collectively, these findings indicate that even though our model was trained exclusively on human OCRs, selecting an appropriate ΔPr threshold enables us to enrich human OCRs with increased accessibility relative to other primates.

  We also tried to evaluate the relationship between the predicted values from the regression model (see Materials and Methods) and the strength of chromatin accessibility. Using the human data from the lymphoblastoid dataset as an example, the regression model achieved a higher correlation between predicted values and chromatin accessibility compared to the classification model (fig. S5, A and B). However, unlike the classification model, as the ΔPr threshold increased, regions with higher prediction differences in the regression model did not show significant enrichment for the experimentally identified regions with increased accessibility in humans (fig. S5C). Therefore, we maintained that the prediction difference from the classification model serves as a more suitable proxy for identifying regions with increased chromatin accessibility in humans.


### Cross-species prediction accuracy persists in single-cell ATAC-seq data
  To evaluate whether the CNN model trained on single-cell ATAC-seq data also exhibits cross-species predictive capability, we collected chromatin accessibility data from six different cell types in the Brodmann area of the brain in humans, chimpanzees, and macaques (data S1) (37). For each species, we aggregated the single-cell ATAC-seq data into pseudo-bulk data and trained the model using the same architecture as that used for the previously described bulk ATAC-seq data. The results showed that CNNs exhibit comparable performance between within-species and cross-species predictions on entire chromosome test sets, as measured by AUROC and AUPRC (Fig. 3A and data S2). This indicates that single-cell ATAC-seq data still enable highly effective cross-species prediction.

  We further evaluated the relationship between cross-species prediction differences and chromatin accessibility differences in the single-cell data. Using different thresholds (ΔPr = 0, 0.1, and 0.2), we observed that the predicted regions were significantly enriched for the experimentally identified regions with increased accessibility in humans, both in the human-chimpanzee and human-macaque comparisons (Fig. 3B and fig. S6, A and B). In summary, our analyses demonstrate that CNNs trained exclusively on human OCRs exhibit robust predictive capability across primates, even when applied to single-cell ATAC-seq datasets.


### Identification of hPICAs based on human single-cell ATAC-seq atlas
  Having established the robust cross-species predictive capability of CNNs in primates, we used CNNs to identify hPICAs relative to the human-chimpanzee ancestor (HCA). Briefly, we independently trained CNN models to predict chromatin accessibility for each cell type and computed prediction differences between human and the ancestor [ΔPr = Pr (human) − Pr (HCA)]. To assess statistical significance, we simulated the neutral evolution of human OCRs using a sequence evolution model and generated a null distribution of ΔPr through 1000 simulations (Fig. 4A; see Materials and Methods).

  Using single-cell ATAC-seq data from 111 adult human cell types (57), we constructed an individual CNN per cell type, achieving mean AUROC and AUPRC values of 0.942 and 0.577 on their corresponding entire chromosome test sets, respectively (Fig. 4B and data S3). For each OCR, we inferred the sequence of the HCA (see Materials and Methods) and calculated ΔPr between the human and ancestral sequences. The overall distribution of ΔPr is shown in Fig. 4C, with the 99th percentile at 0.26. We further evaluated the significance of ΔPr via simulations and set the hPICA threshold at ΔPr > 0.3 with adjusted P < 0.05.

  Across the 111 cell types, we identified 23,414 hPICAs, with gastrointestinal stromal cells exhibiting the highest proportion (1.12%; fig. S7 and data S4). After collapsing across cell types, these represented 13,027 unique genomic regions. Notably, the vast majority (72%) of hPICAs were identified in only one cell type, and compared with other OCRs, hPICAs exhibited significantly higher cell type specificity (Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, P = 4.29 × 10−4; Fig. 4D). Furthermore, we found that hPICAs were most enriched in promoters (enrichment score = 2.1, P = 7.71 × 10−100), proximal enhancers (enrichment score = 1.85, P = 3.96 × 10−145), and distal enhancers (enrichment score = 1.181, P = 5.56 × 10−64; Fig. 4E; see Materials and Methods), indicating that hPICAs are more strongly associated with gene expression regulation than general OCRs.

  To determine whether hPICAs have been subject to positive selection, we examined signatures of selective sweep using single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) data from the 1000 Genomes Project. Analogous to the McDonald-Kreitman test, we calculated the ratio of substitutions to polymorphisms in hPICAs versus non-hPICAs (see Materials and Methods). The ratio in hPICAs was 1.32, which was significantly higher than the 1.12 observed in non-hPICAs (Mann-Whitney U test, P = 2.15 × 10−102; Fig. 4F). This suggests a higher proportion of adaptive substitutions in hPICAs, implying that positive selection may have contributed to the formation of hPICAs.


### Human-specific variants predominantly alter potential TF binding sites in hPICAs
  To investigate whether human-specific variants in hPICAs influence chromatin accessibility by altering TF binding, we first identified cell type–specific TFs using WhichTF (58) (data S5) and predicted their potential binding sites in OCRs via FIMO (59) (see Materials and Methods). Sequence alignment categorized human-specific variants relative to ancestral sequences into single-nucleotide variants (SNVs), insertions, and deletions, which were then overlapped with potential TF binding sites. Compared to non-hPICAs, hPICAs exhibited significant enrichment of SNVs and insertions within potential TF binding sites (Mann-Whitney U test, PSNV = 9.68 × 10−6, PInsertion = 5.50 × 10−22), whereas deletions showed no enrichment (Mann-Whitney U test, PDeletion = 0.21; Fig. 5A). These results suggest that SNVs and insertions might enhance accessibility via affecting TF binding, whereas deletions may act through alternative mechanisms.

  HARs are genomic regions that have evolved rapidly in the human lineage and are often implicated in brain development and cognitive function. We identified HARs (20) overlapping OCRs across 111 cell types and observed minimal overlap between HARs and hPICAs (fig. S8A). Consistent with their definitions, variants within HARs showed higher evolutionary conservation (Mann-Whitney U test, PPhyloP = 1.33 × 10−63, PPhastCons = 4.68 × 10−78; fig. S8B), whereas variants in hPICAs exhibited significantly larger predicted effects (Mann-Whitney U test, P = 1.72 × 10−51; fig. S8C). Notably, the proportion of hPICA variants overlapping potential TF binding sites was significantly higher than that of HARs (Fisher’s exact test, PSNV = 1.75 × 10−69, PInsertion = 1.04 × 10−5, PDeletion = 1.20 × 10−6; Fig. 5B). These results suggest that, compared to HARs—which rely primarily on sequence conservation and lack specific functional annotations—hPICAs may harbor more potentially functional variants that affect transcriptional regulation.

  Given that a single hPICA often contains multiple variants, we quantified the individual effect of each variant by in silico mutagenesis (data S6; see Materials and Methods). The distribution of variant effects is shown in fig. S9A; the 99th percentile of all variant effects was 0.14. We defined variants with effects >0.3 as prioritized variants and found that 63.4% of hPICAs contained at least one such variant. Compared to nonprioritized variants within hPICAs, prioritized variants were significantly enriched for insertions, deletions, and C>G substitutions (fig. S9B). As expected, prioritized SNVs and insertions showed a significantly greater overlap with potential TF binding sites than nonprioritized ones (Mann-Whitney U test; PSNV = 2.61 × 10−25, PInsertion = 2.60 × 10−24), whereas deletions showed no significant difference (Mann-Whitney U test, PDeletion = 0.19; Fig. 5C). These findings demonstrate that cross-species prediction using CNNs can be leveraged not only at the regional level but also at the single-variant level to prioritize functionally important variants. We further evaluated the relationship between conservation and predicted functional effect for each individual variant. Only weak correlations were observed (hPICAs r = 0.066; HARs overlapping OCRs r = −0.058; fig. S10), underscoring the advantage of cross-species prediction in identifying functional variants that may be missed by conservation-based approaches.

  Last, we identified the classes of TFs most frequently affected by prioritized variants (Fig. 5D). These TFs were enriched for functions related to cell proliferation and differentiation (FOS, FOSL1, FOSL2, JUND, and JUNB), immune regulation (BACH1, BACH2, SPIC, and BATF), embryonic development (FOXF2 and TEAD3), lipid metabolism (CEBPA), and intestinal development (CDX2). Together, our CNN-based predictions at both regional and single-variant levels suggest that changes in chromatin accessibility during human evolution may be mediated by genetic variants that alter TF binding.


### Functional and phenotypic associations of hPICAs in human evolution
  To investigate the potential relationship between hPICAs and the evolution of human phenotypes, we applied GREAT (Genomic Regions Enrichment of Annotations Tool) (60) and integrated GWAS Catalog datasets (61) to identify genes and phenotypes associated with hPICAs (Fig. 6A, fig. S11, and data S7; see Materials and Methods). Notably, hPICAs were significantly enriched for phenotypes known to have undergone marked evolutionary changes in humans, such as alterations in bone mass and increased body fat (2, 3, 62). These findings further support the notion that hPICAs may have contributed to the evolutionary transition from ancestral hominins to modern humans.

  To gain deeper insight into how hPICA-associated variants may influence gene regulation and contribute to human-specific traits, we present two illustrative cases (Fig. 6, B and C). In the first case, a human-specific SNV (C>A) located within an adrenal cortex OCR (chr3:119795056–119795456) is predicted by our model to increase chromatin accessibility by 0.63. This SNV creates a binding site for FOSL2, and integration with Hi-C data reveals that this OCR physically contacts the promoter of GSK3B, a gene involved in glucose regulation and previously implicated with skeletal strength (63, 64). In a second case, a human-specific SNV (G>A) with a predicted effect of 0.93 occurs in an OCR active in esophageal epithelial cells (chr7:128115357–128115757). This SNV induces the formation of a BACH1 binding site and is predicted to influence the downstream LEP gene, which encodes leptin—a key hormone in fat metabolism and a well-known marker of adiposity (65, 66).

  Collectively, these cases demonstrate that deep learning models can effectively decipher latent regulatory codes within the genome. Cross-species prediction using CNNs offers a promising strategy for elucidating how genetic variants contribute to the evolution of complex phenotypes.


## DISCUSSION
  Identifying changes in chromatin accessibility during species divergence is critical for understanding the emergence of human-specific traits. Although advances in sequencing technologies have enabled the identification of differentially accessible regions and differentially expressed genes between humans and other species, large-scale identification remains challenging due to the limited availability of epigenomic data from nonhuman samples. To overcome this limitation, we developed a cross-species prediction framework by training a CNN model solely on human OCRs and subsequently applied it to predict chromatin accessibility in other primates. We evaluated the cross-species prediction robustness of CNNs in primates using both bulk and single-cell ATAC-seq datasets. On the basis of an ATAC-seq atlas across 111 human cell types, we systematically identified 23,414 regions with increased chromatin accessibility relative to the human-chimpanzee common ancestor. Further analysis suggested that human-specific variants may alter regulatory activity by affecting TF binding sites. These findings provide a distinct research approach to explore interspecies changes in epigenetic features.

  Recent studies have demonstrated that cis-regulatory elements exhibit rapid turnover across species, as revealed by genome-wide profiling of TF binding and histone modifications associated with enhancer activity (67–69). Nevertheless, gene expression patterns and TF binding preferences remain highly conserved among mammals (70, 71), which facilitates the application of deep learning for cross-species analysis. One study used CNNs and support vector machines (SVMs) to perform cross-species enhancer prediction across six mammalian species, and the results showed that both CNNs and SVMs exhibit cross-species predictive capabilities, with CNNs achieving higher accuracy than SVMs (51). In addition, another study found that incorporating epigenomic data from other model organisms (e.g., mouse) through multispecies joint training frameworks can improve predictive performance, particularly for gene expression (52). Consistent with previous studies, we showed that CNNs can maintain robust cross-species predictive accuracy among primates based on multiple bulk and single-cell ATAC-seq datasets. To rationalize the CNN’s cross-species prediction performance, we found that the types and importance rankings of TF motifs learned by the model were highly consistent across species—a finding previously noted in an SVM-based cross-species prediction study (52). Furthermore, we demonstrated that CNN-predicted differences between orthologous sequences were significantly associated with experimentally measured differences in chromatin accessibility across all datasets analyzed.

  Although prior studies have confirmed that epigenetic features of many cell types or tissues can be predicted across species, functional studies based on these predictions remain limited. Recently, the Zoonomia Consortium used CNNs to establish an open chromatin–phenotype association framework, termed the Tissue-Aware Conservation Inference Toolkit (TACIT). Using the TACIT, the team identified dozens of OCRs significantly associated with brain size (54) and vocal learning (72). In addition, another study developed a method to detect positive selection in regulatory elements through gapped k-mer SVMs (gkm-SVMs), identifying TF binding sites (73) and enhancers (74) under positive selection. However, these studies have been limited to a few tissues. In this study, we leveraged cross-species predictions to identify regions with increased chromatin accessibility across a broad range of cell types. We developed a framework to detect hPICAs by tracing human-chimpanzee ancestral sequences and identified a total of 23,414 hPICAs across 111 human cell types. Compared to other OCRs, we found that hPICAs exhibit greater cell type specificity and are enriched at promoters and enhancers that are more closely related to gene expression regulation. Notably, SNVs and insertions in hPICAs relative to the ancestral sequence were enriched at potential TF binding sites, suggesting that these variants might directly affect TF binding to alter chromatin accessibility. However, deletions did not show enrichment at potential TF binding sites, possibly because deletions may influence chromatin accessibility by altering the spacing of TF binding sites (75, 76).

  Now, most studies on comparative genomics of noncoding regions are based on sequence conservation. One of the most representative regions in humans is HARs, which have been shown to play an important role in neurodevelopment (77, 78). However, the hPICAs identified in our study overlapped minimally with known HARs. We found that, although variants in HARs tend to occur at evolutionarily conserved sites, those in hPICAs are more likely to occur at potential TF binding sites. By predicting the effect of individual variants, we also identified prioritized variants in hPICAs and found that these prioritized variants were further enriched at potential TF binding sites. These results suggest that, compared to traditional sequence conservation comparisons, our method is more advantageous in capturing key variants that affect transcriptional regulation.

  The intrinsic driving force behind human phenotypic adaptive changes is molecular functional alterations. By performing functional enrichment analysis of genes and linked SNPs associated with hPICAs, we found that hPICAs are related to human adaptive traits such as bone mass and increased body fat. We also illustrated that by integrating other multiomics data, we could capture putative causal chains from the identified genetic variants to regulatory alterations and ultimately to phenotypic outcomes.

  This study also has limitations. First, we only evaluated the cross-species prediction performance of CNNs in primates. Whereas some studies suggest that such methods could be applied to a broader range of species (51, 52), others have found that distantly related species may exhibit species-specific sequence features (53), requiring more complex transfer learning approaches. Second, although we observed a statistical association between OCRs with large interspecies prediction differences and actual differences in chromatin accessibility, the strength of this association needs further validation with more multispecies ATAC-seq datasets. Third, our analysis focused specifically on the individual predictive effects of variants that alter potential TF binding sites to modulate chromatin accessibility, leaving more complex mechanisms unexplored, such as combinatorial arrangements of multiple TF binding sites or synergistic effects of variant clusters. Last, it should be noted that all changes in chromatin accessibility and identifications of cell type–specific TF binding sites in our results are predictions derived from our models and software inference. Although our computational analysis suggests that human-specific variants in hPICAs may influence chromatin accessibility by altering potential TF binding sites, the precise impacts of predicted hPICAs and their variants on TF binding dynamics and transcriptional regulation necessitate thorough experimental validation, such as massively parallel reporter assays, CRISPR perturbations, and allele-specific binding assays.

  Overall, in this study, we have elucidated from multiple perspectives the robust cross-species chromatin accessibility prediction performance of CNNs in primates. On the basis of this principle, we used CNNs to identify hPICAs in 111 human cell types and explored the evolutionary characteristics of hPICAs. Our work provides a distinct approach for studying the evolution of noncoding sequences and their impact on epigenetic regulations. Moreover, it offers insights into uncovering the genetic mechanisms underlying species-specific adaptive evolution.


## MATERIALS AND METHODS

### Data collection and preprocessing
  We collected chromatin accessibility sequencing data from lymphoblastoid cell lines (55), prefrontal cortex (56), and Brodmann area of the brain (37) across three primate species (humans, chimpanzees, and macaques) for evaluating cross-species predictive performance of CNNs. The lymphoblastoid and prefrontal cortex datasets were generated using bulk ATAC-seq, whereas the Brodmann datasets were acquired via single-cell ATAC-seq. We also collected single-cell ATAC-seq data from 111 adult human cell types (57) to identify hPICAs.

  For bulk data preprocessing, reads were aligned to respective reference genomes (hg38 for human, panTro5 for chimpanzee, and rheMac8 for macaque) using Bowtie2 (79) with default parameters, followed by quality filtering [samtools (80) view -F 1804 -f 2 -q 30], polymerase chain reaction (PCR) duplicate removal using Picard (https://github.com/broadinstitute/picard), and peak calling [MACS2 (81) with P value <0.01]. Peak reproducibility was assessed using the irreproducible discovery rate (IDR < 0.1) (82) across biological replicates. For single-cell ATAC-seq data, peaks with normalized counts per cell below 0.05 (counts/cell number < 0.05) were filtered as low-quality regions. The numbers of processed peaks are detailed in data S1.


### Training, validation, and test set construction
  For the positive set, we selected IDR-processed peaks and retained only those located on autosomes. For the negative set, we first excluded OCRs, low complexity repeats (83), and ENCODE blacklist regions (84) from the genome. We then performed random sampling on the remaining regions to obtain a negative set with the same number of peaks as the positive set. Meanwhile, we ensure consistency between the negative and positive sets in terms of chromosomal distribution, peak length, and G-C content.

  We designated chromosome 1 as the validation set, chromosome 2 as the test set, and all remaining chromosomes as the training set, maintaining a 1:1 ratio between positive and negative samples during model training. For model testing, to evaluate model performance under a more natural ratio, we assessed CNNs on entire chromosome 2 for each species, where positive sequences were defined as OCRs and negative sequences were derived from all nonoverlapping 1000–base pair (bp) bins that showed no intersection with any OCRs. The partitioning strategy above was consistently applied across all species and datasets.


### Training CNNs
  To demonstrate the cross-species predictive capacity of CNNs, we first trained binary classification models independently for each species. We selected CNNs because they can automatically learn sequence features without requiring manual feature extraction. In addition, the existing literature has demonstrated that CNNs are effective in predicting chromatin accessibility (46, 47).

  All models were designed with six convolutional layers and two fully connected layers. The number of filters in the convolutional layers was 280, 280, 180, 180, 120, and 120, and the kernel sizes were 19, 19, 17, 17, 15, and 15, respectively. After every two convolutional layers, we applied a batch normalization layer (to accelerate training and prevent overfitting), a max-pooling layer, and a rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation function. To allow flexible input sequence lengths, an adaptive average pooling layer was inserted between the convolutional and fully connected layers. The final layer used a sigmoid activation function to constrain the predicted values within the 0–1 range.

  The loss function used was binary cross-entropy, and the Adam optimizer was applied with a learning rate of 10−4. The early stopping mechanism was used during training, where training was halted if the validation AUROC did not improve within seven rounds. The model with the highest validation AUROC was retained.


### Evaluation of cross-species predictive performance of CNNs
  To assess whether CNNs trained exclusively on human data can effectively predict chromatin accessibility in other primate species, we compared the performance of independently trained models with that of cross-species predictions. For each species, AUROCs and AUPRCs obtained on its own test set were defined as the performance of independent-species training. Then, we applied the optimal model trained on human data to the test sets of chimpanzees and macaques and recorded the AUROCs and AUPRCs as cross-species results. We repeated the procedure above four times to ensure robustness. The difference between independent-species and cross-species AUROCs and AUPRCs was used to evaluate the cross-species predictive performance of the CNNs.


### CNN interpretability analysis
  We applied the integrated gradient method implemented in the interpretability toolkit Captum (https://captum.ai/) (85) to assign contribution scores to each base in the input sequences. To identify the most predictive regions learned by the model, we used a sliding window approach (window size = 20 bp; step size = 1 bp), summing the contribution scores within each window. The window exhibiting the highest score was designated as the SRs.

  For each species, we tokenized the SRs into 7-mers and selected the top 50 most frequent 7-mers as the representative sequence features. We used TomTom (86) to identify the corresponding TF motifs for each representative 7-mer (E value < 10), based on the JASPAR2022_CORE_nonredundant motif database (87). We then assessed the significance of shared TF motif types between humans, chimpanzees, and macaques using the hypergeometric test. Furthermore, to evaluate whether the CNNs learned consistent TF motif importance rankings across species, we ranked shared TF motifs in each species by ascending P values and computed the correlation using the Spearman test.


### Identification of common and human-specific peaks
  We first used UCSC LiftOver (minMatch = 0.95) (88) to identify orthologous sequences of human OCRs in chimpanzee and macaque genomes. To ensure confidence in orthologous sequences, we performed global pairwise sequence alignment using Biopython (89) with the following parameters: match score = 2, mismatch penalty = −3, gap opening penalty = −5, and gap extension penalty = −3. Orthologous sequences were filtered out if either of the following criteria was met: (i) the length difference between the orthologous sequences exceeded twofold or (ii) the number of mismatched bases exceeded half of the human sequence length.

  For common peaks, we required at least 1-bp overlap between the orthologous sequences of human OCRs and the OCRs of other species. In contrast, for human-specific peaks, we confirmed that no OCRs in other species were present within ±1–kilobase (kb) flanking regions of the orthologous sequences of human OCRs. We used the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test to assess differences in the distributions of ΔPr [defined as Pr (human) − Pr (ape)] between the common peaks and human-specific peaks (two-sided test).


### Identification of human increased accessibility regions
  We calculated read counts on orthologous sequences using bedtools (90) and bigWig files generated from bulk ATAC-seq sequencing data and subsequently used DESeq2 (91) to identify differential chromatin accessibility regions between the orthologous sequences. Regions with log2(fold change) < −1 and adjusted P < 0.05 were defined as human increased accessibility regions.

  To assess whether CNN prediction differences were associated with experimentally measured chromatin accessibility differences, we calculated the ΔPr between the orthologous sequences. We applied a series of ΔPr thresholds and defined human OCRs as predicted increased chromatin regions if their ΔPr exceeded the given threshold. Last, we evaluated whether the human increased accessibility regions were significantly enriched with the predicted regions at each ΔPr threshold using Fisher’s exact test.


### Regression model construction
  To investigate whether the regression architecture is better suited to our task, we constructed a regression model to predict chromatin accessibility strength. We maintained the original classification model architecture but removed the final sigmoid activation function and used Huber loss as the loss function. The training sequences were defined as OCRs, and the corresponding log2[reads per kilobase per million mapped reads (RPKM)+1] values from normalized ATAC-seq read counts within these regions were assigned as the training labels. We also incorporated an early stopping mechanism during training, which retained the model parameters from the epoch with the lowest loss on the validation set.


### Inferring the human-chimpanzee ancestral sequences
  We first identified orthologous sequences of human OCRs in chimpanzees and gorillas (methods described in the Identification of common and human-specific peaks section). Multiple sequence alignment of these orthologous sequences was performed using Muscle5 (92). A phylogenetic tree was then constructed using phyloFit (93) to estimate branch lengths under the REV nucleotide substitution model (--precision MED). Last, the ancestral sequences of humans and chimpanzees were inferred using FastML (94), also applying the REV substitution model.


### Identification of hPICAs
  We applied the previously established training set construction strategy and classification model architecture to independently train CNNs across 111 human cell types. Model performance was evaluated using AUROC and AUPRC on the held-out test set. For each cell type, we computed the predicted accessibility difference (ΔPr) between human and HCA.

  In addition to computing ΔPr, we assessed whether these differences deviate from neutral evolution by calculating P values. For each OCR, we used AliSim (http://iqtree.org/doc/AliSim) (95) to simulate DNA sequence evolution and generate pseudo-HCA and pseudo-human sequences under the GTR+I+G4 substitution model, with insertion and deletion rates set at 0.05 and 0.01 relative to substitution, respectively. Then, we calculated the ΔPr between the pseudo-human sequence and the pseudo-HCA sequence. We repeated the above simulation processes 1000 times to generate the null distribution of ΔPr. Last, we calculated the mean and standard deviation of the null distribution and used the “scipy.stats.norm.sf” function to compute the P values for ΔPr (upper tail test), with the Benjamini-Hochberg correction applied to the P values.


### Detection of positive selection in hPICAs
  When DNA sequences are under positive selection, beneficial mutations reach higher frequencies within populations, leading to reduced genetic diversity. Therefore, we calculated the substitution-to-polymorphism (S/P) ratio within OCRs, with an elevated ratio indicating signatures of positive selection (96, 97). For substitutions, we determined the number of substitutions by performing pairwise alignment between human OCRs and ancestral sequences. For polymorphisms, we used SNP data from the 1000 Genomes Project (98). To reduce the effects of population bottlenecks caused by human migration events, we only considered individuals from African populations (Esan, Gambian Mandinka, Luhya, Mende, and Yoruba; N = 555 individuals). We then calculated the number of SNP sites within human OCRs, counting only biallelic SNPs with a minor allele frequency of <0.01. Last, we used Fisher’s exact test to compare the S/P ratio between hPICAs and non-hPICAs (two-sided test).


### Human cis-regulatory elements
  We downloaded the ENCODE candidate cis-regulatory elements from UCSC, which collected 926,535 elements from 706 deoxyribonuclease sequencing (DNase-seq) experiments (99). This dataset includes annotations on promoters, enhancers, CTCF binding sites, and H3K4me3 marks throughout the human genome.


### Prediction of cell type–specific TF binding sites
  We first used WhichTF (58) to identify cell type–specific TFs with a TF score greater than 1. Then, we used FIMO (59) to predict TF binding sites on input sequences (E value < 10−4) using the JASPAR2024_CORE_nonredundant motif database (100).


### Determination of variant functional effects
  We performed global pairwise alignment between human and ancestral sequences to identify SNVs, insertions, and deletions in human OCRs. Then, we applied in silico mutagenesis to sequentially introduce each identified variant into the HCA sequence to calculate the functional effect of each variant as the prediction difference. To avoid potential misalignment results due to the uncertainty of penalty scores, we excluded variants located at the ends of the human OCRs.


### Functional enrichment analysis for hPICAs
  We performed functional enrichment analysis for genes associated with hPICAs. To identify putative target genes, we used the GREAT (60) with default parameters and selected the whole genome as the background. To comprehensively characterize the functional roles of the associated genes, we conducted enrichment analysis using the Mouse Genome Informatics (MGI) database (101). Enrichment significance was assessed using the hypergeometric test, and P values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg correction.


### GWAS enrichment analysis
  We first collected variants associated with traits in European populations from the GWAS Catalog (61) and SNP data for European populations (CEPH, British, Finnish, Iberian, and Toscani; N = 670 individuals) from the 1000 Genomes Project (98). To obtain a list of potential causal variants, we used PLINK (102) to compute linkage disequilibrium (R2 = 0.7) for each variant in the GWAS Catalog.

  Next, we applied the approach described previously (103) to prune the SNP sets. Specifically, for each trait and PubMed ID combination, we ranked the SNPs by their P values. We added SNPs sequentially, ensuring that no other SNPs were present within a 5-kb window of each added SNP. This step resulted in a collection of 2045 traits, each containing at least 30 SNPs. For each trait, we tested whether the associated SNPs were enriched in hPICAs using Fisher’s exact test. To reduce false positives, we used adjusted P < 0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg correction) as the threshold for statistical significance.
