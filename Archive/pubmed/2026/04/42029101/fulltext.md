# MiR‐30a‐5p mediates epileptogenesis in epilepsy models by targeting SOX4 to regulate the Wnt/β‐catenin pathway


## Objective
  The pathogenesis of epilepsy is closely associated with neuronal synaptic plasticity. MicroRNAs (miRNAs) can regulate various biological processes by binding to specific sequences on target genes. This study employs bioinformatics, molecular dynamics, and experimental approaches to investigate the mechanism by which MiR‐30a‐5p treats epilepsy through targeting SOX4 and regulating the Wnt/β‐catenin signaling pathway.


## Methods
  Performed bioinformatics analysis to predict the signaling pathways involved in miRNA‐30a‐5p‐mediated intervention in epilepsy. RT‐qPCR was used to detect the expression levels of miR‐30a‐5p in the hippocampal tissues of epileptic rats and in primary hippocampal neurons subjected to Mg2+‐free treatment. The effects of miR‐30a‐5p inhibition on synaptic plasticity were assessed using EEG, the Morris water maze test, Golgi staining, Sholl analysis, and Western blotting. TargetScan and miRTarBase were employed to predict the target genes of miR‐30a‐5p. The interaction was validated through dual‐luciferase reporter assays, IHC, IF, and Western blotting, and the effect of SOX4 inhibition on synaptic plasticity was evaluated. The involvement of the Wnt signaling pathway was assessed by Western blotting.


## Results
  The expression level of miR‐30a‐5p was significantly elevated in the hippocampal region of epileptic rats and in magnesium‐depleted hippocampal neuronal cultures. SOX4 was identified as a direct target of miR‐30a‐5p. Inhibiting miR‐30a‐5p reduces the expression of synapse‐associated proteins (SYP/CaMKII/PSD95), while upregulating SOX4 and downregulating the levels of Wnt3a, β‐catenin, and cyclin D1. miR‐30a‐5p may participate in alterations of neuronal synaptic plasticity by directly targeting and inhibiting SOX4 expression, thereby activating the Wnt/β‐catenin signaling pathway.


## Significance
  This study provides evidence that miR‐30a‐5p participates in the process of synaptic plasticity impairment in epileptic neurons. The study confirms a direct interaction between miR‐30a‐5p and SOX4. Furthermore, miR‐30a‐5p regulates the Wnt/β‐catenin signaling pathway by targeting SOX4, thereby influencing neuronal synaptic plasticity impairment. This discovery provides a novel potential therapeutic target for delaying epilepsy progression.


## Plain Language Summary
  Epilepsy is a common brain disorder that affects millions of people worldwide. This study found that a small molecule called miR‐30a‐5p increases in the brain during epilepsy and disrupts normal communication between neurons. By blocking this molecule in laboratory experiments, we were able to improve neuron function and reduce damage. These findings suggest that targeting miR‐30a‐5p could lead to new treatments to slow down the progression of epilepsy.


## INTRODUCTION
  Epilepsy remains a highly prevalent neurological disorder in pediatric populations, with 60% of cases manifesting during childhood. 1  Temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE), the most common subtype of intractable epilepsy (IE), is frequently associated with cognitive impairment—a condition that substantially compromises patients' quality of life. 2  Epileptogenesis induces neuronal damage or loss, synaptic remodeling, axonal sprouting, and structural and functional alterations in neuroglia (particularly microglia and astrocytes). Antiepileptic drugs (AEDs) serve as the first‐line therapeutic strategy for epilepsy management; however, approximately one‐third of patients develop treatment‐resistant epilepsy. 3  Therefore, elucidating the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying TLE is crucial for the development of targeted and effective therapeutic interventions.

  Synaptic plasticity refers to adaptive changes in the functional state, structural morphology, and number of synapses in response to internal and external stimuli, which enhance the adaptability of neural circuits to alterations in the internal and external environment. 4  Accumulating evidence indicates that TLE involves the formation of aberrant synaptic remodeling and the reconstruction of abnormal neural networks by hippocampal neurons. This process generates pathologically hyperexcitable neural circuits, reflecting a form of plasticity‐driven neural network reorganization in hippocampal neurons. 5  Synaptophysin (SYP) is a phosphorylated protein closely associated with neuronal development and neurotransmitter release, and it plays a crucial role in regulating synaptic transmission between neurons and synaptic plasticity. 6  Calcium/calmodulin‐dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII) is widely expressed in neurons and predominantly enriched in the postsynaptic membrane, where it serves as a major component of the postsynaptic density (PSD) and exerts a key regulatory role in synaptic plasticity. 7  Postsynaptic density 95 (PSD95) is a large protein complex localized at the postsynaptic terminal of excitatory synapses and is responsible for the expression of synaptic plasticity. 8

  Numerous studies investigating microRNA (miRNA) dysregulation in TLE have provided evidence for their critical role in epilepsy pathogenesis. 9  Abnormal miRNA expression is associated with pathways involved in inflammation, cell death, synaptic reorganization, and neuronal excitability, thus exhibiting a strong correlation with epileptogenesis. 10  Using microarray‐based serum miRNA profiling, researchers found that miR‐30a is highly expressed in the serum of patients during epileptic seizures, and its expression level is positively correlated with seizure frequency. 11  Relevant studies have indicated that miR‐30a‐5p is upregulated in chronic epilepsy. 12 ,  13

  SRY‐box transcription factor 4 (SOX4) is an essential developmental transcription factor that regulates stemness, differentiation, progenitor cell development, and multiple developmental signaling pathways, including phosphatidylinositol 3‐kinase (PI3K), Wnt, and transforming growth factor‐β (TGFβ) signaling. 14  Across multiple disease contexts, miR‐30a‐5p has been shown to target SOX4. For instance, Du L et al. demonstrated that the miR‐30a‐5p/SOX4 axis activates the interferon‐γ (IFNγ)‐Janus kinase 2 (JAK2)‐signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1) pathway, thereby promoting the progression of renal cell carcinoma. 15  Additionally, researchers have found that miR‐30a functions as a tumor suppressor by directly targeting SOX4 in chondrosarcoma cells. 16

  This study aims to investigate whether miR‐30a‐5p ameliorates synaptic plasticity impairment in hippocampal neurons during epilepsy by regulating the SOX4/Wnt/β‐catenin signaling pathway, using both a pilocarpine‐induced rat epilepsy model and a magnesium‐free primary hippocampal neuronal culture system. The insights gained from these investigations may lay the foundation for development of innovative therapeutic approaches for epilepsy.

### Rat selection and grouping
  Healthy male SD rats (n = 90; 7 weeks old; 180 ± 20 g) were provided by the Department of Laboratory Animal Science, Yanbian University School of Medicine. Rats were randomly divided into six groups: control groups, TLE groups, TLE+miRNA30a‐5p antagomir groups (miRNA30a‐5p antagomir), and TLE+miRNA30a‐5p NC groups (miRNA30a‐5pNC), TLE+miRNA30a‐5p antagomir+siRNASOX4groups (siRNA SOX4), TLE+miRNA30a‐5p antagomir+siRNANC groups (siRNANC).


### Stereotaxic injection into the hippocampus
  After anesthesia with 2% pentobarbital, rats were fixed on a stereotaxic instrument with the bregma and lambda aligned in the same horizontal plane. Bilateral hippocampal coordinates were then determined according to a rat brain atlas (3.3 mm posterior to bregma, 2.0 mm lateral from the midline, and 3.5 mm ventral from the skull surface). In the TLE+miRNA‐30a‐5p antagomir group, the miRNA‐30a‐5p antagomir reagent (prepared as a 1 nM/5 μL solution in normal saline) was injected into the left and right hippocampal regions at a dose of 1 nmol (RiboBio, China), with an infusion rate of 0.5 μL/min. Rats in the TLE+miR‐30a‐5p NC group received an intracerebral injection of an equal dose of scrambled negative control oligonucleotide (designed and synthesized by RiboBio, China, with the same chemical modification as the antagomir). In the TLE+miRNA‐30a‐5p antagomir + siRNASOX4 group, a mixture of the siRNASOX4 reagent and the miRNA‐30a‐5p antagomir reagent (prepared as a 1 nM/5 μL solution in normal saline) was injected into the bilateral hippocampal regions at a dose of 1 nmol (RiboBio, China), with an infusion rate of 0.5 μL/min. In the TLE+miRNA‐30a‐5p antagomir + NC group, an equivalent mixture of the miRNA‐30a‐5p antagomir reagent and siRNA NC was injected at the same sites.


### Model preparation
  The control group received an intraperitoneal injection of normal saline, and behavioral changes were observed. The TLE group, TLE+miRNA‐30a‐5p NC group, TLE+miRNA‐30a‐5p antagomir group, TLE+miRNA‐30a‐5p antagomir + siRNA SOX4 group, and TLE+miRNA‐30a‐5p antagomir + siRNA NC group were treated as follows: An initial intraperitoneal injection of lithium chloride, followed by intraperitoneal injection of scopolamine 19 h later, and a pilocarpine injection 30 min after the scopolamine administration. The severity of epileptic seizures was evaluated utilizing the Racine scoring system (Table 1), with status epilepticus (SE) being characterized as a prolonged generalized seizure that attained a score of 4 or 5 and persisted for a minimum duration of 50 min. Seizure latency was recorded, and seizures were terminated 3 h post‐onset via intraperitoneal injection of 10 mg/kg diazepam.


### Specimen collection
  Hippocampal tissues were collected from each group on day 30 after rat model establishment. Following 1% sodium pentobarbital anesthesia, rapid decapitation was performed for brain tissue extraction, with one fraction for embedding and sectioning and the other for molecular experiments.


### Cell culture
  Hippocampal neurons isolated from the embryos of pregnant rats at embryonic day 18 were cultured in Neurobasal medium. Neurons were randomly divided into six groups: (1) Control group; (2) Mg2+‐free group; (3) Mg2+‐free + miRNA‐30a‐5p inhibitor group (miRNA‐30a‐5p inhibitor group); (4) Mg2+‐free + miRNA‐30a‐5p NC group (miRNA‐30a‐5p NC group); (5) Mg2+‐free + miRNA‐30a‐5p inhibitor + siRNASOX4 group (siRNASOX4 group); (6) Mg2+‐free + miRNA‐30a‐5p inhibitor + siRNANC group (siRNANC group). Neurons were transfected with miRNA‐30a‐5p inhibitor, miRNA‐30a‐5p NC, siRNASOX4, or siRNANC 6 h prior to Mg2+‐free medium treatment. Except for the normal group, hippocampal neurons in the remaining groups were cultured in MgCl2‐free medium to induce spontaneous recurrent epileptiform discharge patterns, and the cultures were treated under Mg2+ −free conditions for 3 h. The riboFECT™ CP Transfection Kit (RiboBio, China) was used to transfect miRNA‐30a‐5p inhibitor (RiboBio, China), miRNA‐30a‐5p NC (RiboBio, China), siRNASOX4, or siRNANC (RiboBio, China) 6 h prior to the 3‐h Mg2+ −free treatment (See Figure 2B).


### Real‐time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT‐qPCR)

#### RNA extraction
  Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing). After precipitation with chloroform and isopropanol, the RNA pellet was washed with 75% ethanol and dissolved in RNase/DNase‐free ddH2O.


#### For miRNA quantification
  Reverse transcription was performed using 2× miRNA RT Reaction Buffer, miRNA RT Enzyme Mix, total RNA, and RNase/DNase‐free ddH2O. The reaction was incubated at 42°C for 60 min, followed by 95°C for 3 min to obtain cDNA. PCR amplification was carried out using cDNA, forward primer, reverse primer, 2× miRcute Plus miRNA PreMix (SYBR&ROX), ROX Reference Dye (50×), and RNase/DNase‐free ddH2O. The cycling conditions were: 95°C for 15 min (pre‐denaturation); followed by 40 cycles of 94°C for 20 s and 60°C for 34 s; and finally 95°C for 15 s, 60°C for 1 min, and 95°C for 15 s for dissociation curve analysis. The primer sequences used are shown in Table 2.


#### For mRNA quantification
  Reverse transcription was performed using 5× Reaction Buffer, reverse transcription primers, RNA, Ribolock RNase Inhibitor, RevertAid M‐MuLV Reverse Transcriptase, and dNTP Mix. The reaction was incubated at 42°C for 60 min, followed by 70°C for 5 min to obtain cDNA. PCR amplification was carried out using cDNA, forward primer, reverse primer, SYBR mix, ROX Reference Dye (50×), and RNase/DNase‐free ddH2O. The cycling conditions were: 94°C for 30 s (pre‐denaturation); followed by 40 cycles of 94°C for 5 s and 60°C for 30 s; and finally 95°C for 15 s, 60°C for 1 min, and 95°C for 15 s for dissociation curve analysis. The primer sequences used are shown in Table 2.


### EEG system implantation and analysis
  After successful establishment of all group models, electroencephalogram (EEG) recordings were performed using the DSI Ponemah 6.X system (Data Sciences International, USA) on Day 30. Rats were anesthetized with 1% sodium pentobarbital (40 mg/kg, intraperitoneal) and fixed in a stereotaxic apparatus. A 3–4 cm incision was made along the midline of the back, and the tissue was bluntly dissected to create a subcutaneous pocket for transmitter placement. The electrode positions of the implant were as follows: the negative lead was placed 2.0 mm anterior and 1.5 mm lateral to Bregma; the positive lead was placed 7.0 mm posterior and 1.5 mm lateral to Bregma on the contralateral side. A 1.0 mm micro‐drill was used to make burr holes in the skull. The implant was fixed with dental acrylic resin, and the incision was sutured. Rats were allowed to recover for 7 days before recording.

  EEG signals were recorded continuously for 24 h per day, with a sampling rate of 500 Hz. Seizure activity was analyzed offline using NeuroScore software (Data Sciences International, USA). Seizures were identified and classified according to the Racine scale as follows Table 1. The following parameters were quantified: seizure frequency (number of seizures per day), seizure duration (seconds per seizure), and mean seizure severity (average Racine stage). All analyses were performed by an investigator blinded to the experimental groups. Five rats from each group were randomly selected for statistical analysis.


### Morris water maze test
  Approximately, 30 days after establishing the epileptic rat model, the Morris water maze test was performed to evaluate cognitive function. One day before initiating the place navigation test (PNT) experiment, rats were placed in water for 120 s to familiarize themselves with the environment. To commence the PNT experiment, a platform was placed in the target quadrant, and the escape latency (EL, the time taken by the rat from water entry to climbing onto the platform with all four limbs) was recorded to evaluate the spatial learning ability of the rats. Rats were introduced into the water from each of the four quadrants, ensuring their heads faced the maze wall upon water entry. In this experiment, the maximum EL threshold was set to 120 s. If the rat climbed onto the platform within 120 s and remained on the platform for 10 s, the time spent was recorded as its EL; if the rat failed to find the platform within the set time, a guiding rod was used to direct the rat onto the platform for 10 s, and its EL was recorded as 120 s. According to the experimental method described above, each rat underwent four quadrant tests per day for a total of 4 days.

  The spatial probe test (SPT) experiment was conducted on the 5th day. The platform was removed; remaining preparations were identical to before. Rats were introduced into the water from any quadrant. Rats were introduced into the water from the second quadrant (as selected for this experiment) according to the above requirements, and the system automatically recorded the number of times the rats passed through the platform within 120 s.


### HE staining
  Paraffin‐embedded hippocampal tissue sections were subjected to dewaxing, rehydration, and clearing, followed by staining with a H&E staining kit according to the manufacturer's protocol.


### Nissl staining
  Following deparaffinization and hydration, the hippocampal paraffin sections were thoroughly rinsed. The sections were then stained with a Nissl staining solution.


### Immunohistochemical staining
  Hippocampal tissue samples were processed through baking, deparaffinization, hydration, and antigen retrieval, then immunohistochemically assayed using a rabbit two‐step assay kit (Zhongsui Science and Technology, PV‐9001). Five different fields of view of the sections were selected at 400× magnification, and positive cells from each field of view were recorded. Finally, the expression levels of CaMKII and SOX4 were determined.


### Golgi staining
  Rats were anesthetized and then rapidly decapitated to remove the brain. Brain tissue was extracted and cut into 0.5 cm thick tissue blocks, which were fixed in GENMED fixative for 2 weeks, incubated in 30% sucrose solution for 48 h, paraffin‐embedded and sectioned to a thickness of 40–150 μm. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, incubated with GENMED staining solution (30 min), incubated with GENMED color development solution (30 min), and the reaction was terminated as soon as black coloration was observed (protected from light). GENMED Cresol Violet Restaining Kit was used for restaining. Sections were cleared and sealed with a coverslip. Under a microscope, the researchers evaluated the neuronal dendrites and dendritic spine density in the hippocampal CA1 region of rats from each group. Criteria for Dendritic Spine Identification: A dendritic spine was defined as a distinct protrusion extending ≥0.5 μm from the dendritic shaft surface, with a clearly visible connection at its base. Using ImageJ/Fiji software, the base point of each spine was manually marked along the dendritic centerline on maximum intensity projection images. Spine density was calculated as the number of spines per micrometer of dendritic length (spines/μm).


### Immunofluorescence (IF) staining
  Hippocampal tissue slices and cultured cells underwent permeabilization and blocking, then were incubated sequentially with primary and secondary antibodies. Nuclei of the cells were stained with DAPI, and all specimens were examined using a fluorescence microscope.


### Sholl analysis
  On Day 7, hippocampal neurons were verified by immunofluorescence staining using a mouse anti‐MAP2 antibody (1:500, ab183830, Abcam, USA). Sholl analysis of neuronal arborization in each group was performed using FIJI image software. Morphological analysis of hippocampal neuronal cells was then conducted using Sholl analysis.


### Western blotting
  Hippocampal tissues and primary hippocampal neurons were added to EP tubes with PMSF and lysis buffer, fully lysed by centrifugation, and the protein supernatant was extracted for concentration measurement via the biuret method. Subsequent procedures included electrophoresis, membrane transfer, blocking, antibody incubation, and X‐ray exposure. All experimental data were processed and analyzed with ImageJ software.


### Collection of disease‐associated targets
  Potential genes associated with epilepsy were collected from GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/). GeneCards is a comprehensive and authoritative human gene integrator that assembles various descriptions about human genes from over 80 digital sources. 17  We gathered epilepsy‐related targets by searching the aforementioned databases using the keyword “epilepsy.”


### Prediction of miR‐30a‐5p target genes
  The prediction websites employed included miRDB (http://www.mirdb.org/cgi‐bin/search.cgi), 18  Encyclopedia of RNA Interactomes (https://rnasysu.com/encori/index.php), 19  and TargetScan‐gene (https://www.targetscan.org/vert_80/). 20  The main prediction sites included searching for miR‐30a‐5p complementary target loci to its specific region, as well as noncomplementary target loci. The predictions of these target genes were subsequently cross‐screened.


### Construction of the component–common target–disease network
  Venny2.1.0 (http://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/), an interactive tool for comparing lists using Venn diagrams, was used to screen common potential therapeutic targets between miRNA30a‐5p and epilepsy‐related targets. Cytoscape‐v3.7.1, 21  a free software for visualizing complex networks, was used to construct the miRNA30a‐5p component–common target–epilepsy network model for enhanced visualization of their interrelationships.


### Protein–protein interaction (PPI) data
  The Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes (STRING) database (https://string‐db.org/, ver. 10.5), 22  a database that searches for known and predicted protein‐to‐protein interactions, was used to analyze the protein–protein interactions of the common targets between miRNA30a‐5p and epilepsy‐related targets. The database defines PPIs with confidence ranges for data scores.


### GO and KEGG enrichment analyses
  Gene ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis and Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis were performed using R software (a programming language and integrated environment specifically designed for statistical analysis, data mining, data visualization, and data science). Overrepresentation analysis was performed based on the hypergeometric test. To control for false‐positives due to multiple testing, the p‐values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method. Enriched terms with an adjusted p‐value (FDR) < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The required R packages are listed in the Supplementary Document S5.


### Dual‐luciferase reporter assay
  To assess the direct binding of miR‐30a‐5p to SOX4, 293Tcells were co‐transfected using Lipo 8000 (Beyotime, China) with pmirGLO vectors containing wild‐type (WT) or mutant (MUT) SIRT1 3′UTR sequences, along with miR‐30a‐5p mimics or a negative control. The luciferase activity was measured using a dual‐luciferase reporter assay kit (E1910, Promega, China).


### Statistical analysis
  Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 22 and GraphPad Prism 10. Continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. The Shapiro–Wilk test evaluated the normality of quantitative data. For normally distributed data, the t‐test or analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used as appropriate; for non‐normally distributed data, the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis H test was applied as suitable. A two‐sided p‐value of <0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.


## RESULTS

### Bioinformatics prediction indicates that miR‐30a‐5p participates in the pathogenesis of epilepsy by regulating the Wnt/β‐catenin signaling pathway through targeting SOX4
  To identify potential disease‐related targets, 9587 epilepsy‐associated targets were retrieved from the GeneCards database (Supplementary Document S1). The target genes of miRNA‐30a‐5p were collected from the miRDB (Supplementary Document S2), RNA Encyclopedia (Supplementary Document S3), and TargetScan databases (Supplementary Document S4). Using Venn diagram analysis, 656 common target genes of miRNA‐30a‐5p were identified from the three databases (Figure 1A). A total of 332 overlapping target genes were screened via Venn diagram analysis of the 656 miRNA‐30a‐5p target genes and the 9587 epilepsy‐associated genes (Figure 1B). Ultimately, CTNNB1 (β‐catenin) was identified as the gene with the strongest association to both miRNA‐30a‐5p and epilepsy (Figure 1C).

  In GO enrichment analysis, these genes were found to be involved in cell differentiation, synaptic structural remodeling, and activity regulation (Figure 1D–F). Additionally, KEGG enrichment analysis identified several signaling pathways potentially influenced by miRNA‐30a‐5p in epilepsy, including the neurotrophic factor pathway, Wnt pathway, and axon guidance pathway (Figure 1G,H). The enrichment indicated a significant association between the therapeutic targets and the Wnt pathway. We predicted that miRNA‐30a‐5p regulates the Wnt/β‐catenin pathway, which may be involved in the pathogenesis of epilepsy.

  In conclusion, our bioinformatics prediction revealed that miR‐30a‐5p participates in the pathogenesis of epilepsy by regulating the Wnt/β‐catenin signaling pathway.


### MiR‐30a‐5p expression is upregulated in an epilepsy model
  In the present study, RT‐qPCR was used to quantify the expression levels of miR‐30a‐5p in the hippocampal tissues of epileptic rats. Compared with the control group, miR‐30a‐5p expression was significantly upregulated in the TLE group with a fold change of 4.3 ± 0.2 (p < 0.01).(Figure 2C). Consistently, in primary hippocampal neurons, the Mg2+‐free group also exhibited a marked elevation in miR‐30a‐5p expression relative to the control group, with a fold change of 3.2 ± 0.1 (p < 0.01) (Figure 2D).

  In summary, the expression of miR‐30a‐5p is upregulated in the epilepsy model.


### Impact of miRNA‐30a‐5p expression suppression on EEG and behavioral outcomes in epileptic rats
  EEG recordings for the control group rats revealed no evident abnormalities or epileptiform discharges whatsoever. By contrast, the TLE group displayed a notable increase in paroxysmal spike and sharp wave counts, total epileptiform discharge duration, as well as seizure occurrence frequency. Relative to the miRNA‐30a‐5p NC group, the miRNA‐30a‐5p antagomir group presented with a marked decrease in rat epileptiform discharges, total discharge time, and seizure frequency (Figure 3A–C).

  In the Morris water maze assay, the EL of rats in all four groups declined as training days advanced during the first 4 days of the PNT. Pairwise comparisons on day 1 found no notable differences in EL across the four groups (p > 0.05). From 2d through 4d, EL was markedly elevated in the TLE group. In contrast, the miRNA‐30a‐5p antagomir group exhibited a marked decrease in EL. On 5 d, the SPT showed that TLE rats had a far lower number of platform crossings than control rats (p < 0.05). An opposing tendency was shown in the miRNA‐30a‐5p antagomir group (Figure 3D–F).

  In conclusion, epileptic seizures in rats are characterized by abnormal brain discharges, which consequently impair learning and memory functions. Inhibition of miR‐30a‐5p expression can reduce the occurrence of epileptiform waves and ameliorate these cognitive deficits.


### Inhibition of miR‐30a‐5p expression mitigates hippocampal tissue and synaptic plasticity damage in epileptic rats
  In the present study, HE staining showed that neurons in the hippocampal CA1 region of the control group were well‐organized with normal morphology. In contrast, the hippocampal tissue of the TLE group exhibited morphological and structural impairments, characterized by a marked reduction in cell number, irregular and disorganized cell arrangement, significantly enlarged interstitial spaces, cellular swelling, loss of cellular contour, deep cytoplasmic staining, vacuolar lesions, and severe nuclear condensation and fragmentation. Compared with the miR‐30a‐5p NC group, these pathological changes were significantly alleviated in the miR‐30a‐5p antagomir group (Figure 4A). Nissl staining further demonstrated that hippocampal neurons in the CA1 region of the control group had clear morphological features, with evenly distributed and well‐aligned chromatin and abundant cytoplasmic Nissl bodies (blue granules). In the TLE group, neurons were sparsely arranged with a significant reduction in Nissl bodies. Compared with the miR‐30a‐5p NC group, the miR‐30a‐5p antagomir group showed more orderly neuronal arrangement and no significant reduction in Nissl bodies (Figure 4B).

  Golgi staining was used to visualize the synapses and dendrites of hippocampal CA1 neurons. In the control group, the dendrites of hippocampal neurons exhibited regular distribution and formed a densely organized dendritic network. Compared with the control group, the TLE group showed disorganized neuronal dendrite arrangement, numerous dendritic tangles that aggregated into thicker nerve fiber bundles, and a significant reduction in dendritic spine density. Relative to the miR‐30a‐5p NC group, the miR‐30a‐5p antagomir group displayed restored hippocampal neuronal dendritic networks, with relatively regular dendrite arrangement and significantly increased dendritic spine density (Figure 4C–E).

  PSD95, SYP, and CaMKII are key proteins implicated in synaptic remodeling. RtPCR analysis of rat hippocampal tissues demonstrated that, compared with the control group, the expression levels of PSD95, SYP, and CaMKII were significantly decreased in the TLE group. Relative to the miR‐30a‐5p NC group, the miR‐30a‐5p antagomir group exhibited significantly elevated expression levels of PSD95, SYP, and CaMKII (Figure 3F). These findings were consistent with the results obtained from Western blot (WB) analysis of the same proteins (Figure 4G,H).

  In immunohistochemical staining assays, CaMKII protein levels were significantly reduced in the TLE group compared with the control group. Additionally, relative to the miR‐30a‐5p NC group, CaMKII protein levels were significantly increased in the miR‐30a‐5p antagomir group (Figure 4I,J).

  In conclusion, the occurrence of epilepsy leads to impaired synaptic plasticity in the brains of epileptic rats. Inhibition of miR‐30a‐5p expression ameliorates these detrimental alterations in hippocampal neuronal synaptic plasticity.


### Inhibiting miR‐30a‐5p expression alleviates magnesium‐deficiency‐induced synaptic plasticity impairment in primary hippocampal neurons
  In primary hippocampal neuronal cultures, we transfected cells with a fluorescently labeled miR‐30a‐5p inhibitor, and the transfection efficiency was determined to be 90% ± 3%. Primary hippocampal neurons were immunostained using an antibody against microtubule‐associated protein 2 (MAP2). Neurons were randomly selected for analysis following MAP2 staining (Figure 5A). In the control group, the neurites exhibited a uniform distribution. In contrast, neurons in the Mg2+‐free group exhibited a significant reduction in neurite outgrowth. Neurons in the Mg2+‐free control group had significantly fewer protrusions intersecting concentric circles compared with those in the control group. Additionally, compared with the miR‐30a‐5p NC group, neurons in the miR‐30a‐5p inhibitor group had significantly more protrusions intersecting concentric circles (Figure 5B,C).

  Quantification of the number of primary dendrites in hippocampal neurons across groups demonstrated that the Mg2+‐free group had a significant reduction in primary dendrite number relative to the control group. Conversely, the miR‐30a‐5p inhibitor group showed a significant increase in primary dendrite number compared with the miR‐30a‐5p NC group (Figure 5D).

  At the molecular level, RT‐qPCR and WB analyses were performed to evaluate the expression of three key synaptic proteins: PSD95, SYP, and CaMKII. Compared with the normal group, the Mg2+‐free group exhibited significantly downregulated expression of all three proteins. Relative to the miR‐30a‐5p NC group, treatment with the miR‐30a‐5p inhibitor markedly restored the expression levels of these synaptic proteins (Figure 5E–G). In the cellular immunofluorescence assay, CaMKII expression was significantly reduced in the Mg2+‐free group compared with the control group; additionally, CaMKII expression was markedly elevated in the miR‐30a‐5p inhibitor group relative to the miR‐30a‐5p NC group (Figure 5H,I).

  In summary, treatment with Mg2+‐free medium induces synaptic plasticity impairment in primary hippocampal neurons. Inhibition of miR‐30a‐5p expression alleviates this Mg2+‐free‐induced impairment of synaptic plasticity.


### The Effect of miR‐30a‐5p on Regulating SOX4 Expression Levels in Epileptic Hippocampal Neurons
  Using TargetScan and miRTarBase databases, we predicted the targeting relationship between miR‐30a‐5p and SOX4 (Figure 6A,B). Based on this prediction, a dual‐luciferase reporter assay was performed to validate the direct interaction between miR‐30a‐5p and SOX4. As shown in Figure 6C, compared with the mimic negative control (NC) group, the luciferase activity of the wild‐type (WT) SOX4 3′‐untranslated region (3’‐UTR) reporter vector was significantly reduced in the miR‐30a‐5p mimic group (p < 0.05). In contrast, no significant difference was observed in the luciferase activity of the mutant (MUT) SOX4 3’‐UTR reporter vector between the miR‐30a‐5p mimic group and the mimic NC group (p > 0.05). These results demonstrate that miR‐30a‐5p can bind to the 3’‐UTR region of SOX4 mRNA, confirming that SOX4 is a direct target gene of miR‐30a‐5p.

  SOX4 expression abundance in hippocampal tissues across different groups was assessed via reverse transcription RT‐qPCR, WB analysis, and immunohistochemical staining. SOX4 expression abundance was substantially lowered in the TLE group, indicating that epileptogenesis can suppress SOX4 expression. The miRNA‐30a‐5p antagomir group exhibited increase in SOX4 expression levels (p < 0.05), suggesting that inhibition of miRNA‐30a‐5p can upregulate SOX4 expression (Figure 6D–F). Immunohistochemical staining results revealed that SOX4 was predominantly localized in the nucleus. Consistent with the RT‐qPCR and WB findings, SOX4 expression was markedly reduced in the TLE group, while it was markedly upregulated in the miRNA‐30a‐5p antagomir group (Figure 6G,H).

  We further evaluated SOX4 expression in primary hippocampal neurons using RT‐qPCR, WB analysis, and immunofluorescence staining. The expression trends of SOX4 across groups in these neuronal cultures were consistent with those observed in the hippocampal tissues of epileptic rats (Figure 7A–E).

  In conclusion, miR‐30a‐5p directly targets SOX4, whereas inhibition of miR‐30a‐5p up‐regulates SOX4 expression.


### miR‐30a‐5p mediates synaptic plasticity impairment in hippocampal neurons of epileptic rats by targeting SOX4
  In Golgi staining, we observed that the miRNA‐30a‐5p antagomir + SOX4 siRNA group exhibited disorganized dendritic arrangement of hippocampal neurons, accompanied by numerous dendritic tangles (Figure 8A). Additionally, thickened nerve fiber bundles and a significant reduction in dendritic spine density were noted (Figure 8B,C). PCR analysis of rat hippocampal tissue revealed that the levels of PSD95, SYP, and CaMKII were reduced in the miRNA‐30a‐5p antagomir + SOX4 siRNA group (Figure 8D). This observation was in line with the outcomes of WB assays for these proteins (Figure 8E,F). IHC demonstrated that the CaMKII protein level was decreased in the miRNA‐30a‐5p antagomir + SOX4 siRNA group (Figure 8G,H).

  In summary, inhibition of miR‐30a‐5p mediates synaptic plasticity impairment in the hippocampal neurons of epileptic rats by targeting SOX4.


### miR‐30a‐5p targeting SOX4 mitigates synaptic plasticity impairment in epileptic hippocampal neurons
  In Sholl analysis, the miRNA‐30a‐5p inhibitor + SOX4 siRNA group exhibited fewer neuronal synapses and reduced axon intersections with concentric circles (Figure 9A,B). Quantitative analysis of primary dendrite counts in hippocampal neurons across groups revealed significantly fewer primary dendrites in the miRNA‐30a‐5p inhibitor + SOX4 siRNA group (Figure 9C). PCR and WB analyses showed that the levels of PSD95, SYP, and CaMKII were lower in the miRNA‐30a‐5p inhibitor + SOX4 siRNA group (Figure 9D–F).

  In summary, inhibition of miR‐30a‐5p alleviates Mg2+‐free‐induced synaptic plasticity impairment in primary hippocampal neurons by targeting SOX4.


### Experimental validation demonstrates that miR‐30a‐5p modulates the Wnt/β‐catenin signaling pathway by targeting SOX4
  Figure 10A–C shows that the expression abundance of Wnt3a, β‐catenin, and cyclin D1 proteins was significantly elevated in the Mg2+‐free group, suggesting that epileptogenesis can activate the Wnt signaling pathway. The expression abundance of Wnt3a, β‐catenin, and cyclin D1 proteins was significantly reduced in the miRNA‐30a‐5p inhibitor group. In the miRNA‐30a‐5p inhibitor + siRNA‐SOX4 group, the expression levels of Wnt3a, β‐catenin, and cyclin D1 proteins were significantly increased. We performed in vivo experiments in an epileptic rat model, and the findings derived from RT‐PCR and WB assays were consistent with those obtained in magnesium‐depleted hippocampal neurons. These findings suggest that the suppression of miRNA‐30a‐5p impedes the Wnt pathway, whereas the inhibition of SOX4 counteracts the suppressive effect of miRNA‐30a‐5p on this pathway (Figure 10D–F). This suggests that miRNA‐30a‐5p might activate the Wnt pathway through the regulation of its target, SOX4 expression.


## DISCUSSION
  The clinical characteristics of TLE are recurrent spontaneous seizures, and its mechanisms are closely related to neuronal synaptic plasticity. 23  Frequent seizure activity exerts detrimental effects on cognitive function and learning ability, with these impairments mechanistically linked to pathological synaptic remodeling. 24  In the Morris water maze test, TLE rats exhibited a marked prolongation of EL and a significant reduction in platform‐crossing frequency, confirming that epilepsy impairs cognitive and learning capabilities in rodents. Golgi staining revealed disorganized dendritic arbors in epileptic neurons, characterized by extensive dendritic tangles suggestive of aberrant formation of enlarged nerve fiber bundles, along with a substantial decrease in dendritic spine density. Sholl analysis further demonstrated that hippocampal neurons in the Mg2+‐free group displayed reduced neurite outgrowth complexity and a significant decline in the number of primary dendrites. Collectively, these findings indicate that epilepsy induces structural and functional damage to neuronal synapses. Consistent with this, previous studies have confirmed that epilepsy‐induced learning and cognitive deficits are closely associated with dysregulated neuronal synaptic plasticity. 25

  SYP plays a pivotal role in regulating neurotransmitter release and postsynaptic signal transduction. 26  CaMKII remains in a low‐activity state under basal conditions; however, upon the initiation of intracellular signaling cascades, elevated cytosolic calcium ion concentrations trigger CaMKII phosphorylation. Notably, deletion or downregulation of CaMKII has been shown to induce recurrent epileptic seizures, cognitive impairment, and developmental retardation. 27  Similarly, reduced expression or deletion of PSD‐95 lowers the seizure threshold, thereby increasing susceptibility to epilepsy. 28  In our epileptic rat model, RT‐qPCR, immunohistochemistry, and Western blotting consistently demonstrated that epilepsy leads to a significant reduction in the expression levels of SYP, CaMKII, and PSD‐95. Importantly, these synaptic proteins exhibited analogous expression patterns in Mg2+‐depleted primary hippocampal neurons, providing compelling molecular evidence that epilepsy disrupts synaptic plasticity.

  MiRNA‐30a‐5p has been implicated in the regulation of tissue and organ development, the onset of various diseases, and numerous cellular processes. 29  For instance, miRNA‐30a‐5p is upregulated in gliomas, where it inhibits tumor progression by modulating downstream signaling pathways. 30  In addition, inhibition of miRNA‐30a‐5p overexpression can ameliorate cognitive deficits induced by chronic cerebral hypoperfusion. 31  In the present study, RT‐qPCR assays revealed that miRNA‐30a‐5p expression was upregulated in both the hippocampal tissues of epileptic rats and Mg2+‐depleted primary hippocampal neurons, consistent with the findings of previous reports. 32

  SOX4 regulates stem cell pluripotency, cell differentiation, progenitor cell maturation, and various developmental signaling cascades, and its elevated expression have been confirmed in multiple malignant tumors. 33 ,  34 ,  35  In recent years, emerging studies have identified SOX4 as a direct target gene of miR‐30a, and a significant inverse correlation between SOX4 overexpression and miR‐30a downregulation has been verified in clinical samples. 36  Liu et al. demonstrated that miR‐30a targets SOX4 to regulate epithelial–mesenchymal transition and cancer stem cell‐like properties in breast cancer. 37  Based on the above, the criteria for selecting SOX4 for the following reasons: (1) it was predicted as a target by both TargetScan and miRTarBase, thereby increasing confidence in the interaction; (2) previous studies have reported that the miR‐30 family regulates SOX4 expression in various biological contexts. To elucidating the regulatory relationship between miRNA‐30a‐5p and SOX4 in epileptic hippocampal tissues, we performed a dual‐luciferase reporter assay which confirmed a direct target–ligand interaction between these two molecules. Subsequent experiments using rat hippocampal tissues and primary hippocampal neurons further validated that miRNA‐30a‐5p directly targets SOX4, as evidenced by reciprocal changes in SOX4 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels following miRNA‐30a‐5p knockdown (assessed via RT‐qPCR and Western blotting). Notably, Han et al. identified SOX4 as a critical regulator of neuronal synaptic function through high‐throughput RNA sequencing. 38  Based on these findings, we hypothesized that miRNA‐30a‐5p targets SOX4 to modulate epilepsy‐induced alterations in hippocampal synaptic plasticity. Consistent with this hypothesis, interference with miRNA‐30a‐5p expression resulted in a marked upregulation of SOX4 levels. Furthermore, Golgi staining and Sholl analysis revealed significant improvements in synaptic morphological features (e.g., dendritic spine density and neurite complexity) following miRNA‐30a‐5p inhibition. Concomitantly, the levels of synaptic proteins (SYP, CaMKII, and PSD‐95) were substantially restored. Conversely, silencing of SOX4 expression reversed the ameliorative effects of miRNA‐30a‐5p inhibition on synaptic plasticity deficits. We performed additional bioinformatic analysis using TargetScan and miRDB to check for potential miR‐30a‐5p binding sites in the 3′UTRs of SYP, CaMKII, and PSD‐95. No conserved binding sites were identified, suggesting that these proteins are not direct targets of miR‐30a‐5p. These show that miRNA‐30a‐5p modulates epilepsy‐induced synaptic plasticity impairment by targeting SOX4.

  Related studies have shown that SOX4 is also expressed in glial cells. 39  Whether the expression of SOX4 in glial cells affects the occurrence of epilepsy is also an important question. However, due to time and funding constraints in this study, this issue was not further investigated. We will incorporate this inquiry into our future in‐depth research.

  The Wnt signaling pathway is an evolutionarily highly conserved cascade. In the central nervous system, this pathway is also involved in neurogenesis, neuronal differentiation, synaptic development, and plasticity regulation. 40 ,  41 ,  42  Accumulating evidence suggests that dysregulation of the Wnt/β‐catenin pathway may contribute to the development of chronic epilepsy and the pathogenesis of epilepsy‐associated pathological features such as hippocampal sclerosis. 43  Importantly, prior research has indicated that miRNA‐30a‐5p can activate the Wnt signaling pathway. 44  In the present study, protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis identified CTNNB1 (β‐catenin) as the gene most strongly associated with miRNA‐30a‐5p and epilepsy. Furthermore, KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that the Wnt signaling pathway is a key downstream pathway mediating the antiepileptic effects of miRNA‐30a‐5p. Since SOX4 has been identified as a direct target of miRNA‐30a‐5p, and previous studies have demonstrated that SOX4 can regulate β‐catenin, a key protein involved in the Wnt signaling pathway. 45  We proposed that miRNA‐30a‐5p regulates the Wnt/β‐catenin signaling pathway by targeting SOX4. Experimental validation showed that in Mg2+‐free neurons, the expression levels of key Wnt signaling pathway proteins (Wnt3a, β‐catenin, and cyclin D1) were upregulated as detected by WB, indicating that the Wnt signaling pathway was significantly activated in these neurons. Silencing the expression of miRNA‐30a‐5p significantly reduced the levels of these Wnt signaling pathway proteins (Wnt3a, β‐catenin, and cyclin D1), suggesting that miRNA‐30a‐5p activates the Wnt signaling pathway. Conversely, knockdown of SOX4 by siRNA abolished the inhibitory effect of the miR‐30a‐5p inhibitor on the Wnt signaling pathway, also indicating that SOX4 is involved in the Wnt signaling pathway, which is consistent with previous studies. 46  Together, these results demonstrate that miR‐30a‐5p regulates the Wnt/β‐catenin signaling pathway by targeting SOX4 (See Figure 11).

  Despite these key findings, the present study has certain limitations. First, although we confirmed that miRNA‐30a‐5p targets SOX4, the precise molecular relationship between SOX4 and the Wnt pathway requires further investigation. Second, our experiments only characterized the role of miRNA‐30a‐5p dysregulation in synaptic plasticity during the chronic phase of epilepsy. Future research will focus on exploring the expression patterns and functional roles of miRNA‐30a‐5p during the acute phase of epilepsy. Additionally, further studies are warranted to clarify the direct regulatory relationship between SOX4 and the Wnt/β‐catenin signaling pathway in the context of epilepsy, which will provide a more comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying the role of miRNA‐30a‐5p in epileptogenesis.


## CONCLUSIONS
  In summary, this study presents the first evidence that miRNA‐30a‐5p plays a crucial role in the disruption of neuronal synaptic plasticity induced by epilepsy. We confirmed a direct target interaction between miRNA‐30a‐5p and SOX4. Furthermore, our findings demonstrate that miRNA‐30a‐5p modulates the Wnt pathway and alleviates epilepsy‐induced synaptic plasticity deficits by targeting SOX4, thereby identifying the miRNA‐30a‐5p/SOX4 axis as a novel potential therapeutic target for delaying the progression of epilepsy.
