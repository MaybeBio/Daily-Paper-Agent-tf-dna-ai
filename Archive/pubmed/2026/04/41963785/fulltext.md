# Broad complex Z2 functions as an upstream 20E-responsive regulator of the midgut chitinase gene HaCHT4 in Helicoverpa armigera (Hübner) (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae)


## Background
  Chitinases and ecdysone are both crucial for insect growth and development, yet the regulatory interplay between them remains poorly understood. Our previous research demonstrated that the chitinase gene HaCHT4 critically regulates the chitin content and surface structure of peritrophic membrane (PM) in Helicoverpa armigera (Hübner) (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae).


## Results
  Here, a 1286 bp 5’-flanking region of HaCHT4 was cloned to investigate its regulatory mechanism. Bioinformatics analysis predicted the presence of several cis-regulatory elements (CREs) within its 5’-flanking region, including Broad-Complex Zinc-Finger isoforms (BRCs), GAGA, Nuclear Factors of activated T cells (NFAT), and POU domain factor (POU). Notably, genome-wide identification and characterization revealed that the BRC gene of H. armigera shared the highest similarity with that of Bombyx mori (L.) (Lepidoptera: Bombycidae). Molecular docking predicted that HaBRC Z2 exhibits a favorable binding energy (ΔG = -12 kcal/mol) with the sequence containing the BRC Z2 CRE, leading to its selection as the primary candidate for experimental validation. An electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) provided preliminary evidence that HaBRC Z2 might bind to the sequence containing BRC Z2 CRE of the HaCHT4 promoter region in vitro. Expression analysis showed concomitantly upregulated of HaBRC Z2 and HaCHT4 during the late stages of 4th and 5th instar larvae, and were also strongly induced by 20E. In addition, a substantial decrease in the transcript levels of both HaBRC Z2 and HaCHT4 was observed after HaBRC Z2 silencing for 24, 48, 72, and 96 h, and resulting in growth delay and reduced body length and weight.


## Conclusion
  Our results preliminarily suggested that HaBRC Z2 might act as a potential upstream regulator of HaCHT4, thereby regulating the larval molting process in H. armigera. However, direct promoter activation and in vivo occupancy remain to be experimentally validated.

## Background
  Helicoverpa armigera (Hübner) (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae), a polyphagous pest, inflicts substantial annual economic losses on many plants such as cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L.), corn (Zea mays L.), tobacco (Nicotiana tobacum L.), hot pepper (Capsicum frutescens L.), tomato (Lycopersicon esculentum Mill), and pea (Pisum sativum L.) [1–4]. The effective management of this pest remains a challenge due to its wide host range, polyphagous nature, high adaptability, and widespread pesticide resistance [5]. Although chemical pesticides have been widely used, they bring unintended consequences such as environmental contamination, human health threats, and devastation of beneficial non-target species [6, 7]. Another key strategy for H. armigera management is the cultivation of Bt crops owing to its remarkable efficacy, specificity towards target organisms, and environmental safety [8]. In China, Bt cotton (primarily the single-gene GFM Cry1A type) [9] has been widely adopted, accounting for approximately 95% of the national cotton cultivation area by 2018 [10]. Following the adjustment of China’s cotton cultivation structure, Xinjiang has become the dominant production area [11]. In 2025, the cotton planting area and output in Xinjiang accounted for 86.99% and 92.83% of the national totals, respectively [12]. Within this region, our 2024 monitoring of 643 sites revealed that Bt cotton constituted 95.80% of the local sown area, with conventional non-Bt cotton at only 4.20% [13]. These studies indicated that Bt cotton has become the cornerstone of the cotton production system in Xinjiang and across China. However, the evolution of resistance in H. armigera diminishes the benefits of Bt cotton [14]. Therefore, exploring effective and durable eco-friendly control strategies and molecular targets is crucial for delaying resistance evolution and advancing sustainable pest management. Previous work from our team demonstrated the critical role of the HaCHT4 in modulating chlorantraniliprole sensitivity in H. armigera, achieved through its regulation of chitin and architecture of the PM [15]. HaCHT4, which mediates chitin degradation, represents a candidate target for biorational insecticide discovery. Its functional relevance in insects, combined with the absence of chitin systems in vertebrates, offers a strategic avenue for developing pest management solutions with enhanced environmental compatibility.

  Chitin, a fundamental structural polymer in the PM, epidermis, intestine, and trachea, is precisely regulated by molecular interactions [16–18]. Among these processes, chitinase and ecdysone hormone 20-hydroxyecdysone (20E) play central roles [19, 20]. In insects, chitinases catalyze the degradation of high molecular weight chitin into low molecular weight oligosaccharides [21, 22], thus fulfilling a critical function in insect growth and development [23, 24]. The relative expression level of the chitinase gene is significantly upregulated before molting (late stages) and returns to normal or lower levels after molting (early stages) in insects, fluctuating with the physiological state similar to the changing trend of 20E titer [25–27]. The transcription levels of chitinase genes in Manduca sexta (L.) (Lepidoptera: Sphingidae) [28], Locusta migratoria (L.) (Orthoptera: Acrididae) [29], and Acyrthosiphon pisum (Harris) (Hemiptera: Aphididae) [23] were induced by 20E. In B. mori, metamorphosis was marked by 20E upregulating chitinase 5 expression via the transcription factor BmBRC-Z4 [30]. The expression level of HaCHT4 was lower in the early stages of 4th (4E) to 6th (6E) instar larvae, but significantly elevated in the late stages (4 L, 6 L) in H. armigera [15]. This implicated a specific 20E pathway in regulating chitinases during molting, although the precise mechanistic details remain to be fully elucidated.

  The functional diversity of the 20E responsive Broad-Complex (BRC) transcription factor stems from its alternative splicing, which generates isoforms with distinct C-terminal zinc finger domains [31, 32]. This gene is directly activated by the EcR-USP dimer, and possesses a conserved BTB domain alongside its variable DNA binding domain [33, 34]. Functional studies reveal isoform specific roles across insects [6, 35, 36]. In Drosophila melanogaster (Meigen) (Diptera: Drosophilidae), BRC Z1 and BRC Z2 have been shown to regulate appendage elongation (legs/wings) and eversion, whereas BRC Z3 was dedicated to the process of disc fusion [37]. In B. mori, BRC Z2 not only affected yolk protein synthesis and oocyte formation, but also regulated cuticle protein genes [38–40]. The function of BRC Z3 remained poorly characterized, and BRC Z4 mainly regulated genes encoding insect cuticular proteins and those genes related to chitin metabolism in B. mori [41]. Furthermore, RNAi-mediated silencing of LdBRC in Lymantria dispar (L.) (Lepidoptera: Erebidae) resulted in severely impaired chitinase gene expression, causing developmental defects and increased mortality, underscoring its essential role [42].

  Our previous work demonstrated that the midgut-specific chitinase gene HaCHT4 is involved in chitin degradation of H. armigera [15]. To elucidate its transcriptional regulation, in this study, we firstly cloned and analyzed the CREs within the 5’-flanking region of HaCHT4. Through a combination of EMSA, RT-qPCR, and RNAi, HaBRC Z2 was identified as a candidate upstream regulator of HaCHT4. These findings suggested a putative interaction between HaBRC Z2 and HaCHT4, which not only advanced our understanding of the molecular interplay between ecdysone signaling and chitin metabolism, but also provided a theoretical basis for devising novel pest management strategies targeting the chitin metabolic pathway.


## Materials and methods

### Insect rearing
  A laboratory-reared strain of H. armigera, with no history of any insecticide or toxin exposure, was maintained under controlled conditions of 26 ± 2 °C, 65 ± 5% relative humidity, and a 16:8 (L: D) photoperiod. The specific rearing details of H. armigera larvae followed the method described by Liang et al. [43].


### Cloning and characterization of 5’-flanking region of HaCHT4
  Genomic DNA of H. armigera 4th instar larvae was purified with the Easy Pure Genomic Kit (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). The 5’-flanking region of HaCHT4 (Genebank: scaffold_228, KZ150026, region: 175765–177265) was amplified from this DNA using the Genome Walking Kit (TaKaRa, Tokyo, Japan). The cloned fragment was then inserted into pEASY-T1 cloning vector. Subsequently, the positive monoclonals were sequenced by Sangon Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Putative CREs in the promoter region of HaCHT4 were predicted using the JASPAR database (https://jaspar.elixir.no/), with D. melanogaster selected as the reference model insect. The complete primer sequences and nested PCR reaction procedures were shown in Tables S1 and S2, respectively.


### Identification and bioinformatics analysis of the BTB domain-containing gene family
  We obtained the reference genome sequence of H. armigera (GCF_030705265.1) from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database, and employed two methods to identify potential members of the HaBTBs (the BTB domain-containing proteins identified in H. armigera). First, we performed BLASTP homology alignment against the local H. armigera database using the H. armigera HaBRC Z2 protein sequence as the query. Second, a hidden markov model (HMM) for the BTB domain (PF00651) was employed for a domain-based search against the H. armigera genome using TBtools. This profile was then utilized for the identification of HaBTBs from the H. armigera genomic database with the assistance of TBtools [44]. The candidate sequences obtained from both methods were validated for domain structure using Pfam, and only those sequences containing the conserved BTB domain were retained as HaBTB candidates. Protein parameters were derived from ExPASy (https://www.expasy.org/). The chromosomal locations of the HaBTB genes in H. armigera were obtained from the NCBI database, along with the corresponding genome annotation files. MapChart software was used to map the chromosomal position of HaBTB genes. The predicted BTB protein sequences from H. armigera, D. melanogaster, Tribolium castaneum (Herbst) (Coleoptera: Tenebrionidae), and B. mori were aligned using ClustalW with default parameters. Phylogenetic tree was constructed in MEGA 11.0 (neighbor-joining method, 1000 bootstrap replications) and refined using the iTOL online tool. All Figures were assembled and finalized in Adobe Illustrator 2022.


### Analysis of the molecular interaction between HaCHT4 and HaBRC Z2
  To explore the interaction between HaCHT4 and HaBRC Z2, the full-length amino acid sequence of HaBRC Z2 (XP_021199772.1, Fig. S1) and the sequence containing BRC Z2 CRE in 5’-flanking region of HaCHT4 (− 290 ~ − 255, 36 bp) were submitted to AlphaFold 3 (https://alphafold.com/) for molecular docking analysis. The DNA was specified as “DNA” molecule type with both strands provided in 5’ → 3’ orientation. No custom templates or multiple sequence alignments (MSAs) for DNA were supplied; the server automatically generated MSAs for the protein using its internal databases. The inference was performed using the AlphaFold 3 default parameters with 5 independent seeds to assess reproducibility. For each seed, 5 recycling iterations were applied. For the EMSA, a 309 bp DNA fragment containing the BRC Z2 CRE in the HaCHT4 promoter region (-313 ~ -5) was amplified by PCR and labeled with digoxigenin (DIG) using the DIG-High Prime DNA Labeling and Detection Starter Kit II (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. The His-HaBRC Z2 fusion protein had been purified in a previous experiment [45]. In the DNA-binding assay, two independent reaction groups were established, and each group was composed of 2.46 µM DIG-labeled probe, 2 µL binding buffer, and different amounts of His-HaBRC Z2 fusion protein (1 µg for Group 1 and 2 µg for Group 2; stock concentration: 0.63 µg/µL). The final volume of each reaction system was made up to 10 µL with ddH₂O. For the control group, an equal amount of His-PET32a protein was used to replace the His-HaBRC Z2 fusion protein, with all other components unchanged. The mixture was incubated at 25 °C for 30 min. Samples were electrophoresed on 5% polyacrylamide gel in 0.5× TBE buffer at 100 V for 1 h. Subsequently, the DNA-protein complexes were transferred to a positively charged nylon membrane, and UV crosslinking was performed for 15 min. The membrane was rinsed in washing buffer, then blocked with 1× blocking buffer for 30 min at room temperature. The membrane was incubated for 1 h with Anti-Digoxigenin-AP conjugate (Roche) diluted 1:10,000 in blocking solution. After three sequential washes (15 min each), the membrane was equilibrated in detection buffer for 5 min. The membrane was placed on a development folder, and 1 mL of CSPD chemiluminescent substrate was applied onto the membrane. After incubation for 5 min at room temperature, excess liquid was drained, and the membrane was sealed [30]. Signal detection was performed using the Clinx Science imaging system.


### The expression profiles of HaBRC Z2 and HaCHT4 in H. armigera
  To characterize the transcript levels of HaBRC Z2 and HaCHT4 in H. armigera, midgut tissue samples were collected at both the early and late stages of the 4th and 5th instar larvae for RNA extraction. For each developmental stage, 3 biological replicates were prepared, with each replicate comprising a pool of midgut tissues from ten larvae. The cDNA synthesis and RT-qPCR were conducted as previously described [15]. To investigate whether 20E regulates the expression of HaBRC Z2 and HaCHT4, early stage of 4th instar larvae were treated with 5 µL of 20E (200 ng/µL; supplied by Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China, dilute with anhydrous ethanol), corresponding to a total dose of 1.0 µg per larva. Control larvae were injected with the equal volume (5 µL) of anhydrous ethanol and ddH₂O (CK), respectively. Midgut tissues were dissected 24 h post treatment to analyze the expression levels of the target genes. The experiment included 3 biological replicates, with each replicate comprising 30 larvae. The 2−ΔΔCt method [46] was used to quantify the relative expression levels of HaBRC Z2 and HaCHT4, with β-actin serving as the reference gene. 3 biological and 2 technical replicates were used for all reactions. The amplification efficiency of HaBRC Z2, HaCHT4, and β-actin was 93.76, 94.27 and 94.89, and all dissociation curves showed a single peak (Fig. S2).


### Synthesis of dsRNA and RNAi assays
  To investigate the function of the HaBRC Z2 gene, double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) targeting its open reading frame (ORF) was designed, green fluorescent protein (GFP) serving as a control. To ensure the specificity of RNA interference, we performed sequence alignment among the annotated HaBRC Z1 – Z4 and two other putative BRC isoforms in the H. armigera genome (the BRC Z3 isoform has not yet been identified in either B. mori or H. armigera). The alignment showed that the 1 ~ 539 bp region of the HaBRC Z2 ORF is highly conserved among HaBRC Z1 and HaBRC Z4 isoforms. Accordingly, we designed and synthesized a 396 bp dsRNA targeting a specific region of HaBRC Z2 (positions 601 ~ 996 bp) to minimize potential off‑target effects on other BRC isoforms. The dsRNA sequence was subjected to BLAST search against the H. armigera transcriptome to confirm minimal sequence similarity to non-target genes (Fig. S3). Subsequently, dsRNAs were synthesized in vitro employing the T7 RiboMAX™ Express RNAi System (Promega, Madison, USA) (Fig. S3).

  For RNAi bioassay, the artificial diet was meticulously divided into rectangular pieces measuring 4 mm×4 mm×3 mm, which were then allocated to each group. Newly hatched larvae of H. armigera were fed an artificial diet formulated to nourish them until the 2nd instar. On the first day of the 2nd instar, larvae exhibiting consistent growth and development were selected and subjected to starvation treatment for 4 h. Two groups received 10 µL of dsHaBRC Z2 solution at concentrations of 250 ng/µL and 500 ng/µL, respectively. The dsRNA-treated diet was refreshed every 48 h intervals. Control groups were administered an equivalent volume of either ddH₂O or dsGFP solution. Each treatment was applied to 35 larvae, with 3 independent biological replicates established. To evaluate the silencing efficiency of HaBRC Z2, 5 larvae (whole body) from each group were sampled at 12 h (early stage of 2nd instar), 24 h (early stage of 2nd instar), 48 h (middle stage of 2nd instar), 72 h (late stage of 2nd instar), and 96 h (early stage of 3rd instar) for RT-qPCR analysis.

  To further assess the impact of dsHaBRC Z2 on larval phenotypic traits, a separate cohort of 2nd instar larvae were fed a diet containing 10 µL of dsHaBRC Z2 at 500 ng/µL. Control groups were treated with ddH₂O or dsGFP as described above. Larval body length and weight were recorded at 12 h (early stage of 2nd instar), 24 h (early stage of 2nd instar), 36 h (middle stage of 2nd instar), 48 h (middle stage of 2nd instar), 60 h (late stage of 2nd instar), 72 h (late stage of 2nd instar), 84 h (early stage of 3rd instar), and 96 h (early stage of 3rd instar) after treatment initiation. This experiment included 3 biological replicates, with 35 larvae in each group per replicate. As previously reported [47], the 2nd instar of H. armigera typically lasts 3.23 ± 0.98 d, meaning larvae remained within the same instar throughout the 72 h sampling period of our RNAi experiments, avoiding confounding effects from molting.


### Statistical analysis
  All data from 3 experimental replicates were presented as mean ± standard error (SE). Prior to statistical analysis, normality was assessed using the Shapiro ‑ Wilk test and homogeneity of variance was examined using Levene’s test. For data satisfying both normality and homogeneity of variance assumptions, parametric tests were applied; otherwise, appropriate non‑parametric tests (Mann‑Whitney U test or Kruskal‑Wallis test) were used. For gene expression analysis across the 4E to 5 L instars, differences between early and late stages were assessed using independent-samples Student’s t-test (* P < 0.05, ** P < 0.01, *** P < 0.001). For 20E and RNAi data (silencing efficiency, body length, and body weight), one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) coupled with Tukey’s HSD test in SPSS 22.0 was used to evaluate differences among treatment groups at identical time points, where different letters mark significant differences (P < 0.05). All statistical tests were strictly performed using biological replicates (n = 3 per group), with technical replicates averaged to generate a single value per biological replicate.


## Results

### Characterization of the 5’-flanking region from the HaCHT4
  To investigate the transcriptional regulation of HaCHT4, the 5’-flanking region of HaCHT4 was cloned by genome walking (Fig. 1A) and subcloned into the pEASY-T1 vector for sequencing (Fig. 1B). Sequence analysis revealed that a 1286 bp 5’-flanking region was obtained. Using D. melanogaster as the reference species in the JASPAR database, putative CREs within this promoter region were predicted. The analysis identified binding sites for several transcription factors, including BRC Z1 - Z4, GAGA, NFAT, and POU domain factor (Fig. 1C-D; Table S3). The presence of BRC response elements prompted us to systematically characterize the BRC gene in H. armigera. We first performed a genome‑wide identification of the BTB domain‑containing protein family, to which BRC belongs, and established a systematic nomenclature based on domain architecture and chromosomal location (Table S4, Fig. S4). A collinearity plot was constructed for H. armigera, B. mori, T. castaneum, and D. melanogaster. This analysis identified 48 orthologs between H. armigera and B. mori, 2 orthologs between H. armigera and T. castaneum, and no orthologs between H. armigera and D. melanogaster. These results suggested that BTB genes are relatively conserved during the evolution of lepidoptera insects (Fig. 2). Among the 85 HaBTB genes identified, the gene designated HaBTBZF1 (LOC110383294) contains both a BTB domain and a C2H2 zinc finger domain. Subsequent NCBI BLAST and examination of the H. armigera genome annotation files revealed that this gene is officially annotated as broad‑complex core protein isoforms 1/2/3/4/5, which corresponds to the BRC gene in H. armigera (Fig. 3). This gene features eight exons, and can generate distinct transcripts through alternative splicing. To clarify the evolutionary relationship of BRC isoforms, previously reported different BRC isoforms from other insect species were downloaded. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that each of the BRC isoforms fell within its clade, which is consistent with the previously observed evolutionary conservation of these isoforms (Fig. 4).

### HaBRC Z2 protein bound to the BRC Z2 CRE of HaCHT4
  Molecular docking analysis was performed to predict the binding affinity between HaBRC isoforms and the CREs within the promoter region of HaCHT4. The analysis predicted that HaBRC Z2, among all isoforms, exhibited the strongest binding capacity to the sequence harboring the putative BRC Z2 CRE. This interaction was mediated by multiple amino acid residues (R199, S222, R389, C392, S393), with a predicted binding free energy (ΔG) of -12 kcal/mol (Fig. 5A, Fig. S5). Based on this in silico prediction, HaBRC Z2 was selected as the primary candidate for subsequent experimental validation. Moreover, a 309 bp DNA fragment from the HaCHT4 promoter region encompassing the 5’-AACTAATT-3’ (BRC Z2 CRE) was amplified (Fig. 5B) to experimentally validate this interaction. The probe labeling efficiency was validated, showing high sensitivity 10 pg/µL (Fig. 5C). EMSA analysis revealed a specific band shift in the presence of the His-HaBRC Z2 fusion protein, in contrast to the probe-only and His-PET32a empty vector control groups (Fig. 5D). These results preliminarily suggested that HaBRC Z2 might bind to the sequence containing the BRC Z2 CRE from the HaCHT4 promoter region in vitro and serve as a potential upstream regulator of HaCHT4.

### Expression profiles of HaBRC Z2 and HaCHT4 in H. armigera
  We profiled the transcript levels of HaBRC Z2 and HaCHT4 across the 4th to 5th instar larval stages by RT-qPCR. Both genes exhibited significant upregulation in the late stage of each instar compared with the early stage (P < 0.01). Specifically, the expression of HaBRC Z2 was upregulated by approximately 5.01-fold (t = 5.679, df = 4, P = 0.0047) and 2.19-fold (t = 4.461, df = 4, P = 0.0112) in the 4 L and 5 L instar stages, respectively (Fig. 6A). And HaCHT4 expression levels showed a more pronounced induction, with 10.30-fold (t = 9.104, df = 4, P = 0.008) and 3.70-fold (t = 4.672, df = 4, P = 0.009), increasing at the same stages, respectively (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, significant accumulation of HaBRC Z2 (F (2, 15) = 14.988, P < 0.001) and HaCHT4 (F (2, 15) = 36.212, P < 0.001) mRNA was detected in larvae treated with 1.0 µg 20E for 24 h, compared with the anhydrous ethanol and CK groups (Fig. 6C-D). The coordinated expression patterns suggested that HaBRC Z2 may act as a potential upstream regulator of HaCHT4.

### Functional analysis of the HaBRC Z2 using RNAi
  To investigate the putative regulatory role of HaBRC Z2 on HaCHT4, we performed RNAi-mediated silencing of HaBRC Z2. The transcript levels of both genes remained unchanged at 12 h post-treatment with dsHaBRC Z2 under 250 (F (2, 15) = 0.236, P = 0.793) or 500 ng/µL (F (2, 15) = 1.736, P = 0.210) compared with the dsGFP group. A significant suppression of both genes was observed from 24 to 96 h. The expression level of HaBRC Z2 decreased to 23.50% (F (2, 15) = 131.76, P < 0.001) of that in the dsGFP group after treatment with 250 ng/µL dsHaBRC Z2 for 24 h, and the expression of HaCHT4 decreased to 18.45% (F (2, 15) = 52.30, P < 0.001). Under 500 ng/µL for 24 h treatment, the expression levels of HaBRC Z2 and HaCHT4 were reduced to 56.52% (F (2, 15) = 14.76, P < 0.001) and 40.11% (F (2, 15) = 33.59, P = 0.008) of the control group, respectively. By 96 h, the expression of HaBRC Z2 in the 250 ng/µL treatment group had decreased to 31.70% of the dsGFP group (F (2, 15) = 55.499, P < 0.001), and HaCHT4 expression decreased to 11.45% (F (2, 15) = 67.05, P < 0.001), the mRNA expression of HaBRC Z2 was reduced to 26.58% (F (2, 15) = 54.393, P < 0.001), and HaCHT4 expression decreased to 28.24% (F (2, 15) = 60.638, P = 0.002) after treatment with 500 ng/µL dsHaBRC Z2 (Fig. 7).

### Effects of HaBRC Z2 silencing on the growth and development of H. armigera
  We also assessed the impact of HaBRC Z2 silencing on larval growth and development. The results showed that the body length and weight of H.armigera were significantly inhibited after treatment with 500 ng/µL of dsHaBRC Z2 for 48 h, compared with the control groups. The average body length in the dsHaBRC Z2 treated group was 6.53 ± 0.15 mm, which was significantly shorter than both the dsGFP (7.60 ± 0.20 mm) and the CK (7.73 ± 0.23 mm) groups at 48 h (Fig. 8A, Table S4). With the extension of treatment time, the inhibitory effect on larval body weight was further exacerbated. From 48 h post-treatment onwards, the body weight of larvae in the dsHaBRC Z2 group accounted for 68.08% of that in the dsGFP group at 48 h (F (2, 6) = 48.292, P < 0.001), 70.51% at 60 h (F (2, 6) = 12.242, P = 0.008), 73.27% at 72 h (F (2, 6) = 11.975, P = 0.009), 59.96% at 84 h (F (2, 6) = 16.851, P = 0.003), and 53.90% at 96 h (F (2, 6) = 58.654, P < 0.001) (Fig. 8B, Table S5). This indicated that HaBRC Z2 knockdown severely impaired larval development.

## Discussion
  The midgut is a primary organ for food digestion, nutrient absorption, and energy metabolism in insects, and its structure undergoes significant remodeling throughout insect development [48]. Within it lies the PM, a non-cellular, semipermeable barrier synthesized from chitin, proteins, and glycans [49, 50]. This structure acts as the primary defensive barrier of the midgut, protecting the midgut epithelium from mechanical abrasion and preventing invasion by pathogenic microorganisms and exogenous macromolecules [51, 52]. However, this protective barrier also impedes the uptake of pesticides and entomopathogenic microorganisms in pest control. Consequently, disrupting PM structural integrity to improve the bioavailability of pest control agents has become a prominent research focus in insect pest management [53]. Given that chitin is not present in mammals or plants [19], the specific regulation of chitin synthesis and degradation pathways is developing into a new strategy for pest control. In Ostrinia furnacalis (Guenée) (Lepidoptera: Crambidae), group II chitinase gene OfChtII could degrade the cuticular chitin and regulate larval-pupal metamorphosis [54]. In Aedes albopictus (Skuse) (Diptera: Culicidae), the structure of the midgut PM was destroyed or even absent after silencing CHS-2 [55]. Our previous research in H. armigera demonstrated that overexpression of HaCHT4 enhances chitin degradation of the PM, leading to increased PM porosity. This structural compromise severely impairs the gut barrier, which in turn significantly increases larval susceptibility to chlorantraniliprole [15]. To build on this finding, we sought to identify the transcription factor responsible for regulating this pathway. Nevertheless, the upstream signaling cascades and transcriptional regulators controlling this chitinase gene have not yet been fully elucidated.

  To elucidate the upstream regulatory mechanism of HaCHT4, we cloned and analyzed its 5’-flanking region. Multiple CREs were identified, including binding sites for BRC Z1-Z4 isoforms, GAGA, NFAT, and POU domain factor. The BRC gene, a 20E-responsive transcription factor belonging to the BTB domain-containing protein family, is a key regulator of insect growth and development [38]. Its function was exemplified in B. mori, where BRC transmits the 20E signal by directly associating with the promoter regions of genes critical for larval development [56, 57]. Meanwhile, in Spodoptera frugiperda (Smith) (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae), the isoform BRC Z4 has been reported to modulate gene expression by interacting with the 5’-flanking region of targets such as Ecdysone receptor and SfCht5, potentially leading to the upregulation of these and other ecdysteroid biosynthesis genes in berberine-fed insects [58].

  Building on the genomic insight that identified numerous conserved BTB homologs between H. armigera and B. mori, we performed molecular docking as an initial screening tool to prioritize BRC isoforms for experimental validation. Molecular docking predicted that HaBRC Z2 exhibited the strongest binding affinity to the sequence harboring the putative BRC Z2 CRE, which shares homology with the BRC Z2 consensus elements “CTA” and “TAG” characterized in B. mori [59]. Based on this in silico prediction, HaBRC Z2 was selected as the primary candidate for further investigation. Multiple experimental results subsequently supported this putative regulatory relationship. First, EMSA provided preliminary evidence that recombinant His-HaBRC Z2 might bind to labeled probes containing BRC Z2 CRE motif. Second, transcript levels of HaBRC Z2 and HaCHT4 peaked at the 4L and 5L larval instars compared with the early stages. This expression pattern is consistent with previous studies in B. mori [30] and Chilo suppressalis (Walker) (Lepidoptera: Crambidae) [60], where chitinase gene expression was shown to peak in the late larval and prepupal stages. Additionally, our RT-qPCR assay showed that both HaBRC Z2 and HaCHT4 were induced by 20E, further suggesting that HaBRC Z2 might act as a candidate upstream regulator of HaCHT4. Taken together, we proposed a putative regulatory model: 20E signaling might orchestrates HaCHT4 expression by inducing HaBRC Z2, which might subsequently interact with the BRC Z2 CRE in the HaCHT4 promoter. However, several complementary experiments are necessary to fully confirm this regulatory mechanism. For example, firstly, building upon the EMSA experiments already conducted, supplementary experiments using unlabeled competitor probes and binding site-mutated probes should be performed to demonstrate the specificity of HaBRC Z2 binding to the 5’-AACTAATT-3’ motif; Secondly, chromatin immunoprecipitation-quantitative PCR (ChIP-qPCR) assays should be conducted to directly verify whether the HaBRC Z2 protein is specifically enriched at the 5’-flanking region of HaCHT4; Thirdly, a dual-luciferase reporter assay should be performed to confirm the transcriptional activation activity of HaBRC Z2 on the HaCHT4 promoter; Finally, following HaBRC Z2 knockdown via RNAi, larvae could be treated with 20E or subjected to HaBRC Z2 protein rescue expression. Subsequently, the recovery of HaCHT4 expression and the corresponding molting phenotypes (e.g., chitinase activity, molting success) should be systematically examined.

  RNA interference (RNAi) has become an indispensable tool for elucidating gene function and formulating targeted pest control strategies in entomological research because of its high specificity, simple technology and ability to post-transcriptionally silence gene expression [61, 62]. For instance, silencing CsBRC genes in C. suppressalis increased pupal instar number and significantly prolonged the developmental duration [60]. Similarly, knockdown of LdBRC in late 4th instar of L. dispar larvae inhibited chitinase expression in midgut and integument tissues, causing molting arrest and 67% larval mortality [42]. In addition, our research team has employed RNAi to investigate the functions of genes associated with growth, development, and insecticide resistance in Aphis gossypii (Glover) (Hemiptera: Aphididae), H. armigera, and Phthorimaea absoluta (Meyrick) (Lepidoptera: Gelechiidae), which has facilitated the identification of potential targets for the biocontrol of these pests [55, 63–65]. In this study, RNAi-mediated silencing of HaBRC Z2 significantly suppressed its transcript levels, and the optimal knockdown efficacy occurred at 24 h and 96 h. The transcript levels of both HaBRC Z2 and HaCHT4 reached the lowest at 24 h following treatment with 250 ng/µL dsHaBRC Z2 and this suppression persisted through 96 h. In contrast, statistically significant inhibition of larval body length and weight did not emerge until after 48 h (Fig. 8). These results suggested that HaBRC Z2 played a significant role in the growth and development of H. armigera larvae, potentially through regulating the expression of HaCHT4. Although our temporal data support a regulatory relationship, we cannot fully exclude the possibility that HaBRC Z2 knockdown may disrupt the broader molting hormone signaling cascade, thereby indirectly affecting HaCHT4 expression. Future research will adopt more precise hourly-stage divisions and monitor other molting response genes (such as HR3, FTZ-F1) throughout the developmental stage, which will help clarify the exact pathway.


## Conclusions
  In summary, our findings preliminarily suggested that HaBRC Z2 might function as a candidate upstream regulator of HaCHT4 by transducing the 20E signal. Additionally, RNAi-mediated silencing of HaBRC Z2 indicated that it also played a crucial role in the growth and development of H. armigera. These findings will further deepen our understanding of the regulatory relationship underlying ecdysone and chitin metabolism, but also provide a novel molecular target for the development of environmentally friendly control strategies for H. armigera.
