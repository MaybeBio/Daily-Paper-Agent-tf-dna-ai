# CaHoT-GRN: context-aware high-order topology learning for robust single-cell gene regulatory network inference


## Abstract
  Cells regulate their functions through gene expression, driven by a complex interplay of transcription factors (TFs) and other regulatory mechanisms that together can be modeled as gene regulatory networks (GRNs). While the advent of single-cell sequencing has revolutionized our understanding of these networks, current GRNs inference methods rely predominantly on expression data alone, overlooking the sequence semantic context of target genes, and the intrinsic physicochemical properties of TFs. Consequently, the reconstructed networks are often riddled with false-positive connections, significantly compromising their reliability. To address these challenges, we propose CaHoT-GRN, a context-aware high-order topology learning framework for robust single-cell GRNs inference. First, we leverage pretrained biological large language models to extract deep semantic embeddings from gene and protein sequences. This allows the model to explore the potential TF-target binding affinity within a latent semantic space. Second, to model cooperative regulatory mechanisms and capture high-order gene interactions, we construct a heterogeneous information network (HIN) via meta-path generation constrained by protein–protein interactions. Furthermore, we propose a similarity co-attention module to model the topological consistency between the prior GRNs and the HIN, thereby capturing long-range associations among genes. On single-cell transcriptomic datasets across four types of networks, CaHoT-GRN yielded an average AUC of 0.846 and an AUPR of 0.420, matching or outperforming existing methods. Moreover, downstream case studies, pathway analyses, and motif matching confirmed its high biological relevance. CaHoT-GRN is publicly available at https://github.com/ydkvictory/CaHoT-GRN.


## Introduction
  The interplay between transcription factors (TFs) and regulatory elements (REs) drives gene transcription, forming gene regulatory networks (GRNs) [1]. Understanding the regulatory mechanisms within these GRNs is pivotal for elucidating how cellular identities are established and maintained. Mapping the GRNs that drive disease progression enables screening for molecules that correct the network by normalizing core REs, rather than targeting peripheral downstream effectors that may not be disease modifying [2].

  Recent advances in single-cell sequencing technologies have enabled the exploration of gene regulatory processes at single-cell resolution [3, 4]. The accumulation of large-scale single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data and the curation of databases such as TRRUST have facilitated the development of both supervised and unsupervised learning methods for GRNs inference [5–8]. Machine learning-based approaches like GENIE3 [9] and GRNBoost [10] leverage tree-based ensemble methods specifically random forests and gradient boosting to capture gene expression correlations, thereby establishing efficient frameworks for GRNs inference. DigNet leverages discrete diffusion generative models to infer cell-specific GRNs from scRNA-seq data [11]. PIDC [12] pioneered the application of multivariate information theory to infer GRNs from scRNA-seq data, enabling differentiation between direct and indirect associations. Normi [13] innovatively introduces non-redundant information theory and sliding window averaging to mitigate technical noise in scRNA-seq data. CVGAE [14] utilizes FastICA and multi-stacked GraphSAGE to capture global associations among genes. Unsupervised GRNs inference primarily captures statistical associations among genes. Consequently, these methods often fail to elucidate true causal regulation, as they struggle to distinguish direct interactions from indirect correlations and technical noise.

  Supervised learning methods achieve causal modeling of TF–target interactions by integrating scRNA-seq data with known regulatory relationships as prior knowledge, significantly enhancing model prediction accuracy. By integrating diverse inferred networks to augment the network topology, graph transformer (GT)-GRN leverages a GT to accurately discriminate latent TF–target gene pairs within the GRNs [15]. GRNPT [16] and TransGRN [17] utilize prompt engineering and text mining techniques to extract textual knowledge regarding gene functions. By integrating these unified feature representations, they enhance the generalizability of GRNs inference models. DGRNS [18] and MetaSEM [19] achieve precise GRNs inference by integrating various deep learning techniques with structural equation modeling. GAEDGRN [20] captures complex directed topological relationships within GRNs by computing pairwise gravitational relationships between genes and integrating them with a random walk algorithm. AnomalGRN [21] addresses the sample imbalance problem in GRNs by reformulating the link prediction task as a graph anomaly detection problem. Formally, GRNs are modeled as networks composed of nodes and edges, where nodes correspond to genes and TFs, and edges represent regulatory interactions [22]. Consequently, graph learning has found widespread application in GRNs inference. Specifically, methods like MHHGRN [23] Hypergraph-VAE [24] and scHyperLink [25] utilize hypergraph structures to explore many-to-many associations between TFs and target genes, thereby capturing high-order regulatory patterns. To further investigate causal relationships between TFs and target genes, methods such as GRACE [26], DGCRL [27], DGCGRN [28], and RegGAIN [29] utilize directed graph representations. These approaches achieve a precise depiction of TF–target regulatory associations, resulting in more interpretable predictions of unknown links. To fully leverage the advantages of graph learning, LineGRN [30] and AutoGRN [31] utilize linear and adaptive graph learning frameworks, respectively, for GRNs inference. In particular, AutoGRN can generate graph learning architectures tailored to specific datasets, thereby significantly enhancing model adaptability. Methods such as scMGATGRN [32], KA4GANC [33], GATCL [34], GeneLink+ [35], GRANet [36], Q-GAT [37], and ATFGRN [38] utilize graph attention mechanisms to explore node neighborhoods. Furthermore, these approaches incorporate advanced strategies including multi-view learning, Kolmogorov–Arnold Networks, CNN-based attention, and graph residual networks—to mitigate issues like node feature over-smoothing in graph neural networks (GNNs). KEGNI [39] and HGTCGRN [40] leverage known systems biology knowledge to construct supplementary topological structures, aiming to mitigate the sparsity issue in GRNs. Consequently, they generate high-order topologies of greater biological significance than those produced by DuCGRN. MTLGRN [41], scTFBridge [2], and GT-GRN integrate multi-modal gene information to construct multi-view gene representations. By incorporating richer biological context into the model, these methods achieve refined GRN prediction.

  Despite recent advancements, existing methods face two major limitations: they focus solely on isolated TF–target links while ignoring upstream regulatory contexts, and they fail to capture long-range synergistic interactions among functionally similar genes. Addressing these gaps by integrating global contextual information is crucial for inferring stable and reliable GRNs [42]. To transcend the limitations of relying solely on expression data, we define GRNs inference as a multimodal fusion task. Our model integrates target DNA sequences, TFs protein sequences, and single-cell gene expression profiles, contextualized by a prior PPI network. By bridging micro-scale sequence features with macro-scale expression patterns, the framework utilizes fused node representations to predict the GRNs, identifying the regulatory strengths between TFs and target genes. This strategy, which bridges upstream biological features with downstream processes, has proven to be an effective approach for enhancing the robustness of network inference [43–45].

## Materials and methods

### Datasets
  The scRNA-seq datasets we used are from BEELINE [22], which is a comprehensive evaluation framework for inferring GRNs from scRNA-seq data. We use a total of seven different datasets to evaluate CaHoT-GRN: (i) human embryonic stem cells (hESC); (ii) human mature hepatocytes (hHEP); (iii) mouse dendritic cells (mDC); (iv) mouse embryonic stem cells (mESC); (v) mouse hematopoietic stem cells with erythroid-lineage (mHSC-E); (vi) mouse hematopoietic stem cells with granulocyte–monocyte-lineage (mHSC-GM); (vii) mouse hematopoietic stem cells with lymphoid-lineage (mHSC-L). Aforementioned datasets are all with three ground-truth networks from functional interaction networks documented in STRING database [7], non-specific ChIP-seq [5, 6, 46], and cell-type-specific ChIP-seq [6, 47, 48]. For the datasets of these seven kinds of cells, there are known basic network databases that can be used as a reference to verify and evaluate the accuracy and reliability of the prediction algorithm. We extract the same type of cells from the above three ground-truth network databases. For the mESC, there is also a loss-of-function/gain-of-function (LOF/GOF) ground-truth network available [49]. In conclusion, we respectively verify the performance of CaHoT-GRN on four ground-truth networks. The seven scRNA-seq datasets can be downloaded from Gene Expression Omnibus with the accession numbers GSE81252 (hHEP), GSE75748 (hESC), GSE98664(mESC), GSE48968 (mDC), and GSE81682 (mHSC). All above single-cell datasets with four different kinds of ground-truth networks are available at Zenodo.


### Overview of CaHoT-GRN
  CaHoT-GRN integrates multi-modal gene data for accurate GRNs inference as illustrated in Fig. 1. The framework primarily consists of three stage. The first stage consists mainly of the sequence feature extraction module, the second stage consists of the PPI-based high-order neighbor construction module, the similarity co-attention module, the third stage GRNs reconstruction module.

  The sequence feature extraction module utilizes the pretrained large language model DNABERT [50] to encode DNA sequences into DNA sequence features, and employs the ESM [51] model to encode the protein sequences derived from these genes into protein sequence features. Specifically, DNA sequence features effectively capture upstream GRN regulatory information, such as promoters, enhancers, and repressors, while protein sequence features characterize the downstream potential for functional execution. Ultimately, the integration of DNA sequence features, transcriptomic expression features, and protein sequence features jointly constitutes a multi-modal GRNs context specific to the cellular environment, facilitating the further exploration of comprehensive GRNs.

  Proteins execute diverse functions across different biological contexts. Notably, PPI networks effectively capture the contextual functional information of genes, encompassing biological pathways, complex activation mechanisms, and signal transduction processes [52, 53]. A critical limitation of global PPI networks is their inability to capture cell-type-specific dynamics. Integrating scRNA-seq with PPI data to construct GRNs effectively bridges dynamic expression profiles with a reliable functional context. This integration enriches the contextual information of the GRNs, thereby enhancing the biological significance of the inference [54]. To this end, we constructed the PPI-based high-order neighbor construction module, which comprises two key components: meta-path extraction and a PPI network filter. Specifically, the former captures second-order neighborhood information derived from the existing GRNs, while the latter filters this information to construct a potential high-order GRNs corroborated by existing evidence. This module serves to enrich the sparse network structures inherent in current datasets, effectively improving the precision of the topological graph and facilitating the exploration of latent associations between TFs and target genes.

  The similarity co-attention module calculates both intra-view and inter-view similarities between features derived from the GRNs and HIN constructed via the PPI-based high-order neighbor construction module. By leveraging three similarity matrices to determine node-wise similarity weights, this module effectively addresses long-range node dependencies across different networks, thereby further enhancing the accuracy of prediction tasks.

  In the GRN reconstruction module, the gene expression features, DNA sequence features, and protein sequence features generated by the similarity co-attention module are processed through an MLP layer. Subsequently, a dot product operation is employed to calculate the associations coefficients between nodes, thereby predicting potential GRNs.


### Sequence feature extraction module
  Gene DNA and protein sequences were sourced from the NCBI nucleotide [55] and UniProt [56] databases, respectively, matching the gene names in the scRNA-seq dataset. We utilized ESM and DNABERT foundation models to transform these sequences into high-dimensional embeddings. Due to computational constraints and the strategy to let downstream modules learn task-specific representations, the parameters of ESM and DNABERT were frozen.

## Results

### Performance comparison with baseline methods
  To comprehensively evaluate the performance of CaHoT-GRN, we benchmarked it against seven baseline methods, including GeneLink+, scMGATGRN, GCLink, GATCL, GENELink [59], GNE [60], and DeepRIG [61]. Our experiments spanned seven datasets (hESC, hHEP, mDC, mESC, mHSC-E, mHSC-GM, and mHSC-L) under two different scales (TFs + 500 and TFs + 1000) and utilized four types of ground-truth networks (STRING, Non-specific ChIP-seq, cell-type-specific ChIP-seq, and LOF/GOF). To ensure statistical robustness, we conducted 10 independent tests across these 44 experimental scenarios and reported the average AUROC and AUPR. For a fair comparison, we adopted the default parameters provided in the original implementations of all baseline methods. We reported the average scores across independent test sets and performed paired two-sample t-tests to verify the statistical significance of the performance gap between CaHoT-GRN and each baseline method, as detailed in Tables 1 and 2. The experimental results demonstrate that CaHoT-GRN outperforms other competing methods. Table 1 summarizes the GRNs prediction performance in terms of AUROC. Specifically, on the TFs+500 datasets, CaHoT-GRN achieves average improvements of 3.4%, 3%, 2.7%, 1.8%, 3%, 14.9%, and 28.2% over GeneLink+, scMGATGRN, GCLink, GATCL, GENELink, GNE, and DeepRIG, respectively. Similarly, Table 2 presents the performance summary based on AUPRC. On the TFs+500 datasets, CaHoT-GRN outperforms GeneLink+, scMGATGRN, GCLink, GATCL, GENELink, and GNE by average margins of 4.5%, 6.7%, 3.5%, 2.7%, 7.8%, 19.7%, and 18.1%, respectively.

  The superior performance of CaHoT-GRN stems from the innovative integration of the sequence feature extraction module, the PPI-based high-order neighbor construction module, and the similarity co-attention module, which collectively enable a thorough exploration of the complex regulatory relationships between TFs and target genes.

  Furthermore, comparative analysis with existing graph-based models reveals several key advantages: First, compared with GeneLink+, CaHoT-GRN’s GRN-based context construction module effectively mitigates the issue of node feature over-smoothing. Second, unlike the view-level attention mechanism in scMGATGRN, the similarity co-attention mechanism in CaHoT-GRN specifically focuses on extracting strong TFs–target association features, offering enhanced interpretability. Third, in contrast to the contrastive subgraph approach employed by GCLink, our PPI-based high-order neighbor construction generates high-order gene neighborhoods with greater biological significance.

  Collectively, these findings underscore the critical importance of PPI-based high-order exploration, topology-level feature fusion, and context-aware feature extraction in regulatory network inference, providing a robust framework for reconstructing GRNs from scRNA-seq data.


### Ablation study
  To further validate the impact of distinct modules on the global performance of CaHoT-GRN, we conducted a series of ablation studies by systematically removing or replacing the model’s key components. Specifically, we constructed three model variants to analyze their respective contributions, as detailed in Table 3.

  Specifically, we constructed three variants: CaHoT-GRN w/o SFE, which excludes the sequence feature extraction module and relies solely on the raw gene expression matrix as input. CaHoT-GRN w/o PPI-HON, which removes the PPI-based high-order neighbor generation, performing feature aggregation using only the provided GRN topology. CaHoT-GRN w/o S Co-A, which replaces the similarity co-attention mechanism with a standard MLP layer, resulting in a shallow fusion of the dual topological features without capturing complex inter-view interactions.

  To further assess the generalization ability and robustness of CaHoT-GRN, we conducted ablation studies across seven scRNA-seq datasets under three distinct ground-truth networks. For each dataset, all ablation experiments and their corresponding variants were conducted using strictly identical hyperparameter settings. The results are illustrated in Fig. 2. It is important to note that each AUROC and AUPR value plotted represents the average performance calculated across the seven independent subdatasets for the corresponding network architecture.

  As observed, when CaHoT-GRN relies solely on the gene expression matrix for input (w/o SFE), the absence of upstream and downstream contextual information from the GRN hinders the modeling of global gene features. Consequently, the model struggles to capture complex regulatory relationships, resulting in suboptimal prediction performance. In parallel, GRNs inference was performed using protein and DNA sequence features independently. The findings, presented in Supplementary Table S1, clearly demonstrate the necessity of incorporating sequence information.

  A significant degradation in model performance is observed upon removing the PPI-based high-order neighbor construction module (w/o PPI-HON). This decline can be attributed to the fact that the PPI-HON module effectively captures indirect associations between TFs and target genes. Furthermore, it incorporates the biological mechanism where TFs form protein complexes to facilitate transcriptional activation, thereby enriching the structural and functional context of the GRN and improving prediction accuracy. We also evaluated the generation of PPI-HON using either intersection or union approaches in our ablation study. Detailed results are provided in Supplementary Table S2. These results demonstrate that capturing multi-level regulatory associations and hierarchical dependencies is indispensable for robust GRNs inference.

  The experimental results clearly indicate that substituting the similarity co-attention module with an MLP leads to a substantial performance degradation on both Specific and Non-Specific ground-truth networks. This suggests that a simple linear layer is insufficient to capture the latent correlations between the two feature views. In contrast, the similarity co-attention mechanism facilitates deep interaction and fusion of node features both intra-view and inter-view the feature maps. It enables the capture of strong long-range associations between nodes, thereby mitigating the inherent limitation of GNNs, which typically extract only shallow features from direct neighbors. Consequently, this mechanism enlarges the receptive field for feature extraction. Additionally, to evaluate feature integration, we conducted an ablation study within the similarity co-attention module, comparing adaptive fusion against a basic sum operation. Evaluations across multiple scRNA-seq datasets revealed that the simple sum operation consistently outperformed the adaptive approach. Detailed results for these variants are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

  Notably, a substantial decline in AUPR is observed on the STRING ground-truth network upon removing the similarity co-attention module. We hypothesize that this stems from the fact that the STRING network is intrinsically a PPI network, sharing high topological homogeneity with our PPI-based high-order neighbor module. Using a simple MLP to fuse such homogeneous features is prone to inducing feature redundancy and signal interference, thereby hampering prediction performance. In contrast, the similarity co-attention mechanism effectively mitigates these issues by virtue of its adaptive weighting structure, which enables selective feature integration.

  In summary, the experimental results demonstrate that these modules are mutually complementary and work synergistically to substantially improve predictive accuracy.


### Parameter setting

#### Parameter sensitivity of the high-order neighbor module
  To thoroughly evaluate the model’s sensitivity to core architectural configurations, we conducted systematic parameter analyses. First, within the PPI-based high-order neighbor construction module, we varied the topological order of the physical prior networks to determine their specific impact on the model’s denoising capability. We systematically compared the performance of several configurations—single GRN (S-GRN), raw PPI data, as well as 1-hop, 2-hop, and 3-hop neighborhoods—across two data scales, as detailed in Table 4. The experimental results clearly demonstrate that constructing the high-order neighbor module using the 2-hop GRNs topology integrated with PPI networks significantly enhances GRN inference. Crucially, this improvement is evident not only in the substantial gains across absolute metrics but also in its ability to effectively balance the receptive field of the GNNs.


#### Different number of heads in multi-head attention layer
  To investigate the hyperparameter sensitivity of CaHoT-GRN, we systematically evaluated the impact of varying the number of graph attention heads (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $K \in \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$\end{document}). This analysis was conducted across 22 datasets derived from four ground-truth networks, specifically targeting two gene scales: TFs+500 and TFs+1000. To ensure a comprehensive evaluation across different scales, we calculated the average performance metrics for each scale. As presented in Table 5, experimental results demonstrate that the model consistently achieves stable and optimal performance across all metrics AUPRC and AUROC when the number of attention heads is set to 3.

  Specifically, utilizing three graph attention heads facilitates the effective filtration of noise and irrelevant features while simultaneously highlighting critical regulatory relationships. While increasing the number of heads introduces additional attention weights, it also inflates computational complexity. Furthermore, an excessive number of heads may exacerbate the risk of node feature over-smoothing. Consequently, considering the trade-off between feature representation capability and computational overhead, a 3-head configuration proves to be optimal.


#### Impact of feature fusion strategies
  To investigate how to effectively leverage the gene expression, gene sequence, and protein sequence features extracted by the model, we benchmarked our approach against three conventional feature fusion strategies. The comparative experimental results are summarized in Table 6. Experimental results demonstrate that the Concatenation strategy consistently achieves optimal average performance across all 22 datasets derived from four ground-truth networks, spanning both TFs+500 and TFs+1000 scales. This superiority is attributed to the fact that the three modalities reflect gene characteristics at distinct biological layers, making them inherently complementary rather than equivalent or redundant. Aggregation methods like Mean or Max pooling act as forms of lossy compression, which often obscure unique context and fine-grained details provided by specific modalities. In contrast, Concatenation preserves the integrity of features from all sources, offering a rich and high-dimensional learning space for the downstream network. This allows the model to fully exploit and learn comprehensive representations from diverse biological perspectives, thereby facilitating a deeper exploration of latent TF–target associations.


### Case study
  To evaluate the capability of CaHoT-GRN in identifying potential associations between specific TFs and target genes, we applied the model to the hHEP cell line within the context of cell-type-specific ChIP-seq networks. Specifically, the model was trained on a predefined training set, and the learned parameters were subsequently employed to predict TF–target associations. For the analysis, we prioritized the top-ranked target genes corresponding to TFs of interest and validated these predicted interactions using the hTFtarget database. As a repository containing experimentally validated human TF–target interactions, hTFtarget serves as an independent resource to assess the reliability and biological relevance of our predictions.

  In this case study, we selected three biologically significant TFs: JUND, FOXA2, and ELF1. Specifically, JUND, a critical component of the AP-1 transcription complex, functions as a core stress response TF. By forming heterodimers with proteins from the Fos or Jun families, JUND modulates a diverse array of downstream target genes involved in cell proliferation, survival, and inflammation [62–64]. FOXA2 functions as a crucial pioneer TF. By opening condensed chromatin regions to facilitate the access and binding of other regulatory factors, it orchestrates the development of visceral organs, such as the liver and pancreas, and maintains their metabolic functions in adulthood [65–67]. ELF1 serves as a pivotal TF within the immune system. Upon T cell stimulation, it initiates the immune response by activating downstream target genes, such as IL2RA and GM-CSF [68]. Furthermore, ELF1 regulates a broad-spectrum antiviral program and plays an indispensable role in the development of hematopoietic stem cells and macrophages [69]. Figure 3 presents the top-20 predicted target genes for JUND, FOXA2, and ELF1. These findings highlight the CaHoT-GRN superior accuracy and serve as further validation of its capability to uncover potential regulatory links between TFs and their targets. To corroborate these links at the sequence level, we successfully identified high-confidence binding motifs for the top 10 target genes of JUND, FOXA2, and ELF1 using JASPAR [70] (Supplementary Table S4). Moreover, the precise locations of these binding sites were visualized with Benchling Supplementary Figs S1 and S2, providing robust physical evidence for the predicted regulatory edges.

  To validate the biological significance of the CaHoT-GRN predictions, we conducted functional enrichment analysis on the inferred regulatory networks. Specifically, in the hHEP dataset, we analyzed the target genes of FOXA2, a master regulator of liver metabolism and development. As shown in Fig. 4, these predicted targets are significantly enriched in essential hepatic pathways, particularly glutathione metabolism and biosynthesis. Because glutathione is the liver’s primary antioxidant and detoxifying agent, these findings underscore CaHoT-GRN’s ability to identify regulatory modules that are both statistically significant and profoundly meaningful for the given cellular context.


### Gene regulatory network inference on bladder cancer spatial transcriptomics
  To further demonstrate the utility of CaHoT-GRN in elucidating key regulatory factors underlying disease mechanisms, we utilized a spatial transcriptomics dataset of bladder cancer. Specifically, we leveraged experimentally validated TFs–target associations from the hTFtarget database to serve as prior knowledge for the bladder cancer GRN. Our analysis centers on three pivotal TFs: IRF1, ATF3, and BRD4. Notably, BRD4 acts as a super-enhancer hijacker, i.e. frequently overexpressed in bladder cancer. By binding to acetylated histones, it potently drives the aberrant expression of downstream oncogenes [71]. IRF1 functions as a critical “switch” for immunotherapy, playing a pivotal role in modulating tumor immune microenvironments and influencing immune evasion mechanisms [72, 73]. In contrast, ATF3 expression exhibits an inverse correlation with the histological grade and clinical stage of bladder cancer. Elevated levels of ATF3 have been shown to impede tumor cell migration and invasion [74]. Closely associated with this regulatory landscape are FOS and JUN, which functionally interact within this network [75].

  Figure 5 illustrates a causal regulatory network comprising 5 TFs and 60 target genes, representing a bladder cancer-specific gene regulatory subnetwork. Notably, CaHoT-GRN accurately captured several critical causal regulatory relationships. For instance, the regulatory interaction between BRD4 and G6PD was precisely identified. This interaction is known to be mediated via the KEAP1-Nrf2 axis, where BRD4 activates KEAP1 transcription and inhibits Nrf2 in a KEAP1-independent manner. This results in the downregulation of G6PD expression, subsequently affecting the pentose phosphate pathway and cellular antioxidant stress response [76]. NBEAL2, recognized as a significant biomarker associated with various malignancies—including colorectal cancer and primary malignant neoplasms—was predicted to be regulated by the TF BRD4 [77]. Additionally, BRD4 and TRMT112 are known to function synergistically in post-traumatic osteoarthritis, specifically by regulating the expression of pro-inflammatory genes to exacerbate inflammatory responses [78]. Our analysis further investigated the impact of gene regulation on drug resistance mechanisms. specifically, c-FOS, acting as an AP-1 TF, was found to activate AXL expression, thereby driving resistance to BYL719. In vitro and in vivo data across various cancers consistently support this regulatory axis, highlighting JNK-c-FOS-AXL as a potential therapeutic target [79]. Finally, in prostate cancer, ATF3 and DDIT4 are known to act synergistically during RB-induced DNA damage and stress responses. Their primary function involves regulating the cell cycle, apoptosis, and inhibition of proliferation, ultimately influencing cancer cell survival [80].


## Discussion and conclusion
  In this work, we propose CaHoT-GRN, a novel framework for GRN inference that leverages multi-modal biological information to reconstruct regulatory associations and utilizes PPI networks to enrich sparse GRNs topologies. First, we employ large biological language models, specifically ESM and DNABERT, to generate high-quality embeddings for protein and gene sequences, respectively. Second, we utilize meta-paths to explore high-order neighborhood information within the known GRNs, while simultaneously applying a PPI-based evidentiary filtering strategy to derive an effective supplementary topology from biological priors. Finally, we introduce a similarity co-attention mechanism to fuse features extracted from these dual topological structures. CaHoT-GRN fully leverages contextual information from upstream and downstream regulatory pathways to effectively capture potential long-range biological associations, thereby improving the accuracy of GRN inference.

  Despite the outstanding performance of CaHoT-GRN, its current fusion mechanisms exhibit two main limitations. First, rigid topological masking lacks adaptability in sparse networks. In low-confidence or highly sparse networks such as STRING or TFs+1000, error amplification from strict hard-intersection mechanisms and matrix exponentiation frequently generates topological artifacts. To address this, future research will explore adaptive soft masking based on graph information entropy to improve topological resilience. Second, similarity-based attention mechanisms risk under-fusion. Although this strong inductive bias effectively filters noise, it traps the model in information silos by suppressing highly heterogeneous but valuable cross-modal features—a bottleneck that severely limits comprehensive multi-omics fusion amidst the rapid accumulation of multi-omics data [81]. Future work will therefore introduce modality-aware mechanisms such as dynamic routing to bypass single similarity metrics, paving the way for profound and synergistic multi-omics integration.

  Moving forward, to fully decode the regulatory interactions between TFs and target genes, we envision a closed-loop validation framework combining dry-lab and wet-lab approaches. First, advanced structural deep learning models like AlphaFold 3 will be utilized to predict TF–DNA and TF–TF–DNA complexes at atomic resolution, pinpointing critical hotspot residues at binding interfaces [82]. Next, the vast candidate targets generated by these models will undergo rapid and high-throughput in vitro screening and affinity validation using R-PNAI-T technology [83]. Finally, to ensure these interactions hold true in physiological contexts, we will apply live-cell zero-distance photocrosslinking and proteomics. These combined techniques confirm dynamic TF-DNA binding in native chromatin while validating AlphaFold 3’s structural predictions at single-amino-acid resolution in vivo [84]. This comprehensive pipeline spanning AI prediction, in vitro screening, and in vivo omics confirmation will drastically accelerate the accurate mapping of complex GRNs.
