# Machine and Deep Learning Reveal Sequence Determinants Encoding Bivalent Histone Modifications


## Abstract
  Bivalent histone modifications, marked by the coexistence of activating and repressive histone marks, define a distinctive chromatin state with key roles in developmental gene regulation. However, the specific sequence features that distinguish bivalent chromatin regions remain unclear. Here we show that genome-wide profiling of H3K4me3, H3K27me3, and H3K9me3 in mouse embryonic stem cells revealed that bivalent domains have higher GC content and stronger evolutionary conservation than monovalent regions. Genes marked by bivalency were enriched in developmental signaling pathways, including Hippo, MAPK, and TGF-β. Using machine learning models trained on k-mer sequence features, we accurately distinguished bivalent from monovalent regions. Feature analysis identified informative motifs such as TCTGAA and TCACAG, associated with pluripotency transcription factors including OCT4, SOX2, ESRRB, and TCFCP2l1. Deep learning models further improved predictive accuracy and uncovered motifs enriched at the boundaries of bivalent peaks, suggesting positional specificity. These findings reveal that bivalent chromatin states are encoded by distinct sequence features.


## Introduction
  Embryonic stem cells (ESCs) can differentiate into all the cell types of the organism, a process that relies on the complex interplay between transcription factors, epigenetic regulators, and signaling molecules. The coordinated actions of such mechanisms regulate with exquisite temporal and spatial precision the developmental gene expression programs that dictate cell fate1,2.

  Classic bivalent chromatin domains comprised of H3K4me3 and H3K27me3 marks were initially identified in ESCs and postulated to maintain key developmental genes in poised state while can be rapidly activated upon differentiation3,4. Additionally, H3K4me3 and H3K9me3 co-occupy promoters of several critical lineage-specific genes in trophoblast stem cells5. H3K4me3/H3K9me3 bivalent domains were also found to maintain adipogenic master regulatory genes poised for activation upon differentiation in mesenchymal stem cells and preadipocytes5–8. In addition, coexistence of H3K4me3 and H3K9me3 has been detected in human rRNA coding region proximal to the transcription start site9. Interestingly, active and repressive histone marks have also been identified to co-exist as bivalent domains at promoters of developmental genes to poise gene expression upon developmental cues10,11. Genome-wide mapping of histone modifications in ESCs showed that bivalently marked genes are essential for developmental processes and lineage specification4,12,13.

  Currently, machine learning algorithms have garnered extensive application within the realm of biology, particularly in the fields of genomics and epigenetics14–19. For example, Benveniste et al. used the k-mer features of the DNA sequence in the promoter region to construct a logistic regression (LR) classifier that can accurately predict histone modification patterns20. In addition, Colbran and colleagues constructed a k-mer-support vector machine (SVM) machine learning framework and found that the occurrence patterns of short sequence motifs can correctly predict widely active human enhancers18,21. Deep learning has also been successfully applied to histone modification research. DeepHistone predicts histone modification sites by integrating DNA sequence information with chromatin accessibility data22. The information encoded in the DNA sequence is pivotal for initiating the processes involving sequence-specific TFs and histone modifications. Machine learning-based methods have shown great promise in predicting histone modification patterns and regulatory elements from short DNA motifs14,23,24. Beyond histone modification prediction, diverse ML-based frameworks have also been developed for broader biological applications, such as chronic disease risk assessment25, probiotics classification26, embryonic transcriptome resources27, and improved image-based models28.

  Although previous studies have linked bivalent histone modifications to specific histone methyltransferases, the sequence determinants underlying bivalency remain poorly understood. Here, we systematically profiled the genome-wide distributions of H3K4me3, H3K27me3, and H3K9me3 in mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs), and characterized the distinct genomic localization and transcriptional features associated with H3K4me3/H3K27me3 and H3K4me3/H3K9me3 bivalent domains. Furthermore, by integrating machine learning approaches, we identified sequence features and DNA motifs corresponding to transcription factors implicated in pluripotency and differentiation. These findings provide new insights into how DNA sequence encodes chromatin bivalency and offer a predictive framework for dissecting its regulatory roles in stem cell biology.


## Results

### Genome-wide profiling of histone modifications in mouse embryonic stem cells
  To systematically investigate the regulatory patterns of univalent and bivalent histone modifications, we compared the signal intensities and chromosomal distribution preferences of different histone marks. Among them, H3K4me3 displayed globally higher enrichment levels in mESCs compared to the two repressive modifications, H3K27me3 and H3K9me3.   We next investigated the underlying DNA sequence characteristics of monovalent versus bivalent peaks, focusing on peak width, GC content, and conservation. Peak length analysis revealed that most regions spanned 200 to 1000 bp, with bivalent peaks being slightly narrower than monovalent ones (Fig. 1C). Additionally, bivalent peaks exhibited significantly higher GC content and conservation scores, suggesting that these co-modified regions are preferentially located in conserved genomic elements (Fig. 1D, E).

  Bivalent domains are typically found at promoter regions, as exemplified by the overlap of H3K4me3 and H3K27me3 enrichment at gene promoters (Fig. 1F, G). In contrast, H3K9me3 showed predominant enrichment within long terminal repeat (LTR) and long interspersed nuclear element (LINE) repetitive elements (Fig. 1F), consistent with its established role in retrotransposon silencing29. We also evaluated the enrichment of repetitive DNA elements within H3K9me3/H3K4me3 bivalent domains. The results showed that H3K9me3-marked bivalent regions were significantly enriched in repetitive elements in mESCs, with H3K9me3/H3K4me3 domains particularly enriched for LINE/L1 elements (Fig. 1H, I).


### Bivalent modifications coordinately regulate gene expression
  To elucidate the effects of distinct histone modification (HM) patterns on gene expression, we quantitatively assessed the signal intensities of H3K4me3, H3K27me3, and H3K9me3 at trivalently marked genes and correlated these with corresponding transcriptional levels. Our analysis revealed that elevated H3K4me3 signals are strongly associated with high gene expression, whereas enrichment of H3K27me3 or H3K9me3 correlates with reduced expression. These findings suggest that within co-marked genomic regions, H3K4me3 likely functions as the principal activation mark, while H3K27me3 and H3K9me3 appear to contribute predominantly to repressive regulatory outcomes (Fig. 2A).Fig. 2Gene expression patterns associated with bivalent marks.A Heatmap showing the Z-score-normalized signal intensities of H3K4me3, H3K27me3, and H3K9me3, along with expression levels of co-marked genes in mESCs.   We further analyzed the expression patterns of genes associated with the two types of bivalent modifications (H3K4me3/H3K27me3 and H3K4me3/H3K9me3), and performed functional enrichment analyses. Genes marked by H3K4me3/H3K27me3 bivalency exhibited relatively low expression levels, falling between those marked by H3K4me3-only and H3K27me3-only regions, but skewed closer to the H3K27me3-only profile, indicating a general tendency toward transcriptional repression (Fig. 2B). We further categorized bivalent genes into high, medium, and low expression groups and observed a positive relationship between bivalent mark intensity and gene expression, with highly expressed genes showing the strongest bivalent signals (Fig. 2C). Consistently, a genome-wide correlation analysis revealed a weak but statistically significant positive association between bivalent signal intensity and gene expression (Spearman’s ρ = 0.186, p = 9.33 × 10−³⁴; Supplementary Fig. S1), in agreement with previous studies30,31. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of highly expressed bivalent genes revealed significant association with the “breast cancer” pathway, implying that some bivalent genes may contribute to oncogenesis. For example, the bivalent gene POU4F1 has been shown to be essential for promoting and sustaining basal-like breast cancer (BLBC), making it a potential therapeutic target32 Bivalent genes are enriched in majority of the signaling pathways related to the pluripotent maintenance and development, such as signaling pathways regulating pluripotency of stem cells, TGF-beta, Wnt, MAPK, FoxO and Hippo signaling pathways33 (Fig. 2D).

  Next, we attempted to define bivalent genes with different signal intensities by overlapping H3K4me3 and H3K9me3 Chromatin Immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) peaks. The overlapping regions were categorized into four groups based on signal strength: High-High, Mid-High, High-Mid, and Mid-Mid. These groups exhibited a linear distribution pattern, suggesting a certain degree of co-regulation between H3K4me3 and H3K9me3 (Fig. 2E). By integrating RNA-seq data, we found that genes located in regions with both high H3K4me3 and high H3K9me3 signals (High-High) exhibited relatively low expression levels (Fig. 2F). Bivalent domains are known to undergo dynamic transitions during cell fate specification. For instance, changes in bivalent marks are implicated in the differentiation of neural stem cells34. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of H3K4me3/H3K9me3-associated genes revealed enrichment in neurodevelopmental processes such as synapse assembly—a critical step during ESC differentiation into mature and functional neurons (Fig. 2G).


### Interpretable model of bivalent histone modifications driven by sequence features
  In embryonic stem cells, bivalent domains marked by H3K4me3 and H3K27me3 are preferentially enriched at promoters containing CpG islands3 and exhibit strong associations with specific DNA sequence features, such as CpG-rich elements and transcription factor binding sites35. A previous study identified an enrichment of the TCCCC sequence motif at bivalent promoters in both mouse and human ESCs36. Nevertheless, the sequence determinants that encode or direct the formation of bivalent histone modifications remain largely elusive. To explore whether bivalent chromatin regions harbor unique and predictive sequence features, we employed five supervised machine learning algorithms as well as deep learning models to classify bivalent versus monovalent regions. Specifically, overlapping bivalent peaks (200-1000 bp in length) were selected for sequence feature extraction. For machine learning models, we used the widely adopted k-mer approach, which has proven effective in capturing short sequence patterns relevant to chromatin state21. Insights from previous research were leveraged to guide the selection of parameter k, and a range of k values from 3 to 8 was adopted in this study19,37,38. For deep learning models, sequences were encoded using one-hot encoding, enabling the models to automatically learn latent sequence patterns (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Schematic representation of the interpretable model for bivalent modifications.This diagram summarizes the computational workflow employed in this study. Next, a suite of machine-learning models (logistic regression, SVM, random forest, XGBoost, and neural networks) and deep-learning architectures (DanQ, DeepSEA, CNN+Attention, and CNN+Transformer) were trained to distinguish bivalent from non-bivalent sequences. Model performance was assessed using accuracy, AUROC, AUPRC, sensitivity, precision, and F1-score. Finally, motifs learned by the deep models were interpreted via activation mapping and visualized through motif logos and transcription-factor interaction networks.

  We first constructed a three-class classification model to distinguish among H3K4me3-only, H3K9me3-only, and H3K4me3/H3K9me3 bivalent regions. Based on comparative analysis between Lasso regression and Random Forest for feature selection, we adopted Random Forest, which identified 797 informative 6-mer features (Supplementary Fig. S2). Among all models tested, Support Vector Machine (SVM) yielded the highest classification performance, as indicated by ROC curves and overall accuracy (Fig. 4B, C). The confusion matrix of the SVM model confirmed strong classification capacity, particularly for H3K4me3-only regions (Fig. 4D). To exclude the possibility that the observed predictive performance arose from random correlations, we further evaluated a randomized null model in which sequence features were shuffled while class labels were preserved. Under this control, all models exhibited chance-level performance (AUROC ≈ 0.5), in contrast to the clear diagonal enrichment observed when using real genomic sequences (Supplementary Fig. S5), indicating that the predictive signals captured by the models are genuinely encoded in DNA sequence features. Using a similar strategy, we further distinguished H3K4me3/H3K27me3 bivalent regions from monovalent ones. Lasso regression was used to select 616 relevant sequence features. Optimal model parameters were determined via grid search with 5-fold cross-validation. Among all tested classifiers, the XGBoost-6mer model achieved the best predictive performance (Supplementary Fig. S3).   Given the large number of sequence features identified by machine learning, model interpretation remains challenging due to feature complexity and potential redundancy. To address this, we performed HOMER enrichment analysis to prioritize core sequence motifs with potential biological relevance (Supplementary Fig. S6B). For the H3K4me3/H3K9me3 bivalent modifications, four key motifs were significantly enriched. We retrained classification models using only these core features and observed that all models maintained robust predictive performance, with AUC values reaching approximately 0.87. Among them, the Random Forest (RF) model consistently outperformed others, suggesting that these core sequence motifs are sufficient to capture essential information for chromatin state classification (Fig. 4E). SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis revealed that the motif “TCTGAA” exhibited the highest positive contribution to the prediction of bivalent regions, indicating its strong association with bivalent chromatin states. In contrast, the remaining three motifs predominantly contributed to the classification of H3K9me3-only regions. These findings suggest functional divergence among sequence motifs, where distinct short DNA sequences preferentially mark different histone modification states (Fig. 4F). These findings indicate that different short DNA motifs preferentially associate with distinct chromatin states.

  Similarly, five enriched motifs were identified for H3K4me3/H3K27me3 bivalent domains. As expected, the performance was slightly reduced compared to models trained with the full feature set, yet the simplified model offers improved interpretability and highlights biologically relevant sequence patterns (Supplementary Fig. S4). Based on SHAP values, the motifs TCACAG, TTCAAA, and AGGGCT exhibited the highest feature importance, serving as key indicators for distinguishing H3K4me3/H3K27me3 bivalent regions from monovalent counterparts (Supplementary Fig. S4C). Collectively, these results highlight that specific short DNA sequence motifs not only carry predictive power for chromatin state classification but also provide mechanistic insights into the sequence determinants underlying bivalent histone modifications.

  To gain deeper insights into the sequence determinants regulating bivalent chromatin regions, we constructed deep learning models to predict H3K4me3/H3K9me3 bivalency. We systematically evaluated the effect of different input sequence lengths (200 bp, 400 bp, 600 bp, 800 bp, and 1000 bp) on model performance. Overall, most models exhibited improved AUROC values with increasing sequence length. Specifically, the DanQ, CNN+Attention, and CNN+Transformer models achieved optimal performance when trained on sequences of 600 bp or 1000 bp (Fig. 4G), suggesting that these lengths are well-suited for this binary classification task. Among all evaluated models, the CNN+Attention architecture consistently demonstrated the best predictive performance, followed by the DanQ and CNN+Transformer models, based on comprehensive evaluation metrics (Fig. 4H, I). These results suggest that deep learning models are capable of capturing higher-order and context-dependent sequence features relevant to bivalent chromatin, with interpretable architectures further facilitating motif discovery.


### Analysis of sequence regulatory grammar underlying bivalent histone modifications
  To elucidate the regulatory grammar of distinct bivalent histone modifications, we performed a comparative analysis of sequence features associated with H3K4me3/H3K9me3 and H3K4me3/H3K27me3 regions. Histone modification level was reported to be closely related to the overall GC content of the corresponding region41, we categorized the identified motifs into three groups based on their GC content: GC-rich, AT-rich, and neutral motifs. Most motifs were GC-rich, while a smaller proportion were AT-rich (Fig. 5A). Previous studies have implied the role of particular DNA sequences (or motifs) in setting the boundary of histone modification domain or opening chromatin to allow remodeling enzymes to bind DNA42. We next examined the positional distribution of the selected motifs within bivalent sequences. Each bivalent region was divided into ten equal bins, with the outermost bins representing the edges and the central bins representing the center. We observed that motifs associated with H3K4me3/H3K27me3 bivalency were predominantly enriched at the edges of bivalent regions, whereas those linked to H3K4me3/H3K9me3 bivalency were largely classified as neutral.   Next, we further investigate potential transcription factors (TFs) associated with these sequence features, we used the TomTom tool to compare all identified motifs with a mouse motif database. Pluripotency-related transcription factors, including STAT3, ESRRB, KLF4, OCT4, SOX2, and SMAD1, were significantly enriched in both types of bivalent regions (Supplementary Fig. S6), suggesting shared regulatory programs in embryonic stem cells. Notably, the key motif TCTGAA that distinguishes H3K4me3/H3K9me3 bivalent regions was primarily matched to the TF TCFCP2l1 (Fig. 5E). These findings suggest that the DNA sequences co-occupied by H3K4me3/H3K27me3 or H3K4me3/H3K9me3 are potentially regulated by pluripotency factors in embryonic stem cells. We further mapped the identified sequence features of H3K4me3/H3K9me3 bivalent modifications to specific peak regions, annotated the corresponding genes, and identified four bivalent genes containing all four key motifs (Fig. 5C, D). These peaks exhibited intermediate ChIP-seq signal intensities between H3K4me3-only and H3K9me3-only regions (Supplementary Fig. S6E), supporting the notion of a distinct chromatin state.

  To interpret the learned sequence patterns, we applied a sequence alignment-based interpretability method (SABM) to H3K4me3/H3K9me3-labeled regions. This method identifies subsequences that activate convolutional neurons and stacks position weight matrices (PWMs), analogous to traditional estimation methods43. We used information content (IC) to evaluate each model’s ability to identify functional sequence motifs, with the DanQ model achieving the highest IC values, indicating stronger motif definition in this subtype (Fig. 5F). Using the TOMTOM tool, we identified the transcription factors associated with motifs learned by different models. These transcription factors are capable of binding not only to the promoters of pluripotency-related genes in embryonic stem cells, but also to their enhancer regions, thereby maintaining the pluripotent state (Fig. 5G). Subsequently, we applied the FIMO tool to locate the specific positions of each motif (motif sites), and annotated the corresponding bivalent genes. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of these bivalent genes revealed significant enrichment in the Neuroactive ligand–receptor interaction pathway, which is closely associated with nervous system development and stem cell differentiation (Fig. 5H).

  In summary, while H3K4me3/H3K9me3 and H3K4me3/H3K27me3 bivalent regions share common pluripotency-related regulatory factors, they differ in base composition, motif positioning, TF specificity, and associated biological pathways. These findings suggest that distinct sequence grammars underlie the two classes of bivalent chromatin, reflecting their divergent regulatory roles in embryonic stem cells.


## Discussion
  DNA sequences are critical determinants of bivalent histone modifications, yet how they regulate bivalent domains and maintain pluripotency in mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs) remains incompletely understood. In this study, we analyzed the genome-wide distribution of two types of bivalent modifications—H3K4me3/H3K9me3 and H3K4me3/H3K27me3—and revealed distinct chromatin localization patterns. Specifically, H3K4me3/H3K9me3 bivalent regions were predominantly enriched within LINE/L1 repetitive elements, whereas the classical H3K4me3/H3K27me3 bivalent domains, as expected, were concentrated at promoter regions44.

  At the functional level, bivalent domains exhibited higher GC content and greater evolutionary conservation compared to monovalent regions, suggesting that co-marked regions are under selective pressure and may possess important regulatory roles. Notably, Dppa2/4 can act as a trigger of signaling pathways by binding to GC-rich regions to promote zygotic genome activation, providing a potential molecular mechanism for the functional relevance of these GC-rich domains45. Genes marked by bivalent modifications showed lower expression levels and were enriched in developmental and signaling pathways, particularly those related to embryonic stem cell identity and neural differentiation. These findings align with previous studies proposing that bivalent chromatin maintains key genes in a poised state, primed for either activation or repression during lineage commitment46.

  Through systematic classification modeling, we demonstrated that short k-mer features, especially 6-mers, carry significant predictive information about histone modification states. SVM and XGBoost models trained on these features achieved high classification performance, underscoring the capacity of short DNA motifs to discriminate chromatin states. Importantly, the statistical significance and biological relevance of predictive k-mers were rigorously evaluated, and multiple-testing correction was applied in motif enrichment analyses to control false discovery rates. These measures ensured that the identified sequence features reflect robust biological signals rather than statistical artifacts.

  To further exclude the possibility that the observed predictive performance arose from random correlations, we implemented a stringent randomized null-model control in which sequence features were randomized while class labels were preserved. Under this condition, all models exhibited chance-level performance (AUROC ≈ 0.5), in contrast to the clear predictive power observed when using real genomic sequences. This result provides strong evidence that the discriminative signals captured by the models are genuinely encoded in DNA sequence features.

  Model interpretability analysis using SHAP further highlighted specific motifs such as TCTGAA and TTCAAA as key contributors to classification performance, implying potential biological roles in the recruitment of chromatin-modifying complexes or transcription factors. Consistently, motif enrichment and similarity analyses using HOMER and TOMTOM revealed that these enriched motifs significantly overlapped with binding sites of well-known pluripotency regulators including OCT4, SOX2, KLF4, and ESRRB, supporting the hypothesis that DNA-encoded signals contribute to the establishment of bivalent chromatin states in pluripotent cells. The pluripotency of mESCs and their ability to self-renew critically depend on the actions of specific transcription factors, including OCT4 and SOX2, NANOG, KLF4 and ESRRB47,48,all of which can bind and activate promoters as well as enhancers of pluripotency-associated genes in ESCs49. Notably, the TCTGAA and TTCAAA motifs were strongly associated with the transcription factor TCFCP2l1, a key regulator of stem cell self-renewal, suggesting its distinct functional involvement in both H3K4me3/H3K9me3 and H3K4me3/H3K27me3 bivalent regions (Fig. 5E; Supplementary Fig. S3F). These observations point to a shared sequence-level regulatory logic underlying distinct bivalent chromatin configurations.

  Moreover, our deep learning models not only validated the results obtained from traditional machine learning approaches but also exhibited enhanced performance and interpretability. DanQ architectures achieved relatively high AUROC and information content (IC) scores, with convolutional filters effectively capturing functionally relevant motifs. Interpretability analysis using SABM further revealed that most informative motifs were centrally enriched within bivalent peaks, a spatial pattern indicative of localized regulatory activity rather than diffuse sequence bias. Together with the use of strictly separated training and independent test sets, these results reinforce the robustness and generalizability of our predictive framework.

  In summary, our study provides compelling evidence that specific DNA sequence features not only predict the presence of bivalent chromatin states but also point to potential mechanisms involving transcription factor recruitment and developmental gene regulation. By integrating rigorous statistical controls, interpretable machine learning, and deep learning analyses, we demonstrate how DNA-encoded information contributes to epigenetic regulation during pluripotency maintenance and differentiation. These findings deepen our understanding of the interplay between sequence-level and epigenetic regulatory layers and establish a framework for future studies of chromatin state transitions in stem cells.


## Methods

### Dataset collection
  Publicly available ChIP-seq datasets of histone modifications (H3K4me3, H3K27me3, and H3K9me3) in mouse were obtained from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under accession numbers GSE13584150 and GSE9777851. And the RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data of mESC were also obtained in GEO database and GEO accession no. GSE135841.


### RNA-seq analysis
  Paired-end RNA-seq data were downloaded from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Raw sequencing reads were first subjected to quality assessment using FastQC (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). Adapter sequences and low-quality reads were subsequently removed using Trim Galore (version 0.6.5) with the parameters --phred33 -q 20 --length 30 --max_n 3 --stringency 3. Reads with base quality scores below 20 were discarded during the trimming process. The cleaned reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome mm39 (GRCm39) using Hisat252 with default parameters for paired-end sequencing. Reads with mapping quality (MAPQ) scores < 20 were filtered out to retain only uniquely mapped and high-confidence alignments for downstream gene expression analysis. SAM files generated from the alignment were converted to BAM format using Samtools, followed by sorting and indexing. Gene-level read counts were calculated using featureCounts53 by determining whether aligned reads overlapped with gene features defined in the corresponding annotation file. Expression levels for all RefSeq genes were quantified to fragments per kilobase million (FPKM) using Cufflinks (v1.2.0), and FPKM values of replicates were averaged54.


### ChIP-seq data analysis
  The Chromatin Immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) original fastq format data were first controlled by FastQC (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/) and trimmed to remove adapters and low-quality sequences using Trim-galore (version 0.6.5; https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/trim_galore/) with default parameters. Next, the trimmed reads were aligned to the mouse GRCm39 reference genome using Bowtie2 (version 2.2.1)55with the following command: bowtie2 -p 6 -3 5 --local -x <reference_genome_index > -U <input_fastq > -S <output_sam> In this alignment, 6 threads were used, 5 bases were trimmed from the 3′ end, and local alignment mode was applied. The reference genome included both canonical chromosomes and decoy sequences to improve alignment accuracy. Reads that mapped to multiple genomic regions (multi-mapped reads) were filtered out by retaining only uniquely mapped reads. PCR duplicates were removed using Picard’s MarkDuplicates tool with REMOVE_DUPLICATES=true to avoid artificial amplification biases. The resulting BAM files, containing unique, non-duplicate reads, were used for all downstream analyses. The resulting SAM files were subsequently converted to BAM format using Samtools (version 1.9; http://www.htslib.org/). BAM files from biological replicates were then merged. Normalized density was calculated using ‘bamCompare’ module from deepTools (version 3.5.0)56 (with the parameters setting: bamCompare -b1 $treatmentbam -b2 controlbam -o $bw -scaleFactorsMethod None -operation log2 -normalizeUsing RPKM –ignoreDuplicates). Peak calling was performed by MACS2 (version 2.2.1)57 with default parameters. Finally, the R package ChIPseeker58 was used to annotate the position of the peaks in the genome. All intersection calculations were done using the ‘intersect’ module of bedtools (version 2.27.1)59. Further details are available in the ENCODE ChIP-seq pipeline documentation (https://github.com/ENCODE-DCC/chip-seq-pipeline)60.


### Genomic annotations
  Genomic coordinates for promoters, exons, introns, and UTRs were obtained from the GENCODE comprehensive mouse genome annotation (version M39, GRCm39; https://www.gencodegenes.org/mouse/). Annotations for repetitive elements—including LINEs, SINEs, LTRs, and DNA transposons—were retrieved from the UCSC Genome Browser RepeatMasker dataset for the mm39 assembly. Specifically, we downloaded the rmsk.txt.gz table from the UCSC download server (https://hgdownload.soe.ucsc.edu/goldenPath/mm39/database/rmsk.txt.gz), which provides genome-wide Repeat Masker annotations curated for the mouse reference genome (GRCm39). These coordinates were used to quantify the overlap between histone modification peaks and different classes of transposable elements.


### Definition of bivalent and non-bivalent regions
  To define bivalent histone modification regions, we used the intersect function from the bedtools suite to identify overlapping peaks between H3K4me3 and H3K27me3. A region was classified as bivalent if an H3K4me3 peak overlapped with an H3K27me3 peak and the length of the overlapping region exceeded one-tenth of the shorter peak’s length. In contrast, non-bivalent regions were defined as peaks specific to either H3K4me3 or H3K27me3, with no overlap with peaks of the other mark. Similarly, bivalent regions marked by H3K4me3 and H3K9me3 were defined using the same overlap criterion.


### Motif analysis
  For each motif associated with bivalent histone modifications, we classified motifs based on their GC and AT content. A motif was defined as GC-rich if more than 70% of its positions (i.e., at least 4 positions) satisfied the following criteria: (1) the weight of G or C at each position exceeded 0.5; and (2) the sum of G and C weights at each position exceeded 0.75. Similarly, AT-rich motifs were defined based on the weights of A and T at each position. Motifs that did not meet the criteria for either GC-rich or AT-rich were classified as neutral motifs.

  For bivalent histone modification site, the region was divided into ten equal-sized bins, with the first and tenth bins representing the edge and the fifth and sixth bins representing the center. Motif occurrences within each bin were counted for all sequences corresponding to a given modification. For each motif, the proportion of counts in each bin was calculated by dividing the count in that bin by the total counts across all ten bins, resulting in a motif × 10-bin matrix. Since histone modification sites are non-directional, intervals equidistant from the center were merged, resulting in five bins (bin1 representing the edge and bin5 the center). Under a uniform distribution, each bin would account for 0.2 of motif occurrences. Motifs were classified as center if the proportion in bin4 or bin5 exceeded that at the edges by more than 0.05, edge if the edge proportion exceeded that in either central bin by more than 0.05, and neutral otherwise.

  Motif discovery based on the extracted k-mer features was conducted using MEME61 (version 5.5.6). To further identify motifs enriched in bivalent peak sequences, we performed de novo motif enrichment analysis with HOMER (version 5.1), using the HOMER and Embryonic Stem Cell TFs motif databases while excluding fly and yeast as background species.

### Statistics and reproducibility
  Statistical analyses were implemented with R (version 3.6.0, http://www.r-project.org). Representative KEGG pathways were summarized in each gene cluster and P-values were marked to show the significance. All enrichment p-values were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate (FDR), and pathways with an FDR-adjusted p-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The full list of significant GO terms and KEGG pathways is provided in Supplementary Data 2–4. Data are presented as mean ± SD of biological replicates. Statistical significance was assessed using a two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test implemented in R using the wilcox.test function64,65.


### Feature selection and machine learning modeling
  To identify the most informative sequence features associated with bivalent histone modifications, we employed two distinct feature selection strategies tailored to different bivalent types. For H3K4me3/H3K27me3 bivalent regions, we applied the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) to reduce feature redundancy and enhance model interpretability. Specifically, LASSO was implemented using scikit-learn’s LassoCV (v0.23.2). A LASSO regression model was trained with fivefold cross-validation (cv = 5) to optimize the regularization parameter λ, with the maximum number of iterations set to 10,000 to ensure convergence. Features with negligible coefficients were eliminated, retaining only those that made significant contributions to the target variable. For H3K4me3/H3K9me3 bivalent regions, we used the Random Forest (RF) algorithm to rank features based on their importance scores derived from ensemble decision trees. These complementary approaches allowed us to capture both sparse and non-linear discriminative features relevant to each type of bivalent modification.

  We employed the frequency patterns of k-mer (words of length k) with a sliding window with a single nucleotide step as the only feature extraction method for gene sequences. For each given nucleotide sequence containing A, C, T, and G, a sliding window of length k and Step 1 was used to intercept the sequence. Each subsequence with k nucleotides was called a k-mer pattern. For a given k, we can get 4k k-mer patterns. For example, when k  =  3, 64 modes can be obtained, such as ‘AAA’, ‘AAC’, ‘AAT’, and so forth. K-mer patterns are a simpler model than TFBS motifs, as they do not limit information representation to sequences that represent known TFBS motifs. We drew on the experience of previous studies in the selection of k19,37,38,66. In this study, we tested values of k ranging from 3 to 8 and selected the one that yielded the best model performance.

  The dataset was randomly divided into a training set (70%) and a testing set (30%). Five supervised machine-learning classifiers were evaluated: support vector machine (SVM), k-nearest neighbors (KNN), random forest (RF), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), and a feedforward neural network (NN). Hyperparameter tuning was performed for all machine-learning models using five-fold cross-validated grid search implemented with scikit-learn’s GridSearchCV (on the training set only). The best hyperparameter combination for each model was selected based on the mean cross-validated ROC-AUC. All models were implemented using standard Python machine-learning libraries and evaluated on an independent held-out test set. The complete hyperparameter grids and the optimal parameter sets for each classifier are summarized in Supplementary Data 5.


### Random null model analysis
  To assess whether the predictive performance and identified sequence features could arise by chance due to the large k-mer search space, we constructed a randomized null model as an additional statistical control. Specifically, for each k-mer size (k = 3–8), the original genomic sequences were replaced with randomized sequences composed of A, C, G, and T, while preserving the original sequence length distribution and class labels. K-mer frequency matrices were then recomputed from these randomized sequences using the same sliding-window procedure as applied to the real data. Machine-learning classifiers were trained and evaluated on the randomized datasets using exactly the same feature selection, preprocessing, hyperparameter optimization, and evaluation pipeline as for the real genomic sequences. Model performance under the null condition was assessed using ROC-AUC and accuracy on the held-out test set. This randomization-based null model provides an empirical baseline for chance-level performance and serves to validate that the predictive signals observed in the real data are not artifacts of multiple testing or the high dimensionality of k-mer features.


### Construction of deep learning model
  The dataset was initially split into 70% training and 30% testing. The training set was further divided into an internal training set (75% of the training data, ~56% of the total dataset) and a validation set (25% of the training data, ~14% of the total dataset), resulting in final allocations of approximately 56% training, 14% validation, and 30% testing.

  In addition, we employed several deep learning architectures that have demonstrated superior performance in genomics-related tasks. Specifically, we utilized DeepSEA, a convolutional neural network (CNN)-based model designed to predict chromatin features directly from DNA sequences. We also incorporated DanQ, a hybrid architecture combining CNNs with bi-directional long short-term memory (BiLSTM) networks, enabling the model to capture both local sequence motifs and long-range dependencies. Furthermore, we explored CNN models enhanced with attention mechanisms (CNN + Attention), which improve model interpretability by directing focus to informative regions of the sequence. Lastly, we implemented a CNN + Transformer model that replaces recurrent layers with Transformer encoders, leveraging self-attention to efficiently model long-range interactions within genomic sequences.

### Model interpretation
  The SHAP algorithm is a game theoretic approach for explaining the output of machine learning models67. The SHAP value quantifies the contribution of each feature to the prediction outcome. We ranked the input features according to the mean absolute SHAP value and displayed the distribution of SHAP values for the entire dataset for each species in our model. The SHAP method was implemented by the shap python package (version 0.45.0).

  Sequence alignment based method (SABM) is a feature map-based method used to interpret model parameters43. This approach begins by extracting the filters from the first convolutional layer of the network and converting them into Position Weight Matrices (PWMs) by statistically counting the occurrences of deoxyribonucleotides in the feature maps. We set the activation threshold at 0.85, meaning that only nucleotides that activate the filter by at least 85% of its maximum value are included in the PWMs. Subsequently, the TOMTOM algorithm68 is employed to query the JASPAR database, annotating the filters represented by the PWMs with known transcription factor binding sites (TFBS). As a result, corresponding transcription factor motifs are assigned to each filter.
