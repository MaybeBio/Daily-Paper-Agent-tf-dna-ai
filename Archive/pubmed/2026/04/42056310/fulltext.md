# SAC regulates glutathione metabolism via the Keap1/Nrf2/GPX4 signaling pathway to inhibit ferroptosis and alleviate ulcerative colitis


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46788-0.


## Introduction
  Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic inflammatory bowel disease affecting the colon and rectum. With a high prevalence in developed countries and a significant increase in incidence in developing countries, it has grown to become a global burden1. Multiple factors, such as genetic background, environmental and intraluminal factors, and mucosal immune dysregulation, have been implicated in the pathogenesis of UC2,3. In recent years, a better understanding of the underlying mechanisms of UC has led to the emergence of new therapeutic approaches. Since the beginning of the 21 st century, TNF therapy has significantly improved outcomes4. Advances in medical therapy have shifted the goal of treatment from symptomatic relief to endoscopic and histological healing. However, a significant proportion of patients are still refractory to pharmacological treatments, so the development of new drugs targeting the underlying mechanisms of UC is necessary to achieve optimal disease control.

  Oxidative stress plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of UC. It promotes the production and release of inflammatory mediators, while the inflammatory response conversely exacerbates oxidative stress, forming a vicious cycle5. Excessive production of ROS not only directly damages cellular membranes, proteins, and DNA, but also activates inflammatory signaling pathways such as NF-κB, thereby promoting the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6. These collectively exacerbate intestinal inflammation6. Furthermore, oxidative stress disrupts intestinal barrier function and increases intestinal permeability, allowing bacteria to more easily penetrate the mucosal layer, thereby further activating immune responses7. Breaking the “oxidative stress-inflammation” vicious cycle. However, helps reduce damage to intestinal epithelial cells and promotes the recovery of the intestinal mucosal barrier. Meanwhile, the accumulated ROS and the imbalance in the antioxidant system lead to lipid peroxidation, promoting the occurrence of ferroptosis8. Ferroptosis is a strictly regulated form of cell death characterized by uncontrolled lipid peroxidation9. However, GPX4 (Glutathione peroxidase 4) is the central antioxidant defense enzyme that combats ferroptosis10. GSH (Glutathione) is an essential substrate for GPX4, which utilizes GSH as a reducing agent to convert toxic lipid peroxides into non-toxic lipid alcohols, thereby suppressing lipid peroxidation and the occurrence of ferroptosis11. Simultaneously, the synthesis and recycling of GSH are critical for maintaining intracellular antioxidant capacity, and the depletion of GPX4 leads to the disruption of this cycle12. Furthermore, GSH is synthesized from cysteine imported via xCT (Cystine/glutamate antiporter). Inhibition of xCT or depletion of GSH results in GPX4 inactivation, leading to increased intracellular accumulation of lipid peroxides and promoting ferroptosis13. The Nrf2-Keap1 system is a defense mechanism that safeguards cellular homeostasis. Nrf2, acting as a transcription factor, enhances the expression of xCT and GPX4. Nrf2 is predominantly regulated by Keap1. When cells are exposed to oxidative stress, Keap1-mediated ubiquitination of Nrf2 is suppressed. Consequently, Nrf2 translocates into the nucleus, triggering the expression of a suite of antioxidant target genes14. This mechanism also plays a crucial anti-inflammatory role in inflammation. HO-1, an Nrf2-regulated antioxidant factor, catalyzes heme degradation and releases anti-inflammatory mediators, thereby exerting anti-inflammatory effects15. Therefore, the Keap1/Nrf2/GPX4 signaling pathway inhibits ferroptosis associated with intestinal epithelial cell damage in UC by maintaining GPX4 levels and balancing lipid peroxidation, thereby alleviating mucosal inflammation and promoting intestinal barrier integrity.

  S-Allyl-L-cysteine (SAC), an organosulfur compound extracted from aged garlic (Allium sativum), exhibits high oral bioavailability16. SAC eliminates the generation of ROS, regulates redox balance, and protects mitochondrial function, effectively alleviating cellular damage caused by oxidative stress, while exhibiting antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties17. Studies have shown that aged garlic alleviates ulcerative colitis (UC) by improving intestinal homeostasis in mice18. To further elucidate the potential molecular mechanisms by which SAC alleviates UC, this study aims to investigate how SAC targets the Keap1/Nrf2/GPX4 signaling pathway to suppress ferroptosis in intestinal epithelial cells during UC, thereby restoring intestinal barrier integrity.


## Materials and methods

### Cell culture and treatment
  IEC-6 cells (The IEC-6 cells was purchased from iCell Bioscience Inc. (Shanghai, China).) were thawed from liquid nitrogen storage and resuscitated in a 37℃ water bath. They were then supplemented with DMEM medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Following thorough mixing, the cells were centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min, the precipitate was resuspended in fresh culture medium, and the cells were cultured in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37℃. After 24 h, the cellular morphology was assessed, and the culture medium was refreshed.

  Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were harvested from culture flasks and adjusted to a density of 6.0 × 103 cells/well in 96-well plates (100 µL/well), followed by incubation at 37℃ for 24 h. After removing the medium, SAC solutions (1.25, 2.5, 5, 10, and 20 mM) prepared in complete medium were added to respective wells. The plates were further incubated for 24 h, and cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay.

  To construct an oxidative stress IEC-6 cell model using H₂O₂, cells were seeded in 96-well plates and exposed to H₂O₂ at concentrations of 100, 200, 300, 400, 500, 600, and 700 µmol/L. After incubation at 37℃ for 4 h, cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay.

  Cellular Thermal Shift Assay (CETSA): After treating IEC-6 cells with SAC for 24 h, the cells were lysed. The supernatant from centrifugation was subjected to gradient heating. Following heating, the denatured protein precipitates were removed by centrifugation. The target protein was detected and quantified by Western blotting.

  Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) Detection: ROS levels were measured using the DCFH-DA fluorescence probe (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, S0033S, Nanjing, China). DCFH-DA was diluted 1:1000 in serum-free culture medium to a final concentration of 10 µmol/L. The culture medium was removed from the cells, and DCFH-DA was added. Cells were incubated at 37℃ for 20 min in a cell culture incubator. Cells were then washed three times with serum-free culture medium or an appropriate solution to thoroughly remove extracellular DCFH-DA. Fluorescence was observed directly using a fluorescence microscope. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.


### Evaluation of antioxidant capacity of SAC
  DPPH Free Radical Scavenging Capacity Assay Kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, A153-1-1, Nanjing, China): Prepare the working solution according to the kit instructions, mix with different concentrations of SAC solution, incubate in the dark at 25℃ for 30 min, centrifuge at 4000 rpm for 5 min, transfer 100µL to a cuvette, zero with 80% methanol, and measure absorbance at 517 nm.

  Total Antioxidant Capacity Assay Kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, A015-1–2, Nanjing, China): Prepare working solution I as per the kit instructions, mix thoroughly with different concentrations of SAC solution using a vortex mixer, incubate in a 37℃ water bath for 30 min, add working solution II, mix again, incubate at 25ཞ37℃ for 10 min, zero with double-distilled water, and measure absorbance at 520 nm.


### Animals
  Ten-week-old C57BL/6 mice (♂, average weight: 20 g) were provided by the Guangdong Experimental Animal Centre (Foshan, China). The animals were acclimatized to laboratory conditions (23℃, 12 h/12 h light/dark, 50% humidity, free access to food and water) for 2 weeks prior to experimentation and no animals died before the experiments. For all animal experiments, we used a randomised experimental setup. The number of animals in each group was 8. No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed. The sample size (n = 8 mice per group) was chosen based on comparable published studies in this field and our own laboratory’s extensive experience with this model, which has shown consistent and detectable biological effects with this group size. All efforts were made to minimize animal use while ensuring reliable data interpretation. The sequence of processing and measurement is random, and the experiment is performed at similar times of the day. The investigators responsible for drug administration, behavioral testing, and outcome assessment were blinded to the group allocation throughout the experiment.To minimize batch effects, all surgical procedures were performed in a randomized order each day. Animal cages were rearranged weekly in a systematic manner to prevent location-based confounding.

### Experimental design
  Male C57BL/6 were acquired from Guangdong Experimental Animal Centre (Guangdong, China). All mice had free access to food and water and were maintained under 12 h of light/dark cycling conditions. Mice were randomly assigned to control, 2.0% DSS (model), 2.0% DSS+SAPA (200 mg/kg, positive control), 2.0% DSS + SAC high dose (SAC, 200 g/kg) and 2.0% DSS + SAC low dose (SAC-L, 100 g/kg) groups. SAPA and SAC were administered orally from day 1 to day 7, and the mouse model of colitis was induced with 2.0% DSS (W/V) for 7 days (from day 1 to day 7). Mice were executed 2 h after termination of SAC or SASP treatment. The purity of SAC was 98.0%. The animal study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Zhongshan Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine Affiliated to Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine (protocol code AEWC-2024364). Mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium. At the experimental endpoint, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation following anesthesia. Absence of pedal reflex was confirmed prior to cervical dislocation.The study is reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.


### Assessment of the disease activity index (DAI)
  DAI is a composite score combining weight loss percentage, stool consistency and stool bleeding. DAI = (body mass index + stool shape + bleeding)/3. The scoring system for DAI is shown in STable 1.


### Sample collection
  After treatment, the mice were anaesthetised to collect serum and then executed for dissection. Briefly, blood was collected and serum was obtained by centrifugation at 3500 rpm for 15 min at 4℃. The colon was excised to measure length. Part of the colon tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and the rest was stored at −80℃.


### Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and immunohistochemistry (IHC)
  The collected colon tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. The paraffin-embedded tissues were sectioned into 4 μm thickness and then stained using hematoxylin (H8070, Solarbio, Beijing, China) and eosin (A600190, Sangon, Shanghai, China). The morphological changes of tissues were observed under an optical microscope, and photos were taken. Colon sections were scored based on four parameters: epithelial damage (0–3), crypt damage (0–3), goblet cell depletion (0–3), and inflammatory cell infiltration (0–3), and the average score of each parameter was calculated. This scoring system has been validated in multiple murine colitis studies and reliably assesses disease severity.

  The paraffin-embedded samples were cut into Sect. (4 μm) and sealed with 3% H2O2 at room temperature to inactivate the enzyme, then boiled in 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 10 min and cooled at room temperature. The sections were blocked with normal goat serum and then incubated with anti-MUC2, anti-Nrf2 (1:200) at 4℃ overnight, followed by biotinylated secondary antibodies. The expressions of each protein in colon tissues were captured by optical microscope (magnification, 200× for colon tissues). For each section, random fields were selected and photographed under an optical microscope (at 200× magnification). To ensure objectivity, the sections were coded and subjected to blinded evaluation. The staining intensity was quantified using ImageJ software, and the results were expressed as the percentage of positively stained area.


### Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
  ELISA kits were obtained commercially, and biochemical analysis was performed according to the manufacturer’s introduction. Briefly, the colon was washed in cold phosphate buffer saline (PBS, 0.01 M, pH 7.4) and homogenized with 10 times of PBS (V/W). The samples were centrifuged at 5,000 g for 10 min at 4℃, and the supernatants were collected for biochemical analysis.

### Western blot analysis
  Western blotting was conducted under standard procedures. Colon tissues protein samples were prepared with RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor and phosphatase inhibitor (Beyotime, China) for 30 min on ice (RIPA lysis buffer: protease inhibitor: phosphatase inhibitor = 50:1:1). The protein supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 15,000 rpm at 4℃ for 10 min and the protein concentrations were measured by bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Beyotime, China). After separation by 8%−12% sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel, proteins (40 µg) were transferred to a PVDF membrane (Millipore, Boston, MA, USA). After blocking with 5% nonfat milk, the PVDF membrane was incubated with primary antibody in a cold room overnight. After three washes with phosphate buffer saline Tween (PBST) for 15 min, the PVDF membrane was incubated with secondary antibody (1:10000) at room temperature for 1 h. The immunoreactive protein bands were visualized by ECL Kit. The images were acquired using a Bio-Spectrum Gel Imaging System (UVP, USA). The integrated density of the blots was determined by the software ImageJ, and normalized to that of β-actin.


### UHPLC-QTOF-MS non-targeted metabolomics
  Collect blood samples and separate serum. Using a pipette, transfer 50 µl of serum to a 1.5 ml EP tube. Add four times the volume of acetonitrile: methanol (1:1) solution, vortex for 30 s, and sonicate for 5 min on ice. Place the tube in a −20℃ freezer for 1 h to precipitate proteins. Centrifuge at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4℃, collect the supernatant, and lyophilize it in a vacuum freeze-dryer. Reconstitute the sample by adding 100 µl of acetonitrile: water (1:1), vortex for 30 s, and sonicate for 5 min on ice. Centrifuge again at 12,000 rpm for 5 min at 4℃. Transfer the supernatant to an autosampler vial for analysis. Metabolites are separated and detected using UHPLC-QTOF-MS, with differential metabolites identified (VIP > 1) and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis performed.Statistical analysis was performed using MetaboAnalyst (http://www.metaboanalyst.ca/).


### Statistical analysis
  Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software (version 8). All data were expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). The statistical differences between the two groups were compared by Student’s t-test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for multiple comparisons were used. Differences at p < 0. 05 were considered statistically significant.

### SAC mediates the effect of Keap1/Nrf2/GPX4 signaling pathway on H2O2-stimulated IEC-6 cells
  Oxidative stress, by increasing ROS levels, depleting GSH, and inhibiting GPX4 activity, promotes ferroptosis. In this study, an IEC-6 oxidative stress model was established using H₂O₂ to induce ROS accumulation19. As shown in Fig. 1D, H₂O₂ concentrations exceeding 600 µmol/L reduced cell viability below 80%. Therefore, an oxidative stress-induced cellular injury model was established by treating IEC-6 cells with 600 µmol/L H₂O₂ for 4 h. Figure 1E indicates that SAC at or below 10 mM does not affect IEC-6 cell viability. Cellular ROS accumulation was measured using the DCFH-DA fluorescence probe to evaluate ROS-scavenging ability of SAC. Figure 2A-B shows increased ROS in H₂O₂-treated IEC-6 cells, with SAC treatment reducing ROS levels compared to untreated cells. Nrf2, a key transcription factor in the antioxidant response, translocates to the nucleus upon ROS accumulation, triggering the transcription of antioxidant genes like NQO1. Western blot analysis was used to assess the expression levels of Keap-1, Nrf2, NQO1, and GPX4, revealing SAC protective effects against H₂O₂-induced oxidative stress in IEC-6 (Fig. 2C-G). Molecular docking analysis (Fig. 2H-I) demonstrated that SAC could bind to Keap1 and GPX4. These interactions were further validated by CETSA (Figs. 2J-M).

### Therapeutic potential of SAC to UC
  The study demonstrates the therapeutic efficacy of SAC in UC. In a murine model of UC induced by DSS, SAC administration mitigates weight loss and DAI scores in a dose-dependent manner, while also preventing colon shortening (Fig. 3A-D). Histopathological analysis reveals that SAC attenuates mucosal damage and inflammatory infiltration (Fig. 3E-F). Furthermore, SAC enhances MUC2 expression, fortifying the intestinal barrier (Figs. 3G-H), and modulates the expression of inflammatory cytokines by decreasing IL-1β、IL-6 and TNF-α levels (Fig. 3I-K). In conclusion, these results indicate that SAC exerts its therapeutic effects in UC by suppressing inflammation and preserving intestinal barrier function.

### SAC modulate the Glutathione Metabolism Pathway
  To further evaluate the impact of SAC on UC-specific metabolites, we intersected the 68 upregulated metabolites in the Model group with the 39 downregulated metabolites post-SAC treatment, identifying 6 differential metabolites (Fig. 5A). Similarly, intersecting the 111 downregulated metabolites in the Model group with the upregulated metabolites post-SAC treatment yielded 30 differential metabolites (Fig. 5A). The heatmap of these differential metabolites is shown in Fig. 5C, indicating that SAC can normalize the levels of these potential biomarkers. Using MetaboAnalyst 5.0 for pathway enrichment analysis, we found that SAC could effectively modulate the glutathione metabolism pathway20,21(Supplementary material “Metabolomics”). The level of GSH critically regulates both the stability and activity of GPX4. Adequate GSH maintains GPX4 in its reduced state, preventing oxidative inactivation, whereas GSH depletion leads to decreased GPX4 activity or even complete inactivation. This process creates a vicious cycle by exacerbating intracellular oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation22.

### SAC regulates the Keap1/Nrf2/GPX4 signaling pathway to inhibit Ferroptosis
  Iron-mediated lipid peroxidation constitutes the central process of ferroptosis. The iron storage protein FTH1 dynamically regulates intracellular iron levels in response to cellular demands23. Excessive oxygen free radicals induced by intracellular iron overload further lead to the accumulation of lipid peroxidation product MDA. The antioxidant xCT/GPX4 signaling pathway has been demonstrated to be implicated in ferroptosis, where xCT protects cells from oxidative stress by importing cystine and reducing it to cysteine for GSH synthesis24. As shown in Fig. 6A-C, compared with the Control group, the DSS group exhibited significantly increased ROS levels, decreased GSH levels, and elevated MDA levels in colonic tissues, indicating that DSS induced oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation. SAC treatment markedly reduced ROS levels, restored GSH levels, and attenuated MDA accumulation, demonstrating its efficacy in alleviating oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation in UC mice. RT-qPCR analysis (Fig. 6D-I) revealed that the DSS group showed significantly upregulated Keap1 mRNA expression but downregulated Nrf2, NQO1, xCT, GPX4, and FTH1 mRNA levels compared to the Control group. SAC treatment reversed these effects, downregulating Keap1 and upregulating Nrf2, NQO1, xCT, GPX4, and FTH1 expression compared to the DSS group. Immunohistochemical staining (Fig. 6J-O) demonstrated that Keap1 protein expression was significantly upregulated, while Nrf2 and GPX4 protein levels were downregulated in DSS-treated colonic tissues versus the Control group. SAC administration increased Nrf2 and GPX4 expression while reducing Keap1 levels. Western blot analysis further confirmed SAC’s regulatory effects on the Keap1/Nrf2/GPX4 signaling pathway, showing elevated Nrf2, GPX4, NOQ1, HO1 and xCT protein levels and reduced Keap1 expression in the SAC group compared to the DSS group (Fig. 6P-Q). We hypothesize that SAC inhibits the E3 ubiquitin ligase activity of KEAP1 through interaction, thereby blocking the ubiquitination-mediated degradation of NRF2, stabilizing the NRF2 protein, and activating the transcription of various protective genes, including antioxidant enzymes. In conclusion, SAC exerts antioxidant and anti-ferroptotic effects by modulating the Keap1/Nrf2/GPX4 signaling axis.

### SAC activates the Keap1/Nrf2/GPX4 pathway to inhibit ferroptosis induced by Erastin
  To further confirm the anti-ferroptotic effect of SAC, we induced ferroptosis in IEC-6 cells using Erastin. The cell viability assay (Fig. 7A) demonstrated that Erastin treatment reduced cellular activity in a dose-dependent manner, with IEC-6 cell survival rates dropping below 80% at Erastin concentrations exceeding 5 µM. Intracellular ROS levels were quantified via fluorescent probe detection (Fig. 7B-C). Compared to the control group, Erastin-treated cells exhibited significantly elevated ROS levels, confirming successful induction of oxidative stress and ferroptosis. SAC co-treatment dose-dependently attenuated Erastin-induced ROS accumulation, as evidenced by progressively reduced ROS levels with increasing SAC concentrations. Western blot analysis (Fig. 7D-H) revealed that Erastin upregulated Keap1 protein expression while downregulating Nrf2, NQO1, and GPX4. SAC co-treatment counteracted these effects, restoring Nrf2/NQO1/GPX4 expression and suppressing Keap1 levels. These findings demonstrate that SAC inhibits Erastin-induced ferroptosis through activation of the Keap1/Nrf2/GPX4 signaling axis.

## Discussion
  Oxidative stress drives the pathogenesis and progression of UC through multifaceted mechanisms25,26. Concurrently, excessive accumulation of ROS and dysregulation of the antioxidant system directly drive the exacerbation of lipid peroxidation in intestinal epithelial cells, thereby triggering ferroptosis27. Targeting the modulation of redox homeostasis to disrupt the vicious cycle between redox imbalance and inflammation can effectively alleviate ulcerative colitis (UC). SAC, a naturally occurring antioxidant, possesses a wide range of biological activities, including antioxidation, anti-inflammation, and anti-apoptosis effects. In this study, we explored in depth the role of SAC in regulating the Keap1/Nrf2/GPX4 signaling pathway to restore redox homeostasis and inhibit ferroptosis. Through experimental methods such as molecular docking, CESTA, and non-targeted metabolomics, we found that SAC can act on the Keap1/Nrf2/GPX4 signaling axis and regulate glutathione metabolism, effectively inhibiting ferroptosis and oxidative damage in colonic epithelial cells, thereby alleviating UC.

  In cells induced by H2O2, SAC significantly reduces ROS levels in IEC-6. Further mechanistic studies revealed the key role of SAC in regulating the Keap1/Nrf2/GPX4 signaling pathway. Under normal physiological conditions, Keap1, as the substrate adaptor of the E3 ubiquitin ligase complex, maintains intracellular redox balance by promoting the ubiquitination and degradation of Nrf2. When cells are exposed to oxidative stress, the conformation of Keap1 changes, allowing Nrf2 to stabilize and translocate into the nucleus, where it activates the expression of a series of antioxidant genes28. Our study indicates that SAC interferes with the interaction between Keap1 and Nrf2, stabilizing Nrf2 and promoting its accumulation in the nucleus, thereby activating the expression of downstream antioxidant genes such as NQO1 and GPX4. Molecular docking revealed that SAC can directly bind to Keap1 and GPX4 proteins. Using CETSA experiments, we further confirmed that SAC can directly act on Keap1 and GPX4 proteins. To further verify the therapeutic efficacy of SAC in vivo and assess its effect on DSS-induced colitis, we conducted animal experiments. Utilizing a DSS-induced colitis mouse model, we evaluated the impact of SAC on DAI scores, colon length, histopathological changes, and the expression of inflammatory factors. The animal experiment results showed that SAC treatment significantly improved weight loss, increased DAI, shortened colon length, and histopathological damage induced by DSS, and was able to inhibit the expression of inflammatory factors such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, and restore the expression of MUC2, promoting the recovery of the mucosal barrier. To assess the impact of SAC on UC and explore its potential mechanisms of action, we further conducted metabolomic analysis. Through differential metabolites, we enriched the glutathione metabolic pathway. GSH metabolism plays a key role in the cellular antioxidant defense system. GSH, a pivotal antioxidant, eliminates ROS through redox cycling between its reduced (GSH) and oxidized (GSSG) forms, thereby preserving intracellular redox homeostasis29. Emerging evidence delineates an intricate dynamic interplay between glutathione metabolic circuitry and ferroptosis modulation30. GSH depletion triggers ferroptosis by inactivating GPX4 and promoting the accumulation of cytotoxic lipid peroxides10. In UC murine models, colonic tissues exhibited significantly elevated levels of ROS and MDA, alongside depleted GSH content. SAC treatment effectively reduced ROS and MDA accumulation, restored GSH levels, and activated the Keap1/Nrf2/GPX4 signaling axis, markedly upregulating key genes including FTH1, xCT, NQO1, and GPX4. Studies utilizing GPX4 degradation to induce ferroptosis revealed that lipid peroxidation propagates ferroptotic signaling through intercellular transfer via plasma membrane contact, amplifying necrotic tissue expansion31. Thus, ferroptosis exacerbates intestinal barrier dysfunction and aggravates UC progression, whereas the intervention of SAC against ferroptosis is beneficial for promoting the healing of the intestinal mucosal barrier.

  In the context of ferroptosis, the effect of SAC is particularly remarkable. Erastin, a ferroptosis inducer, inhibits the activity of the cystine/glutamate antiporter, blocking the synthesis of glutathione and weakening the cell’s antioxidant capacity, ultimately leading to ferroptosis. Our study shows that SAC can effectively reverse Erastin-induced ferroptosis by activating the Keap1/Nrf2/GPX4 signaling pathway, enhancing the cell’s antioxidant capacity, reducing lipid peroxidation, and thus protecting cells from ferroptotic damage.

  However, this study has not yet elucidated the specific binding site of SAC to KEAP1. Future studies will further validate the direct target of SAC and its specific regulatory mechanism on the Keap1/Nrf2 signaling pathway by constructing KEAP1 mutants and performing nuclear/cytoplasmic fractionation.

  In summary, our study demonstrates that S-allyl-L-cysteine (SAC) alleviates UC by suppressing ferroptosis and oxidative damage in colonic epithelial cells through the Keap1/Nrf2/GPX4 signaling axis-mediated regulation of glutathione (GSH) metabolism. Mechanistically, SAC directly interacts with Keap1 to stabilize Nrf2, thereby enhancing the transcription of antioxidant genes (GPX4 and NQO1) and restoring GSH biosynthesis. And sufficient GSH will also affect the stability and antioxidant activity of GPX4. Notably, SAC also exhibited significant anti-inflammatory effects. SAC disrupts the “oxidative stress-inflammation” vicious cycle by suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β). As a multi-target therapeutic agent bridging antioxidant defense and anti-inflammatory intervention, SAC provides a safe and effective adjuvant therapeutic option for UC management (Fig. 8).

## Conclusions
  Our findings demonstrate that S-allyl-L-cysteine (SAC) ameliorates ulcerative colitis (UC) by targeting the Keap1/Nrf2/GPX4 axis to suppress ferroptosis and oxidative injury. Mechanistically, SAC directly interacts with Keap1 to promote Nrf2 into the nucleus, enhance the transcription of antioxidant genes (such as GPX4 and NQO1), and restore the biosynthesis of GSH. The replenished GSH pool further enhances GPX4 stability and antioxidant capacity. Notably, SAC concurrently attenuates inflammatory responses by downregulating pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β). As a multi-target therapeutic agent bridging antioxidant defense and anti-inflammatory intervention, SAC represents a safe and effective adjunctive strategy for UC management.
