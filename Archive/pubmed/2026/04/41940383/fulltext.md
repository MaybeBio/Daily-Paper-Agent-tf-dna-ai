# Machine learning integrated bulk and single cell transcriptomic analyses reveal oxidative stress associated mitochondrial polarization signatures in diabetic foot ulcers


## Abstract
  Diabetic foot ulcers (DFU) are a major complication of diabetes, and its pathogenesis remains incompletely elucidated. Converging evidence indicates that oxidative stress and dysregulated mitochondrial polarization participate in DFU progression, nominating these processes as therapeutically actionable targets. This study integrates bulk and single-cell transcriptomic data with machine learning to reconstruct cross-scale, cell type–resolved molecular atlases and regulatory networks. Macrophages and fibroblasts emerged as communication hubs, dominating pathway enrichment and ligand–receptor programs such as macrophage migration inhibitory factor signaling pathway (MIF), ANNEXIN signaling pathway, and COMPLEMENT signaling pathway. Peptidylprolyl isomerase F (PPIF), which encodes cyclophilin D (CypD) and apolipoprotein E (APOE) were further prioritized as putative drivers within macrophages and fibroblasts, and a five-gene classifier was derived with robust performance (internal/external AUC = 0.833/0.933). Within DFU lesions, under the control of non-coding RNA circuitry, SOX5 may shape the inflammatory microenvironment, APOE may participate in lipid-metabolic remodeling, and PPIF (CypD) likely links reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation to a p53-dependent mitochondrial death pathway (necroptosis/apoptosis). Orthogonal validation showed significantly increased CypD in diabetic foot ulcer skin (DFUS) and diabetic foot ulcer tendon (DFUT) relative to diabetic foot skin (DFS) and DFT (Diabetic foot tendon), with up-regulated p53 and Cytc and down-regulated ApoE in DFUS; in primary foot-skin fibroblasts, a high-glucose plus tert-butyl hydroperoxide (HG+TBHP) model reproduced elevated ROS, loss of mitochondrial Δψm (mitochondrial membrane potential), growth restriction, and apoptosis, supporting a ROS–CypD/mPTP (mitochondrial permeability transition pore)–Δψm depolarization–p53/Cytc apoptosis axis. The delineated PPIF-centered regulatory network includes upstream transcription factors CEBPB/REL/SPI1 and a downstream ceRNA axis comprising miR-128-3p/miR-23a-3p–long non-coding RNA OIP5-AS1. Additionally, the significant role of polarization-specific reprogramming in regulating macrophage function highlights therapeutic strategies focused on metabolic reprogramming and inhibition of the PPIF/mPTP pathway. Collectively, a cell type–resolved molecular map of DFU is provided, healing-relevant cell populations and regulatory circuits are prioritized, and a translational, testable intervention framework is proposed.


## Introduction
  Diabetic foot ulcers (DFU) is a severe complication of diabetes, with a lifetime risk of 19–34%. Approximately 50–60% of cases develop secondary infection, ~20% of moderate-to-severe infections progress to lower-limb amputation, and the 5-year mortality after major amputation exceeds 70% (Armstrong et al., 2023; McDermott et al., 2023). DFU arises from the interplay among peripheral neuropathy, vasculopathy, and immune-mediated inflammation (Armstrong, Boulton & Bus, 2017; Chang & Nguyen, 2021). In DFU, multiple cellular lineages—including fibroblasts, macrophages, T cells, and keratinocytes—together with oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, drive disease progression (Theocharidis et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2022). Disturbed glucose metabolism impairs mitochondrial OXPHOS and sustains reactive ROS production, destabilizing the mitochondrial membrane and triggering mitochondrial Δψm depolarization with downstream pathological sequelae (Bradford et al., 2024). Accordingly, targeting oxidative stress and mitochondrial polarization is considered a translationally promising avenue for intervention.

  Bioinformatics has been widely applied to decipher complex disease ecosystems; wound healing proceeds through inflammatory, proliferative, and remodeling phases and exhibits pronounced lineage- and stage-specificity. However, publicly available transcriptomic datasets are largely derived from chronic ulcer tissue, and heterogeneity in staging and sampling constrains the resolution of stage-specific programs. How oxidative stress and mitochondrial polarization sculpt functional states in fibroblasts and macrophages—and how these processes engage cross-lineage interactions with keratinocytes and T cells—remains insufficiently defined at a cell type-resolved level. In contrast to the work by Theocharidis et al. (2022), which focused on the single-cell transcriptomic landscape of diabetic foot ulcers (DFU), and the study by Chen & Zou (2024), which combined bulk and single-cell transcriptomics to investigate non-healing mechanisms in DFUs, our study offers a unique multi-modal approach that integrates bulk and single-cell RNA sequencing with machine learning to construct a detailed molecular atlas centered around Peptidylprolyl isomerase F (PPIF), which encodes cyclophilin D (CypD) (Chen & Zou, 2024; Theocharidis et al., 2022).

  We therefore hypothesize that hyperglycemia-related oxidative stress reprograms the oxidative-stress-mitochondrial-polarization circuitry in fibroblasts and macrophages, precipitating depolarization-associated apoptosis, disrupting immune-inflammatory homeostasis, and prolonging the inflammatory phase to impede transition to proliferation and remodeling. To test this hypothesis, we integrated bulk transcriptomes from DFUS and DFS, systematically identified differentially expressed genes related to oxidative stress and mitochondrial polarization, and constructed multi-layer regulatory networks. We further combined scRNA-seq to annotate cellular subpopulations and pathway activities, infer lineage trajectories, and resolve ligand-receptor communication (Aibar et al., 2017; Jin, Plikus & Nie, 2024; Park et al., 2018), and used transcription-factor enrichment together with competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) analysis to delineate upstream and post-transcriptional regulation of key genes (Aibar et al., 2017). Finally, we trained machine-learning classifiers to select discriminative feature genes and candidate biomarkers for subtyping and risk stratification, while pre-specifying orthogonal validation to bridge computational predictions with subsequent experimental assays (Lai et al., 2022).


## Materials and Methods

### Data acquisition
  GSE134431 (Sawaya et al., 2020), GSE80178 (Ramirez et al., 2018), GSE68183 (Ramirez et al., 2015), and GSE165816 (Theocharidis et al., 2022) were retrieved from GEO. Microarray/high-throughput datasets included 14 DFS and 19 DFUS samples, and single-cell datasets included eight DFS and five DFUS samples. A total of 1,225 OS genes and 702 MP genes (relevance score ≥7) were compiled from GeneCards.


### Processing of microarray/high-throughput data
  Normalization was performed with limma (v3.58.1 (Ritchie et al., 2015)). GSE80178 and GSE68183 were integrated with batch-effect correction to generate GSE80178-68183. Using p-value < 0.05 and |log2FC| ≥ 0.585 (log2[1.5]), 3,889 DEGs were identified in GSE80178-68183 and 3,773 in GSE134431; both DEG sets underwent GSEA (Subramanian et al., 2005). Intersecting DEGs with OS- and MP-gene lists yielded 18 and 46 OMRGs, respectively, followed by GO/KEGG enrichment and STRING-based PPI analyses visualized in R (v4.3.1) and Cytoscape (v3.10.1) (Walter, Sanchez-Cabo & Ricote, 2015; Wu et al., 2021b; Yu et al., 2015).


### Single-cell data processing
  Data from GSE165816 were imported into R (v4.3.1) to create Seurat (v4.4.0) objects (Hao et al., 2021). For QC, we computed the proportion of counts mapping to mitochondrial genes (mt_percent) and to canonical haemoglobin genes (HB_percent) for each cell. Cells were removed if nFeature_RNA ≤ 300 or ≥5,000, if nCount_RNA ≤ 1,000 or above the 97th percentile of the nCount_RNA distribution, or if mt_percent ≥ 10% or HB_percent ≥ 3%. To address doublets, we employed DoubletFinder (v2.0.3) with a threshold of 0.05 for doublet prediction probabilities. Cells with probabilities above this threshold were excluded. Filtered matrices were normalized, integrated with Harmony batch correction (Korsunsky et al., 2019), and embedded by PCA followed by UMAP and t-SNE; clusters were annotated using canonical markers. CellChat (v1.6.1) was used to infer ligand-receptor communication (Jin, Plikus & Nie, 2024). Monocle3 (v1.3.7) was applied to construct pseudotime trajectories (Cao et al., 2019). Seurat::FindMarkers identified 158 DEGs (Wilcoxon) between DFU and DFS fibroblasts, and intersection with the 46 OMRGs nominated apolipoprotein E (APOE). Pathway scoring was performed with AUCell (v1.24.0) on a random set of 1,500 cells for visualization. SCENIC was used to resolve cell type-specific regulons (Aibar et al., 2017). KEGG enrichment of CEBPB (n = 218) and SPI1 (n = 695) target genes was conducted on the WeiShengXin platform.


### Construction of ceRNA network
  ENCORI and miRWalk were integrated to obtain IMGs-25 and SML-133, which were merged into GML-462. ceRNA interactions were inferred and visualized in Cytoscape (v3.10.1). lncRNA localization was annotated with RNALocate, and cytoplasmic entries were retained to construct a PPIF-centered gene-miRNA-lncRNA network.


### Machine-learning analyses of OS-MP genes
  In GSE134431, the 46 OMRGs underwent limma-based differential and correlation analyses, and heatmaps plus chromosomal distributions were generated. Immune-cell proportions were estimated using CIBERSORT (LM22, p < 0.05), and Spearman correlations were calculated between OMRGs and infiltrating immune cells.


### Construction of DFU prediction models
  ConsensusClusterPlus (v1.66.0) was used to stratify DFU samples into two OMRG-defined molecular subtypes (reps = 1,000, pItem = 0.8, pFeature = 1, k-means clustering with Euclidean distance; seed = 123,456) (Wilkerson & Hayes, 2010); GSVA compared pathway activities (Hanzelmann, Castelo & Guinney, 2013); and WGCNA identified co-expression modules (Langfelder & Horvath, 2008). For supervised subtype prediction, we implemented eight classifiers using caret (v6.0-94): random forest (RF) (Rigatti, 2017), support vector machine with radial kernel (SVM), XGBoost with DART boosting (XGB), generalized linear model with binomial link (GLM), k-nearest neighbors (KNN), single hidden-layer neural network (NNET), regularized logistic regression via glmnet (elastic-net; referred to as Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) when alpha = 1), and decision tree (DT; rpart). To ensure reproducibility, a fixed random seed was set prior to partitioning and model training (set.seed = 123). Expression matrices were arranged as samples × genes, and class labels were parsed from sample identifiers. Data were split into training (70%) and held-out testing (30%) sets using stratified sampling (createDataPartition), preserving class proportions. Within the training set, hyperparameter tuning was performed by 5-fold cross-validation (trainControl: repeatedcv, number = 5; savePredictions = TRUE) with stratified fold generation for classification; no additional class-imbalance correction (up-/down-sampling, SMOTE, or class weights) was applied in the current implementation. Model-specific tuned hyperparameters were explicitly as follows: RF tuned mtry; SVM (svmRadial) tuned C and sigma; XGB (xgbDART) tuned nrounds, max_depth, eta, gamma, colsample_bytree, min_child_weight, subsample, rate_drop, and skip_drop; GLM (binomial) did not require hyperparameter tuning; KNN tuned k; NNET tuned size and decay; glmnet tuned alpha and lambda; and DT (rpart) tuned cp. Models generated class probabilities on the held-out test set, and receiver operating characteristics (ROC) curves/AUC were computed using pROC (v1.18.5) (Robin et al., 2011). Model-agnostic permutation feature importance was estimated using DALEX with RMSE as the loss function, and the top predictor genes were retained for downstream clinical modeling. A nomogram was constructed using rms (v6.7-0) based on the selected signature and evaluated by bootstrap calibration (B = 1,000) and decision curve analysis; external ROC validation was performed in the independent GEO cohort (GSE80178-68183) after normalization.


### Clinical data and eligibility criteria
  Demographic and perioperative data were prospectively recorded and anonymized. Skin and tendon biopsies were collected after ethics approval (Affiliated Hospital of Gansu University of Chinese Medicine; Ethics [2024]125) and written informed consent; specimens were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C; clinically infected ulcers were excluded. Eligibility followed IWGDF: inclusion—type 2 diabetes (WHO/ADA), Wagner grade II-IV, duration ≥2 weeks, area ≥ 0.5 cm2, HbA1c ≤12%, ABI < 0.9 or >1.3, or TcPO2 ≥ 30 mmHg; exclusion—systemic contraindications, non-diabetic ulcers, uncontrolled infection, and procedural constraints. No a priori sample-size calculation was performed; sample size was limited by ethics and availability.


### Western blot
  Tissues were processed in ice-cold RIPA, clarified, and quantified by BCA. Samples were mixed with 5× loading buffer (4:1), denatured at 100 °C for 5 min, separated by 10% SDS-PAGE (80 V/30 min; 120 V/90 min), and transferred at 300 mA for 30 min. Membranes were blocked (5% skim milk/TBST, 1–2 h), probed with antibodies to Cyclophilin D, Apolipoprotein E, p53 and Cytochrome c (overnight, 4 °C), incubated with HRP-anti-rabbit secondary antibodies (1–2 h, room temperature), and visualized by ECL. After stripping, membranes were reprobed with β-actin, and molecular weights were calibrated using pre-stained markers. Independent biological specimens were used for each group, with three technical replicates per blot. Quantification employed Image Lab (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).


### Isolation and culture of primary fibroblasts
  Cell procurement was performed using discarded surgical skin that was briefly disinfected with alcohol, rinsed three times with physiological saline, immersed in 0.25% chloramphenicol solution for 30 min, minced into ~2 mm3 pieces, and separately digested with collagenase and 0.25% trypsin at 37 °C for 30 min each. After centrifugation, the pellet was washed with PBS and recentrifuged, resuspended in complete medium, and appropriate volumes were transferred to culture flasks; cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Human skin sampling and use were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Gansu University of Chinese Medicine (Ethics [2024]125) in strict accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Experiments were supported by the Gansu Joint Scientific Research Fund (“Mechanisms by which Shengji Yuhong Ointment promotes healing of refractory DFU wounds”; Project No. 24JRRA881). Reagents included low-glucose medium (1.0 g/L D-glucose (+), L-glutamine (+), sodium pyruvate (−), HEPES (+); final glucose 5.5 mmol/L) and high-glucose medium (4.5 g/L D-glucose (+), L-glutamine (+), sodium pyruvate (+), HEPES (+); final glucose 25 mmol/L); serum was adjusted with exosome-depleted FBS to a final concentration of 10%, and antibiotics consisted of 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Cat#15140122; Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA). Culture conditions were 37 °C and 5% CO2; assays were performed at 80–90% confluence using passages 3–5.


### Fibroblast model and grouping
  Four conditions were used: physiological control (LG, 5.5 mmol/L, 48 h), oxidative control (LG+TBHP, 30 μM, 48 h), high-glucose (HG, 25 mmol/L, 48 h), and model (HG+TBHP, 30 μM, 48 h) to induce fibroblast injury and apoptosis.


### EdU proliferation assay
  Fibroblasts (5 × 103 cells/well) were seeded into 24-well plates and incubated for 48 h. After PBS washes, cells were stained with EdU (1:1,000) for 2 h at 37 °C in the dark, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 min, washed, and counterstained with DAPI for 15 min. Inverted fluorescence microscopy showed blue nuclei and green EdU-positive cells.


### Phalloidin staining
  Cells were incubated for 48 h, washed, and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. After 0.5% Triton X-100 permeabilization (10 min, 37 °C), iFluor™488-phalloidin staining (2 h, 37 °C) and DAPI counterstaining (10 min) were performed. Imaging revealed blue nuclei and green F-actin.


### Mitochondrial ROS assay
  Following 48 h incubation and PBS washes, cells were stained with ROS working solution (1:1,000) for 30 min at 37 °C and imaged; intracellular ROS appeared as green fluorescence.


### JC-1
  Cells were stained with JC-1 working solution (1:160:40 as specified) for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark, washed with pre-chilled buffer, and counterstained with DAPI (15 min). Imaging revealed blue nuclei, red JC-1 aggregates (polarized mitochondria), and green monomers (depolarized mitochondria).


### Flow cytometry
  Fibroblasts (5 × 104 cells/well) were cultured for 48 h, detached at ~80% confluence with EDTA-free trypsin, collected, adjusted to 2 × 106/mL, washed, resuspended in pre-chilled binding buffer, and stained with Annexin V-FITC/PI for 15 min in the dark. Apoptosis was measured by flow cytometry.


### Statistical analysis
  For transcriptomics, limma linear models with empirical Bayes moderation were applied with Benjamini-Hochberg (BH)-adjusted FDR < 0.05; single-cell data were preprocessed with Seurat/Harmony, differential expression was tested by the Wilcoxon method, and permutation tests (n = 1,000) were used for CellChat and WGCNA. Wet-lab statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 10.0 and R/RStudio (v4.3.1): For independent samples, normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test in the Normality and Lognormality Tests module. For homogeneity of variance and significance testing, when data satisfied normality and equal variances (F-test p > 0.05), an unpaired t-test assuming equal standard deviations between groups was applied. When the data were non-normally distributed, the Mann–Whitney test (unpaired, nonparametric) was used to compare ranks, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05. one-to-many comparisons within four groups used two-sided Dunnett’s tests; pairwise comparisons among four groups used Welch’s t-tests with Holm correction; overall effects were assessed by Welch’s ANOVA. Machine learning (LASSO/RF) used five-fold cross-validation; performance was evaluated by area under the ROC curve (AUC) with the pROC package, and feature importance was ranked by RMSE.


## Results

### Identification of key pathways in DFU
  GSEA of DFU differential-expression data (Figs. 1A, 1B) revealed activation of RNA degradation, the spliceosome, the cell cycle, and p53 signaling, indicating enhanced RNA metabolism (Wang, Gao & Yang, 2023), context-dependent regenerative programs (Baek et al., 2022), and DNA-damage surveillance/clearance processes (Wansleben et al., 2013). Conversely, neuroactive ligand-receptor, complement/coagulation, and tyrosine-metabolism pathways were downregulated, pointing to impaired neural function (Bortell et al., 2017), immune disequilibrium (Chen et al., 2020), and altered energy metabolism (Huang et al., 2024). In single-cell profiles, cell types were annotated and AUCell scoring was performed (Figs. 1C, 1D, 1N). Type I diabetes signatures were enriched in macrophages and B cells (Fig. 1D), reflecting autoimmunity-related programs rather than pancreatic targeting (Meyer et al., 2016; Redondo & Eisenbarth, 2002). Type II diabetes signatures were prominent in macrophages and fibroblasts (Fig. 1E), consistent with metabolic dysregulation and chronic inflammation (Phu et al., 2022; Yaney et al., 1995). Glycolysis/gluconeogenesis was active across multiple cell types (Fig. 1F), indicating high energy demand and metabolic reprogramming (Gimenez-Cassina et al., 2014). Oxidative phosphorylation and calcium signaling were enriched in keratinocytes, smooth muscle cells, fibroblasts, and macrophages (Figs. 1G, 1H), linking energy metabolism with repair and immune regulation (Cox et al., 2016; He et al., 2024b). In DFU, impaired mitochondrial OXPHOS together with elevated ROS drove oxidative stress; downstream injury involved p53-related mitochondria-dependent necrosis and apoptosis (He et al., 2024b). Apoptosis signatures were enriched in macrophages, T cells, and fibroblasts; both extrinsic and intrinsic pathways were evident in macrophages and keratinocytes. Activation of the p53 pathway and TP53-impairment molecular features were observed in smooth muscle cells, keratinocytes, and a subset of fibroblasts (Figs. 1I–1L) (Zavileyskiy & Bunik, 2022). Immunologically, complement/coagulation was enriched in fibroblasts and macrophages, whereas cytokine-receptor and JAK-STAT signaling were prominent in macrophages and T cells. TLR2/4 and MAPK were enriched in macrophages and fibroblasts (Figs. 1M–1O), supporting roles in DFU progression and inflammatory regulation (Guan et al., 2021; Sharifiaghdam et al., 2022).


### Intercellular interactions
  Ligand-receptor communication in DFU single-cell data was analyzed using CellChat. Secreted signaling predominated (61.8%), followed by ECM-receptor interactions and cell-cell contact; heteromeric complexes accounted for 47.9%, and most pathways were curated from KEGG (73%), underscoring the importance of secreted signals in DFU (Fig. 2A). Fibroblasts and macrophages communicated most frequently and interacted broadly with T cells, smooth muscle cells, and endothelial cells (Fig. 2B) (Aitcheson et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2020b; Liu et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023; Zeng et al., 2022). Fibro1 communicated with immune cells via CXCL and macrophage migration inhibitory factor signaling pathway (MIF), whereas Fibro2 primarily executed matrix functions (Fig. 2C). M1 corresponded to TNF/IL1B (Kasiewicz & Whitehead, 2016; Zhang et al., 2024), whereas M2 corresponded to TGF-β (Fig. 2D) (Geng et al., 2023). NMF identified two outgoing and two incoming patterns: Outgoing-1 (structural cells such as fibroblasts and smooth muscle cells secreting PDGF/FGF) and Outgoing-2 (regulation by immune cells including macrophages and T lymphocytes) (Figs. 2E, 2F) (Zubair & Ahmad, 2019; Zubair et al., 2013). Incoming-1 (inflammatory cells receiving MIF, ANNEXIN, and COMPLEMENT) and Incoming-2 (matrix/structural cells receiving PDGF and non-canonical WNT) (Figs. 2G, 2H) (Sumaiya et al., 2022). Secretion profiles suggested fibroblast autocrine loops (ncWNT/MK) and paracrine delivery of MIF to macrophages (Figs. 2F, 2H). CellChat grouped ligand-receptor pathways by structural and functional similarity (Fig. 3A). By structural similarity, Group 1 comprised soluble cytokines/chemokines with inflammatory regulatory roles (MIF, GAL, MK) (Choi et al., 2024; Sumaiya et al., 2022; Tao et al., 2020); Group 2 included immune modulators and surface receptors with transmembrane/extracellular domains (IL2, COMPLEMENT) (He et al., 2024a); Group 3 consisted of growth factors and matrix-associated molecules involved in proliferation, differentiation, and tissue remodeling (PARs, SEMA3s) (Meng et al., 2022; Peach et al., 2022); and Group 4 contained structurally conserved growth factors/chemokines (VEGF, TGFB) that regulate angiogenesis and cell migration (Dhamodharan et al., 2019a; Kerstan et al., 2022; Song et al., 2023). By functional similarity, Group 1 comprised pro-inflammatory signals (IL1, TNF) (Deng et al., 2022); Group 2 related to proliferative and angiogenic signaling (CXCL, FGF) (Dhamodharan et al., 2019a); Group 3 encompassed pathways mediating angiogenesis, metabolic regulation, and cell migration (VEGF, APJ) (Dhamodharan et al., 2019a; Hong et al., 2023); Group 4, including PERIOSTIN, participated in matrix remodeling and tissue repair with prominent roles in wound healing and fibrosis (Kormann et al., 2020); and Group 5 featured immune-regulatory and differentiation-related signals such as CHEMERIN and BAFF that may be critical for immune homeostasis and repair (Dhamodharan et al., 2019b; Lin et al., 2022). Overall, CellChat analysis indicated that macrophages and fibroblasts act as communication hubs coordinating immune regulation and tissue repair in DFU.


### Key regulatory factors of macrophages and fibroblasts
  Within the MIF pathway (Fig. 3B), fibroblasts predominantly engaged in autocrine signaling while receiving paracrine inputs from other cells; concomitantly, they delivered paracrine cues to M1/M2 macrophages and B cells, and these immune cells possessed autocrine capability and expressed receptors for paracrine signals. This pattern suggests that fibroblast-derived MIF signaling can drive immune inflammation and cell migration, thereby shaping the DFU microenvironment (Kang & Bucala, 2019; Nickel et al., 2021; Sumaiya et al., 2022; Ye et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2005). ANNEXIN signaling facilitated M1-M2 crosstalk in cell migration, signal transduction, and inflammatory responses (Huang et al., 2020a; Wu et al., 2021a; Zhao et al., 2014); Fibro1 interacted strongly with macrophages, with particularly prominent paracrine outputs to M1 (Fig. 3C). ANXA1-FPR1 constituted a key ligand-receptor pair (Figs. 3E–3H). COMPLEMENT signaling occurred predominantly between fibroblasts and M1/M2 macrophages (Fig. 3I); fibroblast-derived C3 engaged macrophage receptors to form C3-C3AR1, C3-ITGAM/ITGB2, and C3-ITGAX/ITGB2 pairs (Figs. 3D, 3J–3L). These interactions activate the host defense system, clearing pathogens and damaged cells via the complement cascade to combat infection (Leaper, Assadian & Edmiston, 2015), while promoting inflammation and maintaining tissue homeostasis. However, excessive complement activation may also propagate chronic inflammation and tissue injury (Foley et al., 2015; Leaper, Assadian & Edmiston, 2015).


### Identification of oxidative stress and mitochondrial polarization-related genes
  Results support a central role for oxidative stress and mitochondrial polarization in DFU (Ding et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2022). Intersecting DFU DEGs with the OM gene list yielded 18 and 46 OMRGs (Fig. 3M), and APOE was prioritized as a significantly dysregulated fibroblast-associated gene (Figs. 3N–3Q). HIF1A, PPIF, CYCS, and IL1B were upregulated, whereas TP53 and APOE were downregulated (Figs. 4A, 4B). IL1B-APOE and PPIF-TXN showed positive correlations (ρ ≈ 0.63), whereas PPIF-APOE showed a negative correlation (ρ ≈ −0.60) (Fig. 4C). APOE correlated positively with M2 macrophages, and PPIF correlated positively with mast cells; PPARGC1A/TRAP1/BCL2/CYCS correlated negatively with immune infiltration (Figs. 4D, 4E). TP53, PPIF, BAK1, and BCL2 were associated with mitophagy, chemical carcinogenesis-reactive oxygen species, and responses to ROS (Fig. 4F), suggesting that through complex regulatory relationships they may jointly promote mitochondrial polarization abnormalities and oxidative-stress responses, augment inflammatory activity and p53-related mitochondrial apoptotic pathways, and diminish antioxidant capacity. PPI analysis identified TP53, CYCS, BCL2, HIF1A, IL1B, PPIF, and APOE as hubs (Fig. 4G) (Vaseva et al., 2012; Zavileyskiy & Bunik, 2022). Given the prominence of PPIF/APOE, reclustering and annotation were performed at the single-cell level (Figs. 4H–4J). Based on gene enrichment during cellular differentiation and pseudotime trajectory analyses (Figs. 4K–4M), PPIF was predominantly observed in M1 macrophages (also detected in fibroblasts), whereas APOE was enriched in Fibro1 and expressed across multiple lineages (Figs. 4N–4Q). These observations suggest that PPIF and APOE have cell-context-dependent roles in oxidative stress and mitochondrial polarization abnormalities in DFU (Nakagawa et al., 2005; Nankar et al., 2020).


### Transcription factor enrichment and ceRNA networks for PPIF
  PPIF (cyclophilin D, CypD) localizes to the mitochondrial matrix and regulates the mPTP; Ca2+ overload or oxidative stress can trigger mPTP opening and mitochondrial depolarization, thereby activating apoptotic or necrotic programs (Marcu et al., 2015; Nakagawa et al., 2005). Transcription factor enrichment (NES > 3) indicated that CEBPB, REL, and SPI1 are putative upstream regulators of PPIF. CEBPB displayed higher expression/activity in macrophages (M1 > M2) and keratinocytes (Figs. 4R–4W); REL was active in M1/M2 macrophages and T/B cells (Figs. 5A–5F); SPI1 exhibited prominent activity/expression in M1/M2 macrophages (Figs. 5G–5L). To further interrogate whether CEBPB, REL, and SPI1 regulate oxidative stress and mitochondrial polarization in DFU via PPIF (CypD), KEGG pathway enrichment was performed for high-confidence target sets: CEBPB targets (n = 218) were enriched for chemical carcinogenesis-ROS pathways (Fig. 5M), whereas SPI1 targets (n = 695) were enriched for calcium signaling and neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation (Figs. 5N, 5O). A ceRNA network centered on PPIF (CypD)—involving miR-128-3p, miR-23a-3p, and lncRNA OIP5-AS1—was delineated to predict downstream regulatory mechanisms (Fig. 5P). Given the role of macrophage polarization in the chronic inflammatory milieu of DFU (Aitcheson et al., 2021), transcription factors in macrophages (M1, M2) were ranked by activity and specificity, yielding the top 10 most active and top 30 most specific TFs for each subset (Figs. 5Q, 5R and 5S, 5T). Among them, the strongly specific and highly active regulators in M1 macrophages were NFKB1 (31 genes), FOSL2 (19 genes), and ETV3_extended (13 genes), whereas in M2 macrophages MAF_extended (201 genes) predominated. Collectively, these findings suggest that reprogramming these TFs may perturb the M1/M2 balance in DFU, a hypothesis that warrants functional validation (Takahashi et al., 2007).


### Constructing DFU predictive models using machine learning
  To delineate expression patterns of oxidative-mitochondrial metabolism (OM)-related genes in DFU, OMRGs expression profiles were used to cluster 13 DFU samples with ConsensusClusterPlus (k = 2; stable CDF and consensus ≥ 0.8) (Figs. 6A–6D), and PCA further separated them into C1 (n = 7) and C2 (n = 6) (Fig. 6E). C1 exhibited higher expression of CYP24A1, MFN1, and MAPT, whereas C2 showed higher expression of IL1B and PPARGC1A (Figs. 6F, 6G). GSVA indicated differences in fatty acid metabolism for C1 and complement/coagulation for C2 (Fig. 6H); functional analyses suggested an association of C1 with H4K20 monomethyltransferase activity and of C2 with macrophage migration (Fig. 6I). Accordingly, Cluster 2 may play a more prominent role in immune responses. WGCNA (β = 38) identified nine modules (Figs. 6J–6M), with the green module (686 genes) showing the strongest correlation with DFU (Figs. 6N–6O); OM subtype-stratified WGCNA yielded 17 modules, among which the purple module (1,362 genes) was most associated with the OM subtypes (Figs. 6P–6U), and their intersection produced 16 subtype-specific DEGs (Fig. 6V). This panel of 16 genes was subsequently treated as a fixed, a priori–defined feature set for downstream machine-learning modelling. Eight machine-learning models (RF, LASSO, DT, SVM, GLM, XGB, KNN, NNET; caret) were trained on the 16-gene panel. LASSO and RF yielded the lowest residuals (Figs. 7A, 7B), and feature importance was ranked by RMSE (Fig. 7C). Five-fold cross-validation (pROC) indicated that LASSO achieved the best discriminative performance (AUC = 0.833) (Fig. 7D), from which five genes—SOX5, WISP2, C7, HSD11B2, and LRP4—were selected for downstream analyses. A nomogram, together with calibration and decision curves, was used to evaluate subtype risk (Figs. 7E–7G). On the external dataset GSE80178-68183, the five-gene model achieved an apparent AUC of 0.933 (Fig. 7H); however, given the limited sample size and cohort heterogeneity, this estimate may be optimistic and requires validation in independent, non-overlapping cohorts. Taken together, the LASSO-driven machine-learning framework enabled DFU subtyping and nominated five candidate biomarkers, suggesting—but not yet establishing—their diagnostic utility.


### PPIF and APOE dysregulation in DFU consistent with hyperglycemia-driven oxidative stress and mitochondrial depolarization
  Clinical sampling was conducted following ethics approval and written informed consent (Fig. 8A). Immunoblotting showed that PPIF protein levels in DFUS and DFUT were significantly higher than in DFS and DFT (Fig. 8B); in the DFS vs DFUS comparison, opposite changes were observed—elevated PPIF (CypD) and reduced APOE (ApoE)—consistent with key pathways suggested by the multi-omics analyses; in the same batch, high expression of TP53 (p53) and CYCS (Cytc) was also detected in DFUS (Figs. 8C, 8D). To establish a mechanistic link, the hyperglycemic and oxidative-stress microenvironment of DFU was modeled in primary foot fibroblasts. Relative to physiological controls, either HG or TBHP alone reduced fibroblast proliferation, with the combined treatment (HG+TBHP) producing the most pronounced suppression (Fig. 9A); phalloidin labeling showed thinning and disorganization of F-actin stress fibers that further worsened under HG+TBHP (Fig. 9B); and DCFH-DA fluorescence revealed a marked intracellular increase in ROS in the HG+TBHP group (Fig. 9C). With respect to mitochondrial and death phenotypes, the JC-1 red/green ratio decreased under TBHP and HG conditions, indicating mitochondrial Δψm depolarization (Fig. 10A); Annexin V-FITC/PI flow cytometry confirmed increased proportions of early and late apoptosis (Fig. 10B); and these effects were exacerbated in the HG+TBHP group. Convergent evidence from clinical specimens and the in vitro model supports the transcriptome-derived framework of oxidative stress-mitochondrial polarization imbalance-cellular apoptosis, suggesting that PPIF (CypD)-dependent mPTP opening may couple ROS accumulation to depolarization/apoptosis, thereby undermining fibroblast homeostasis and impeding wound repair in DFU (For detailed information, see the Supplemental Material and Tables S1–S3).


## Discussion
  This study integrated microarray, bulk RNA-seq, and single-cell data to reconstruct a cell type–resolved regulatory network of DFU wounds and systematically delineated ligand–receptor communication between macrophages and fibroblasts. Differential genes associated with oxidative stress and mitochondrial polarization were further identified, and a multilayer regulatory atlas was assembled by integrating transcription factor–target relationships and ceRNA axes, thereby elucidating molecular mechanisms related to macrophage polarization. On this basis, machine-learning strategies were used to prioritize DFU candidate genes and potential biomarkers, aiming to generate testable mechanistic hypotheses rather than immediate clinical conclusions; critically, tissue Western blots and a primary fibroblast model (hyperglycemia/oxidative stress) verified up-regulation of PPIF and CYCS, down-regulation of APOE, ROS accumulation, loss of Δψm, proliferation restraint, and apoptosis, thereby directly linking computational predictions with phenotypic evidence and strengthening the credibility of the bioinformatic inferences.

  Extensive evidence supports the central roles of macrophages and fibroblasts in DFU pathogenesis (Aitcheson et al., 2021), and the single-cell annotations encompassed major lineages including M1/M2 macrophages, Fibro1/Fibro2 fibroblasts, keratinocytes, T/B cells, smooth muscle cells, and endothelial cells. GSEA and AUCell indicated enrichment of gluconeogenesis/glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation, calcium signaling, p53-related apoptosis, and immune pathways, particularly in macrophages and fibroblasts. CellChat further showed that fibroblasts interact with multiple lineages via CXCL, MIF, MK, and PTN, whereas macrophages communicate through TNF, IL1B, and TGF-β; together they form a communication hub centered on MIF, ANNEXIN, COMPLEMENT, and TNF. Considering the enrichment patterns and prior studies, disturbances in OXPHOS and lipid metabolism can sustain ROS production, provoking oxidative stress, mitochondrial membrane instability, and depolarization (Cleveland & Schnellmann, 2023; Nakagawa et al., 2005); conversely, depolarization further impairs OXPHOS and lowers ATP, establishing a bioenergetic-oxidative stress positive feedback loop that diminishes cytoprotection, promotes necrosis/apoptosis, compromises fibroblast function, and perturbs macrophage-polarization balance, ultimately delaying wound healing (Jiang et al., 2023; Van den Bossche et al., 2016; Xiao et al., 2017). Given that public datasets are largely derived from ulcer-stage tissue with inconsistent staging labels, stage-specific programs could not be fully resolved; thus, the metabolic-immune imbalance depicted here likely reflects persistent inflammatory phases and should be validated in prospective cohorts with clearly defined temporal staging.

  To further delineate the impact of oxidative stress and mitochondrial polarization on macrophages, fibroblasts, and DFU pathology, multi-omics information was used as the backbone and machine-learning as the selection engine to identify key genes. The results indicate cell-context-dependent roles of PPIF and APOE in macrophages and fibroblasts. The results indicate cell-context-dependent roles of PPIF and APOE in macrophages and fibroblasts, although these relationships are correlative and do not establish direct causal effects on DFU pathogenesis. LASSO nominated SOX5, WISP2, C7, HSD11B2, and LRP4 as candidate biomarkers; this hypothesis-generating finding requires testing in independent, non-overlapping external cohorts. SOX5 modulates the inflammatory microenvironment under non-coding RNA control and promotes pro-inflammatory/injury phenotypes, suggesting a plastic role in tissue repair and inflammatory disease. In DFU, SOX5 may participate in upstream TF networks and non-coding RNA axes that interface with Wnt/OXPHOS/ROS, APOE contributes to lipid-metabolic regulation, and PPIF (CypD) may regulate p53/Cytc-related mitochondrial cell-death programs, collectively implicating disequilibrium along the metabolism-mitochondria-inflammation axis. Notably, omics analyses together with wet-lab assays revealed opposing trends between TP53 transcription and p53 protein expression in DFUS, which may reflect activation of the p53 mitochondrial apoptotic pathway within the ulcer microenvironment, leading to suppression of TP53 transcription but increased p53 expression and translocation, thereby mediating mitochondrial-polarization–related cell-death routes.

  Experimental validation showed higher PPIF protein in DFU/DFUT than in DFS/DFT, aligning with the phenotypes of increased ROS and reduced Δψm/apoptosis in the cell model and consistent with the transcriptomic association between PPIF and depolarization, yet not sufficient to establish causality. Differences between ulcerated and non-ulcerated tissues suggest correlative potential for PPIF in monitoring progression or therapeutic response, but temporal stability and clinical utility require evaluation in prospective, multicenter designs. Integrated analyses implicate CEBPB, REL, and SPI1 as candidate upstream regulators of PPIF and outline a ceRNA axis centered on miR-128-3p, miR-23a-3p, and lnc-OIP5-AS1; their targets are enriched in chemical carcinogenesis-ROS, calcium signaling, and NET formation pathways, providing a testable regulatory framework around the PPIF pathway. CEBPB, REL, and SPI1 are likewise prominent in macrophages, and ranking by TF activity and specificity identifies NFKB1, FOSL2, and ETV3 (M1) and MAF (M2) as potential determinants of polarization, indicating that strategies to reprogram macrophages via metabolic/mitochondrial pathways merit further functional validation (Chen & Zou, 2024; Van den Bossche, O’Neill & Menon, 2017; Wculek et al., 2023).

  In sum, this integrative bioinformatic analysis clarifies and refines several key correlative facets of the DFU microenvironment but does not by itself demonstrate causality, providing resources and direction for subsequent in vivo and in vitro studies. Next steps will validate the five-gene signature and subtype model in prospective, non-overlapping multicenter cohorts; implement targeted pharmacologic inhibition and genetic knockdown of CypD/PPIF, together with mPTP-inhibition experiments centered on the PPIF/CEBPB-REL-SPI1-ceRNA axis, to test the ROS-Δψm-apoptosis cascade; and extend functional interrogation of the APOE-lipid-metabolism axis while incorporating spatial transcriptomics to localize sites of cell-cell interaction. Limitations include modest sample size, potential partial overlap between discovery and validation sets, cross-sectional design, inconsistent staging annotations, the correlational nature of both omics and validation experiments, and single-protein experimental confirmation, all of which may lead to overestimation of effect sizes and constrain causal inference.


## Conclusion
  Single-cell and bulk transcriptomes together with clinical phenotypes were integrated to systematically depict the DFU cellular ecosystem and its regulatory networks associated with oxidative stress and mitochondrial polarization. Using machine learning with multimodal cross-validation, SOX5, WISP2, C7, HSD11B2, and LRP4 were prioritized as hypothesis-generating candidate markers for subtyping and risk stratification, and cell-context-dependent signals of PPIF (CypD) and APOE were consistently localized across both bulk and single-cell layers. Orthogonal wet-lab experiments showed significantly elevated CypD in DFU skin/tendon, together with up-regulated p53 and Cytc and down-regulated ApoE in DFUS; in primary foot fibroblasts, high glucose and tert-butyl hydroperoxide (TBHP) synergistically induced ROS accumulation, loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (JC-1), F-actin cytoskeletal disorganization, and increased apoptosis with suppressed EdU proliferation, findings that are consistent with, but do not in themselves prove, involvement of a ROS–CypD/mPTP–depolarization–p53/Cytc–apoptosis pathological axis. In sum, this study bridges computational inference with tissue/cellular evidence and generates testable mechanistic hypotheses, including potential intervention routes such as inhibiting CypD/mPTP to restore mitochondrial polarization, correcting M1/M2 disequilibrium through metabolic reprogramming, and modulating the lipid-metabolism/lipoprotein axis to re-establish APOE-related homeostasis, all of which remain speculative until supported by targeted functional knockdown or pharmacologic inhibition experiments. The integrated transcription factor–ceRNA–ligand/receptor atlas offers a conceptual resource that may inform the design of future stage-specific interventions and prospective, multicenter, stratified studies and target-validation efforts; meanwhile, the predictive model should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and requires rigorous external validation in independent, non-overlapping cohorts before it can meaningfully contribute to precision diagnosis or translational decision-making. These conclusions should also be interpreted in light of key limitations, including modest sample sizes, partial overlap and heterogeneity among the public cohorts, the cross-sectional nature of the transcriptomic datasets, and reliance on single-protein experimental confirmation, underscoring the need for larger, longitudinal, and in-vivo functional studies—particularly genetic or pharmacologic inhibition of CypD/MPTP—to validate the PPIF-centered pathways and refine the proposed biomarkers before any therapeutic claims can be made.
