# Loop Plasticity Drives Paralog-Specific Recognition in BET ET Domains


## Abstract
  The bromodomain and extraterminal domain (BET) family uses its conserved ET domains to recognize diverse peptide motifs, yet exhibits paralog-specific binding preferences whose structural origins remain poorly understood. Using extensive molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of BRD3-ET and BRD4-ET and experimental data for the unbound and peptide-bound states, we show that paralog selectivity arises not from large structural rearrangements but from subtle differences in the dynamics of the α2–α3 loop. Two divergent residues at positions 35 and 36 in this loop modulate the formation of flanking helices (η1 and η2), which in turn control the opening of the peptide-binding cavity and determine how each paralog accommodates distinct binding modes. These sequence-encoded dynamical differences shape the number, stability, and geometry of accessible binding modes and provide a structural rationale for paralog-specific targeting of BET proteins.


## INTRODUCTION
  Bromodomain and Extra-Terminal (BET) proteins are key regulators of gene expression, particularly genes involved in immune responses.1,2 Their misregulation has been linked to a range of diseases, including cancer, inflammation, and metabolic disorders.3-6 Humans express four paralogs—BRD2, BRD3, BRD4, and BRDT—that share high sequence similarity but differ in tissue distribution and biological function.7 These paralogs engage diverse host and viral partners, making BET proteins attractive yet challenging therapeutic targets.7,8

  Each BET protein contains two bromodomains (BD1 and BD2), which bind acetylated lysine residues, and a C-terminal extraterminal (ET) domain that mediates protein-protein interactions. Drug development has traditionally focused on bromodomains,9 but their strong sequence conservation has resulted in pan-inhibition, toxicity, and limited selectivity.10-13 The ET domain, by contrast, binds a wide range of transcription factors, chromatin remodelers, and viral proteins through short linear motifs.14-19 Through these interactions, the ET domain acts as an integrative signaling module and is exploited by viral proteins such as Murine Leukemia Virus Integrase (MLV) and the Kaposi’s Sarcoma Herpesvirus protein LANA.20,21 This functional versatility, combined with emerging evidence for paralog-specific interactions, highlights the ET domain as a promising but underexplored target for selective modulation.

  Structurally, the ET domain is a compact, 68-residue three-helix bundle with a flexible ~20-residue loop between helices α2-α3. ET-binding peptides, typically originating from intrinsically disordered regions of partner proteins, fold into defined structures upon binding. NMR structures reveal remarkable conformational diversity, including helices, extended strands, and β-hairpins.18,19,22,23 Reported affinities span nearly 4 orders of magnitude,24 reflecting both the adaptability of the ET domain and the sequence-dependent nature of peptide recognition. Despite high conservation across paralogs, certain peptides—such as epitopes from LANA and JMJD6—bind preferentially to BRD4-ET over BRD3-ET.19,25 This raises a fundamental question: How does paralog specificity arise in such a conserved domain?

  Recent advances in integrative modeling—particularly MELD24,26,27 and AF-CBA28—have accelerated the discovery and ranking of ET-binding peptides. These methods have revealed noncanonical motifs and helped experimentally validate new epitopes.29 However, while powerful for identifying likely binders, they are not sufficiently quantitative to resolve the subtle structural and energetic factors that underlie paralog selectivity, in part due to the complexities of modeling folding-upon-binding.

  Conventional molecular dynamics (MD) simulations provide a complementary route to understanding ET recognition. When initiated from well-defined bound and unbound states, MD allows direct comparison of receptor dynamics, binding-site accessibility, and conformational plasticity across paralogs—without conflating differences in peptide folding energetics. By examining how the same peptide interacts with different ET domains, and how different peptides engage each receptor, MD can reveal the underlying sequence-dependent features that shape binding selectivity.

  In this study, we use MD simulations of BRD3-ET and BRD4-ET to investigate the molecular origins of paralog-specific peptide recognition. By characterizing bound and unbound conformational ensembles and mapping how subtle sequence variations modulate the dynamics of the α2-α3 loop, we uncover mechanistic principles governing ET-mediated selectivity.


## METHODS

### Systems of Study
  Out of the four BET proteins, structural efforts involving the ET domain have focused on BRD3 and BRD4 as they are the ones more relevant in disease. However, the experimental data does not describe these systems uniformly: only the BRD3-ET domain has been studied in its free form, and the complexes include different peptides for BRD3 and BRD4.

  We retrieved the seven existing NMR structures involving BRD3/4-ET from the PDB. BRD3-ET was found in its unbound form (PDB ID: 7JMY23) and binding four different peptides: BRG1 (PDB ID: 6BGH22), CHD4 (PDB ID: 6BGG22), TP (PDB ID: 7JQ823), and NSD3 (PDB ID: 7JYN23). While there was no unbound structure for human BRD4-ET, there are two structures of complexes binding different peptides: JMJD6 (PDB ID: 6BNH19) and LANA (PDB ID: 2ND018). Supporting Table S1 summarizes the sequences and other features of the peptides that bind the ET-domain in these structures.

  The high degree of sequence and structural homology allowed us to model complementary systems by mutating the seven residues that differ between BRD3-ET and BRD4-ET and rebuilding their structures based on backbone and Cβ coordinates using tleap.30 With this approach, we generate models for BRD3-ET in complex with LANA and JMJD6, and for BRD4-ET in complex with CHD4, BRG1, NSD3, and TP. We also modeled the unbound BRD4-ET state by removing LANA and JMJD6 from their respective NMR complex structures. After energy minimization of these systems, the resulting equilibrated MD ensembles quickly lost memory of the starting conformations and adopted features consistent with the unbound state (see SI).


### Molecular Dynamics Protocol
  All the simulations were performed using the AMBER 22 biomolecular simulation suit.31 Each system was set up using the Amber ff19SB protein force field32 and the OPC water force field33 with explicit solvent in an octahedral solvent box of 10 Å. Na and Cl ions were added to neutralize the charges. The energy of the systems was minimized over six steps. The initial steps applied restrictions to the protein that were gradually removed. Each step involved 4000 cycles of minimization implementing steepest descent followed by conjugate gradient. The systems were then heated to 298.15 K using a Langevin thermostat with a collision frequency of 1 ps−1 for 1 ns, followed by a NPT equilibration using a Berendsen barostat for 10 ns. After equilibration, each simulation was run in a NPT ensemble at 298.15 K using a Langevin thermostat and a Monte Carlo barostat. Hydrogen mass repartition34 and SHAKE35 algorithm were implemented to reach a time step of 4 fs. We ran simulations in triplicate for 7.2 μs each, saving coordinates every 100 ps. Thus, for each system we had an accumulated 21.6 μs sampling time. The first 10 ns were discarded as equilibration time and not considered for the analysis.


### Analysis
  MD trajectory analysis was performed using CPPTRAJ,36 along with custom Python and R scripts. Calculations of secondary structure content were performed with the DSSP plugin,37 implemented in CPPTRAJ.

  Meta-ensembles were constructed by combining three replicates per system; in some cases, trajectories from different peptide complexes were merged to define consensus receptor ensembles for PCA. Conformational space was analyzed via principal component analysis (PCA) using backbone atoms of residues 6–61. An average structure from the concatenated trajectories defined the reference frame, and eigenvectors were computed from the covariance matrix. Kernel density estimation (KDE) was used to project PC1 and PC2 distributions, from which free energy surfaces were estimated as ΔG =−kBT log Pi. Free energy profiles along PC1 and PC2 were compared using Kullback–Leibler divergence.38

  Representative conformations were extracted via k-means clustering (k = 10), reading the trajectories each 10 frames, by implementation of MDAnalysis39 and Scikit-learn40 combined in a Python script, with RMSD as clustering metric.

  To complement PCA, we used ELViM (Energy Landscape Visualization Method),41 a multidimensional projection based on pairwise structural dissimilarities, applied to the same backbone region. Trajectories from both BRD3-ET and BRD4-ET, in bound and unbound states, were combined into a single meta-ensemble using a common reference topology.

  Additional analysis details are provided in the Supporting Methods.


## RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

### Loop Rearrangements Differentiate BRD3 and BRD4 Binding Behavior
  The BRD3 and BRD4-ET domains differ by seven residues (Figure 1A), yet adopt the same 3-helix bundle fold in NMR structures of their peptide-bound complexes (see SI Table S2 and Figure SI 3). While there is an NMR structure of the unbound BRD3-ET domain in solution (PDB ID 7JMY23), there is no structure for the unbound BRD4-ET domain. Notably, none of the seven mutations are directly involved in forming the hydrophobic binding pocket (Figures 1B and SI4). Interestingly, many of these changes are conservative in nature, considering the amino acid type (e.g., D3E, R14K, R35K, T52S).

  The main difference between the bound and unbound forms are residues in the loop region connecting helices α2 and α3 (expanding residues 31 to 50). Upon peptide binding, this loop undergoes significant conformational changes driven by a zipper-like interaction interface alternating hydrophobic and charged residues. Four central residues in this loop form key interactions with peptide side chains, including two conserved negatively charged residues (Glu43 and Asp45) that engage positively charged complementary residues on the peptides.

  Flanking these core residues, both the N- and C-terminal regions of the loop exhibit secondary structure preferences. In particular, the N-terminal part adopts a short helical structure (η1 helix) in some NMR structures (Figure SI 5) and our simulations (Figure 1C) show that this helix has a transient nature. Two of the seven BRD3/4-ET sequence differences are located in this η1 helix region. On the C-terminal side, our simulations support the appearance of a less frequent helix or turn-like feature (η2). Additionally, the simulations reveal that BRD4-ET occasionally, in a small fraction on the frames, samples a short β-hairpin conformation in this region – a feature not observed in BRD3-ET (Figures 1C and SI 26). These intrinsic structural preferences set the stage for paralogspecific conformational responses upon peptide binding.


### Peptides That Bind as Hairpins Occupy the Full ET Pocket and Form Stable Complexes
  We analyzed six known ET-binding peptides based on published NMR structures: four with BRD3 (BRG1, CHD4, TP, NSD3) and two with BRD4 (LANA, JMJD6). These peptides span helical, extended, and β-hairpin conformations (see SI Table S1 for sequences and structural classes). A key motivation of this research was the difference observed between Kd. The strongest binder in the set is TP (Kd = 13 ± 10 nM at 10 °C),24 BRG1 and CHD4 are moderate binders (Kd = 7 ± 1 μM and 95 ± 10 μM, respectively22) and LANA, JMJD6 and NSD3 are weaker binders (Kd = 250 ± 150 μM at 10 °C for BRD3-ET:NSD3).24

  Comparison across the NMR ensembles reveals the inherent flexibility of the ET loop and its ability to accommodate different binding modes, with flanking helices appearing transient (Figures SI 1 and SI 5). To understand how these motifs engage the ET domain and assess possible determinants of selectivity, we performed extensive molecular dynamic simulations starting from these structures. For completeness, we modeled all complementary complexes (see Methods section), resulting in 12 systems: six peptides bound to BRD3-ET and six bound to BRD4-ET. Although the ET domains remain structurally stable across all simulations (Figures SI 8-9), the peptides exhibit markedly different behaviors (Figure SI 10). The high-affinity TP maintains a stable interface, moderate binders (BRG1 and CHD4) display intermittent unbinding events, and weaker binders frequently sample dissociated states. A notable exception is NSD3, which remains bound despite its weak affinity. A common feature of NSD3 and TP is that both bind as hairpins spanning the full binding pocket, whereas the other peptides bind as single strands and only partially occupy the site.

  These differences in stability cannot be directly compared to experiment, as kinetic parameters (kon and koff) are not available. It is reasonable to expect that folding-upon-binding will impact kon, making the observed lability in MD physically meaningful. At the same time, given the known tendency of MD simulations to overstabilize compact structures,43-45 the frequent dissociation observed on these time scales is surprising and limits detailed analysis of bound-state states. Consistent with this, our previous NMR-guided MD work (MELD approach26) showed that binding for some of these peptides is problematic,24 and more recent work identified longer epitopes that bind as hairpins and fully occupy the site.29 Small force field biases can have amplified effects in protein–peptide systems, where peptides lie at the edge of stability (disordered when unbound and structured when bound). Encouragingly, the ff19SB/OPC combination used here performs among the best for capturing peptide behavior,46 lending confidence to these observations.

  Based on these results, we focus the remainder of the work on TP and NSD3. Both peptides fully occupy the binding site as β-hairpins but bind in opposite orientations: TP in a C-to-N direction and NSD3 in an N-to-C direction.23 In both cases, alternating hydrophobic and positively charged residues align with complementary features in the ET loop (Figure SI 11). The ability of the ET domain to accommodate these distinct binding modes, together with differences in affinity across paralogs, suggests the presence of paralog-specific determinants of recognition.


### Comparative Conformational Dynamics of BRD3 and BRD4
  The NMR structures already point at a significant rearrangement of the loop region connecting helices α2 and α3. To characterize conformational differences between BRD3 and BRD4 we compared their unbound and peptide-bound ensembles using principal component analysis (PCA). Both paralogs share a similar core ensemble (state S0) in the unbound state but diverge markedly upon peptide binding (Figures 2 and SI 12).

  Binding shifts each paralog into distinct metastable states. Two states (S1 and S2) are sampled by both receptors, whereas a third state (S3) appears only in BRD4. Bound BRD4 collapses into a dominant, compact ensemble centered on S1, independent of whether TP or NSD3 is bound. In contrast, BRD3 distributes more evenly across S1 and S2, and the preferred bound state depends on the peptide—consistent with greater conformational plasticity.

  We qualitatively compared these populations by calculating population log-likelihoods along PC1 and PC2 (Figure SI 14). Along the first component, BRD3 samples one major basin, whereas BRD4 populates two. Along the second principal component, both paralogs sample two states. However, BRD3 shows comparable weights between the two basins, whereas BRD4 consistently favors one basin, regardless of whether TP or NSD3 is bound. Though qualitative, this indicates differences in binding-mode plasticity across paralogs.

  To further quantify ensemble differences along the first two PCs, we computed the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence. Given that this metric is asymmetric, we calculated the divergence in both forward and reverse directions (Table 1 and Figures SI 15-17). This confirms that (1) both peptides induce large conformational changes relative to the unbound state; (2) TP- and NSD3-bound ensembles are similar within each paralog; and (3) paralog differences are amplified upon binding. In particular, BRD4 samples a region along PC1 inaccessible to BRD3, suggesting paralog-specific stabilization of distinct bound conformations.

  To explore the structural basis of the observed PCA meta-states, we extract representative structures from the ensembles. In the unbound forms (Figures SI 18-19), differences point to the loop secondary structure propensities and the spatial positioning of helix α1. Upon peptide binding (Figures SI 20-21), structural rearrangements are observed in the loop and in the adjacent short helices: η2 in BRD3 complexes and η1 in BRD4 complexes. Positioning of α1-helix also shifts. These observations are consistent with experimental NMR chemical shift perturbation patterns observed for BRD3-ET upon peptide binding (Figure SI 22).23,24

  A known challenge when comparing ensembles with PCA is the requirement to project all systems onto a shared set of eigenvectors, which necessarily depends on the chosen reference structure (see Supporting Information). Since different reference choices can mask or hide certain states and hence the interpretation of how ensembles relate to one another, we complemented PCA with reference-free low-dimensional embeddings generated using ELViM.41 ELViM recapitulates the same qualitative trends observed with PCA (Figure SI 23): BRD4 adopts compact, peptide-independent bound ensembles, whereas BRD3 maintains broader, peptide-dependent heterogeneity. This agreement between PCA and ELViM points to peptide binding reshaping each ET domain in a paralog-specific manner, providing a mechanistic basis for differential peptide preferences and affinity profiles. While our conclusions are necessarily based on finite trajectory sampling, the differences between unbound and bound states are consistent across replicates and robust to the analysis approach.


### The α2–α3 Loop as a Determinant of Paralog-Specific Peptide Recognition
  Global conformational analyses across BRD3 and BRD4 reveal that differences in peptide binding and selectivity trace back to structural and dynamical features of the α2 and α3 loop. We examined the loop’s overall structural variability via RMSD distributions (Figure 3A), which shows that unbound BRD3 populates a broader, more heterogeneous ensemble, whereas BRD4 remains comparatively compact. Peptide binding stabilizes this loop in both paralogs, but the effect is stronger for TP than for NSD3. The magnitude of stabilization is most pronounced in BRD4, consistent with a more preorganized binding interface.

  Secondary-structure analysis (Figure 3B) reveals that residues 40–46 form a peptide-induced β-strand in all complexes, flanked by short, transient helices (η1 and η2). BRD3 displays higher intrinsic helicity in η1, while BRD4 exhibits a shorter 310 helical segment in this region. Notably, the unbound BRD4 ensemble contains a low-population hairpin near the η1 region that preorganizes the loop into a geometry compatible with the bound-state strand (see also Figures SI 26-27). These observations point to subtle differences in local secondary-structure propensities that shape how much rearrangement each paralog must undergo to accommodate a peptide.

  Local flexibility analysis further supports this view. Residues 35 and 36 – two of the few nonconserved positions between paralogs– arise as key contributors to modulating loop dynamics (Figures SI 28-29). In BRD4, this region influences loop predisposition to the bound state even in the absence of a peptide, whereas in BRD3 this behavior emerges only upon binding. The binding cleft where the peptides bind is delimited by the strand region of the loop on one side and helix-α1 on the other side. Normal-mode analysis (Figures SI 30-32) shows that BRD4 employs more collective motions that constrict or open the binding cleft, while BRD3 exhibits a hinge-like mode involving the loop and α1, enabling broader sampling of cavity geometries. Correlation matrices (Figures 4 and SI 33-34) reveal that these dynamical signatures are paralog-specific and largely independent of the bound peptide, indicating that the receptor—rather than the ligand—dictates the dynamic response. This is further supported by the perresidue (side-chains) distance matrices (Figure SI 35), which show nearly identical contact patterns across all systems. The conserved structural features suggest that binding relies on the paralog dynamic and the frequency of the contacts.

  While the nature of the paralog dictates differences in the binding signature, the nature of the peptide dictates the breadth of the ensemble upon binding. Thus, the high-affinity TP peptide binds both paralogs in a compact, well-defined mode, whereas the low-binding-affinity NSD3 adopts a broader positional distribution (Figure 5). A Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistical test comparing the RMSD distribution for each peptide bound to BRD3 and BRD4 yielded p-values smaller than 0.05, confirming that observed differences in the location of the peptide for each receptor are statistically significant. The combination of broader distributions of NSD3 with the distinct dynamical response in BRD3-ET creates two distinct binding orientations for BRD3-ET:NSD3. This suggests that BRD3 supports multiple binding modes for weaker ligands, consistent with a conformational-selection mechanism, while BRD4 enforces a more constrained induced-fit geometry. Looking at the peptide sequences, both peptides contain aromatic residues (Trp5 in TP and Phe18 in NSD3) that form stabilizing interactions with α2 (Figure SI 36), supplying a secondary anchor complementing the central β-strand interface. The stronger anchoring contribution of TP Trp5 may underlie its higher affinity and tighter binding modes.

  Overall, these results identify the α2–α3 loop as a dynamic regulatory element whose intrinsic flexibility, secondary-structure preferences, and collective motions differ markedly between BRD3 and BRD4. These paralog-specific loop properties shape the geometry of the binding cleft, modulate peptide-binding modes, and likely contribute to the observed differences in affinity and selectivity.


### Local Helix Formation in the η1/η2 Regions Regulates Binding-Site Opening and Drives Paralog-Specific Recognition
  The previous section established that the α2–α3 loop adopts paralog-specific conformations, but did not explain how these differences arise. Here we examine whether transient helical structure and orientation in the two flanking elements, η1 and η2, modulate loop geometry and thereby regulate access to the ET-domain binding cleft.

  Analysis of i → i + 4 helical distances and loop–α1 distances (Figures SI 37-41) shows a consistent pattern for the C-terminal η2 region (residues 44–50): in both paralogs, its broad multimodal distributions in the apo state collapse into narrow unimodal peaks upon peptide binding. This indicates that binding stabilizes pre-existing turn/helix-like conformations rather than inducing new structure. The adjacent β-strand forming segment (residues 40–46) behaves similarly in both paralogs, with little overlap between bound and unbound distributions. Distances between η2 or the β region and helix α1 similarly become sharply unimodal, consistent with the required “open” binding-site cleft geometry in the presence of peptide.

  In contrast, the N-terminal η1 region (residues 31–38) behaves differently across paralogs and contains two key sequence differences at positions 35 and 36 (R35/D36 in BRD3; K35/N36 in BRD4). In BRD3, these residues form a stabilizing salt bridge (Figure SI 42), generating a constrained local structure in the unbound state. Yet upon peptide binding—particularly with NSD3—η1 displays persistent bimodal behavior and populates conformations not observed in the apo ensemble (Figure 6A). This reflects the switching between helical and extended states and correlates with the broader opening of the loop. BRD4 lacks this salt bridge and exhibits substantially less peptide-dependent heterogeneity.

  Distances between η1 and helix α1 show the largest peptide-induced changes (Figure 6B). While unbound distributions are nearly identical across paralogs, peptide binding shifts both the dominant populations and the shapes of these distributions. The effect is most pronounced for BRD3:NSD3, where residues 35–36 strongly modulate loop opening. This variability correlates with the presence of multiple binding modes for NSD3 in BRD3 and with its lower affinity relative to TP.

  Together, these observations support a mechanistic model in which the α2–α3 loop acts as a dynamic gate for the ET binding cleft (scheme in Figure 7). The flanking η1 and η2 elements modulate loop opening by stabilizing or releasing specific local geometries. Sequence differences at the end of η1 expand the conformational ensemble of BRD3, enabling greater plasticity and alternative binding modes for weaker ligands. BRD4, by contrast, retains a more preorganized and restricted loop architecture that promotes a single, well-defined binding mode. Thus, despite their high sequence identity, BRD3 and BRD4 differ in how local helix formation and loop dynamics couple to peptide recognition, providing a structural rationale for paralog-selective binding.

  Beyond geometric complementarity, the zipper-like pattern of alternating positively charged and hydrophobic residues in the peptide with complementary features in the ET binding groove provides a clear enthalpic driving force for binding. At the same time, complex formation is expected to involve substantial entropic contributions that are considerably more difficult to quantify. One component arises from peptide folding upon binding, which necessarily differs across ligands depending on their intrinsic disorder in solution. A second contribution comes from desolvation entropy of both the peptide and the ET surface, facilitated by the absence of interfacial water molecules in the bound complexes.

  A third, and more subtle, contribution emerges from changes in ET-domain dynamics upon binding. Our analysis indicates that ET motions are not fully quenched in the bound state: Figure SI27 shows persistent residual conformational entropy in the η1 region, with magnitudes that depend on both the paralog (BRD3 vs BRD4) and the bound peptide (TP vs NSD3). As a result, even when binding geometries appear similar, paralog-dependent retention—or suppression—of loop flexibility provides a plausible thermodynamic route by which the same binding motif can exhibit different affinities across BET paralogs.

  Besides the observations involving the binding cleft, our simulations also suggest novel implications for drug design. Interestingly, analysis of the BRD3-ET simulations reveals a transient pocket between α2 and α3-helices (see Figure SI 43). This observation suggests the potential existence of a cryptic pocket, which could be further explored as a novel target for drug development. To reinforce our observations, we perform a pocket prediction with the software fpocket47 for selected frames. The transient pocket that we observe between α2 and α3-helices has the highest fpocket score, which means that it is likely to be a site where small molecules could bind. The emergence of this pocket deserves further investigation and could provide an alternative strategy for paralog-specific inhibition.


## CONCLUSION
  In this study, we use molecular dynamics simulations to examine the structural and dynamical landscapes of BRD3 and BRD4 ET domains and to identify the features that give rise to paralog-specific peptide recognition. Focusing on complexes with the MLV integrase tail peptide (TP) and a segment of the host protein NSD3—two ligands that share a conserved, β-strand–forming ET-binding motif—we find that the α2–α3 loop serves as a dynamic regulatory element governing paralogspecific peptide recognition. Residues 35–36 rewire the local helix/strand equilibrium within η1/η2, thereby affecting the loop’s accessible conformations. As a result, BRD3 supports a broader array of ligand-bound geometries, whereas BRD4’s preorganized architecture restricts binding to a single dominant mode. Notably, these dynamical signatures remain intrinsic to each paralog and persist across different peptide ligands, providing a mechanistic basis for selective engagement. Beyond clarifying how the BET ET domains discriminate among binding partners, this work establishes a structural and dynamical framework for the rational design of paralog-selective therapeutics targeting this conserved epigenetic reader family.
