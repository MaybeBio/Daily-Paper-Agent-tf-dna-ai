# From collapse to comeback: Luteolin rejuvenates nucleus pulposus progenitor cells via SIRT1/ATF5–UPRmt to reverse intervertebral disc degeneration cascade


## Background
  Intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD) is a major contributor to low back pain (LBP) and one of the foremost causes of disability worldwide. Oxidative stress–induced senescence of nucleus pulposus progenitor cells (NPPC) and mitochondrial dysfunction are key drivers of IVDD. The mitochondrial unfolded protein response (UPRmt), orchestrated by the Silent Information Regulator 1 (SIRT1)–Activating Transcription Factor 5 (ATF5) axis, plays a pivotal role in maintaining mitochondrial proteostasis. However, its involvement in IVDD remains insufficiently characterized. Luteolin (Lut), a naturally occurring flavonoid with well-documented antioxidant and anti-senescence properties, has emerged as a promising disease-modifying candidate.


## Methods
  We employed an integrated approach combining network pharmacology, molecular docking, and Mendelian randomization to identify and validate SIRT1 as a central target of Lut in IVDD. Human nucleus pulposus (NP) tissues spanning Pfirrmann grades II–V were analyzed to assess the expression of SIRT1 and UPRmt-related proteins. Functional assays in TBHP-induced NPPC senescence models were conducted to evaluate the effects of Lut on proliferation, senescence, mitochondrial function, and UPRmt activation, with or without SIRT1 knockdown. Finally, a puncture-induced rat IVDD model was employed to assess the therapeutic efficacy of Lut with imaging, histological, and behavioral analyses.


## Results
  Human NP specimens exhibited a progressive decline in SIRT1, ATF5, and UPRmt effectors (HSP60, LONP1, CLPP) with advancing degeneration grades. Lut was found to directly bind SIRT1, thereby stabilizing mitochondrial function and alleviating NPPC senescence through activation of the SIRT1–ATF5–UPRmt pathway. Genetic silencing of SIRT1 abolished the protective effects of Lut on cellular senescence, UPRmt activation, and bioenergetics. In vivo, Lut administration attenuated disc height loss, preserved extracellular matrix integrity and partially alleviated pain-like behaviors in rats with IVDD.


## Conclusion
  This study establishes SIRT1 as a central therapeutic target of Lut and underscores the SIRT1–ATF5–UPRmt axis as a critical mechanism mediating its protective effects. By restoring mitochondrial proteostasis and delaying NPPC senescence, Lut emerges as a promising disease-modifying strategy for IVDD management.

## Introduction
  Low back pain (LBP) ranks among the leading causes of global disability and imposes a substantial socioeconomic burden worldwide [1–3]. IVDD has been recognized as a predominant pathological contributor to LBP [4, 5]. The integrity of a healthy intervertebral disc (IVD) depends on a delicate balance between anabolic and catabolic processes within the nucleus pulposus (NP). Increasing evidence indicates that the decline of resident stem/progenitor cells in the IVD is closely associated with impaired regeneration and progressive structural deterioration. Among these, nucleus pulposus progenitor cells (NPPC) have been identified as a distinct progenitor population that contributes to disc homeostasis by synthesizing extracellular matrix (ECM) components such as type II collagen and proteoglycans, replenishing degenerated NP cells through self-renewal and differentiation, and regulating the balance between anabolic and catabolic activities. NPPC also modulate the inflammatory microenvironment and adapt to the hypoxic, nutrient-deficient conditions of IVD [6, 7]. However, aging and diverse stressors drive progressive senescence and functional decline of NPPC, resulting in a catabolic shift, impaired ECM synthesis, and exacerbated inflammatory responses [8–10]. These pro-inflammatory changes are predominantly driven by the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), characterized by the secretion of cytokines including interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) [5, 11] ultimately leading to the structural and functional breakdown of the IVD.

  Among the numerous factors contributing to cellular senescence, oxidative stress has been particularly implicated in the pathogenesis of IVDD [12, 13]. The avascular and hypoxic microenvironment of the NP renders it highly vulnerable to oxidative damage, which in turn induces mitochondrial dysfunction—a defining hallmark of aging [14, 15]. Dysfunctional mitochondria not only generate excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby exacerbating oxidative stress, but also fail to meet cellular bioenergetic demands, ultimately triggering programmed cell death and senescence [16, 17]. To counteract mitochondrial damage, cells have evolved sophisticated quality control mechanisms, among which the mitochondrial unfolded protein response (UPRmt) plays a pivotal role in maintaining proteostasis and mitochondrial integrity [18]. Activation of the UPRmt entails the upregulation of mitochondrial chaperones such as heat shock protein 60 (HSP60) and proteases such as lon peptidase 1 (LONP1) and caseinolytic mitochondrial matrix peptidase proteolytic subunit (CLPP), which function to refold or degrade damaged proteins [19, 20]. Activating transcription factor 5 (ATF5) has been identified as a central regulator that translocate to the nucleus and induces the transcription of UPRmt-related genes in mammals [21, 22]. The NAD+-dependent deacetylase sirtuin 1 (SIRT1), a well-established guardian against cellular aging and oxidative stress, has been proposed as an upstream regulator of ATF5 and UPRmt, constituting a protective axis that safeguards mitochondrial homeostasis and promotes cell survival [23, 24].

  At present, therapeutic options for IVDD are mainly confined to symptomatic management or invasive surgery, underscoring the urgent need for effective disease-modifying strategies [25, 26]. Natural compounds with strong antioxidant and anti-aging properties have emerged as promising therapeutic candidates [27]. Luteolin (3′,4′,5,7-tetrahydroxyflavone), a widely distributed flavonoid in fruits and vegetables, has attracted increasing attention for its diverse biological activities, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and senolytic effects [28, 29]. Preliminary studies indicated that Lut can activate SIRT1 and alleviate mitochondrial dysfunction in several other disease models [30–32]. However, its potential role in attenuating NPPC senescence and IVDD has not yet been investigated.

  Based on this rationale, we hypothesized that oxidative stress–induced NPPC senescence represents a critical event in IVDD pathogenesis, primarily driven by mitochondrial dysfunction and impaired UPRmt. We further postulated that Lut activates the SIRT1/ATF5 signaling pathway, thereby enhancing UPRmt, restoring mitochondrial function, reducing cellular senescence, and ultimately slowing the progression of IVDD. To test this hypothesis and elucidate the underlying mechanisms, we employed a comprehensive approach that integrates in vitro oxidative stress models, genetic knockdown experiments, and a well-established in vivo rat IVDD model. Our findings aimed to establish Lut as a potential disease-modifying therapeutic agent for IVDD.


## Methods

### NP tissue collection
  Human NP tissue samples were collected from 12 patients who underwent posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF) surgery for IVDD. The tissues used in this study were discarded intervertebral disc tissues removed during surgery, which would otherwise have been discarded. All patients provided written informed consent prior to tissue collection. Among them, three samples were classified as Pfirrmann grade II, three as grade III, three as grade IV, and three as grade V [33]. The expression levels of SIRT1 and UPRmt-related proteins (including ATF5, HSP60, LONP1, and CLPP) were assessed in all specimens using immunohistochemistry and Western blot analysis. The use of human tissue samples in this study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Northern Jiangsu People’s Hospital Affiliated to Yangzhou University(Approval No. 2021ky050).


### Network pharmacology

#### Potential target proteins of Lut and IVDD
  The potential human targets of Lut were retrieved from several databases, including PharmMapper, SwissTargetPrediction, SymMap, and BATMAN-TCM. After removing redundant entries, the remaining targets were considered as candidate targets of Lut. Disease-related targets were obtained from GeneCards, CTD, and OpenTargets databases using “intervertebral disc degeneration” as the keyword, and the target gene names and weights were summarized. Ultimately, a set of disease-related targets was identified. A Venn diagram of the overlapping targets among different databases was generated. The intersection between the Lut targets and disease-related targets was defined as the set of common targets for subsequent analysis. The common targets were visualized using the R package VennDiagram (version 1.7.3). The one-to-one correspondence between Lut and IVDD targets was imported into Cytoscape (version 3.9.1) to construct the Lut–target–IVDD interaction network. In the network, nodes represent Lut and its targets, while edges denote the interactions between them. The Network Analyzer plugin was used to evaluate the topological properties of the network and calculate node degree values.


#### Construction and analysis of protein-protein interaction networks
  A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using the STRING database. The common targets of Lut in IVDD were imported into STRING, with the species restricted to “Homo sapiens”. The confidence score was set to ≥ 0.4 to obtain protein–protein interaction data. The resulting TSV and PNG files were imported into Cytoscape (version 3.9.1). Isolated nodes without connections were removed, and the topological properties of the network were analyzed. Core targets were identified based on degree centrality, betweenness centrality, and closeness centrality. In addition, the CytoHubba plugin was used with the MCC algorithm to further screen hub genes.


#### Gene ontology and kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes pathway enrichment analysis
  The common targets of Lut and IVDD were analyzed using the R package clusterProfiler (version 4.6.2) for Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment. Both GO functional enrichment and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses were conducted, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. The top 15 significantly enriched GO terms (p < 0.05) were selected to generate bar plots. KEGG enrichment analysis was also performed with p < 0.05 as the cutoff, and the top 30 significantly enriched pathways were visualized as bubble plots.


### Molecular docking
  The compound structure of Lut was retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and converted from SDF to PDB format using Open Babel (version 2.3.2). The receptor protein structure was obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB). The receptor protein was preprocessed by removing water molecules and ligands using PyMOL (version 2.3.4). Hydrogen atoms were added and charges were balanced using AutoDockTools, and both receptor and ligand were converted into PDBQT format. Molecular docking was performed with AutoDock Vina (version 1.1.2), and docking interactions were analyzed using PLIP. Docking results were visualized with PyMOL.


### Molecular dynamics (MD) and simulation
  To further evaluate the binding between the protein and ligand, a 100 ns MD simulation was performed on the docked complex. The MD simulation was conducted using GROMACS (version 2019.6) under constant temperature, constant pressure, and periodic boundary conditions. The AMBER99SB-ILDN protein force field and the GAFF force field for small molecules were applied with the TIP3P water model. All hydrogen bonds were constrained using the LINCS algorithm, with an integration time step of 2 fs. Electrostatic interactions were calculated using the particle–mesh Ewald (PME) method. A cutoff of 12 Å was applied for nonbonded interactions, updated every 20 steps. The V-rescale method was applied to maintain the temperature at 300 K, and the Parrinello–Rahman method was used to maintain the pressure at 1 bar. Energy minimization was first performed using the steepest descent method to remove unfavorable atomic contacts. The system was then gradually heated to 300 K within 100 ps, followed by a 100 ns MD simulation, during which conformations were saved every 20 ps. Simulation results were visualized using GROMACS utilities and PyMOL. To further investigate system properties, the MM-PBSA method was applied to calculate binding free energy, providing mechanistic insights into protein–ligand interactions.


### The mendelian randomization study identified the relationship between SIRT1 and IVDD
  Mendelian Randomization (MR) uses genetic variants as instrumental variables to infer causal relationships between an exposure and an outcome [34]. This approach helps determine whether an observed association reflects a true causal link. In MR, confounding is minimized by the random allocation of genetic variants at conception, and reverse causation is avoided because variants are fixed prior to disease onset. In this study, we conducted two-sample MR analyses to investigate the causal relationship between SIRT1 and IVDD. We used publicly available expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) and protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL) data associated with IVDD susceptibility. eQTL summary data were obtained from the IEU OpenGWAS database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/) and the deCODE database (https://www.decode.com/summarydata/) [35]. Genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary data for IVDD outcomes were obtained from the FinnGen database (https://www.finngen.fi/). Causal estimates were primarily obtained using the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method.


### Isolation and culture of NPPC
  Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (250–350 g, 2.5–3.5 months old) were obtained from the Animal Experimental Center of Yangzhou University (License No. SYXK (SU) 2021–0027). All experimental procedures were approved by the Yangzhou University Animal Ethics Committee(Approval No. 202403237). NP tissue separation and NPPC isolation were performed as previously described [36, 37]. For NPPC isolation, SD rats were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (40 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection) and subsequently euthanized with an overdose of pentobarbital sodium (150 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection). After confirmation of death, NP tissues were aseptically isolated from the caudal intervertebral discs under sterile conditions, followed by washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, USA). The tissue was cut into 1 mm³ fragments and digested at 37 °C for 12 h with collagenase type II (Gibco, USA). After washing with saline and centrifuging at 1000 rpm for 4 min, the cells were resuspended in MSC complete medium (Cyagen, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, HyClone, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Cells were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, and the medium was replaced every three days. When adherent cells reached 80% confluence, they were subcultured at a 1:3 ratio. Third-passage cells were used for subsequent experiments. To ensure biological reproducibility, NP tissues were collected from three adult Sprague–Dawley rats for each independent isolation, and the isolation procedure was repeated three times. All in vitro experiments were performed using third-passage NPPC derived from independent isolations.


### Cell treatment and cell viability assay
  To establish an oxidative stress-induced NPPC model, cells were cultured in complete medium containing various concentrations of TBHP (0, 25, 50, 100, 200, and 400 µM; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 12 h and 24 h. Prior to TBHP exposure, cells were pretreated with different concentrations of Lut (0, 5, 10, 20, and 40 µM; MedChem Express, China) for 12 h and 24 h to determine the optimal dose. Cell viability was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 2 × 103 cells/well were seeded into 96-well plates and cultured overnight at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Following the treatments, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent and 100 µL of fresh medium were added to each well and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad, USA). Cells were assigned to three groups: Control (untreated), TBHP (100 µM TBHP), and Lut+TBHP (10 µM Lut plus 100 µM TBHP).


### Cell proliferation assay
  Cell proliferation was assessed using a 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay (Beyotime, China). NPPC (5 × 105 cells/well) were seeded in 12-well plates and cultured at 37 °C in 5% CO2. According to the manufacturer’s protocol, cells were incubated with EdU for 2 h, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 10 min, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 15 min, and incubated with a Click reaction mixture for 30 min. After washing with PBS, cells were counterstained with Hoechst 33,342 in the dark for 10 min. Finally, cells were visualized under a fluorescence microscope and quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, USA).


### Senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) staining
  NPPC were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 4 × 105 cells/well and cultured at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Cellular senescence was assessed using a Senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) staining kit (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were fixed with the provided SA-β-Gal fixative solution for 15 min at room temperature and then incubated overnight with SA-β-Gal working solution at 37 °C under CO₂-free conditions. Stained cells were visualized under a microscope and quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, USA).


### Cell cycle assay
  Cell cycle distribution of TBHP-induced senescent was determined by flow cytometry using a Cell Cycle Detection Kit (Keygen, China). Briefly, NPPC were collected from 6-well plates, washed twice with ice-cold PBS, fixed in 75% ethanol at 4 °C for 2 h, and stained with a propidium iodide (PI)/RNase A solution for 30 min in the dark. Cell cycle distribution was analyzed using a flow cytometer (BD, USA).


### Western blot analysis
  Total protein from NPPC was extracted using a Whole Cell Lysis Kit (Keygen Biotech, China), and protein concentrations were measured with a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, China). Equal amounts of protein (30 µg per sample) were separated by 10%–12% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA). After blocking with 5% skim milk for 2 h at room temperature, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: p16 (1:1000; Proteintech, China), p21 (1:1000; Proteintech, China), SIRT1 (1:1000; Abcam, China), IL-1β (1:1000; ABclonal, China), IL-6 (1:1000; ABclonal, China), ATF5 (1:5000; Proteintech, China), LONP1 (1:5000; Proteintech, China), HSP60 (1:2000; Zenbio, China), CLPP (1:2000; Zenbio, China), GAPDH (1:1000; Servicebio, China) and β-ACTIN (1:5000; Attinity, China). Membranes were subsequently incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000; Abcam). Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system, and band intensities were quantified with ImageJ software (NIH, USA).


### Immunofluorescent staining
  NPPC was subjected to different treatments underwent immunofluorescence staining for SIRT1, ATF5, LONP1, HSP60, p16, p21, IL-1β, and IL-6. Briefly, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 25–30 min, followed by permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min. After blocking with 5% BSA for 60 min at room temperature, cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the respective primary antibodies. The cells were then washed three times with cold PBS and incubated with a 1:500 dilution of secondary antibody (ABclonal, China) for 60 min at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (2-(4-amidinophenyl)−1 H-indole-6-carboxamidine). Finally, cells were mounted using an anti-fade mounting medium (Servicebio, China) and observed under a fluorescence microscope.


### JC-1 assay for mitochondrial membrane potential
  Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) was assessed using a JC-1 Detection Kit (Keygen, China). Briefly, NPPC were incubated with 2 µM JC-1, a cationic fluorescent dye, for 20 min. After washing twice with incubation buffer, cells were resuspended in complete medium, visualized by fluorescence microscopy, and quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, USA).


### ROS assay
  Intracellular ROS levels were measured using a fluorescent probe-DHE kit (Keygen, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. NPPC were incubated with 20 µM dihydroethidium (DHE) for 30 min at 37 °C. Fluorescence signals were visualized under a microscope and quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, USA).


### Transmission electron microscopy
  Mitochondrial function was evaluated on transmission electron microscopy (TEM) sections by examining mitochondrial swelling and changes in cristae density.


### ATP quantification
  Mitochondrial function was further assessed by quantifying intracellular ATP levels with a luminometric assay. Following designated treatments (e.g., TBHP-induced oxidative stress and Lut pretreatment), NPPC cultured in 6-well plates were washed twice with ice-cold PBS. Subsequently, 200 µL of ATP lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Cat. No. S0026, China) was added to each well. Cells were fully lysed by repeated pipetting and incubated on ice for 10 min. The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C to pellet cellular debris, and the supernatants were collected into fresh microtubes for immediate analysis. A standard curve was generated using serial dilutions of ATP standards (0.01–10 µM) provided in the kit. For quantification, 20 µL of each supernatant or standard was mixed with 100 µL of ATP detection working solution in opaque 96-well plates. Luminescence signals [relative light units (RLU)] were detected using a microplate luminometer (BioTek Synergy H1, USA). ATP concentrations were calculated from the standard curve and normalized to total protein content determined by the BCA assay.


### Cell transfection
  SIRT1-specific siRNA (si-SIRT1) and scrambled negative control siRNA (si-NC) were synthesized by GenePharma (China). Transfection was carried out with Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. For each well, 5 µL of Lipofectamine 3000 was diluted in 125 µL of Opti-MEM medium (Gibco, USA), while 100 pmol of siRNA (si-SIRT1 or si-NC) was separately diluted in 125 µL of Opti-MEM. The diluted siRNA and Lipofectamine 3000 solutions were mixed, incubated at room temperature for 15 min, and then added dropwise to the cells. After 6 h of incubation, the transfection medium was replaced with fresh complete culture medium.


### Establishment of rat IVDD model
  Thirty male SD rats, aged 2–4 months and weighing 200–300 g, were randomly assigned to three groups: Control (n = 10), IVDD (n = 10), and Lut + IVDD (n = 10). The IVDD model was established in SD rats according to a previously described method [37]. Briefly, rats were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (40 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection), and the tail skin was disinfected with povidone–iodine. A 21-gauge needle was inserted into the coccygeal intervertebral discs (Co 4–5, Co 6–7, Co 8–9), rotated 180°, and maintained in place at a depth of 5 mm for 5 s. Two weeks post-surgery, the Lut + IVDD group received intragastric administration of Lut (100 mg/kg, 5 mg/mL suspended in 0.5% sodium carboxymethyl cellulose) every other day for 4 or 8 weeks. Control and IVDD groups received 0.5% sodium carboxymethyl cellulose solution for 4 or 8 weeks.


### Behavioral assessment
  Pain-related behaviors in rats were assessed using an open-field test at 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 weeks [39, 40]. Rats were placed in a 60 × 60 cm open-field box, and their behavior was recorded for 10 min from a bird’s-eye view. Animals were habituated for 10 min in a pen on the day prior to testing. The lighting and testing time were kept consistent. A gait analysis system (SANS SA114, SCIMON Science Intelligent Technology, China) was used to evaluate 11 parameters related to LBP, as described previously [40]. Mechanical sensitivity was evaluated using a Von Frey filament pain meter. Three rats were acclimated for 15 min in a transparent acrylic chamber placed on a metal grid before testing. A maximum force of 30 g was applied, gradually increased over 10 s, and maintained for up to 40 s. Withdrawal threshold and latency were sequentially and alternately assessed by applying the probe to the tail base and surgical site. Each measurement was repeated at least three times, and the average value was calculated. All behavioral assessments were performed by investigators blinded to group allocation.


### Radiographic evaluation and histological analysis
  At 6 weeks post-puncture, five SD rats from each of the Control, IVDD, and Lut +IVDD groups (n = 15 total) were selected. At 10 weeks post-puncture, the remaining 15 rats were collected. All 30 SD rats were anesthetized with pentobarbital and placed in a prone position. Radiographic (X-ray) and MRI images were acquired, and the disc height index (DHI) [41] was calculated using ImageJ software. The severity of IVDD was evaluated according to the Pfirrmann grading system [42]. After euthanasia with an overdose of pentobarbital, tail specimens were harvested for NP area measurement and histological evaluation[43]. The specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, decalcified in EDTA for 1 month, dehydrated in graded ethanol, and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin sections were stained with Safranin O–Fast Green (SF), hematoxylin and eosin (HE), and Masson’s trichrome.


### Immunohistochemistry
  Paraffin-embedded intervertebral disc (IVD) sections underwent antigen retrieval by incubation in citrate buffer (pH 6.0; Servicebio, G1202) followed by microwave heating at high power for 6–8 min. To block nonspecific protein binding, sections were incubated with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at room temperature for 30 min. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. After washing, sections were incubated with the corresponding secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, sections were dehydrated, mounted, and digitized using a slide scanner. Detailed information on the primary antibodies used for human NP immunohistochemistry is as follows: SIRT1 (Abcam, Ab110304, 1:500), ATF5 (Zenbio, 381431, 1:500), HSP60 (Zenbio, R24630, 1:200), LONP1 (Proteintech, 15440-1-AP, 1:200), and CLPP (Zenbio, R389240, 1:200). All staining was performed under identical conditions using the same batch of reagents, and imaging parameters were kept constant to ensure comparability across samples. To further ensure anatomical consistency across human intervertebral disc specimens, all disc tissues were removed during posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF) surgery. Immediately after extraction, the outer annulus fibrosus layers were carefully dissected away under direct visualization, and only the centrally located gelatinous NP tissue was retained for analysis. This approach ensured that each specimen originated from the same well-defined anatomical compartment. The sampling procedure was performed by the same surgical team following a standardized intraoperative protocol. Degeneration grade was determined preoperatively by MRI and reconfirmed intraoperatively to exclude severely collapsed or structurally distorted discs. Only NP tissue free of annulus fibrosus or endplate contamination was included.


### Data statistical analysis
  All experiments were performed in triplicate, and data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Statistical analyses were conducted with GraphPad Prism 12.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). Statistical significance was defined as follows: ns, not significant; *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001.


## Results

### Network pharmacological analysis
  Valid information on Lut was retrieved from PubChem, including Chi SMILES and related structural data. Potential targets of Lut were predicted using BATMAN (probability ≥ 80%), PharmMapper (score ≥ 0.7), SymMap, and SwissTargetPrediction (probability ≥ 0.01). After eliminating duplicates, 245 unique compound targets were identified (Fig. 1A). IVDD-related targets were retrieved from GeneCards (score ≥ 15), CTD (score ≥ 15), and OpenTargets (score ≥ 0.5), yielding 408 disease-associated targets (Fig. 1B). Intersecting these sets revealed 48 common targets (Fig. 1C). A Lut–target–IVDD interaction network was constructed (Fig. 1D), consisting of 50 nodes and 96 edges, with Lut connected to 48 targets. These 48 overlapping targets were imported into the STRING database to generate a PPI network (Fig. 1E) and further analyzed in Cytoscape (Fig. 1F). The final network contained 46 nodes and 561 edges, representing protein–protein interactions, excluding isolated nodes. Topological analysis with the Network Analyzer tool identified the 30 most significant core nodes (CC/BC/degree), which included IL6, TNF, CASP3, MMP9, TP53, ESR1, JUN, PPARG, MMP2, NFKB1, IL10, SIRT1, and HSP90AA1 (Fig. 1G). GO enrichment analysis of the 48 common targets was performed using the R package clusterProfiler, with the top 15 biological process (BP), molecular function (MF), and cellular component (CC) terms (P < 0.05) visualized as bar plots (Fig. 1H). BP analysis indicated significant enrichment in stress response–related processes, such as responses to amyloid-beta, reactive oxygen species, oxidative stress, and hypoxia, as well as extracellular matrix organization and epithelial cell apoptosis. CC terms showed enrichment in nuclear complexes, transcriptional regulatory assemblies, membrane microdomains, and nuclear subcompartments, suggesting spatial specialization of these proteins. MF terms demonstrated strong enrichment in catalytic functions, particularly serine-type endopeptidase and metalloendopeptidase activities, along with notable enrichment in binding functions including cytokine receptor binding, transcription coregulator binding, and signaling receptor activator activity. KEGG pathway analysis identified 134 enriched pathways, with the top 30 visualized as bubble plots (Fig. 1I). Notably, SIRT1 exhibited high degree centrality within the compound–disease network, supporting its role as a key hub of Lut’s action. Consistent with this, enriched GO terms emphasized responses to oxidative stress and hypoxia, and KEGG pathways clustered within stress- and metabolism-related categories, aligning with the established role of SIRT1 in mitochondrial homeostasis. Guided by these insights, we next investigated whether Lut engages the SIRT1–ATF5 axis to activate the UPRmt.


### Molecular docking, molecular dynamics (MD) and simulation
  To investigate the direct molecular interaction between Lut and SIRT1, we performed molecular docking analysis. As shown in Fig. 1J, Lut (yellow stick model) was found to dock securely within a well-defined binding pocket of the SIRT1 protein (blue-gray ribbon structure). The binding conformation demonstrates excellent complementarity between the Lut molecule and the SIRT1 active site. The detailed view (right panel) reveals specific molecular interactions that stabilize the Lut-SIRT1 complex. The bonding information is as follows: Proteins 273PHE and 297PHE form hydrophobic interactions with small molecules, 262ALA, 280TYR, 345GLN, 412VAL, 440GLY, 441SER, 442SER, 443LEU, and 444LYS form hydrogen bonds with small molecules, and 414PHE forms a π-π stacking with small molecules. The flavonoid structure of Lut appears to fit snugly within the binding cavity, with its hydroxyl groups positioned to form critical hydrogen bonds with polar residues in the SIRT1 active site. The docking results indicated a strong binding affinity between Lut and SIRT1, with a calculated binding energy of −9.5 kcal/mol, suggesting a spontaneous and stable interaction. This molecular docking analysis provided structural evidence supporting our hypothesis that Lut directly binds to and activates SIRT1, thereby initiating the downstream signaling cascade that activates the UPRmt through ATF5 activation. These docking results suggested that Lut may interact directly with SIRT1, and potentially exert biological effects through SIRT1 in the process of IVDD. In the molecular dynamics simulation, the RMSD of the complex protein skeleton and the RMSD of small molecules were analyzed first. The RMSD value of the skeleton atoms stabilized at around 0.43–0.54 nm after 40 ns, confirming that the system has achieved a good balance. After 40 ns, the RMSD of the small molecule stabilized at approximately 0.47–0.70 nm, indicating that the small molecule could stably bind to the hydrophobic port pocket of the protein (Fig. 1K). Then the Rg cyclotron radius of the protein during the simulation process was analyzed. It can be found that the Rg of the protein was in a stable state during the simulation process, and the surface proteins were relatively stable during the simulation process (Fig. 1L). Then, the changes in the number of hydrogen bonds directly formed between the protein and the small molecule during the simulation process were analyzed. One to three hydrogen bonds were formed between the protein and the small molecule after 40 ns during the simulation process, stabilizing the binding of the protein and the small molecule (Fig. 1M). Finally, the MM-PBSA method was adopted to calculate and decompose the free energy of the system in the last 10 ns, in order to deeply understand the mechanical basis of protein–small molecule interactions. ### Protein expression of SIRT1 and UPRmt-related markers is inversely correlated with the severity of human IVDD
  To determine the clinical relevance of SIRT1 and mitochondrial quality control in IVDD, we analyzed human disc tissues across pathological Grade II to V. Representative T2-weighted MRI images (Fig. 2A) revealed progressive loss of hydration and structural integrity with advancing grade. IHC staining (Fig. 2B) showed a marked decline in SIRT1 and key mitochondrial proteases (ATF5, HSP60, LONP1, CLPP) from Grade II to V. In Grade II/III tissues, strong positive staining indicated high protein expression, whereas Grade IV/V tissues exhibited substantially reduced staining intensity and area. These observations were supported by quantitative data: histograms of protein immunoreactivity (Fig. 2C) and WB analysis (Fig. 2D) consistently showed significantly higher levels of SIRT1, ATF5, HSP60, LONP1, and CLPP in Grade II/III than in Grade IV/V tissues. Densitometric analysis of WB bands (Fig. 2E) confirmed a statistically significant (p < 0.01) stepwise decrease in protein expression with each grade of degeneration. Collectively, these findings demonstrate a strong inverse correlation between SIRT1/UPRmt protein expression and IVDD severity, implicating them in disease pathogenesis and progression. Notably, ATF5, HSP60, LONP1, and CLPP, as canonical UPRmt components, showed a stepwise decline, indicating breakdown of mitochondrial proteostasis in vivo. Integrating network pharmacology prioritization of SIRT1 as a core hub, these human data highlight the SIRT1–ATF5–UPRmt axis as clinically relevant to IVDD, providing mechanistic rationale for subsequent in experimental validation.

### Effects of Lut on NPPC viability
  NPPC were exposed to varying concentrations of Lut or TBHP for 12 and 24 h, and cell viability was measured using the CCK-8 assay. At concentrations below 10 µM, Lut showed no evident cytotoxicity toward NPPC at either time point (Fig. 3A). Consequently, 10 µM Lut was selected for subsequent experiments, with 12 h as the standardized treatment duration. Upon TBHP exposure, NPPC viability declined in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3B). At 100 µM, TBHP reduced NPPC viability to approximately 50% of control levels, establishing a suitable oxidative stress model without complete loss of cell viability. Therefore, 100 µM TBHP for 12 h was chosen for subsequent experiments.


### Lut attenuates oxidative stress-induced cellular senescence in NPPC​
  To evaluate the protective role of Lut against TBHP-induced cellular senescence, NPPC were pretreated with Lut before TBHP exposure. An EdU incorporation assay revealed that TBHP markedly suppressed DNA synthesis, whereas Lut pretreatment effectively restored proliferative capacity (Fig. 3C). SA-β-gal staining, a hallmark of cellular senescence, showed that TBHP significantly increased the proportion of senescent cells, while Lut pretreatment substantially reduced SA-β-gal positivity (Fig. 3D). Flow cytometric analysis of the cell cycle demonstrated that TBHP increased the proportion of NPPC arrested at the G2/M phase, whereas Lut pretreatment alleviated this arrest (Fig. 3E, I). Western blotting confirmed that Lut downregulated TBHP-induced upregulation of senescence markers p16 and p21. Moreover, Lut pretreatment significantly suppressed TBHP-induced expression of SASP cytokines IL-1β and IL-6 (Fig. 3F). To further confirm these findings, immunofluorescence staining was conducted for senescence markers (p16 and p21) and pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β and IL-6) (Fig. 3K–O). Compared with the control group, TBHP-treated NPPC exhibited markedly increased fluorescence intensities of these proteins, confirming enhanced senescence and inflammation under oxidative stress. Lut pretreatment markedly diminished these fluorescence signals, demonstrating effective suppression of senescence and inflammation. Quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensities corroborated these results, showing significantly lower levels of p16, p21, IL-1β, and IL-6 in the Lut + TBHP group compared with the TBHP group (Fig. 3K). Collectively, these findings indicated that Lut effectively protects NPPC against oxidative stress-induced senescence.

### Lut activates UPRmt and restores mitochondrial function under oxidative stress​
  Based on the network-derived prioritization of SIRT1 and the observed decline of UPRmt markers in human tissues, we hypothesized that Lut restores mitochondrial proteostasis through activation of the UPRmt via the SIRT1–ATF5 axis. To test this hypothesis, we evaluated mitochondrial function—including MMP, ROS, and ATP production—as well as the expression of UPRmt-related proteins (ATF5, HSP60, LONP1, CLPP) in NPPC subjected to oxidative stress with or without Lut treatment. Mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed using the JC-1 probe. Under normal conditions, high MMP results in red fluorescence from J-aggregates, whereas low MMP produces green fluorescence from monomers. As shown in Fig. 4A and I, TBHP treatment markedly decreased MMP, which was partially restored by Lut. ROS levels were elevated in TBHP-treated NPPC relative to controls but were reduced by Lut treatment (Fig. 4C and J). TEM revealed that TBHP caused mitochondrial shrinkage and increased cristae density, while Lut treatment restored mitochondrial morphology and reduced cristae abnormalities (Fig. 4B). Western blotting revealed that TBHP markedly downregulated UPRmt-related proteins (SIRT1, ATF5, HSP60, LONP1, CLPP), whereas Lut treatment upregulated their expression (Fig. 4D and K). Immunofluorescence results corroborated these findings, demonstrating reduced fluorescence intensity of these proteins in the TBHP group, which was reversed in the Lut-treated group (Fig. 4E–G, L). Finally, consistent with mitochondrial functional assays, intracellular ATP levels were quantified to assess mitochondrial bioenergetic recovery, confirming that Lut significantly alleviated TBHP-induced ATP depletion.

### Inhibition of SIRT1 attenuates the protective effect of Lut on cellular senescence of TBHP-treated NPPC
  To determine whether SIRT1 mediates the anti-senescence effects of Lut, SIRT1 expression was silenced in NPPC using specific siRNA prior to Lut and TBHP treatment. SA-β-gal staining revealed that TBHP significantly increased cellular senescence, whereas Lut pretreatment markedly reduced the proportion of SA-β-gal–positive cells. However, SIRT1 knockdown (si-SIRT1) largely abolished this protective effect, as evidenced by increased SA-β-gal–positive cells compared with the Lut+TBHP + si-NC group (Fig. 5A and D). The EdU incorporation assay demonstrated that TBHP markedly reduced DNA synthesis activity, while Lut restored proliferative capacity. Importantly, SIRT1 inhibition prevented Lut-mediated recovery of DNA synthesis, with EdU-positive cells remaining at low levels similar to the TBHP group (Fig. 5C and E). Flow cytometric cell cycle analysis revealed that TBHP exposure increased the proportion of NPPC in G2/M phase, indicating cell cycle arrest, which was alleviated by Lut treatment. Western blotting confirmed that Lut suppressed TBHP-induced upregulation of senescence markers p16 and p21 and pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-6. Immunofluorescence analyses corroborated these results, showing that SIRT1 knockdown attenuated Lut-mediated reductions in the fluorescence intensity of p16, p21, IL-1β, and IL-6 (Fig. 5G–K). Collectively, these findings indicated that SIRT1 is indispensable for the protective effects of Lut against oxidative stress-induced cellular senescence.

### Lut effectively prevents IVDD in rat model
  To investigate the effects of Lut on in vivo IVDD model, we established an IVDD rat model and performed radiological assessments at different postoperative time points (6 weeks and 10 weeks) to observe the progression of IVDD, including changes in the DHI and Pfirrmann grading. X-ray and MRI imaging were used to evaluate changes in DHI and NP signal intensity [44]. In the IVDD group, DHI significantly decreased, while Lut treatment reversed this reduction (Fig. 8A, B, G). In the IVDD group, NP signal intensity significantly decreased, and the Pfirrmann grade significantly increased, whereas Lut treatment reversed these changes (Fig. 8C-F). Additionally, we performed measurements of the NP area and histological scoring on gross specimens of discs from different groups. In the IVDD group, the relative area of the NP progressively decreased over time, while histological scores progressively increased; however, Lut treatment reversed these changes (Fig. 8H-L). In addition to radiographic assessments, histological analysis was conducted to evaluate the efficacy of different treatments. Specifically, histological assessments were conducted using HE, Safranin O–Fast Green (SF), and Masson’s trichrome staining to evaluate disc morphology and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling across different groups (Fig. 9A, B). HE staining revealed that, at 6 weeks, the control group exhibited intact disc architecture with a well-preserved nucleus pulposus and regularly aligned annulus fibrosus (AF) lamellae. Both the IVDD and Lut+IVDD groups still retained visible NP tissue; however, the NP area was reduced compared with controls, and the AF lamellae were disorganized. The Lut+IVDD group showed slightly better preservation of disc structure than the IVDD group.

  By 10 weeks, the IVDD group displayed near-complete loss of NP tissue and severely disrupted AF organization. Similarly, the Lut+IVDD group also showed almost complete disappearance of the NP, but the AF lamellae remained relatively more orderly compared with the IVDD group, indicating that Lut treatment mitigated AF disorganization despite the loss of NP.

  SF staining further highlighted ECM alterations. In the control group, the NP exhibited strong red staining, indicating abundant proteoglycan content, while the AF showed dense green lamellae. At 6 weeks, the IVDD group demonstrated diminished NP red staining with increased green deposition, reflecting proteoglycan loss and fibrotic replacement. The Lut+IVDD group retained stronger red staining in the NP, suggesting preserved proteoglycan levels. At 10 weeks, NP staining in the IVDD group was almost entirely lost, replaced by disorganized fibrous tissue, whereas the Lut+IVDD group displayed a larger residual NP area with greater proteoglycan enrichment and more regular AF lamellae. Masson’s trichrome staining revealed differences in collagen fiber deposition. At 6 weeks, all groups retained recognizable NP tissue. Both IVDD and Lut+IVDD groups showed increased blue collagen staining within the NP compared with controls, but differences between the two groups were not obvious at this stage. By 10 weeks, the NP was nearly absent in both IVDD and Lut+IVDD groups. However, the AF in the IVDD group exhibited severely disorganized collagen bundles, while the Lut+IVDD group showed relatively more orderly and lamellar alignment. Collectively, these histological results demonstrated that Lut treatment slowed IVDD by preserving NP morphology and maintaining proteoglycan content. The SIRT1 and ATF5 immunohistochemistry results were shown in Fig. 9D-E. The IVDD group displayed a reduced NP area and lower staining intensity compared with the control group. In the Lut+IVDD group, the staining appeared slightly stronger than in the IVDD group, suggesting a modest increase in SIRT1 and ATF5. The results of HE, SF and Masson staining, as well as the immunohistochemical scoring, were shown in the heatmap in Fig. 9F. In summary, these results indicated that Lut can alleviate the IVDD process in the puncture-induced rat model Fig. 10.

## Discussion
  The present study integrated human, cellular, and in vivo evidence demonstrating that Lut attenuates IVDD by restoring mitochondrial proteostasis via the SIRT1–ATF5–UPRmt axis. In human NP tissues, we observed a progressive decline in SIRT1 and canonical UPRmt effectors, including ATF5, HSP60, LONP1, and CLPP, across Pfirrmann grades II–V, thereby linking impaired mitochondrial quality control with increasing disease severity. In TBHP-induced NPPC senescence models, Lut stabilized the MMP, ROS, ATP, and SASP markers. Genetic knockdown of SIRT1 abrogated these protective effects, establishing SIRT1 as indispensable for Lut’s actions. In the rat model of puncture-induced IVDD, oral administration of Lut mitigated disc height loss, alleviated histopathological deterioration, and improved mechanical pain sensitivity. Collectively, these findings established SIRT1 as a central therapeutic target and UPRmt reactivation as a promising disease-modifying strategy for IVDD. Oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction are widely recognized as key drivers of disc cell senescence, extracellular matrix catabolism, and the progression of IVDD [45]. Mitochondrial dysfunction perpetuates a vicious cycle of excessive ROS production, bioenergetic collapse and programmed cell death within disc tissues [46].

  Our findings demonstrated that Lut restores mitochondrial function and alleviates senescence align well with this framework but extend it further by emphasizing the collapse of the UPRmt as a critical bottleneck in IVDD. The UPRmt is a conserved adaptive mechanism that preserves organelle proteostasis by upregulating mitochondrial chaperones and proteases, thereby refolding or eliminating damaged proteins [47]. In mammals, ATF5 is the core transcription factor that activates UPRmt target genes and preserves mitochondrial integrity under stress [48]. Our results that ATF5 and its downstream effectors declined progressively with human IVDD grades underscores the clinical relevance of this pathway in IVDD. Interestingly, our findings align with those of Xu et al., who reported a progressive decline in UPRmt markers in human degenerative disc, thereby supporting the notion that UPRmt collapse is a hallmark of advanced IVDD.

  SIRT1 is well established as an NAD+-dependent deacetylase with broad roles in anti-aging and mitochondrial regulation. In IVDD, SIRT1 activation counteracted NP cell senescence, promotes mitophagy, and suppresses inflammatory and catabolic responses, whereas its depletion accelerates degeneration [49]. Prior studies, however, have largely examined mature NP cells and downstream pathways such as NF-κB suppression, autophagy enhancement, or PGC-1α–mediated mitochondrial biogenesis [50–52]. In contrast, our study provided a complementary perspective by evaluating the role of SIRT1 in NPPC and examining its potential involvement in ATF5-associated UPRmt signaling. Our results suggested that impaired mitochondrial proteostasis may be an upstream contributor to NPPC senescence and that SIRT1 helps maintain this process partly through ATF5-dependent mechanisms. These observations do not redefine SIRT1’s established role but refine current understanding by indicating that mitochondrial protein-folding homeostasis may be one pathway through which SIRT1 contributes to disc cell resilience. Further supporting this mechanism, studies in cardiac tissue have demonstrated that SIRT1-dependent activation of ATF5 can trigger UPRmt and restore mitochondrial function under stress, a signaling framework consistent with our findings in NPPC [52]. This cross-tissue evidence suggested that the SIRT1→ATF5→UPRmt cascade may represent a conserved protective module relevant to multiple degenerative contexts.

  Lut is a pleiotropic flavonoid with well-established antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and senolytic properties. Previous studies across diverse biological systems reported that Lut activates SIRT1, thereby improving mitochondrial function and tissue resilience during aging [31, 53]. In the present study, molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations suggested direct binding of Lut to SIRT1, while genetic epistasis confirmed that Lut required SIRT1 to activate UPRmt, stabilize mitochondrial function, and suppress SASP. These findings elevate Lut from a general antioxidant to a direct modulator of mitochondrial proteostasis. Evidence from related natural products reinforces this view; for instance, quercetin has been shown to protect discs through SIRT1-dependent autophagy [54]. Our results positioned the UPRmt, rather than autophagy alone, at the core of mitochondrial rescue in disc cells, while acknowledging that crosstalk between these pathways is probable and may confer therapeutic synergy. In our in vivo study, oral Lut administration attenuated disc structural degeneration and restored mitochondrial proteostasis in NP cells, suggesting that systemically delivered Lut can exert biological effects within the disc. Although the NP is an avascular tissue, it is not completely isolated from systemic circulation. Small solutes can enter the NP through diffusion-driven transport across the cartilage endplate (CEP) and, to a lesser extent, the outer AF, both of which interface with vertebral subchondral capillaries [55]. These pathways constitute the physiological routes through which nutrients, metabolites, and small pharmacologic agents reach the disc. Importantly, several orally administered natural compounds—such as Quercetin and Urolithin A have been shown in vivo to modulate NP inflammation, mitochondrial homeostasis, and extracellular matrix metabolism [56, 57], providing functional evidence that systemically delivered small molecules can indeed penetrate the disc.

  In our in vivo rat IVDD model, oral administration of Lut improved mechanical sensitivity in Von Frey testing and preserved disc structure, as demonstrated by imaging and histological analyses. We found Lut treatment also partially reduced AF disorganization. This observation suggested a potential beneficial effect on AF structure; however, the mechanistic work in this study was focused solely on NPPC, and we currently do not have direct evidence demonstrating that Lut acts on AF cells. Therefore, the AF improvement may reflect indirect changes resulting from a stabilized NP microenvironment rather than a confirmed direct pharmacological effect. Further studies are needed to determine whether Lut exerts specific actions on the AF. Additionally, although open-field locomotor measures did not achieve statistical significance, this is consistent with their relatively low sensitivity for detecting axial or caudal pain compared with evoked measures, in line with previous studies [58–60]. Taken together, these behavioral and structural outcomes supported the translational potential of systemic Lut delivery as a disc-protective intervention. From a clinical perspective, biomarker-guided strategies may be considered, with patients exhibiting low NP expression of SIRT1/UPRmt markers potentially deriving the greatest benefit. Moreover, serum or imaging-based correlates of mitochondrial stress could serve as pharmacodynamic indicators of treatment response. Given reports that the UPRmt may also support tumor survival in certain contexts, careful dose-optimization strategies and tissue-targeted delivery approaches—such as disc-localized formulations or short systemic pulses—will be critical for safe clinical translation.

  This study still has several limitations that warrant consideration. The tail puncture model recapitulates certain aspects of mechanical injury and inflammatory sequelae but does not fully capture the chronic and multifactorial characteristics of human IVDD, and interspecies differences in disc composition remain an important caveat. While our docking and genetic evidence strongly support SIRT1 as the primary target of Lut in NPPC, potential off-target effects cannot be excluded, and the pharmacokinetic properties of Lut require further optimization. Additionally, although our Mendelian randomization analysis supports a causal link between SIRT1 and IVDD risk, the genetic instruments for SIRT1 remain relatively weak, and future studies incorporating tissue-specific associations would further strengthen the inference. Another limitation of this study is that only male SD rats were used in the in vivo experiments. This decision was made to reduce biological variability in behavioral assessments, as female rodents display well-documented fluctuations in nociceptive sensitivity related to estrous cycle–dependent hormonal changes, which could substantially increase variability in pain-related outcomes [61]. Moreover, the use of male rats is consistent with common methodological practice in needle-puncture–induced IVDD models; a recent systematic review reported that approximately two-thirds of published rat caudal puncture studies employed male animals, partly due to their larger body size, which facilitates disc localization and intradiscal delivery [62]. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that excluding female animals is a limitation. Future studies incorporating both sexes will be necessary to determine whether Lut produces sex-specific effects on disc pathology or therapeutic response.


## Conclusion
  In summary, this study demonstrated that oxidative stress–induced senescence of NPPC plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of IVDD. We identified a critical decline in the SIRT1/ATF5-dependent UPRmt during progressive of IVDD, underscoring the clinical relevance of impaired mitochondrial quality control. Through a combination of network pharmacology, molecular docking, Mendelian randomization, and in vitro and in vivo validation, we confirmed that Lut directly targets SIRT1, activates the SIRT1/ATF5 axis, enhances UPRmt, restores mitochondrial function, and alleviates oxidative stress–induced NPPC senescence. Importantly, in a rat IVDD model, Lut treatment delayed IVDD.

  Collectively, our findings highlighted Lut as a promising disease-modifying compound with multifaceted protective effects against IVDD progression. By targeting mitochondrial proteostasis and cellular senescence pathways, Lut offers a mechanistically informed therapeutic strategy that warrants further translational investigation for the prevention and treatment of IVDD disease.
