# Impact of Chemical Quality on High-Throughput In Vitro Assays – A Tox21 Study


## Abstract
  The Tox21 10K chemical library, an in vitro toxicology toolbox consisting of more than 8,900 unique chemical entities including environmental chemicals and drugs, has undergone analytical quality control (QC) testing after storage at room temperature for 0 and 4 months (T0 and T4). Each chemical was previously assigned a QC grade based on purity, identity, and concentration. In parallel, the Tox21 10K library has been tested across approximately 80 in vitro assays in quantitative high-throughput screening (qHTS) format, generating >120M datapoints to date. These data were used to analyze the correlation between chemical quality and bioassay activity, as well as chemical structure. The chemical characteristics of poor-quality and unstable compounds were explored to identify structural features that should be avoided. In addition, one of the high-throughput assays measuring the induction of p53 activity by small molecules was used to test the Tox21 10K compound library at T0 and T4 due to its robust performance and reproducibility. Approximately 2% of compounds in the library showed a significant change in activity in the p53 assay between T0 and T4 (active to inactive or vice versa), which also correlated with chemical stability. Here, machine learning models were constructed using bioassay data or chemical structure to predict poor-quality (low QC grades at T0) and unstable (grade drop from T0 to T4) chemicals. Chemical structure was found to be highly predictive (0.75) of chemical quality and stability, whereas bioassay data was less predictive (0.66) but still showed better than random performance. Taken together, these findings provide valuable guidance for interpreting the Tox21 assay results and informing best practices for future chemical selection and handling.


## Introduction
  Compound libraries are used extensively in drug discovery and development, enabling researchers to efficiently assess the bioactivities of chemicals in an automated fashion, i.e., high-throughput screening (HTS), which can test a large number of small molecules at a time. Screening a compound library allows for the rapid identification of priority endpoints that can be used to characterize the biological or toxicological properties of individual chemicals or chemical classes.1 As such, evaluating the quality and stability of the constituent library compounds is a critical step to ensure high-quality screening and reproducibility. Chemical quality assessment generally includes the verification of chemical identity and determination of purity.2 For high-throughput testing, compounds are often dissolved in a solvent, such as dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and stored as solutions.3 Accordingly, concentration is another quality metric commonly assessed for samples used in HTS experiments. Moreover, chemical stability refers to the change in quality of the compound over a course of time.4 There has been growing interest in assessing the quality of large compound libraries, as sample quality and compound stability can interfere with the interpretation of the activities observed of compounds in an assay. A compound library that is ready for use will be thawed at room temperature and exposed to atmospheric test conditions for a period of time, during which degradation of compounds may occur. Degraded compounds may result in false negatives or missed detections due to a lower-than-expected biological activity of the target compound, or false positives attributed to activities caused by degradation products. Similarly, variations in compound qualities can interfere with assay outcomes and lead to inconsistent data. A better understanding of time-dependent changes can highlight compound integrity and limitations that may arise from the usage of compounds stored in a repository collection.

  In recent years, there have been increasing concerns over library quality due to the impact of compound quality on activities observed in biological assays. It is often difficult to distinguish whether low assay activity is simply the result of minimal interaction with the biological target or a consequence of quality control issues related to a compound. Errors of unknown origins that may arise from the compound collection or assay set-up can often be minimized through the implementation of quality control procedures. Therefore, it is helpful to evaluate the quality and stability of compounds early in the drug development process before significant resources are invested. Especially with the wide applications of HTS, compound quality and stability have become a focal point as of late.

  Designed for high-throughput toxicity testing, the Tox21 10K compound library consists of ~10,000 plated samples representing more than 8900 unique chemical entities including drugs and environmental chemicals.5–9 The Tox21 10K library was constructed by consolidating three separately sourced, partially overlapping, and approximately equal-sized compound libraries from three Tox21 partners (NCATS, NIEHS/DTT, and EPA). The NCATS contribution was the NCGC Pharmaceutical Collection (NPC), a library of approximately 3800 drugs registered or approved for use in humans or animals. The NIEHS/DTT contributed approximately 3000 unique compounds of environmental and toxicological concerns. Lastly, the EPA contribution was approximately 4000 compounds procured from ToxCast chemical inventory. The completion of the Tox21 10K library allowed for the development of new computational approaches to predict toxicity for a large number of chemicals to better understand mechanisms of action. Compounds in the Tox21 10K library were selected based on several criteria including solubility in DMSO, commercial availability, cost, known or perceived environmental hazards or exposure concerns, and physicochemical properties indicating suitability for HTS.9 The Tox21 10K library includes environmental chemicals such as PFAS, pesticides, and industrial contaminants that have possible or definite toxicity risks. In addition, the Tox21 10K library also contains a collection of drugs that are inherently stable by design to exert their intended biological purpose.

  The entire Tox21 10K library of plated sample solutions in DMSO was subjected to quality control (QC) for chemical identity, purity, and concentration at two different time points: 1) when the compound plates were freshly taken out of the −80 °C storage and thawed at room temperature (T0), and 2) when the compounds were exposed to room temperature for 4 months (T4).10 A grade was assigned to each sample at each time point (T0 or T4) based on its identity, purity and concentration determinations.10 The results revealed that 92% of newly thawed samples received a QC grade and 76% of samples exceeded 90% purity. Of the 76% samples tested at T4, the majority (89%) had no evidence of degradation. The Tox21 10K library has been screened against ~80 cell-based assays for potential biological pathway disruptions that may result in toxicity.6, 11, 12 When combined with the QC results, this set of activity profiles afforded the opportunity to investigate the impact of chemical quality and stability on assay activity. All assay data and detailed descriptions of these assays are publicly available through the National Center for Advancing Translational Sciences (NCATS) Tox21 public data browser7 and PubChem13. To further investigate the relationship between compound quality, stability, and bioactivity, we utilized the p53 assay, a robust and reproducible assay that monitors activation of the p53 tumor suppressor pathway14, to screen the Tox21 10K library at multiple time points (i.e., 0, 0.5, 2, 4, and 6 months) following room temperature exposure. The p53 protein is a critical regulator of cellular stress responses, and its activation can indicate compounds that disrupt cellular homeostasis, making this assay particularly informative for monitoring compound integrity over time.

  In this study, we analyzed the structural features and physicochemical properties of poor-quality (failed QC at T0) and unstable (passed QC at T0 but failed at T4) chemicals to reveal characteristics of these problematic compounds. To investigate the impact of compound stability on assay activity, the library age-dependent p53 assay activity data were analyzed in parallel with the T0/T4 chemical QC results. In addition, we built computational models to further explore the correlation between chemical quality/stability and chemical structure, as well as biological response. That is, we sought to understand if chemical structure is predictive of compound quality/stability, and the extent of impact of compound quality/stability on bioassay activity. The chemical structure-based models could be used to predict chemical quality/stability to avoid problematic compounds during the construction of screening libraries.


## Materials and Methods

### p53-bla reporter assay
  The p53RE-bla HCT-116 cell line that stably expresses a β-lactamase (bla) reporter gene under control of the p53 response element was purchased from Invitrogen (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Carlsbad, CA). These p53RE-bla cells were cultured in McCoy’s 5A medium supplemented with 10% dialyzed fetal bovine serum (FBS), 5 mg/mL blasticidin, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. As previously described14, p53RE-bla cells were suspended in OPTI-MEM medium containing 0.5% dialyzed FBS and dispensed at 4,000 cells/5 μL/well in 1536-well black wall/clear bottom plates using a Multidrop Combi dispenser (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). Compounds were dissolved in anhydrous DMSO (≥99.9%, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) to a target concentration of 20 mM, with a small number of chemical samples plated at lower concentrations (5–10 mM) due to DMSO insolubility and a few highly potent estrogenic compounds plated at 0.2 mM. Two positive controls, Mitomycin C and Nutlin-3, were used in this assay. The final concentration of DMSO within each well was less than 0.5%, which is within the safe/standard range for cell health. After 5 h incubation of the assay plates at 37 °C, 23 nL of compounds dissolved in DMSO, positive controls or DMSO only was transferred to the assay plate by a Wako Pintool station (Wako Automation, San Diego, CA). The plates were then incubated at 37 °C for 16 h. After 1 μL of LiveBLAzer™ B/G FRET substrate was added into the assay plates using a Flying Reagent Dispenser, the plates were incubated at room temperature for 2 h, and fluorescence intensity at 460 nm/530 nm emission and 405 nm excitation were measured by an Envision plate reader (Perkin Elmer, Shelton, CT). Full summary information for the p53 assay is available on the PubChem database (AID 1963584).


### In vitro assay data
  The assay data used in this study were generated by screening the Tox21 10K library against a panel of qHTS assays, and the data are publicly available on the NCATS Tox21 public browser7 (https://tripod.nih.gov/pubdata/) and PubChem13 (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The data were collected from ~80 assays with 241 readouts. For each assay, compound concentration-response curves were fitted to the Hill model, yielding half-maximal activity concentration (AC50) values15, which were used to compare the p53 assay activities at different time points. For modeling purposes, curve rank was used as a numeric measure of chemical activity.15 Compounds with absolute curve rank >0.5 were assigned as active (1) and inactive (0) otherwise.


### Compound library and structure data
  The Tox21 10K compound library is a collection of ~10,000 (8969 unique) compounds, including drugs and environmental chemicals.9 The Tox21 10K library is composed of contributions from three Tox21 partners: U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) (4078), NCATS (3764), and the National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences (NIEHS) Division of Translational Toxicology (DTT) (3115). The Tox21 chemical structures file used in the present study was downloaded from the EPA CompTox Chemicals Dashboard list download page (https://comptox.epa.gov/dashboard/chemical_lists/TOX21SL). Chemical structures were processed using the publicly available ToxPrint chemotypes (v2.0_r711, https://toxprint.org/) generated within the associated ChemoTyper application (https://chemotyper.org/).16 The ToxPrint set includes 729 uniquely defined chemical features coded in XML-based Chemical Subgraphs and Reactions Markup Language (CSRML). ToxPrints include organic chemistry building blocks (e.g., alkyl and aromatic groups, functional groups, elemental, and generic metals), toxicologically important chemical scaffolds (e.g., steroidal backbone, bicyclics, and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons), and additionally include sets of known toxicity structural “alerting” features (e.g., Ashby carcinogenicity alerts and Threshold of Toxicological Concern (TTC) approach). The structure for each chemical was processed by the public ChemoTyper application to produce a binarized ToxPrint fingerprint file in which each structure is represented as a bit vector where the presence or absence of the feature is recorded as 1 or 0, respectively.16 In addition, a total of 29 physicochemical properties (Supplementary Table 1) were calculated using the Chemical Developer Kit (CDK)17 package as implemented in KNIME18.


### Compound grouping by QC
  Each sample in the Tox21 10K library was assigned a QC grade as described previously.10 For purposes of the present analysis, the QC grades were further separated into two bins, PASS and CAUTION (Table 1). Compounds with grade A, B, or I according to Table 1 were considered as QC PASS, representing good quality compounds for present analysis purposes. Compounds with CAUTION grades were poor-quality compounds that either failed QC (i.e., F, Fc, Fns) or displayed suboptimal QC metrics (i.e., low concentration and/or low purity).

  For chemical stability assessment, chemicals with no significant quality drop from T0 to T4 (i.e., PASS at T0 and T4) were considered STABLE whereas chemicals with a significant grade drop from T0 to T4 (i.e., PASS at T0 and CAUTION at T4) were considered unstable or likely to have significantly degraded. The latter were labeled as UNSTABLE. Since QC grades were determined at the sample level (i.e., Tox21 ID level), each grade was further assigned a numerical score (Table 1) for ease of analysis at the compound level (i.e., CASRN level). As redundancies appear in compounds from the three Tox21 partner libraries (e.g., a compound is procured from multiple vendors or different stock solutions are acquired from the same sample), multiple Tox21 IDs can correspond to a single CASRN, resulting in a one-to-many mapping. Grades in the PASS bin were assigned a score of 80 or above and grades in the CAUTION bin were assigned a score of 60 or below. For each CASRN, the scores from all samples with different Tox21 IDs were averaged. CASRNs with scores >60 were considered to PASS, and CAUTION otherwise. For CASRNs that passed at T0, the compounds with a >20 score drop (equivalent to a change from PASS to CAUTION) at T4 were considered UNSTABLE, and STABLE otherwise.


### Identification of significant chemical features and physicochemical properties
  Statistical tests were performed to identify chemical features and physicochemical properties significantly associated with chemical quality and stability. Characteristics of chemicals in the CAUTION category were compared with those in the PASS category, and statistical significance was determined by the Fisher’s exact test for chemical features and the Student’s t-test for physicochemical properties. Similarly, chemical features significantly enriched in the UNSTABLE chemicals were identified by the Fisher’s exact test. A Student’s t-test was performed to identify physicochemical properties significantly associated with chemical stability (i.e., STABLE vs. UNSTABLE).


### Correlation between compound stability monitored by p53 assay and QC results
  Compound activity change in the p53 assay between T0 and T4 was determined by calculating the difference in logAC50 at the two time points. Compounds that did not change in grade from T0 to T4 (e.g., grade A at T0 and T4) were sorted into one group “G1: no decrease in purity”, and compounds that decreased in grade from T0 to T4 (e.g., grade A at T0 and grade C at T4) were grouped together as “G2: decrease of purity”. Compounds with initial grades of A, B, C, D, and I as well as F, Fc, and Fns (combined into one category as X) at T0 were considered for this analysis; all other grades were excluded. Additionally, compounds that showed a grade increase over time or had a large potency difference between the two time points (logAC50 >2.0), both likely due to inappropriate analytical method applied at T0, were excluded from this analysis. As a result, there were 739 compounds with no decrease in purity and 108 compounds that showed a decrease in purity. The time dependent AC50 changes between these two groups of compounds were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with continuity correction (Wilcoxon test), performed using the rstatix package in R (v4.5.0), to investigate the link between chemical quality changes and changes in assay activity. The AC50 of inactive compounds was set to the top test concentration of 100 μM.


### Modeling dataset preparation
  Compounds with a previously assigned QC grade assigned as in Table 1 were considered for modeling. Compounds were excluded from the modeling dataset if they have a QC grade of I (isomers: two or more isomers), M (mixtures: two or more components), U (unknown/inconclusive), W (sample withdrawn), or Z (molecular weight confirmed, no purity information). Chemicals with quality change from low grade at T0 to high grade at T4 due to inappropriate analytical method applied at T0 were excluded from the modeling dataset as well. After this filtering process, 7904 compounds remained for further selection according to one of three criteria to obtain the modeling sets: First, if a compound has a defined chemical structure, it was retained for chemical structure-based modeling. Second, if a compound has physiochemical properties available from KNIME, it was retained for physiochemical property-based modeling. Third, if a compound has activity readout available from all Tox21 assays, it was retained for assay activity-based modeling. As a result, 7723 compounds were included for structure-based modeling, 7547 compounds were included for physiochemical property-based modeling, and 4887 compounds were included for assay activity-based modeling.


### Model development
  Compounds with grade C or above (i.e., A, Ac, B, Bc, C) according to Table 1 were considered as passed QC and good quality compounds. Compounds with lower grades (i.e., Cc, D, F, Fc, Fns) were considered failed or poor-quality compounds. Supplementary Table 2 shows the distribution of compounds by QC grade (based on Tox21 ID) in different modeling sets. Chemicals with high quality grades (i.e., level C and above) at T0 were considered high quality (blue shaded area in Supplementary Table 2) whereas chemicals with low quality grade (i.e., level Cc and lower) at T0 were considered low quality (red shaded area in Supplementary Table 2). For modeling purposes, compounds that passed QC at T0 (good quality compounds; blue shaded area in Supplementary Table 2) were assigned a 0 and compounds that failed QC at T0 (poor-quality compounds; red shaded area in Supplementary Table 2) were assigned a 1. This dataset was used to build models to predict compound quality. For ease of chemical stability assessment, a score was assigned to each chemical based on the QC grade change from T0 to T4 (Table 1). Chemicals with no significant change in quality, i.e., grade drop, from T0 to T4 (QC score change ≤40) were considered stable (blue shaded area in Supplementary Table 3) and assigned a 0 whereas chemicals with a significant grade drop from T0 to T4 (QC score change >40) were considered unstable and assigned a 1 (red shaded area in Supplementary Table 3). This dataset was used to build models to predict chemical stability.

  However, the derived datasets were highly unbalanced since the majority of chemicals in the screening library are of good quality and stable. For example, the QC fail/pass ratio at T0 was 384/7339 (5%) for the chemical structure dataset, 440/7107 (6%) for the physiochemical property dataset, and 249/4638 (5%) for the assay activity dataset (Supplementary Table 1). The unstable/stable ratio was 696/7027 (10%) for the chemical structure dataset, 671/6876 (10%) for the physiochemical property dataset and 526/4361 (12%) for the assay activity dataset (Supplementary Table 3). To obtain balanced modeling sets, a subset of the good quality or stable compounds with a size roughly equal to the set of poor-quality or unstable compounds was randomly chosen from the original dataset and combined with the poor-quality or unstable compounds for modeling. This down-sampling process was repeated 10 times for each dataset. The balanced modeling set was then randomly split into two sets, 70% for training and 30% for model validation. For each training/validation split, the random partitioning process was repeated 10 times for each model to ensure model robustness. The same modeling procedures were repeated for the physiochemical property and assay activity-based models.

  Models were built using the Random Forest (RF) method19 within R (v3.5.0). RF is an ensemble learning method for classification and regression that operates by constructing multiple decision trees. Each tree is trained on roughly two-thirds of the total training data and gives a classification on the remaining one-third of data. The forest chooses the classification having the most votes over all the trees in the forest. For a binary dependent variable, the vote will be either YES or NO. The percentage of YES votes received is the predicted probability. Model performance was evaluated by calculating the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC).20 The ROC curve is a graphical plot that illustrates the predictive ability of a binary classification model across different thresholds. The ROC curve is created by plotting true positive rates against false positive rates at various thresholds. The AUC-ROC provides an aggregate measure of model performance. A larger AUC-ROC value indicates better classifier performance. A perfect predictive model for binary classification would have an AUC-ROC of 1, while an AUC-ROC of 0.5 indicates a random classifier.


## Results and Discussion

### Chemical features and physicochemical properties of compounds
  The QC results at T0 were used to identify poor-quality compounds (i.e., compounds that failed either identity, purity, and/or concentration test). Compounds that passed QC at T0 but failed at T4 (i.e., grouped into the CAUTION bin) were considered to be unstable under the current storage conditions. Compounds that failed QC at T0 could be a result of several uncertainties including the receipt of an incorrect compound or decomposition during shipment, handling and/or storage. To identify the chemical features and physicochemical properties of poor-quality or unstable compounds, we compared these compounds with the compounds that passed QC. We first analyzed the presence of discrete chemical features, e.g., functional groups, chains, rings, and scaffolds, in compounds that fall into different QC groups. Figure 1 shows the frequency of the top 20 chemical features, as determined from ToxPrint, that are most significantly enriched (Fisher’s exact test; p<0.05) in (a) CAUTION and (b) UNSTABLE compounds in comparison to those in PASS and STABLE compounds, respectively. The features enriched in CAUTION compounds are predominantly metal or metalloid containing features. The stability and reactivity of many metal compounds are highly sensitive to variations in the surrounding environment including temperature and pH.21 Other frequent features of CAUTION chemicals include primary amines, isocyanates, halides, aldehydes, and sulfhydrides. These features are more susceptible to hydrolysis and/or oxidation and thus degradation.22–24 On the other hand, the most frequent features in UNSTABLE vs. STABLE compounds are primarily carboxylic esters and alkene or alkane chains, which may be volatile or more susceptible to oxidation or hydrolysis. Note that the analysis of the UNSTABLE vs. STABLE compounds included only compounds that passed QC at T0, so the unstable features that resulted in the compounds to fall into the CAUTION bin at T0 in the first place are not reflected in this analysis. Representative compounds that contain these structural fragments are displayed in Figure 1 with significantly enriched chemical features of CAUTION and UNSTABLE compounds highlighted in red.

  We further examined the physicochemical properties, i.e., the physical and chemical characteristics that define a compound’s behavior and interactions, of CAUTION and UNSTABLE compounds in comparison to PASS and STABLE compounds. Using the Student’s t-test, significance of different physicochemical properties was determined for compounds of different chemical qualities and stability. Compared to the compounds that passed QC (good quality), the CAUTION compounds (poor quality) at T0 tend to have fewer aromatic features with smaller numbers of aromatic atoms and bonds, large π-chains, and heavy atoms (Figure 2A). Considering compound stability, unstable compounds, which passed QC at T0 but went to the CAUTION bin at T4 due to degradation, tend to have fewer aromatic features with smaller numbers of aromatic atoms and bonds, large π-chains, and less polar characters (Figure 2B). These results suggest that larger compounds with more aromatic features tend to be more stable than small, saturated organic molecules, such as small alkanes, which are more likely to be volatile. Aromaticity tends to enhance resonance stability by delocalizing electrostatic energies, leading to overall molecular stabilization.21


### Compound stability monitored by the p53 reporter gene assay
  Previously, a biochemical assay that measures P450 enzyme activity was used to monitor library age-dependent change in activity of a collection of bioactive or drug-like compounds.25 A significant change in compound AC50 (concentration at 50% activity compared to the positive control) values was observed after 4 months. To further investigate the impact of chemical stability on activity of drugs and environmental chemicals within the Tox21 10K library in cell-based assays, we screened the Tox21 compound library against the p53 reporter gene assay at five different library ages ranging from 0 to 6 months (i.e., 0, 0.5, 2, 4, and 6 months). The number of compounds that changed from active to inactive, inactive to active, or had an AC50 change >2.45-fold (cutoff was determined by the average AC50 change in replicate assay runs) between a fresh and an aged library was counted for different library ages and plotted in Figure 3. To further assess the reproducibility of potency estimates obtained from the p53 assay (tox21-p53-bla-p7), the Minimum Significant Ratio (MSR) was calculated. The MSR is a measurement that represents the smallest ratio between the potencies of two compounds that is statistically significant.26 The calculated MSR value for the tox21-p53-bla-p7 assay was 1.41, which meets the Replicate-Experiment MSR criteria of <3 required for an assay to move into production. The library was screened in triplicate at 0.5 months and compared against each other as a baseline comparison measurement. We can see that the number of compounds with a significant change in activity increased over time (from day 1 to 6 months). This number plateaued roughly at 4-month, which is consistent with the change of stability trend of the bioactive collection monitored by the P450 assay.25 This suggests that compound degradation occurs mostly within the first 4 months after thawing and storage at room temperature under testing conditions. At 4 months, ~200 compounds in the Tox21 10K library showed activity changes likely due to degradation, which is about 10% of the compounds that showed activity in the p53 assay at any time point. Therefore, it is recommended that the chemicals be stored at the room temperature within a limited time when used for screening. Adopting this guideline, the current protocol is that Tox21 library plates are retired from screening after exposure to room temperature for 4 months.


### Correlation between compound quality change and activity change
  Furthermore, 1046 chemicals in the Tox21 10K library exhibited activity in the p53 assay at one of two time points, T0 or T4. Compound grades with uncertain identities (see Methods for details) were excluded and concentrate grades were used in separate concentration analysis (Supplementary Figure 1), yielding a final set of 878 compounds for purity analysis. We studied the QC grade change patterns for these 878 compounds to see if there was any correlation between change in compound quality and change in assay activity. For each T0 vs. T4 QC grade pair, we calculated the total number of compounds for each grade pair combination (Table 2). Compounds that did not show any change in stability and remained the same grade at T0 and T4 are located along the diagonal, and compounds that showed signs of degradation are located above the diagonal. There were 746 compounds (71%) that were active in the p53 assay and remained the same grade during a span of 4 months. Subsequently, 125 compounds (14%) showed signs of purity loss over the same time course and the remaining 7 compounds (<1%) showed an “improvement” in grade from T0 to T4 likely due to inadequate analytical methods applied at T0.

  To further quantify the correlation between compound quality change and assay activity change, we sorted the compounds that showed activity in the p53 assay at either T0 or T4 into two groups based on their QC grade change, and calculated the assay activity change for each compound, i.e., absolute difference in logAC50 between T0 and T4. Compounds that did not change in grade from T0 to T4 (e.g., grade A at T0 and T4) were sorted into one bin “G1: no decrease of purity”, and compounds that declined in grade from T0 to T4 (e.g., grade A at T0 and grade F at T4) were grouped together as “G2: decrease of purity” (see Methods for details). Figure 4 shows the distribution of p53 assay activity changes for the two groups of compounds. The G1 group accounts for 87% of compounds (n = 739) that were found to have no decrease in purity over a four-month period and had a mean activity variation score of 0.20 (1.58-fold of AC50 difference). Furthermore, 13% of compounds (G2; n = 108) showed a decrease in purity from T0 to T4 with a mean activity variation score of 0.27 (1.86-fold of AC50 difference). The Wilcoxon rank-sum test indicated that the difference between the two groups of activity variation scores is statistically significant (p-value = 0.02), which confirms that assay activity change is correlated with compound purity change.

  In addition to purity, variations in sample concentration were explored (Supplementary Figure 1). Sample concentration, previously independently verified using a validated quantitative LC-MS/MS method10, indicates the amount of a chemical administered provided that its identity is confirmed. Compounds were categorized into two groups based on whether their concentration decreased over time. Compounds that exhibited no change in grade and concentration over time (e.g., grade A at T0 and T4) were grouped together, whereas compounds that showed a decline in concentration (e.g., grade A at T0 and grade Ac at T4) were grouped separately. Over a four-month period, the majority of compounds (n = 705) showed no changes in concentration, whereas a small subset (n = 10) exhibited a decrease in concentration. Compounds with no concentration changes demonstrated a lower mean p53 activity variation (mean logAC50 difference = 0.21; 1.62-fold of AC50 difference) compared to those with a decrease in concentration, which exhibited a substantially higher mean p53 activity variation (mean logAC50 difference = 0.40; 2.51-fold of AC50 difference). A Wilcoxon rank-sum test (p-value = 7.6 × 10−3) revealed that the difference in activity variations between the two groups is statistically significant and suggests that time-dependent changes in compound concentration influence compound activity as well.


### Impact of chemical quality on assay activity
  To assess the overall impact of compound quality on assay activity, we compared the assay hit rates of compounds that passed and failed QC at T0 across all Tox21 assays (Figure 5). The hit rate of a compound is calculated as the percentage of Tox21 assays in which the compound showed activity. As shown in Figure 5, the hit rate distribution of the good quality compounds (i.e., compounds that passed QC at T0 with grade “A”, “B”, or ”I”) is right-shifted compared to the distribution of the CAUTION compounds. That is, CAUTION compounds tend to have lower hit rates than PASS compounds. For example, ~80% of the CAUTION compounds had a hit rate less than 10%, which surpasses the percentage of the good quality compounds (~69%) detected at the same hit rate. At higher hit rates (20% and above), the good quality compounds consistently outnumbered the CAUTION compounds by showing a higher percentage. This indicates that high quality compounds showed more activities in the assay panel than the poor-quality compounds in general. Compounds often fail QC due to degradation and changes in chemical structure. Additionally, the freeze-thaw process during compound preparation may also contribute to changes observed in chemical quality and stability. These results show that degraded compounds tend to lose their biological activity and result in loss of signal or false negatives observed in assays.


### Prediction of chemical quality and stability using assay activity vs. chemical structure
  To determine the extent to which chemical quality and stability are reflected in their assay activity profile, we developed RF models using assay activity to predict compounds in the Tox21 10K library that are poor quality or unstable. For comparison, similar models were also built using chemical structure data as model descriptors. Model performance was measured by calculating the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC). A good model performance would indicate that poor-quality compounds in the Tox21 library are distinguishable by chemical structure or assay activity. Figure 6A shows examples of ROC curves from the chemical quality prediction models based on structure or assay activity data. For the structure-based models, we used two types of structure descriptors: fragment-based fingerprints (ToxPrint) and physiochemical properties. Both types of models performed well with an average AUC-ROC score of 0.75 ± 0.03 for the fingerprint-based model, 0.75 ± 0.03 for the property-based model, and 0.66 ± 0.05 for the assay activity-based model. These results indicate that the chemical structure features of poor-quality compounds in the Tox21 library are sufficiently different from the good quality compounds, and these features can be used to identify poor-quality compounds. The same conclusion holds true for the physiochemical property profiles. In contrast, the assay-based model exhibited a lower AUC-ROC than the structure-based model. This is not unexpected, as poor-quality compounds are likely to produce false negative or positive results in assays, making assay-based models less predictive than their structure-based counterparts. This result also suggests that while the assay activity profile used for modeling alone may not be sufficient to distinguish poor-quality from good-quality compounds, the assay activities of the poor-quality compounds are sufficiently distinct enough to produce better than random prediction models of chemical quality.

  Similarly, Figure 6B shows example ROC curves from the RF models built to predict chemical stability based on the T0 vs. T4 QC results. Once again, both types of structure-based models performed well with an average AUC-ROC of 0.73 ± 0.03 for the fingerprint-based model and 0.73 ± 0.03 for the property-based model, while the assay activity-based model showed lower predictive performance with an average AUC-ROC of 0.62 ± 0.03. These results reiterate that chemical structure is a better predictor of chemical stability than assay data, while assay data still showed better than random predictive power of chemical stability. Collectively, these modeling results demonstrate that both chemical quality and stability have moderate impact on biological assay results, and that chemical structure is a major determinant of chemical quality and stability. This result is likely attributed to the high quality of more than 90% of compounds in the Tox21 10K library.

  While the findings of this study provide valuable insights, there are a few limitations that should be considered. When concentration-response curves are based on poor quality data or are used to profile compounds with diverse efficacy, relying solely on traditional activity metrics (i.e., AC50) can lead to inaccurate or misleading conclusions on compound potency and activity.27 In turn, this approach can potentially introduce outliers that may affect data analysis and result interpretation. Additionally, library age-dependent compound activity was assessed solely using the p53 assay across different time points, which limits the findings only applicable to p53 targeting compounds. Screening the Tox21 10K compound library at multiple time points against alternative targets and pathways can complement the p53 assay results and provide more statistical power in assessing the connection between compound quality change and activity change. A comprehensive evaluation of the quality and stability of chemicals in the Tox21 10K library underscores both the inherent limitations and valuable opportunities for applying this dataset in future toxicological research. The significantly enriched structural features and physicochemical properties identified in this study, as well as the structure-based chemical quality prediction models can support informed chemical selection decisions, including the construction of compound libraries.


## Conclusions
  This study is the first attempt to systematically evaluate the impact of compound quality and stability on biological activity outcomes based on results from a large and diverse library of drugs and environmental chemicals. We explored the chemical and biological space of compounds with varying quality and stability. In parallel to the analytical QC efforts, we used a cell-based assay to monitor the stability of the Tox21 10K compound library. Compounds were found to show a time-dependent change in assay activity, which correlated with their change in QC grade. Comparative analyses of the QC results and Tox21 assay activity data show that chemical quality has a significant impact on assay activity, and poor-quality/degraded chemicals tend to be less active than the chemicals that passed QC. Additionally, we developed and assessed the utility of machine learning models to analyze the impact of chemical quality and stability on bioassay activity. Compound quality and stability were found to impact bioassay outcomes, and chemical structure was shown a good predictor of chemical quality. We identified chemical features characteristic of poor-quality/unstable chemicals, which can be used to guide future chemical selection and handling.
