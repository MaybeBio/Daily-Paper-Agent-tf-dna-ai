# Plasma proteomics identifies proteins and pathways associated with incident migraine in 50,668 adults


## Background
  Migraine is a prevalent neurological disorder, but the systemic molecular alterations that precede its onset and their biological relevance remain incompletely understood.


## Methods
  We conducted a prospective plasma proteomics analysis among 50,668 migraine-free participants from the UK Biobank. Baseline levels of 2,923 plasma proteins were quantified using the Olink Explore platform. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were applied to assess associations between protein levels and incident migraine during follow-up, with adjustment for demographic, lifestyle, and clinical covariates. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was used to account for multiple testing. Trajectory analyses were performed to characterize temporal protein patterns prior to diagnosis. Functional enrichment, protein–protein interaction, and transcription factor analyses were conducted to explore biological pathways. Single-cell transcriptomic data were integrated to assess cell-type specificity. Genetic analyses, including polygenic risk score associations, Mendelian randomization, and Bayesian colocalization, were used to evaluate potential causal relationships. Associations between migraine-related proteins and global brain structural measures derived from magnetic resonance imaging were examined. Machine-learning models were developed to evaluate the predictive performance of plasma proteomic profiles for future migraine risk.


## Results
  After FDR correction, 126 proteins were significantly associated with incident migraine, of which 124 were retained for downstream analyses. Distinct protein trajectories were observed several years before clinical diagnosis. Enrichment analyses consistently implicated immune regulation and neuro-immune signaling pathways. Genetic analyses supported potential causal roles for a subset of proteins, and several migraine-associated proteins were linked to global brain structural measures. Proteomics-based models achieved modest but reproducible discrimination of future migraine risk.


## Conclusion
  These findings demonstrate that migraine is preceded by long-term systemic proteomic alterations, highlight immune-related molecular pathways and genetically supported proteins potentially involved in migraine pathogenesis, and provide a foundation for future mechanistic studies and early risk stratification strategies.

## Introduction
  Migraine is a highly prevalent and disabling neurological disorder affecting more than one billion individuals worldwide and represents a leading cause of years lived with disability across all age groups [1, 2]. Characterized by recurrent headache attacks accompanied by sensory hypersensitivity and autonomic symptoms, migraine imposes substantial personal, societal, and economic burdens [3–5]. Despite extensive research, the biological mechanisms underlying migraine initiation and progression remain incompletely understood. Current hypotheses emphasize the interplay between neurovascular dysfunction, cortical excitability, trigeminovascular activation, and immune–inflammatory signaling [6–13]. However, most mechanistic insights are derived from experimental models or cross-sectional clinical observations, which provide limited information on the systemic molecular changes that precede disease onset. Identifying early circulating biomarkers and biological pathways associated with future migraine risk is therefore critical for advancing etiological understanding, improving risk stratification, and enabling preventive intervention strategies [14, 15].

  Plasma proteomics offers a powerful approach to interrogate systemic biological processes at scale, reflecting integrated signals from genetic regulation, immune activation, metabolic status, and environmental exposures [16–18]. Recent advances in high-throughput affinity-based platforms, such as the Olink Explore proteomic assay, have enabled the simultaneous quantification of thousands of circulating proteins with high sensitivity and specificity [19–21]. While several studies have reported altered inflammatory or vascular-related proteins in patients with established migraine, these investigations have largely been cross-sectional, restricted to small clinical cohorts, or focused on candidate markers [22–25]. Consequently, it remains unclear whether observed proteomic alterations represent causal drivers, compensatory responses, or secondary consequences of recurrent migraine attacks. Large-scale prospective studies that systematically examine plasma proteomic profiles before migraine onset are notably lacking, leaving a critical gap in our understanding of the temporal and potentially causal molecular architecture of migraine.

  The UK Biobank (UKB) provides a unique opportunity to address this gap by integrating deeply phenotyped longitudinal health data with large-scale plasma proteomics, neuroimaging, and genetic information [26]. Leveraging a prospective cohort of over 50,000 participants free of migraine at baseline, combined with long-term follow-up and comprehensive covariate assessment, enables robust evaluation of protein–migraine associations while minimizing reverse causation. Moreover, the availability of brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)–derived phenotypes allows exploration of whether migraine-associated proteins are linked to global brain structural features, offering insight into potential neurobiological correlates [27, 28]. Integration with genetic analyses, including polygenic risk scores (PRSs), Mendelian randomization (MR), and Bayesian colocalization, further facilitates the distinction between correlational and potentially causal protein signals. In addition, single-cell transcriptomic data provide cellular context for proteomic findings, helping to identify immune and neural cell populations that may mediate observed associations.

  To identify proteins associated with the risk of incident migraine in the UKB, we conducted a comprehensive plasma proteome–wide analysis (Fig. 1). We combined Cox proportional hazards models, cross-sectional analyses, and pre-diagnostic trajectory profiling to characterize both static and dynamic proteomic signatures preceding migraine onset. Functional enrichment, protein–protein interaction (PPI), and transcription factor analyses were used to elucidate underlying biological pathways, while single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data were leveraged to define cell type–specific expression patterns. We further examined associations between migraine-related proteins and brain structural MRI phenotypes, assessed shared genetic architecture using PRS and causal inference methods, and evaluated the predictive utility of proteomic profiles using machine learning models. ## Materials and methods

### Ethics
  The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval for UKB was obtained from the North West Multi-centre Research Ethics Committee. All participants provided written informed consent, and analyses were performed under approved UKB applications. GWAS and scRNA-seq data were publicly available, de-identified, and derived from studies with appropriate local ethics approval and consent.


### Study population and design
  This study was conducted within the UKB (RRID:SCR_012815), a large prospective population-based cohort that enrolled approximately 500,000 participants aged 40–69 years between 2006 and 2010 [26]. Baseline data collection included questionnaires, physical measurements, and biological samples. Plasma proteomic measurements were obtained at baseline for a subset of participants. Individuals with a diagnosis of migraine prior to baseline assessment were excluded to ensure an incident disease design. Participants were followed longitudinally from baseline until first diagnosis of migraine, death, loss to follow-up, or the end of follow-up, whichever occurred first.


### Plasma proteomic profiling and preprocessing
  Baseline plasma proteomic profiling was performed using the Olink ExploreTM platform (Olink Proteomics, Uppsala, Sweden), which applies proximity extension assay (PEA) technology combined with next-generation sequencing to enable high-throughput and highly specific protein quantification. Plasma samples were collected at baseline according to standardized UKB protocols, processed centrally, and stored at − 80 °C prior to analysis [29, 30]. The Explore platform measured 2,923 plasma proteins spanning a wide range of biological pathways, including immune regulation, inflammation, and neurological processes. Protein abundance was quantified using normalized protein expression (NPX) values on a log2 scale, as provided by Olink. Standard quality control procedures implemented by Olink were applied, including the removal of assays failing internal controls for incubation, extension, amplification, or detection, as well as samples with low overall quality metrics. Proteins with a missing value proportion exceeding 20% (N = 12) were excluded, resulting in 2,911 proteins retained for downstream analyses. For the remaining proteins, sporadic missing values were imputed using the cohort-wide mean value, consistent with prior large-scale Olink proteomic studies in population cohorts [31]. Following imputation, the overall proportion of missing data was minimal. To minimize technical and batch-related variation, NPX values were further adjusted for predefined technical covariates, including assay batch, sample processing plate, measurement center, season of blood draw, fasting status, and time from blood collection to processing [29, 30]. Protein levels were subsequently standardized to Z-scores to facilitate comparability across proteins. All preprocessing steps were performed prior to outcome ascertainment and statistical modeling to avoid information leakage. Primary analyses were conducted using the imputed data after preprocessing.


### Assessment of migraine status
  Migraine status was ascertained using linked electronic health records in the UKB, primarily based on hospital inpatient data, where diagnoses are recorded with high clinical specificity. Migraine cases were identified using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) code G43. In the UKB inpatient data, ICD-10 diagnosis codes are available under field ID 41270, with the corresponding diagnosis dates captured in field ID 41280 across inpatient episodes. The event date was defined as the date of the first recorded hospital diagnosis of migraine, determined as the earliest available diagnosis date across all eligible inpatient records. Prevalent migraine was defined as any ICD-10 G43 diagnosis recorded prior to or at the baseline assessment date. Incident hospital-diagnosed migraine was defined as a first-ever ICD-10 G43 diagnosis occurring after baseline among participants without evidence of a prior migraine history at or before baseline. This definition aims to capture clinically recognized migraine recorded in hospital inpatient settings and therefore represents a conservative, high-specificity phenotype.


### Measures of covariates
  Covariates were selected a priori based on established associations with migraine risk and their potential to confound plasma proteomic measurements. Demographic variables included age at baseline (field 21022), sex (field 31), and ethnicity (field 21000), categorized according to standard UKB classifications. Socioeconomic status was assessed using the Townsend deprivation index (TDI, field 22189) and treated as a continuous variable, with higher values indicating greater material deprivation. Educational attainment was derived from baseline questionnaire data (fields 845, 6138, and 6142) and harmonized into ordered categories or years of education as appropriate. Lifestyle-related covariates included body mass index (BMI, field 21001), smoking status (field 20116), and alcohol consumption status (field 20117), all assessed at baseline. Additional phenotype-specific covariates were incorporated given their known associations with systemic inflammation, metabolic health, and migraine susceptibility. These included daily screen time spent watching television (field 1070) and using a computer (field 1080), average sleep duration (field 1160), diabetes status (field 2443), cardiovascular disease (CVD) status (field 6150), and use of medications for cholesterol, blood pressure, or diabetes (field 6177) to better capture underlying diabetes and cardiovascular disease status.

  Categorical covariates were modeled using indicator variables where appropriate. All covariates were defined at baseline and prior to migraine onset to ensure temporal precedence. Missing values in covariates were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) (v3.18.0) in R (v4.2.1), with variable-specific imputation models applied according to data type. All covariates were incorporated into multivariable models as fixed effects unless otherwise specified and were handled consistently across primary, stratified, and sensitivity analyses.


### Association analyses of plasma proteins with incident and prevalent migraine
  Time-to-event analyses were conducted to evaluate the associations between baseline plasma protein levels and the risk of incident migraine. Participants were followed from the date of baseline assessment until the first recorded diagnosis of migraine, death, loss to follow-up, or the end of follow-up, whichever occurred first. Individuals with a history of migraine at baseline were excluded from all survival analyses. For each protein, Cox proportional hazards regression models were fitted using follow-up time since baseline as the time scale. Protein levels were modeled as continuous variables standardized to Z-scores, and hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated per 1-standard deviation increase in protein abundance. HRs therefore represent the relative change in hazard associated with a 1-SD difference in circulating protein level. Given the multifactorial nature of migraine, we interpret these as modest effect sizes that may inform biological pathways rather than deterministic clinical predictors. Multivariable models were adjusted for the covariates described above.

  To assess robustness to preprocessing choices, we compared the primary analysis with (i) complete-case analysis (CCA) and (ii) models adjusted for the top 10 genetic principal components (PCs). Consistency was evaluated using direction concordance and Pearson correlations of effect estimates, with additional assessment of whether proteins identified as significant in the primary analysis showed concordant effect directions and retained nominal significance in sensitivity analyses. Additionally, to assess the robustness of high-dimensional proteomic associations, we performed internal validation using both split-sample and resampling approaches. First, we conducted internal split-sample validation by randomly dividing the cohort into discovery (70%) and validation (30%) subsets and applying the same analysis pipeline used in the primary analysis to each subset. Effect estimates were compared across subsets, with directional concordance defined as consistent beta signs. Among proteins identified as significant in the discovery subset, we further evaluated directional consistency and nominal significance in the validation subset. Second, bootstrap resampling was conducted with 200 iterations, and the selection frequency of proteins identified as significant in the primary analysis was calculated.

  To explore potential effect modification and evaluate the consistency of associations, exploratory stratified Cox regression analyses were further performed within subgroups defined by sex (male and female) and age at baseline (<55 years and ≥55 years). Within each stratum, the same modeling strategy and covariate adjustments were applied, with the stratifying variable omitted from the corresponding model. Consistency of protein–migraine associations across subgroups was evaluated by comparing the direction and magnitude of effect estimates and their confidence intervals, and subgroup findings were interpreted with caution given the reduced event counts within strata. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals and graphical diagnostics, and no major violations were observed. In addition, linear regression analyses were performed to examine the cross-sectional associations between baseline plasma protein levels and prevalent migraine. Protein levels were standardized to Z-scores prior to analysis. Multivariable models were adjusted for the same set of covariates as described above. Effect estimates were reported as regression coefficients (β) with corresponding 95% CIs, representing the difference in standardized protein levels associated with prevalent migraine. To account for multiple testing across the large number of proteins analyzed, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, with statistically significant associations defined as those with an FDR-adjusted p-value < 0.05. All survival and linear regression analyses were performed using R (v4.2.1).


### Trajectory modeling and clustering prior to migraine diagnosis
  To characterize inferred pre-diagnostic patterns of plasma proteins relative to time-to-diagnosis, we conducted a matched case–control analysis anchored to the diagnosis time of incident hospital-diagnosed migraine. Participants were first re-defined as incident migraine cases and potential controls. For each incident migraine case, eligible controls were restricted to participants who were free of migraine at the case’s diagnosis date, ensuring that all controls were at risk at the corresponding event time. Using the MatchIt package (v4.7.2) in R (v4.2.1), cases were matched to controls at a 1:10 ratio by nearest-neighbor matching with Mahalanobis distance, with exact matching on sex [32]. Matching variables included covariates mentioned above. Matched datasets were extracted using match.data, and covariate balance was evaluated using MatchIt diagnostics. A pre-diagnostic time axis was then constructed for each matched set. For each matched subclass, the event time of the case was assigned to all individuals within the same matched set, and multiplied by − 1 to represent “years before diagnosis” (time_scale), thereby aligning all matched controls to the case’s diagnosis time. Controls were allowed to subsequently develop migraine during follow-up, consistent with an incidence-density sampling framework [33].

  To reduce confounding effects on protein levels, each plasma protein was residualized in the matched dataset by fitting a linear regression model with the above covariates and extracting model residuals. Residualized protein values were retained at the individual level and subsequently used for trajectory modeling. Within each matched subclass, case-specific standardized deviations were computed for each protein by comparing the case residual to the distribution of control residuals in the same subclass. Subclasses in which the control standard deviation was zero were handled using a protective rule and assigned missing values for that protein.

  For each protein, LOESS smoothing was applied to model the relationship between subclass-level Z-scores and years before diagnosis [33–35]. LOESS-predicted values were generated on a uniform grid to obtain comparable temporal profiles across proteins. LOESS was used as a descriptive smoothing approach to summarize patterns and may accentuate apparent temporal structure. Because protein levels were measured only once at baseline, these profiles should be interpreted as inferred cross-sectional gradients by time-to-diagnosis rather than within-individual longitudinal trajectories. Trajectory analyses and visualizations focused on proteins identified as significant in the primary association analyses. Hierarchical clustering was performed on the LOESS-predicted trajectory matrix using Euclidean distance and Ward’s minimum variance method, and proteins were classified into three trajectory clusters. Cluster patterns were visualized using a heatmap of LOESS-predicted Z-scores across the pre-diagnostic period and by plotting protein-level trajectories with cluster-wise mean trends.


### Functional enrichment and network analyses
  To explore the biological functions and pathways associated with migraine-related plasma proteins, functional enrichment and network analyses were performed for proteins identified in the association analyses. Gene symbols corresponding to significant proteins were mapped using official gene annotations and used as input for downstream analyses. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analyses were conducted to evaluate overrepresented biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF), while pathway enrichment analyses were performed using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database. These analyses were implemented using the Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) [36, 37]. Enrichment results were evaluated using nominal p-values with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. PPI networks were constructed using the STRING database to investigate potential interactions among migraine-associated proteins [38]. The resulting PPI networks were visualized using Cytoscape software (v3.10.2) [39]. To identify key proteins within the interaction network, the cytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape was applied to rank hub genes based on network topology measures [40]. In addition, upstream transcriptional regulators of migraine-associated proteins were explored using the TRRUST database accessed via Metascape, which curates 8,444 transcription factor–target regulatory relationships [41, 42]. This analysis aimed to identify potential transcription factors that may regulate the expression of migraine-associated proteins and provide further insight into their regulatory mechanisms.


### Single-cell expression and cell-type contextualization
  To investigate the cell-type–specific expression patterns of migraine-associated proteins, single-cell transcriptomic data were used to provide cellular context for the identified plasma proteomic signatures. The scRNA-seq data were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database under accession number GSE269117. This dataset comprises over 70,000 single-cell transcriptomes derived from peripheral blood samples of 24 Spanish participants without diagnosed allergies or autoimmune diseases, including eight patients with Ménière’s disease (MD), five with vestibular migraine (VM), five with migraine, and six healthy controls (HC). Detailed cohort information and experimental procedures have been described previously [43]. Raw gene count matrices were processed using the Seurat package (v5.1.0) in R (v4.2.1) [44]. Quality control procedures were applied to exclude low-quality or dying cells, including cells with mitochondrial gene content exceeding 25%, fewer than 600 detected genes, or more than 13,500 detected genes. Genes expressed in fewer than three cells were removed, and cells with total unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts outside the range of 500– 18,000 were excluded. Normalization and variance stabilization were performed using the SCTransform function, with regression of mitochondrial gene content and sequencing depth to mitigate technical effects. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used for dimensionality reduction, and batch effects across individuals and disease groups were corrected using the Harmony algorithm. Graph-based clustering was performed using the FindClusters function with a resolution parameter of 0.40, and low-dimensional visualization was achieved using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). Cell type annotation was conducted based on the expression of established canonical marker genes and cluster-specific transcriptional signatures, as reported in the original publication and further validated using curated immune cell marker databases. Annotated cell populations were subsequently used for downstream cell-type–specific expression analyses of migraine-associated proteins. This single-cell–based contextualization enabled the linkage of circulating migraine-associated proteins to their potential cellular origins and immune cell subsets, thereby providing biological insight into the cellular processes underlying migraine susceptibility.


### Brain MRI acquisition and protein–brain structural association analyses
  Our study utilized structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data from the UKB imaging study. Brain MRI data were acquired using a standardized protocol on a Siemens Skyra 3.0-T scanner equipped with a 32-channel head coil. Detailed procedures for image acquisition, quality control, and image processing have been described previously and are publicly available through the UKB documentation and related publications [27, 28]. In brief, structural T1-weighted images and T2-weighted FLAIR images were processed using established automated pipelines to generate imaging-derived phenotypes (IDPs), which were used in the present analyses without additional image reprocessing [45–47]. In this study, we focused on four global brain structural phenotypes provided by the UKB: white matter hyperintensity (WMH) volume (field 25781), cortical gray matter volume (CGV, field 25001), total gray matter volume (TGV, field 25005), and subcortical gray matter volume (SGV, field 26517). WMH volume represents the total burden of white matter hyperintensities segmented from T2-weighted FLAIR images and is considered a marker of cerebral small vessel disease. Owing to its highly right-skewed distribution and the presence of zero values, WMH volume was log-transformed after adding one [log(WMH + 1)] prior to analysis. Gray matter volume measures (CGV, TGV, and SGV), derived from T1-weighted structural imaging, were analyzed on their original scales. All imaging analyses were adjusted for total intracranial volume (TIV, field 26521) to account for inter-individual differences in head size, as well as for assessment centre (field 54) to control for site-related variability. Brain MRI data were obtained during dedicated imaging visits occurring after baseline assessment and at varying times relative to migraine onset. As such, the temporal ordering between brain structural measures and migraine onset was not uniform across participants. Therefore, protein–MRI findings are interpreted as associations and do not imply directionality.

  Associations between circulating protein levels and brain structural phenotypes were examined using multivariable linear regression models, with each protein analyzed separately as the independent variable and each brain imaging phenotype treated as the dependent variable. All linear regression models were adjusted for covariates mentioned above, with additional adjustment for assessment centre and total intracranial volume. To facilitate comparison of effect sizes across proteins and across different brain structural outcomes, protein levels were standardized to Z-scores prior to analysis. Two types of regression coefficients were reported. Protein-standardized coefficients were obtained by fitting linear regression models in which imaging phenotypes were analyzed on their original scales with standardized protein levels as predictors, representing the absolute change in brain structural measures associated with a one standard deviation increase in protein concentration. In addition, fully standardized coefficients were derived by standardizing both protein levels and brain imaging phenotypes prior to regression, such that regression coefficients represent the standard deviation change in brain structural measures per standard deviation increase in protein levels, enabling direct comparison of association magnitudes across different imaging phenotypes. Accordingly, a fully standardized coefficient (β) corresponds to an approximately β-SD difference in the brain imaging phenotype per 1-SD higher protein level. Such effect sizes are typically modest for multifactorial brain structural traits and should be interpreted as associational findings that do not imply directionality or clinical actionability. To account for multiple testing, p-values were corrected using both the Bonferroni method and the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR procedure. For analyses restricted to proteins identified as significant in Cox proportional hazards models, multiple testing correction was performed separately within each brain imaging phenotype after restricting the analysis to this subset of proteins.


### Polygenic risk score analyses
  PRSs for migraine were constructed using summary statistics from the largest genome-wide association study meta-analysis of migraine to date, conducted by Hautakangas et al., which included 48,975 migraine cases and 540,381 controls of European ancestry [48]. This meta-analysis integrated data from multiple cohorts, including IHGC2016, UKB, GeneRISK, and HUNT, while data from 23andMe were excluded due to data-sharing restrictions. The UKB component comprised 10,881 cases and 330,170 controls, accounting for approximately 57.9% of the total sample (and ~22.2% of cases). Accordingly, partial sample overlap with the proteomic data is possible and may bias estimated associations. Therefore, genetic findings should be interpreted cautiously. PRSs were generated using PRSice software [49]. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping was applied with an r2 threshold of 0.1 and a clumping window of 250 kb. Polygenic scores were calculated across several representative p-value thresholds, including p < 5 × 10−8 (pT_5e-8), p < 5 × 10−6 (pT_5e-6), p < 1 × 10−5 (pT_1e-5), p < 1 × 10−4 (pT_1e-4), and p < 1 × 10−3 (pT_1e-3) [50]. Associations between migraine PRSs and selected plasma protein levels were examined using multivariable linear regression models. Protein levels were modeled as continuous variables, and PRSs were treated as continuous predictors. All models were adjusted for the same set of covariates mentioned above, with the exception of ethnicity, which was not included given the restriction to participants of European ancestry.


### Mendelian randomization and colocalization analyses
  Potential causal relationships between plasma protein levels and migraine risk were investigated using summary-level genetic data through two-sample MR analyses. This approach was applied to assess whether genetically proxied plasma protein levels exert a causal effect on the risk of migraine. Summary statistics for migraine were obtained from a large-scale genome-wide association study meta-analysis by Hautakangas et al., while genetic associations with plasma proteins were derived from a proteome-wide genome-wide association study conducted in European participants from the UKB [29, 48]. The protein GWAS provided cis- and trans-pQTL information and was restricted to individuals of European ancestry to minimize population stratification. Although partial sample overlap may exist because both the migraine GWAS and the proteomic GWAS included UKB participants, the use of strong pQTL instruments and multiple robust MR methods reduces the potential impact of such overlap on causal inference. Genetic instruments for each protein with cis-pQTL were selected as single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with protein levels at genome-wide significance (p < 5 × 10−8). Independent instruments were identified through linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping using an r 2 threshold of 0.001 and a clumping window of 10,000 kb, based on the European reference panel from the 1000 Genomes Project. To reduce weak instrument bias, SNPs with F-statistics less than 10 were excluded [51]. Steiger filtering was applied to ensure correct causal direction, and palindromic SNPs with intermediate allele frequencies were removed. Two-sample MR analyses were performed using the TwoSampleMR package (v0.6.12) in R (v4.2.1) [52]. The inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method was used as the primary estimator of causal effects. Sensitivity analyses, including MR-Egger regression, weighted median, and mode-based methods, were performed to assess the robustness of causal estimates and potential horizontal pleiotropy [53]. Heterogeneity across instrumental variables was evaluated using Cochran’s Q statistic, and leave-one-out analyses were performed to assess the influence of individual SNPs on the overall causal estimates.

  To further determine whether shared genetic variants underlie both protein level and migraine risk, we conducted colocalization analysis using the coloc package (v5.2.3), which applies a Bayesian framework to evaluate the probability of five competing hypotheses about trait associations [54]. Harmonized summary statistics from the MR analysis, combining cis-pQTL and GWAS data, were used as input. Following standard practice, default prior probabilities were set at P1 = 1 × 10−4, P2 = 1 × 10−4, and P12 = 1 × 10−5. Only loci where at least one SNP showed association with both traits were included.


### Cross-trait overlap and mediation analyses
  Sleep-related traits and affective vulnerability were considered as plausible explanatory factors for migraine susceptibility. Sleep duration was assessed using self-reported average hours of sleep per day (field 1160), while insomnia symptoms were derived from baseline questionnaire responses regarding sleep difficulties (field 1200). Affective vulnerability was characterized using self-reported anxiety- and depression-related measures obtained from the baseline mental health questionnaires (anxiety: fields 130894 and 130896; depression: fields 130904 and 130906). Multivariable linear regression models were used to examine the associations between plasma protein levels and these sleep-related and affective traits. Each trait was separately modeled as the independent variable, with standardized plasma protein levels treated as the dependent variable. All models were adjusted for the same set of covariates as described in the primary analyses.

  Furthermore, structural equation modeling (SEM) was conducted to evaluate the potential intermediary role of plasma protein levels in the associations between stress-related traits (sleep and affective measures) and the propensity for migraine onset. This framework allowed the decomposition of total effects into indirect effects mediated through protein levels and direct effects independent of the mediator. Statistical significance was assessed using two-tailed tests, with p-values < 0.05 considered statistically significant after correction for multiple testing. All regression and SEM analyses were performed using R (v4.2.1) and the survival package (v3.7–0).


### Machine learning–based risk prediction
  Machine learning–based models were developed to evaluate the predictive utility of plasma proteomic profiles for migraine risk. All analyses were conducted using Python 3 with implementations based on Scikit-learn–compatible utilities and the LightGBM framework [55, 56]. The input dataset consisted of baseline plasma protein levels after residualization, with incident migraine during follow-up treated as a binary outcome. Participant identifiers were excluded from modeling.

  A gradient boosting decision tree classifier (LightGBM) was implemented using a stratified five-fold cross-validation scheme to preserve the case–control ratio across folds. Models were trained using predefined hyperparameters, including the number of estimators, tree depth, number of leaves, learning rate, subsampling rate, and feature subsampling rate. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the curve (AUC). Feature importance was quantified within each cross-validation fold using both split-based and gain-based importance metrics derived from the trained LightGBM models. Feature importance scores were normalized within each fold and subsequently aggregated across folds to obtain cross-validated importance estimates. Proteins were subsequently ranked according to their aggregated importance scores, and the top 50 features were retained for subsequent stepwise modeling. To identify an appropriate number of predictive features, a stepwise feature inclusion strategy was applied. Proteins were sequentially added to the model according to their ranked importance, and five-fold cross-validated AUCs were calculated at each step. The cumulative predictive performance was summarized by the mean and standard deviation of AUC values across folds. An apparent plateau in the cumulative AUC curve was used to identify a parsimonious set of core predictive proteins.

  Final prediction models were constructed using the selected protein set and evaluated using stratified five-fold cross-validation. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated for each fold, and a mean ROC curve was obtained by interpolating true positive rates across a common false positive rate grid. Variability across folds was illustrated using shaded bands representing performance dispersion. To further examine the robustness and potential heterogeneity of predictive performance, the top 50 ranked proteins were additionally used to construct stratified migraine prediction models by age group (<55 years and ≥55 years) and by sex (male and female). Separate LightGBM classifiers were trained and evaluated within each subgroup using the same cross-validation framework and performance metrics.


### Druggability and drug repurposing analyses
  To explore the therapeutic relevance and drug repositioning potential of migraine-associated plasma proteins, druggability analyses were performed using the Genome for REPositioning (GREP) framework [57]. GREP integrates genetic and pharmacological information to evaluate whether disease-associated genes are enriched for known or potentially actionable drug targets. Genes encoding plasma proteins identified in the association analyses were used as input for druggability assessment. These genes were tested for enrichment within ICD-10 categories and for overlap with known drug targets. Enrichment analyses focused on drugs classified as approved or under investigation and were conducted using curated drug–target information from DrugBank and the Therapeutic Target Database (TTD). Statistical significance of enrichment was evaluated using GREP’s built-in framework, with a two-sided p-value < 0.05 considered indicative of significant enrichment. This analysis aimed to identify candidate proteins and pathways that may be amenable to pharmacological modulation and to highlight opportunities for potential drug repurposing in migraine.


## Results

### Characteristics of the study population
  A total of 50,668 participants free of migraine at baseline were included in the analysis, among whom 694 developed incident migraine during follow-up and 49,974 remained migraine-free (Supplementary Table S1). In addition, of the 2,923 proteomic biomarkers tested, 2,911 proteins were retained for our analysis after quality control (Supplementary Table S2).


### Association between protein levels and incident migraine

### Druggability assessment of migraine-associated proteins
  Drug target enrichment analysis demonstrated that migraine-associated proteins were significantly enriched for genes with existing or investigational pharmacological targeting (Supplementary Table S34). Across enriched disease categories, a restricted set of genes repeatedly emerged as core contributors to the enrichment signals. These prominently included CD38, IL2RA, IL4R, TNF, TGFBR2, TNFSF10, FGFR4, TXN, and GSR, which were consistently represented across multiple enrichment groups. Among these, CD38 appeared most frequently, followed by IL2RA, IL4R, and TNF, each contributing to enrichment across a wide range of disease categories, predominantly malignant neoplasms. Additional recurrent targets included TGFBR2, TNFSF10, FGFR4, and TXN, which were also shared across multiple malignant neoplasm groups. Beyond malignant neoplasm categories, overlap of drug-target genes was also observed for IL4R and TNF in non-neoplastic disease categories, including respiratory-related diseases. These overlaps likely reflect shared inflammatory and immune-regulatory themes and should be interpreted cautiously. Further experimental and clinical studies are required to evaluate target relevance, directionality, and feasibility for migraine.


## Discussion
  Migraine is a highly prevalent and disabling neurological disorder with substantial individual and societal burden, yet its biological mechanisms remain incompletely understood, particularly during the preclinical phase preceding disease onset [3, 58]. In this large prospective proteome-wide investigation, we systematically characterized circulating protein signatures associated with the future development of migraine in a population-based cohort free of migraine at baseline. By leveraging longitudinal follow-up, high-throughput plasma proteomics, genetic data, neuroimaging phenotypes, and single-cell transcriptomics, our study provides a comprehensive and temporally informed view of the systemic molecular landscape preceding migraine onset. Several key findings emerge. First, we identified a broad set of plasma proteins associated with incident migraine risk, many of which have not previously been linked to migraine. Second, we demonstrated that a substantial proportion of these proteins exhibit dynamic alterations years before clinical diagnosis, suggesting involvement in early, preclinical disease processes. Third, functional, cellular, and genetic integration consistently pointed toward immune regulation and neuro–immune interactions as central biological themes. Finally, we showed that proteomic profiles carry modest but reproducible predictive value for future migraine, supporting their potential utility in risk stratification.

  The identification of over one hundred plasma proteins associated with incident migraine underscores the systemic nature of migraine pathophysiology. Although migraine is traditionally viewed as a neurological disorder, increasing evidence indicates that peripheral immune and inflammatory processes critically modulate central pain pathways [43, 59–61]. Because our analyses focused on incident migraine in individuals free of prior disease, the observed associations are unlikely to reflect secondary effects of attacks or treatment, and instead likely capture biological susceptibility states. The coexistence of risk-increasing and protective proteins suggests that migraine arises from an imbalance between pro-nociceptive and protective systemic pathways. In particular, AGER (RAGE) and C4BPB point to immune–vascular mechanisms that may create a permissive milieu for migraine development. AGER is a well-established mediator of chronic inflammatory amplification and endothelial activation, integrating oxidative stress signals with NF-κB–dependent transcriptional responses [62, 63]. Its association with future migraine risk supports the concept that a pro-inflammatory and endothelial-stressed systemic environment may lower the threshold for trigeminovascular activation long before clinical onset [64]. Complement regulation via C4BPB further implicates innate immune pathways and microvascular inflammatory balance, consistent with emerging evidence that complement and coagulation systems contribute to neuroinflammatory sensitization relevant to migraine [65, 66].

  Proteins related to neuroendocrine and autonomic regulation, such as CHGA and GAST, suggest an additional role for systemic stress and gut–brain signaling. CHGA, a marker of neuroendocrine secretory activity, links sympathetic activation, vascular tone, and immune modulation, which are processes closely aligned with known migraine triggers such as psychological stress and sleep disruption [67, 68]. Additionally, previous study using genome-wide association data has implicated CHGA as a biologically relevant and druggable target in migraine, consistent with its role in stress-related neuroendocrine dysregulation [69]. GAST, while primarily a gastrointestinal hormone, may reflect broader autonomic and vagal states, supporting the growing recognition of gut–brain axis involvement in migraine susceptibility, potentially acting through indirect autonomic and inflammatory mechanisms [70]. Notably, recent studies have identified GAST as a novel candidate in depression [71, 72]. Several proteins point more directly toward neuronal and synaptic mechanisms. RBFOX3 (NeuN) is a canonical neuronal marker, and its circulating association may reflect increased neuronal vulnerability, excitability, or stress-related extracellular vesicle–mediated signaling preceding migraine onset [73–75]. SEPTIN8 and SNX15 implicate cytoskeletal organization and membrane trafficking, processes essential for synaptic transmission and receptor recycling, which are central to neuronal excitability and sensory gain control [76–78]. ATXN3, a regulator of protein homeostasis and cellular stress responses, further suggests that impaired proteostatic resilience may contribute to heightened neuronal sensitivity under inflammatory or metabolic stress [79, 80]. These proteins converge on a shared pathogenic framework rather than a single linear pathway, supporting a systems-level model in which immune–endothelial activation, neuroendocrine stress signaling, and neuronal vulnerability interact to increase migraine risk.

  Functional enrichment analyses consistently implicated immune-related pathways, including inflammatory signaling, cytokine-mediated communication, and membrane-associated immune processes. These findings reinforce the concept that migraine is closely linked to immune dysregulation, extending beyond acute neurogenic inflammation during attacks [81]. Importantly, our integration with scRNA-seq data provided cellular resolution to these proteomic signals. Monocytes and macrophages are increasingly recognized as key players in neuro–immune crosstalk, capable of modulating blood–brain barrier (BBB) permeability, releasing pro-inflammatory mediators, and interacting with sensory neurons [82–85]. The migraine-associated signals in these cell types support a model in which peripheral immune activation primes central nociceptive circuits, lowering the threshold for migraine initiation. Moreover, the differential immune cell composition observed between migraine cases and controls suggests that systemic immune remodeling may accompany or precede migraine susceptibility.

  CD83 is a canonical activation marker of dendritic cells and is also expressed on activated B cells and subsets of T cells, where it plays a critical role in antigen presentation, immune tolerance, and T-cell priming [86]. Elevated or dysregulated CD83-related signaling has been linked to chronic inflammatory states and altered adaptive immune balance [87, 88]. In the context of migraine, CD83-associated immune activation may contribute to sustained low-grade inflammation, facilitating peripheral immune–central nervous system crosstalk [81]. Such immune priming could sensitize trigeminovascular pathways indirectly, even in the absence of overt immune cell infiltration into the brain, supporting the concept of migraine as a disorder influenced by maladaptive systemic immune activation or tone rather than purely local neuroinflammation [89, 90]. TGFBR2, encoding the type II receptor for transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), represents a complementary but distinct immunoregulatory axis [91]. TGF-β signaling is essential for maintaining immune homeostasis, restraining excessive inflammation, and regulating T-cell differentiation [92]. Dysregulated or context-dependent activation of TGFBR2-related signaling may reflect impaired immunosuppressive capacity. In migraine, reduced effectiveness of anti-inflammatory checkpoints could lower the threshold for neurogenic inflammation and promote long-term sensitization of pain-processing circuits [93, 94]. Importantly, TGF-β signaling also influences endothelial stability and BBB function, providing a potential mechanistic bridge between immune dysregulation and neurovascular vulnerability [95, 96]. Additional proteins further support immune and cellular stress pathways. BST2 and TNFRSF4 (OX40) implicate innate–adaptive immune communication and T-cell costimulation, reinforcing the role of immune activation in migraine predisposition [97, 98]. PSME2 and ST13 highlight proteostasis and antigen-processing machinery, suggesting that cellular stress handling and immune surveillance efficiency may shape inflammatory responsiveness [99, 100]. Proteins such as GCLM and CHCHD10 point toward oxidative stress and mitochondrial resilience, processes known to modulate immune cell function and neuronal excitability [101, 102]. Notably, previous studies reported increased GCLM expression in migraine, which may represent a stress-induced or compensatory response to heightened oxidative burden rather than a protective mechanism [103]. These immune and cellular signals converge on a model in which altered immune activation, insufficient regulatory control, and cellular stress responses interact to create a permissive environment for migraine development. This immune context, evident years before clinical onset, supports migraine as a systemic disorder with a substantial immunological component rather than an exclusively neuronal disease.

  By examining associations between migraine-related plasma proteins and global brain MRI phenotypes, we sought to bridge peripheral molecular signals with central neuroanatomical features. A subset of proteins implicated adaptive immune regulation as a potential upstream influence on brain structure. IL2RA, a key regulator of T-cell activation and immune homeostasis, may reflect sustained immune imbalance that influence neuroplastic processes through chronic cytokine exposure [104]. Such immune-mediated effects have been shown to modulate synaptic remodeling, myelination, and neuronal survival, thereby shaping gray matter volume over time. LAIR1, an inhibitory immune receptor, further supports this interpretation, as impaired immune inhibitory signaling may permit persistent low-grade inflammatory signaling capable of subtly influencing brain structure without overt neuroinflammation [105, 106]. Neurovascular and extracellular matrix–related proteins also featured prominently. COL15A1 and COLEC12 are involved in vascular basement membrane organization and endothelial function, implicating microvascular integrity as a determinant of brain morphology [107, 108]. Subtle alterations in cerebral microvasculature may affect metabolic support, oxygen delivery, and BBB dynamics, contributing to long-term structural variation, particularly within subcortical regions that are highly metabolically active and central to pain modulation [109]. Several proteins further suggested direct relevance to neuronal architecture and cytoskeletal organization. PALM and LMOD1 are involved in membrane-associated cytoskeletal dynamics, processes essential for dendritic stability and synaptic structure [110, 111]. DTNB, which links cytoskeletal elements to membrane complexes, reinforces the importance of intracellular scaffolding in maintaining neuronal morphology [112]. Rare variants in DTNB have been associated with CSF markers of neuronal injury and inflammation in Alzheimer’s disease, suggesting a broader role of DTNB in neuronal vulnerability and inflammatory stress responses rather than disease-specific neurodegeneration [113, 114]. Additionally, DLL1, a Notch pathway ligand, points toward developmental or activity-dependent signaling mechanisms that may influence long-term neuroplasticity [115]. These findings suggest that migraine-associated proteins are linked to brain structure through integrated immune, vascular, and neuronal structural pathways. Rather than reflecting focal brain damage, the observed associations are more consistent with long-term modulation of neurovascular and neuroimmune homeostasis, contributing to a brain environment that is structurally and functionally more susceptible to migraine.

  The integration of genetic analyses provides an important layer of evidence supporting a potential causal role for specific circulating proteins in migraine susceptibility. PRS analyses demonstrated that inherited migraine liability is associated with variation in plasma protein levels, indicating a shared genetic architecture between migraine risk and systemic molecular phenotypes. Extending beyond correlation, MR and Bayesian colocalization analyses highlighted several proteins whose genetically predicted levels appear to influence migraine risk, strengthening causal inference. These genetically supported proteins highlight pathways involving endothelial signaling, immune-related proteostasis, and cellular stress responses as potential causal contributors to migraine pathogenesis. Among these proteins, YAP1 emerged as a prominent risk-associated protein. As a key effector of the Hippo signaling pathway, YAP1 regulates cell proliferation, mechanotransduction, and stress-responsive transcription [116, 117]. Genetically elevated YAP1 levels may therefore promote a vascular or endothelial state that lowers the threshold for trigeminovascular activation. PSMG4 and UBE2L6 implicate protein turnover and immune-related proteostasis pathways. PSMG4 is involved in proteasome assembly, while UBE2L6 participates in ubiquitin-mediated protein degradation, particularly in interferon-stimulated and inflammatory contexts. Notably, UBE2L6 has been identified as a potential target for migraine with biological mechanisms [118]. Genetic associations with these proteins suggest that altered protein quality control and immune stress responses may contribute to migraine susceptibility by amplifying inflammatory signaling or impairing cellular stress resilience.

  CDHR5, a cadherin-related adhesion molecule, points to dysregulated cell–cell adhesion and epithelial or endothelial barrier function [119, 120]. Altered adhesion signaling may compromise vascular integrity or neurovascular coupling, creating a permissive environment for migraine-related neurovascular dysfunction. Supporting this interpretation, CDHR5 has also been implicated in interaction analyses linking environmental exposures to neuropsychiatric outcomes, underscoring its sensitivity to systemic stressors [121]. Dysregulated adhesion signaling may affect vascular integrity or neurovascular coupling, providing a plausible structural and functional link between systemic molecular alterations and migraine risk.

  We further demonstrated that plasma proteomic profiles have a modest but reproducible ability to predict future migraine onset using machine learning approaches. Although the discrimination is insufficient for immediate clinical application, it is notable given the marked heterogeneity of migraine and the long interval between biomarker assessment and disease onset. Importantly, this predictive capacity is supported by the temporal dynamics of migraine-associated proteins. Through incidence-density matching and trajectory modeling, we identified distinct pre-diagnostic protein patterns, including monotonic declines, rise–then–fall patterns, and U-shaped changes, indicating that different biological processes may dominate across stages of migraine development. Many proteins diverged from control trajectories years before clinical diagnosis, suggesting a prolonged preclinical phase characterized by gradual shifts in immune activity, neurovascular sensitivity, and neuronal excitability. Together, these findings imply that circulating proteins capture early biological vulnerability rather than disease consequences. When integrated with genetic, clinical, and lifestyle information, proteomics may therefore contribute to multifactorial risk stratification and identify a potential window for preventive intervention before migraine onset.

  This study has several notable strengths, including its prospective design, large sample size, long follow-up duration, and comprehensive integration of proteomic, genetic, imaging, and single-cell data. The use of incident migraine minimizes reverse causation, and the consistency of findings across multiple analytic layers enhances confidence in their biological relevance. Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the UKB cohort is predominantly of European ancestry, which may limit the generalizability of our findings and preclude evaluation of ancestry-specific proteomic differences. Second, migraine outcomes were defined as hospital-diagnosed migraine using inpatient ICD-10 records. This conservative, high-specificity definition likely captures a more selected subset of individuals with clinically recognized migraine who interact with hospital services and may under-ascertain community-managed cases. Therefore, our findings may not fully generalize to the broader migraine population. Moreover, selection into hospital care is influenced by comorbidities and healthcare utilization, which may introduce selection/collider bias and should be considered when interpreting the identified proteomic signals. In addition, delays between symptom onset and clinical diagnosis may introduce temporal misclassification, potentially biasing inferred pre-diagnostic protein patterns. Third, subtype-specific analyses (with vs without aura) were not performed because aura/non-aura coding is incompletely captured in hospital ICD-10 records and subgrouping would markedly reduce statistical power. We speculate that aura and non-aura migraine may share core neurovascular–inflammatory pathways, but aura-related mechanisms (e.g., cortical spreading depolarization) could contribute to subtype-specific proteomic patterns. Thus, future studies with curated subtype phenotyping are warranted. Fourth, despite FDR control, correlation among proteins may affect the stability of individual associations and inflate pathway-level enrichment. Accordingly, we emphasize consistent biological themes rather than treating highly correlated proteins as independent evidence, and we interpret subgroup findings cautiously given reduced event counts. Future studies should incorporate resampling- or penalization-based approaches where feasible to further assess robustness. Fifth, although circulating proteins offer accessible biomarkers, we did not directly assess protein expression in brain or cerebrospinal fluid, which is essential for mechanistic interpretation. In addition, the temporal ordering between brain MRI acquisition and hospital-diagnosed migraine could not be determined at the individual level in the current dataset. Therefore, the observed protein-MRI associations should be interpreted as non-directional associations, and reverse causation cannot be excluded. This limitation restricts biological interpretation, as it remains unclear whether the identified circulating proteins precede, accompany, or follow the observed brain structural differences. Finally, the limited availability of large-scale prospective cohorts combining high-dimensional plasma proteomics with incident migraine outcomes constrains independent replication and highlights the need for future validation studies.


## Conclusion
  This study provides a comprehensive, multi-layered characterization of plasma proteomic signatures associated with the future development of migraine. Our findings reveal that migraine risk is preceded by widespread and dynamic alterations in circulating proteins, particularly those involved in immune regulation and neuro–immune communication. By integrating temporal, cellular, neuroimaging, and genetic perspectives, we advance a systems-level understanding of migraine pathogenesis and identify candidate biomarkers and pathways for future investigation. These results lay the groundwork for translational efforts aimed at early identification, prevention, and personalized management of migraine.
