# An end-to-end generalizable deep learning framework to comprehensively analyze transcriptional regulation


## Abstract
  Genome annotation currently requires performing dozens of molecular assays in hundreds of cell and tissue samples, an expensive endeavor which is impractical to replicate across all species and conditions of interest. Here, we introduce BioSeq2Seq, a deep learning framework that infers cell-line-specific molecular assays widely used for genome annotation by leveraging a tri-modal input: evolutionarily conserved DNA sequence features, together with cell-line-specific transcriptional activity and directionality captured by a single run-on sequencing assay. BioSeq2Seq enables flexible genome annotation tasks through parameterized configurations of input features and output targets, combined with gradient-guided architectural refinement for specific biological objectives. Our model demonstrates high accuracy across four downstream tasks, showing improvements of 14.27% in histone modification prediction, 2.50% in functional element identification, and 2.90% in gene expression prediction compared to state-of-the-art methods. In transcription factor binding site (TFBS) prediction, it maintains performance comparable to that of leading existing approaches. By achieving competitive performance across tasks with single-cell-line input data, BioSeq2Seq provides an efficient and low-cost alternative for genome annotation.


## Introduction
  Genomes encode numerous types of functional elements that play important roles in transcriptional regulation, DNA packaging, recombination, repair, and a myriad of other biological processes1. Histone modifications dynamically regulate chromatin structure and function by serving as epigenetic switches for transcriptional control and genome maintenance2–11. Specific classes of functional elements establish transcriptional regulatory programs: promoters initiate transcription through RNA polymerase II loading, enhancers amplify transcriptional signals over long distances, and insulators prevent aberrant regulatory crosstalk12–15. Transcription factors (TFs) regulate gene expression by binding to specific DNA sites (TFBSs), forming interactions that control when and where genes are activated16. These interactions link DNA sequence to gene expression patterns, making them key elements for mapping transcriptional regulatory networks17. Moreover, these regulatory layers are highly interconnected, collectively determining cellular phenotypes and enabling context-specific gene expression programs. Systematically annotating these functional elements, from chromatin states (histone modifications) to regulatory logic (TFBSs) and phenotypic manifestations (gene expression), is essential for understanding genome function, interpreting regulatory mechanisms, and reconstructing transcriptional networks. Although experimental techniques such as chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq)18 can precisely capture various aspects of transcriptional regulation, their high costs in instrumentation, labor, and time make it impractical to systematically analyze tens of thousands of species and diverse cellular states. Therefore, computational approaches that serve as a complementary strategy to experimental assays, enabling scalable and cost-efficient exploration of transcriptional regulatory landscapes, are of critical importance.

  Accompanied by the advancement of intelligent hardware and deep learning, computational biology has made remarkable strides in uncovering the mechanisms of transcriptional regulation on a genome-wide scale19–21. Deep learning methods can accurately predict various biological functions, such as epigenomic information2,22–26, functional variants27,28, gene expression features29–31, 3D-structure of chromosomes32,33, and functional element activities12,25 from DNA sequences or epigenome data derived from sequencing experiments. The key to these methods is the use of DNA sequence or various sequencing datasets to train powerful deep learning models for predicting marks associated with transcriptional regulation. However, current studies do not provide models with enough information about cell line specificity, as these studies rely solely on DNA sequences24,26,27 or a single epigenetic profile2 (e.g., one DNase-seq sample) from the training cell line. As a result, models fail to predict epigenomic markers in additional cell lines or cell states that are not represented in their training data. Additionally, prevailing methodologies employ strand-agnostic genomic features2,26,33, missing intrinsic strand-specific signals that encode transcriptional polarity critical for discerning regulatory element orientation. Addressing these two challenges is crucial for improving a model’s accuracy and generalizability.

  One strategy for efficient yet comprehensive genome annotation is to leverage the rich correlation structure between maps of bidirectional transcription and other functional marks. Bidirectional transcription measured using run-on and sequencing methodologies, including PRO-seq, GRO-seq, and ChRO-seq (henceforth referred to simply as RO-seq), is a sensitive and accurate way to measure the distribution of RNA polymerase in virtually any cell or tissue sample34–36. Transcriptional activity maps derived from RO-seq are highly correlated with several key aspects of regulatory function. For instance, histone modifications such as H3K4me2 and H3K27ac, which mark active enhancers and promoters; H3K36me3, which indicates transcription elongation; and H3K9ac, which marks transcriptional activation, are all closely linked to transcription37. Leveraging this advantage, Danko et al. used Support Vector Regression (SVR) to accurately annotate multiple functional elements based on RO-seq data34,35,38. In another study, Wang et al. employed SVR models and RO-seq data to predict nine histone modification profiles across multiple species, achieving resolution down to single nucleosomes and accuracy approaching ChIP-seq experimental replication39. The success of these approaches reveals the potential for RO-seq as an effective strategy in mapping functional elements. Nonetheless, there are still several challenges that must be addressed before RO-seq can serve as an efficient alternative for functional element discovery: (i) SVR-based models do not presently recover CTCF-bound insulators, an important architectural element that can efficiently be discovered using DNA sequence, but for which RO-seq data provides only indirect information25,40–42; (ii) Each SVR model is focused on predicting a single genomic mark, which is both inconvenient for users and prevents models learning better features through jointly learning partially correlated functional marks, reducing model accuracy relative to state-of-the-art approaches; (iii) SVR-based methods are computationally inefficient, making them impractical for routine use. For instance, even predicting only histone modifications on chromosome 22, dHIT39 takes 3–5 h.

  In this work, we introduce a generalizable deep learning framework called BioSeq2Seq for predicting major functional genomic activities. BioSeq2Seq integrates RO-seq data, which captures cell-line-specific transcriptional activity and directionality, with static features derived from DNA sequences to predict genomic profiles that are typically used for functional genome annotation. Through comprehensive evaluation of four downstream prediction tasks, including histone modification prediction, functional element identification, gene expression prediction, and TFBS prediction, we demonstrate that BioSeq2Seq achieves state-of-the-art performance across diverse biological contexts. The fully trained BioSeq2Seq models can now be used in a variety of cell lines, tissues, and species, providing researchers with diverse functional genomic data.


## Results

### A framework for mapping sequencing data to multiple transcriptional regulation patterns
  We developed an advanced deep learning framework called BioSeq2Seq to predict transcriptional regulatory modalities from two types of sequencing data: RO-seq data and DNA sequence (Fig. 1). By integrating RNA polymerase location and density information from RO-seq39 with DNA sequence, BioSeq2Seq can accurately infer the genome-wide regulatory profiles at 128-bp resolution. It is user-friendly and does not require the complex preprocessing demanded by other frameworks. Users only need to provide the input RO-seq data, reference genome files, and ground truth “.bigwig” files to perform model training and evaluation. BioSeq2Seq automatically handles sample segmentation and feature extraction based on the provided files.Fig. 1Overview of the BioSeq2Seq framework.a BioSeq2Seq adopts a transformer architecture comprising three modules: a feature embedding module, an attention module, and a downstream task head module. RO-seq signals and DNA sequences are used as inputs and are integrated through a learnable projection layer. b BioSeq2Seq is used to perform four downstream prediction tasks, including histone modification prediction, functional element identification, gene expression prediction, and TFBS prediction. The model employs a unified deep learning algorithm across all tasks, with task-specific architectural fine-tuning for different downstream tasks. c We conduct comprehensive analyses and evaluations across multiple downstream tasks, including predictive performance assessment (e.g., signal correlation and peak classification), evaluation of cross-cell, tissue, and species generalization, peak calling, analysis of functional genomic regions, and enrichment analysis, among others.

  BioSeq2Seq employs transformer43 architecture as its backbone, which is an attention-based deep learning architecture that has demonstrated impressive performance in modeling both nucleotide-level sequence features and chromosome-scale spatial interactions24,44. Its attention mechanism enables the model to have a receptive field larger than 100k bp to capture long-range interactions between distal functional elements, such as those between enhancers and promoters45. To exploit this capability, we set the sample length to exceed 100 kb. Under the default parameters, the whole genome is segmented into 114k-bp fragments, with 41k-bp flanking regions added upstream and downstream of each fragment, resulting in a long region with a total length of 197k bp. BioSeq2Seq automatically extracts DNA and RO-seq features from this long region and predicts the transcriptional regulation patterns of the central 114k-bp region.

  For different downstream tasks, BioSeq2Seq consistently uses regression models and designs a statistics-based peak calling algorithm to perform classification tasks, such as functional element identification. In different tasks, we adjusted the model architecture of BioSeq2Seq. We defined a combination of an attention module and a head module, which follows the feature embedding module, as a branch. The primary differences between models used for different tasks lie in the number and complexity of branches. Based on this design, we trained four dedicated models to predict 10 types of histone modifications, 4 types of functional elements, 10 types of RNA-seq expression levels, and 90 types of TFBS. The task types, evaluation metrics, input and output formats, and data sources for all downstream tasks are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

  For model training and evaluation, we utilized a total of 18 RO-seq datasets derived from 11 cell lines and tissues. During training, seven RO-seq datasets from the K562 cell line (G1–G7; see Supplementary Table S2) were employed, using all chromosomes except chromosomes 21 and 22 for model fitting, chromosome 21 for validation, and chromosome 22 for testing. An additional RO-seq dataset from K562 (G8), together with ten datasets from other cell lines or species, served as independent test sets to assess model generalization across the whole genome.


### Histone modification prediction
  In this task, we aimed to predict ten histone modifications across the whole genome, covering both transcriptionally active (e.g., H3K4me3, H3K27ac) and repressive (e.g., H3K27me3, H3K9me3) modifications (Supplementary Table S3). We trained the model on data from K562 and then evaluated the cross-cell line generalization using data from mouse liver, horse liver, Drosophila melanogaster (Dmel) ovary, and seven human cell lines, including GM12878, HCT116, HeLa, CD4+ T cells, IMR-90, MCF-7, and HepG2. Supplementary Table S3 provides detailed descriptions of this dataset.


#### BioSeq2Seq shows excellent prediction performance
  Genome browser visualizations46 reveal that BioSeq2Seq predictions for various histone modifications strongly correlate with experimental data (Fig. 2a). For transcriptionally activating modifications, such as H3K4me3, H3K27ac, and H3K9ac, the model achieves the highest accuracy. For transcriptional elongation-associated marks H3K36me3, the predicted signals closely recapitulate experimentally measured enrichment patterns. For transcriptionally repressive marks, the model successfully captures H3K27me3 enrichment in non-transcribed regions and slight enrichment in transcribed regions. The results show that BioSeq2Seq successfully identifies RO-seq bimodal peaks arising from transcription-independent sources and correctly suppresses histone-modification predictions in these genomic regions. The model avoids misinterpretation in genomic regions with weak RO-seq signal and can filter out low-intensity peaks likely arising from mapping errors.   For a genome-wide perspective on signal distributions, we compared the predicted and experimental profiles at a 10k-bp resolution. Supplementary Fig. S1 summarizes the results across all cell lines and tissues. For histone modifications such as H3K4me3 and H3K9ac, BioSeq2Seq yields a strong linear relationship between predicted and experimental signals, demonstrating that the model effectively captures transcription-associated histone modification patterns across diverse biological contexts. In contrast, for repressive modifications including H3K27me3, H3K9me3, and H4K20me1, the predicted signals exhibit weaker correlations with the corresponding ChIP-seq data, consistent with the trends observed in Fig. 2b.

  To quantify the predictive performance, we computed Pearson and Spearman correlations at a 1k-bp resolution. BioSeq2Seq exhibits strong predictive accuracy in the K562 cell line, as well as across other test cell lines and tissues (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. S2). In the holdout evaluation on chromosome 22 of the K562 cell line, BioSeq2Seq performs the worst on the sample G2, likely due to differences in sequencing depth (18 million for G2, 27 million for G6 and G7, and more than 54 million for the remaining datasets; Supplementary Fig. S3 and Supplementary Table S2). Across other human cell lines, BioSeq2Seq achieves performance comparable to that observed in the K562 cell line. In cross-species evaluations, performance decreases modestly relative to human cell lines; nevertheless, the Pearson correlations for most histone modifications remain above 0.6, underscoring the framework’s potential for cross-species prediction. For histone modifications associated with transcriptional activity, such as those involved in transcription elongation (H3K36me3) and transcription activation (H3K122ac, H3K4me1, H3K4me2, H3K4me3, H3K9ac, H3K27ac), the predicted signals exhibited high concordance with experimental data. In contrast, for modifications linked to transcriptional repression (H3K27me3, H3K9me3, H4K20me1), where transcription is infrequent, prediction accuracy was generally lower, with most correlation values falling below 0.6. This reduced performance is attributable to the characteristic ChIP-seq signal profiles of repressive modifications, which tend to form continuous, dense domains rather than sharp, well-defined peaks, lacking clear boundaries or spatial distribution patterns (Supplementary Fig. S4). Consequently, it is more challenging for the model to capture the underlying relationships among these histone modifications, RO-seq signals, and DNA sequences.


#### BioSeq2Seq achieves state-of-the-art performance in histone modification prediction
  We benchmarked BioSeq2Seq against the state-of-the-art histone modification prediction model dHIT39. Using a 1k-bp window, we computed genome-wide correlations for each cell line predicted by both models. For the K562 cell line, we evaluated performance on the holdout chromosome 22 of the RO-seq dataset, G1 (see Supplementary Table S2).   In addition to improving prediction performance, BioSeq2Seq offers several notable advantages over dHIT. First, BioSeq2Seq can predict histone modifications within a 114k-bp window using 197k-bp of genomic features, substantially exceeding dHIT’s capacity, which predicts histone modifications at a single site using 50k-bp of genomic features. Even at the edge of each genomic window, BioSeq2Seq leverages over 80k-bp of contextual information to annotate the target site. Second, BioSeq2Seq predicts ten histone modifications simultaneously using a single model, whereas dHIT requires ten separate models. This parallel multi-histone framework enables BioSeq2Seq to capture shared latent features across different histone modifications while greatly accelerating the prediction process. By contrast, dHIT suffers from extremely slow computational speed, rendering multi-histone predictions infeasible within a reasonable timeframe. BioSeq2Seq achieves prediction efficiency dozens of times higher than dHIT (Supplementary Fig. S5), completing whole-genome predictions in just 2.5 h.

For reference, the base model integrating both RO-seq and DNA sequence is referred to as the RD model, the model using only RO-seq as the R model, and the model using only DNA sequence as the D model.

For certain repressive marks such as H4K20me1, the D model occasionally surpasses the R model.   We further assessed predictive accuracy using mean-square error (MSE), which measures the average squared difference between the predicted signal and ground truth, reflecting agreement with experimental data (Supplementary Table S4). Since MSE is sensitive to the scale of values, this comparison was restricted to the RD, R, and D models. To balance contributions from high- and low-signal regions, an inverse hyperbolic sine (arcsinh) transformation was applied to compress the data scale prior to MSE calculation. This procedure allows more sensitive detection of predictive performance differences across genomic intervals. Consistent with the correlation evaluation, the RD model exhibits the lowest MSE overall, demonstrating superior predictive accuracy and stability across both high- and low-signal regions (Fig. 3a).

  Across both correlation and MSE metrics, the joint use of DNA sequence and RO-seq information substantially enhances predictive accuracy and ensures robust performance across diverse cell lines and species. Importantly, BioSeq2Seq consistently surpasses dHIT across all cell lines when using only RO-seq input (R model), which is the same input as dHIT; the inclusion of DNA sequence features further amplifies this performance advantage (Figs. 2c, d and 3b, Supplementary Figs. S8 and S9).


#### BioSeq2Seq identifies modification patterns in regulatory genomic regions
  To provide a comprehensive evaluation of BioSeq2Seq across transcriptionally relevant genomic contexts, we summarized model performance in three representative regions: (i) regions surrounding transcription start sites (TSSs)47, (ii) histone peak regions stratified by their distance to the TSS, and (iii) regions associated with key functional elements, including promoter, enhancer, insulator, poly(A) and gene body48–51 (see Supplementary Method S1).

  Genome-wide, chromatin states are closely associated with the positions of TSSs and Pol II pausing sites39. This relationship can be visualized by aligning histone signals upstream and downstream of the TSS52,53. We observed nucleosome enrichment of H3K4me2, H3K4me3, H3K9ac, H3K122ac, and H3K27ac near the TSS (Fig. 4e, f and Supplementary Fig. S10). Notably, nucleosomes are concentrated in the proximal upstream and downstream regions, whereas nucleosome occupancy at the TSS itself is markedly lower. H3K4me1 is also enriched flanking the TSS, but its peak lies farther from the TSS compared with other transcriptionally active histone modifications. By contrast, H3K36me3 exhibits a distinct distribution: its signal remains low around the TSS and gradually increases along the downstream gene body, consistent with its established role as a hallmark of transcription elongation37. These patterns underscore a clear distinction between promoter-associated histone modifications and elongation-associated marks.

  To evaluate histone peak regions stratified by their distance to TSSs, we classified histone modification peaks35,54,55 into four groups: near 1k bp, 1k–10k bp, 10k–30k bp, and greater than 30k bp from the TSS. For different histone modifications, the correlation between predicted and experimental signals is markedly higher in regions proximal to the TSS (<1 kb) than in distal regions (>1 kb) (Fig. 4a). In distal regions, the correlation exhibits less fluctuation with increasing distance. Notably, BioSeq2Seq maintained relatively high prediction accuracy even in cell lines where dHIT’s performance is near zero (e.g., CD4+ T cells and mouse liver cells), indicating that BioSeq2Seq’s cross-cell-line and cross-species prediction capability is substantially stronger than that of dHIT (Supplementary Fig. S11).

  To assess prediction performance in regions associated with key functional elements, we used a 1k-bp window for shorter elements (promoter, enhancer, insulator, and poly(A)) and the original region length for longer elements (gene body). As shown in Fig. 4c, d, predicted values for promoters, enhancers, and gene bodies by BioSeq2Seq are relatively concentrated, indicating stable and consistent predictions in these regions. In contrast, predictions for insulators and poly(A) sites exhibit greater dispersion, reflecting higher variability and uncertainty. These results suggest that BioSeq2Seq achieves robust performance across most transcriptional regulatory elements.


### Functional element identification
  To explore the regulatory mechanisms in more detail, we applied BioSeq2Seq to identify the locations of key genomic regions defining gene architecture, including transcription initiation region (TIR)35, insulator, poly(A), and gene body. Among these, TIR marks genomic regions associated with transcription initiation, typically encompassing promoters and enhancers34,35. In addition to the training cell line K562, data from nine other cell lines and tissues across different species were used to comprehensively evaluate the predictive performance.


#### BioSeq2Seq enables precise identification of functional elements
  Functional element identification is often formulated as a classification problem. In contrast, we adopted a regression-based strategy by reformulating functional element labels into continuous signals resembling genomic assay profiles (Supplementary Method S2). This representation enables the use of regression models for functional element annotation. To translate continuous predictions into biologically interpretable element calls, we developed a dedicated statistical peak-calling algorithm (Supplementary Method S3). Candidate annotations are first identified using a predefined signal threshold, after which each candidate’s likelihood of representing a functional element is evaluated via hypothesis testing under a multivariate normal distribution. Applying a p value threshold of 1e-3, we selected the final set of predicted genomic regions.

  Using this peak-calling approach, BioSeq2Seq accurately identifies the locations and distributions of diverse functional elements (Fig. 5a). Importantly, the method captures cell-line-specific patterns, precisely identifying functional element sites across distinct cellular contexts. Quantitative evaluation demonstrates high predictive performance, with a mean AUC of 0.9737 (±0.0559) for TIRs and 0.9241 (±0.0323) for gene bodies across all cell lines (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. S12). The mean AUPR for TIRs also exceeds 0.7766 (±0.2016), highlighting the robustness of the model.Fig. 5BioSeq2Seq demonstrates robust performance in functional element identification.a Genome browser view on chromosome 1 of the GM12878 cell line showing predicted signals, peak calling results, and functional element labels.   We further assessed identification accuracy by defining a predicted functional element as correct if it overlapped more than 60% with the corresponding label, ensuring a one-to-one correspondence between predictions and labels. Across almost all cross-cell-line predictions, TIR and gene body identification accuracy exceeded 0.85, with recall surpassing 0.90 in most cases (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Table S5). These results demonstrate BioSeq2Seq’s ability to provide both precise and reliable functional element identification.


#### BioSeq2Seq outperforms the state-of-the-art method for identifying functional elements
  We compared BioSeq2Seq with the state-of-the-art method, dREG35. We used TIR data from the K562 and GM12878 cell lines, which serve as benchmark datasets for dREG, to evaluate both methods. The TIR label used here was defined as the intersection between DNase I hypersensitive sites identified by Duke and UW DNase-seq assays56 and GRO-cap HMM calls57. The benchmark consists of clearly defined TIRs and non-TIR regions. To make the assessment more accurate, we excluded predictions from genomic regions that were not clearly defined. Overall, BioSeq2Seq consistently outperformed dREG across most evaluation metrics in both cell lines, yielding an average performance gain of 2.50% (0.0183) (Fig. 5d). Notably, BioSeq2Seq exhibited stronger discrimination of non-TIR regions, producing fewer false positives and yielding higher overall accuracy in both cell lines compared with dREG (Supplementary Table S6).


### Gene expression prediction
  Existing gene expression prediction methods, such as AttentiveChrome29, formulate the problem as a binary classification task, predicting whether a gene is highly or lowly expressed using a 10k-bp window around its TSS. In contrast, we directly used genome-wide gene expression data (RNA-seq) as the learning target, enabling a more comprehensive and quantitative prediction. We collected RNA-seq data from five human cell lines, K562, GM12878, IMR-90, MCF-7, and HepG2, all generated by the same laboratory58,59. These cell lines include 10, 8, 4, 4, and 6 samples, respectively (Supplementary Table S7). Different samples within the same cell line represent distinct subcellular types. RNA-seq samples from K562 were used for training, while the remaining samples were used for evaluation.


#### BioSeq2Seq accurately predicts gene expression

#### BioSeq2Seq outperforms existing gene expression prediction models
  Among existing gene expression prediction methods, DeepChrome60, AttentiveChrome29, and TransformerChrome61 primarily rely on histone modification data, whereas EPCOT21 integrates both histone modification and TF information for gene expression prediction. These approaches exhibit strong predictive performance and have motivated further research on computational strategies for gene expression inference.

  We trained a support vector machine classifier using the gene expression values predicted by BioSeq2Seq to perform gene expression status prediction. The classifier was trained and evaluated on the same datasets employed by benchmark methods21,29,60,61, with K562, GM12878, and HepG2 as the evaluation cell lines. Notably, although BioSeq2Seq was trained exclusively on the K562 cell line, the classifier achieved the highest performance across all three cell lines, improving the AUR by 2.90% (0.0259) and the AUPR by 18.36% (0.1401) relative to the state-of-the-art method TransformerChrome (Fig. 6b).


### Transcription factor binding site prediction
  Since the development of RO-seq technology, there has been an urgent demand for computational biology tools capable of accurately predicting multiple TFBS from RO-seq data. Previous methods have been limited to datasets containing only a small number of TFBSs62,63, until the recent introduction of maxATAC16, which constructed the first benchmark dataset with extensive TFBS coverage and provided an important methodological reference for subsequent studies. BioSeq2Seq adopts the same ENCODE ChIP-seq datasets for each TFBS as those used in maxATAC. Our goal is to develop a model based on RO-seq, and through architectural optimization, the model is capable of simultaneously predicting 90 TFBSs. #### BioSeq2Seq enables accurate and efficient genome-wide prediction of TFBS
  BioSeq2Seq demonstrates outstanding performance in TFBS prediction, with its predicted signal profiles showing high concordance with experimental data (Fig. 7d). For quantitative evaluation, a bin size of 1k bp was adopted. Specifically, chromosome 22 of K562 and the whole-genome regions of the remaining holdout cell lines were used for benchmarking. We performed a comprehensive statistical assessment across 90 TFBSs, computing the AUPR, Pearson correlation, and precision at 5% recall. On the K562 test set, BioSeq2Seq achieves median values of 0.4854 for AUPR, 0.6197 for Pearson correlation, and 0.9442 for precision at 5% recall (Fig. 7b, c, and Supplementary Fig. S16). Together, these results demonstrate that BioSeq2Seq advances RO-seq-based TFBS prediction to a state-of-the-art level.


#### BioSeq2Seq achieves superior TFBS prediction accuracy with a multi-target model
  For methods comparison, we adopted a bin size of 200 bp, consistent with maxATAC. We first compared BioSeq2Seq with the TF motif scanning method MOODS64. The results show that BioSeq2Seq surpasses MOODS for nearly all TFBS (Fig. 7f, h). We further compared BioSeq2Seq with the state-of-the-art algorithm maxATAC16. Because BioSeq2Seq was not trained on data from other cell lines, this comparison was performed exclusively on the holdout chromosomes of the K562 cell line. The results indicate that BioSeq2Seq achieves overall performance comparable to maxATAC (Fig. 7e, g), with maxATAC attaining a higher AUPR, whereas BioSeq2Seq achieves significantly higher precision (p = 5.8e-4). Among the four TFBSs identified by maxATAC as particularly challenging (GATAD2B, NFXL1, ZBTB40, and ZNF592), BioSeq2Seq accurately predicts all except NFXL1. Relative to maxATAC, BioSeq2Seq improves the prediction performance for GATAD2B, ZBTB40, and ZNF592 by 109.80% (0.3203), 28.71% (0.1250), and 49.90% (0.1993), respectively. Furthermore, BioSeq2Seq predicts binding sites for 90 TFs using a single model, eliminating the need to train 90 independent models and representing a substantial methodological advantage over maxATAC.


## Discussion
  Understanding transcriptional regulation from RO-seq data remains a fundamental challenge in regulatory genomics. BioSeq2Seq addresses this challenge by offering an innovative deep learning framework for RO-seq to predict histone modification, functional elements, gene expression, and TFBS. To our knowledge, it is the first framework to offer such a broad suite of predictive capabilities tailored specifically for RO-seq. It is also the first model to leverage a tri-modal representation, combining evolutionarily conserved DNA sequence features with cell-line-specific transcriptional activity and directionality captured by RO-seq, to enable comprehensive analysis of transcriptional regulation. The framework requires only RO-seq data, DNA sequences, and user-provided target data, such as histone modification ChIP-seq, to train or evaluate a genome annotation model.

  BioSeq2Seq achieves robust generalization performance using training data from a single cell line. Data scarcity remains a major challenge in transcriptomic research, particularly for rare or understudied cell lines and tissues. In contrast, well-characterized human cell lines such as K562 and GM12878 are supported by abundant datasets. However, the generalization performance of many existing models depends heavily on training with data from multiple cell lines16,21, which further intensifies the demand for diverse and extensive training resources. Achieving comparable generalization performance using data from a single cell line would therefore be of substantial value. Leveraging multiple RO-seq samples from the K562 cell line, BioSeq2Seq attains generalization performance comparable to models trained on multi-cell-line data, while markedly outperforming a model trained on a single K562 sample (see Supplementary Fig. S17).

  BioSeq2Seq demonstrates state-of-the-art performance across diverse downstream tasks. For histone modification prediction, it achieves a 14.27% (0.0891) improvement over the current leading algorithm dHIT39, accompanied by a computational speed increase of several orders of magnitude. For functional element identification, BioSeq2Seq provides a 2.50% (0.0183) enhancement over dREG35. For gene expression prediction, it attains performance gains of 2.90% (0.0259) in AUC and 18.36% (0.1401) in AUPR relative to TransformerChrome61. For TFBS prediction, BioSeq2Seq achieves performance comparable to the current state-of-the-art method maxATAC16, marking a significant milestone as the first RO-seq-based algorithm to reach this level of accuracy in the domain. These performance gains are attributed to BioSeq2Seq’s flexible and modular architecture, along with its extensive receptive field, which enables effective capture of contextual information across upstream and downstream genomic regions. Moreover, BioSeq2Seq provides comprehensive downstream analysis tools for RO-seq, allowing users to leverage its inference outputs for detailed functional assessment. In addition to strand-specific RO-seq data, the framework can incorporate unstranded genomic assay data such as ATAC-seq, demonstrating substantial potential for adaptation to other sequencing data types. To facilitate broad accessibility and adoption, the complete BioSeq2Seq codebase, including data processing and model training components, is publicly available on GitHub. Pre-trained model weights are provided for four downstream tasks, allowing researchers to readily reproduce the results and apply the model to their own datasets.

  Compared to traditional predictive frameworks21,27, BioSeq2Seq offers greater flexibility, enabling adaptation to diverse downstream tasks with only minor modifications to the model architecture and training strategy. For instance, during model training for TFBS prediction, a base model structure similar to that used for histone modification prediction yielded suboptimal results, particularly for certain TFs such as CTCF. Analysis of the fitting curves allowed us to categorize TFs into three groups. In subsequent experiments, a multi-branch model structure was employed, with different branches dedicated to different TF groups. This approach, combined with strategies such as learning rate adjustments and parameter freezing, successfully addressed the performance issues. A similar strategy was applied for functional element identification, whereas gene expression and histone modification prediction utilized the base model, consisting of a single attention module and a single head module. In addition, BioSeq2Seq performs real-time feature extraction rather than relying on pre-constructed feature datasets, facilitating future improvements and extension to other transcriptional regulatory prediction tasks. Overall, BioSeq2Seq constitutes a unified, modular framework in which each downstream task is managed by an independent model following a consistent architecture. This design prevents interference between tasks and supports parallel development and rapid iteration. Standardized input and output data structures allow users to extend the framework to new tasks by ensuring data conformity, enabling modification of existing models or integration of new models with minimal effort. By combining flexible task-specific adaptation with consistent overall architecture, BioSeq2Seq achieves both high predictive performance and efficient scalability across a broad range of transcriptional regulatory applications.

  As a bioinformatics tool, BioSeq2Seq provides rapid and cost-effective genome annotation, offering predictions of histone modification, functional elements, gene expression, and TFBS. Importantly, BioSeq2Seq output can be used to evaluate the regulatory activity using markers widely explored by ENCODE and other projects. To illustrate a disease-relevant application, we examined the IL2RA locus, a well-characterized super-enhancer in CD4+ T cells65. Predicted H3K27ac signals closely coincide with dense clusters of genome-wide association study (GWAS) catalog single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) (Supplementary Fig. S18). Candidate SNPs from eight autoimmune diseases all fall within regions of high predicted H3K27ac signal, consistent with experimental observations reported for the IL2RA super-enhancer65. This example demonstrates how BioSeq2Seq predictions can serve as a preliminary computational validation, linking disease-associated variants to putative functional elements.

  With the rapid accumulation of high-resolution multi-omics and single-cell datasets, BioSeq2Seq holds promise as a foundational platform for regulatory genomics, enabling unified modeling across diverse biological contexts. Inspired by the success of unsupervised and self-supervised learning in natural language processing, computer vision, and computational biology66–70, we aim to adopt similar strategies to learn general-purpose regulatory representations. These embeddings will serve as informative priors for downstream applications such as enhancer-promoter interaction prediction, chromatin conformation modeling, and transcriptional regulatory network reconstruction. We also aim to address the challenges of high-resolution long-sequence modeling, which is increasingly required to capture subtle regulatory signals across extensive genomic regions. To meet this demand, we are exploring advanced architectural improvements, including sparse attention mechanisms67–69 and state-of-the-art state-space models such as Mamba71,72. These innovations extend the effective receptive field without substantially increasing computational cost, enabling fine-grained modeling of long-range dependencies and high-resolution features. Furthermore, additional modalities, including DNA methylation73 and Polycomb complex ChIP-seq74, can be integrated with the existing model to enhance the prediction of transcriptionally repressive regulatory marks, such as H3K27me3 and H3K9me3. These improvements will further enhance BioSeq2Seq’s practical relevance, establishing it as a versatile platform for advancing predictive regulatory genomics.


## Methods

### Datasets
  In this study, we used data from eight human cell lines, including K562, GM12878, HCT116, HeLa, CD4+ T cells, IMR-90, MCF-7, and HepG2, as well as the mouse liver cells, horse liver cells, and Dmel ovary cells. For all experiments, GRCh37 (hg19), GRCm38 (mm10), EquCab2, and dm6 were used as the reference genomes for human, mouse, horse, and Dmel cell lines, respectively75. We used 18 RO-seq samples, including eight from K562 (denoted as G1, G2, …, G8) and one from each of the other ten cell lines or tissues (see Supplementary Table S2). For training, all autosomal regions of G1–G7 exclusive were used except for chromosomes 21 and 22, which were held out. Chromosome 21 was used for validation, whereas chromosome 22 served as the test set. The whole-genome data of G8 and those from the other ten cell lines or tissues served as the test set for cross-cell-line generalization evaluation. The datasets for each downstream task are described below.

  For the histone modification prediction, we utilized ten histone modification datasets from ENCODE and NIH databases, including: H3K122ac, H3K4me1, H3K4me2, H3K4me3, H3K27ac, H3K27me3, H3K36me3, H3K9ac, H3K9me3, and H4K20me1 (see Supplementary Table S3).

  For the functional element identification, we used four types of functional element annotations, including TIR, insulator, poly(A), and gene body, which were obtained from NCBI and other experimental or curated annotation sources35,76,77 (see Supplementary Table S1). We used dREG peak-calling results as the reference annotations for TIR, which dREG defines as genomic loci exhibiting distinctive patterns of actively elongating RNA polymerase35. We retained TIR annotations passing a threshold of p value of 1e-2. If the p value of a dREG peak was less than the threshold, the peak was labeled as a nominated TIR. The insulator annotation used in our study came from the annotations provided by Ernst et al.76,77. For gene body annotations, we downloaded the genome annotation files GCF_000001405.25 [hg19], GCF_000001635.20 [mm10], GCF_000002305.2 [EquCab2], and GCF_000001215.4 [dm6] from NCBI78. We selected genes and the corresponding transcripts whose “gene biotype” or “transcript biotype” is “mRNA,” “protein coding,” or “primary transcript.” We then calculated the transcription intensity within each gene (see details in Supplementary Method S4). The statistical results revealed a clear split threshold of 0.4 for distinguishing expressed genes from non-expressed genes (Supplementary Fig. S19). Based on this threshold, we selected the expressed genes from all gene annotations as the label of the gene body in each cell line. The 200 bp region after the end of the gene body was used as the label of poly(A). Unlike other downstream tasks, the identification of functional elements is a classification task. However, when the positive labels were very few (such as TIRs), the model often predicted all sites as 0. To overcome this problem, we used regression prediction and a peak calling algorithm to identify functional elements. Due to the high density and extended length of gene body labels, using a simple binary (0–1) assignment did not accurately capture the characteristics of gene bodies, particularly in distinguishing adjacent genes. Therefore, we transformed each label into a signal-like format by generating an arithmetic distribution in which the signal reached its maximum at the peak center and gradually decreased toward both sides. Specifically, each peak was processed into an isosceles-triangle-shaped signal, where the signal values increased uniformly from the flanks to the center point and decreased symmetrically away from the center, with predefined minimum and maximum values (See details in Supplementary Method S2). To avoid overfitting the model, we added Gaussian noise to the background regions.

  For the gene expression prediction, we used 32 RNA-seq samples58,59 representing ten subcellular types from five cell lines provided by NCBI (see details in Supplementary Table S7). We used RNA-seq datasets from the same laboratory to reduce technical variability and batch effects, ensuring that model performance reflected true predictive capability rather than artifacts from heterogeneous sources.

  For the TFBS prediction, we used TFBS datasets sourced from the ENCODE database. We selected TFBSs based on the K562 cell line as a benchmark, and downloaded the data of K562, GM12878, HCT116, IMR-90, MCF-7, and HepG2 cell lines. For TFBS data that was only available in the hg38 reference genome, we used UCSC liftOver to convert the reference genome to hg19. We referred to the data screening method used in maxATAC16 to remove TFBS ChIP-seq data with too sparse signals from the dataset. Finally, our dataset contained 90 TFBS ChIP-seq data, and the data distribution of these TFBSs in different cell lines was shown in Fig. 7a. We downloaded “.bam” files of these TFBS ChIP-seq data and used MACS2 (v2.2.9.1)’s79 “callpeak” to locate TFBSs. We used the peak calling results as positive labels. These genome regions were assigned a value of 1, and the remaining regions were assigned a value of 0 as negative samples. The purpose of this design is to allow the model to predict the probability of whether a site is a TFBS. The ENCODE IDs of the TFBSs data used in this study were recorded in Supplementary Table S8.


### Sample preprocessing
  BioSeq2Seq was primarily trained in signal-enriched genomic regions (i.e., peaks) identified from RO-seq and the target datasets of downstream tasks. We set the sample length to 114k bp, and each sample spanned one or more peaks. If multiple peaks fell within the same 114k-bp window, they were treated as a single sample. Otherwise, a 114k-bp region centered on each peak was used as an individual sample. We regarded these peak-based samples as positive samples. Negative samples were uniformly generated from non-peak regions using a stride of 32 kb. For validation and testing, samples were uniformly sampled across the whole genome with a stride of 114 kb to ensure full-genome coverage without overlapping regions. If a sample exceeded the genome boundary, it was replaced with one starting from the boundary position.

  Each sample consists of three components: RO-seq data, DNA sequence data, and ground truth for downstream tasks. The RO-seq data included the positive strand, the absolute value of the negative strand, and their summation. Each positive sample was matched to its corresponding RO-seq data. For instance, samples from the G1 peak dataset were paired with the G1 RO-seq signal. For samples from downstream target peaks, the appropriate RO-seq data was determined based on the overlap with RO-seq peaks from samples G1–G7. Specifically, we calculated the overlap between each target peak and nearby RO-seq peaks, and assigned the RO-seq type with the greatest overlap to the sample. If a positive or negative sample did not overlap with any peak of G1–G7, we used the G1 data by default. The DNA sequence was encoded using one-hot representation (A = [1, 0, 0, 0], C = [0, 1, 0, 0], G = [0, 0, 1, 0], T = [0, 0, 0, 1], N = [0, 0, 0, 0]). The ground truth data was provided at a 128-bp resolution, where the 114k-bp region was divided into 896 bins, and each value represented the mean signal within a 128-bp bin. To provide the model with sufficient contextual information surrounding each target site for accurate prediction, the input data was extended by 41k bp on both upstream and downstream sides, resulting in a total input length of 197k bp. Accordingly, the RO-seq data and DNA sequence have shapes of [196,608, 3] and [196,608, 4], respectively. The ground truth has a shape of [896, N], where N denotes the number of outputs for each task (see Supplementary Table S1).

  The choice between using normalized or raw RO-seq signals was determined based on comparative analyses (see Supplementary Method S5, Supplementary Figs. S23–S25 and Supplementary Table S10). Normalized signals were used for histone modification prediction, gene expression prediction, and TFBS prediction, whereas raw signals were used for functional element identification.


### Model architecture
  The basic BioSeq2Seq architecture consists of three modules: feature embedding module, attention module, and head module (Fig. 1a). A detailed description of the functionality and architectural design of each module is provided below.

  The feature embedding module consists of seven convolutional blocks, each comprising two 1-D convolutional layers, a batch normalization layer, a GELU activation function, and a max pooling layer that downsamples the width dimension by a factor of 2. This module accepts one-hot-encoded DNA sequences or RO-seq signals of length 196,608 as input. When both RO-seq and DNA sequence data are employed, two parallel feature embedding modules are utilized, one for each input modality. The output of the feature embedding module is a 1536-dimensional vector with C channels, where each position corresponds to a 128-bp genomic bin. The representations from the two parallel modules are integrated via a learnable projection layer into a combined output of size 1536 × C. To further enhance the representation, a relative positional encoding is incorporated into the fused features, following the Enformer formulation24.

  The attention module comprises 11 transformer blocks, each consisting of a layer normalization layer, a multi-head attention (MHA) layer with eight heads, a 0.05× dropout layer, and a multi-layer perceptron (MLP). The MLP includes a layer normalization layer, two 0.4× dropout layers, and two fully connected layers with 2C and C neurons, respectively. The MHA captures long-range interactions of distal transcriptional regulatory elements within the genome. ### Model training
  We trained the model on the training set, used the validation set to monitor performance and tune hyperparameters, and evaluated generalization on multiple holdout test sets. The model was trained using the MSE as the loss function80, which quantified the average squared difference between the predicted values and the ground truth. We used Adam as the optimizer, with an initial learning rate of either 1e-4 or 1e-5, depending on the downstream task. In each epoch, BioSeq2Seq randomly selected 5000 samples from the training set for model training. To mitigate overfitting, we employed strategies such as learning rate decay and gradient clipping to enhance model stability and generalization. Due to hardware limitations, the batch size for training all downstream task models in this study was set to 1. The BioSeq2Seq model was implemented using TensorFlow (v2.4.0) and trained on an NVIDIA Tesla V100 GPU (32G). For human whole-genome predictions, the runtime was approximately 2–2.5 h for histone modification prediction, functional element identification, and gene expression prediction, whereas TFBS prediction required about 8 h.

  To select the training strategy that yields the strongest cross-cell-line generalization, we systematically evaluated three alternative training strategies in histone modification prediction: (i) single-cell-line training, (ii) multi-cell-line training, and (iii) single-sample training. For the single-cell-line strategy, the model was trained using multiple RO-seq samples (G1–G7, see Supplementary Table S2) from the K562 cell line. The multi-cell-line model was trained using RO-seq data from K562, GM12878, and HeLa cell lines, whereas the single-sample model used only one K562 RO-seq sample (G1). All strategies were evaluated exclusively on cell lines not included in their respective training sets. The single-cell-line strategy achieved the best performance, outperforming the multi-cell-line and single-sample strategies by 1.22% (0.0070) and 3.92% (0.0231), respectively (Supplementary Fig. S17). This indicates that integrating multiple RO-seq samples from a single cell line allows the model to more accurately capture histone modification patterns, even across unseen cell lines.

  For histone modification prediction and gene expression, the initial learning rate was set to 1e-4 and reduced by one-third at each decay step until model convergence. For functional element identification, we first froze the parameters of the transcription-related branch and trained the model for 80 epochs using a learning rate of 1e-5. We then unfroze these parameters and continued training for 120 epochs. Subsequently, the learning rate was reduced by a factor of 10 every 10 epochs, and the model was further trained for 50 epochs. For TFBS prediction, we first froze the model branches for the Group 1 and 2 while training the TFBS of Group 3. We then gradually unfroze them until all TFBS training was completed. We used an initial learning rate of 1e-5. The learning rate was reduced by one-third at each decay step. During the training phase in which only Group 3 branches were unfrozen, the learning rate decayed every 30 epochs, and training lasted for a total of 120 epochs. In the phase where Group 2 and Group 3 branches were unfrozen, the learning rate decayed every 10 epochs, with a total of 120 epochs of training. In the final training phase, the learning rate was decayed every 2 epochs, and the model was trained for 60 epochs.

### Peak calling
  In the functional element identification, the BioSeq2Seq model outputs continuous signals. We developed a statistics-based peak calling approach to convert the continuous signals into discrete functional elements. The peak calling procedure could be divided into two stages: threshold segmentation and statistical testing.

  In the threshold segmentation stage, we used a threshold to divide the continuous signals into two categories: peak and background. Since the predicted TIR signal exhibited the strongest correlation with the ground truth, the segmentation threshold was determined based on its genome-wide distribution. We divided the TIR signal of the whole genome into multiple non-overlapping 128-bp regions and analyzed the distribution of predicted values across all regions. After logarithmic transformation, the noise distribution approximately followed a Gaussian distribution. We used the right percentile of the TIR’s Gaussian distribution (p value = 0.01) as the segmentation threshold. We then defined consecutive regions with values exceeding the threshold as candidate peaks, while treating the remaining regions as background.

  In the statistical testing stage, we used a five-dimensional multivariate Gaussian distribution to evaluate the likelihood that a candidate peak belongs to background noise. For each candidate peak, five representative values were extracted from its signal profile to form a feature vector. Specifically, we selected: (i) the peak maximum value; (ii) the minimum value located to the left of the peak maximum; (iii) the midpoint value between the left-side minimum and the peak maximum; (iv) the minimum value located to the right of the peak maximum; and (v) the midpoint value between the peak maximum and the right-side minimum. Since we were only interested in peaks located on the right tail of the distribution, we used the cumulative distribution function as the p value. We applied task-specific p value thresholds to distinguish candidate peaks from background noise, with the resulting peaks considered as predicted positives. For functional element identification, the threshold was set to 1e-3. BED files generated during this process were processed using Bedtools (v2.26.0). Further details are provided in Supplementary Method S3.

  In this peak calling algorithm, each label matched at most one predicted peak, while a single predicted peak could cover multiple labels. For peaks of functional elements like TIRs, which are relatively short, it is rare for one peak to cover multiple labels. However, the situation is different for the gene body, where there is a possibility that a predicted region could be too long and incorrectly classified as a positive. Therefore, we applied a valley-based segmentation method to further split gene body predictions and separate adjacent genes. Additionally, since the loss used during training is not correlated with accuracy, the model will not be biased towards producing longer peaks to reduce loss, which prevents exceptionally long peaks from inflating true positive counts.
