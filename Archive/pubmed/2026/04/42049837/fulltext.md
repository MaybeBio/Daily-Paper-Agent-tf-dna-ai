# Development of potential CDK9 inhibitors through pharmacophore-based virtual screening, 3D-QSAR, molecular docking, MD simulation, and in vitro anticancer evaluation


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50502-5.


## Introduction
  Cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) represent a highly conserved family of serine/threonine kinases, and their catalytic activity is precisely regulated by cyclin binding, jointly responsible for various fundamental cellular processes1. Based on function, CDKs are broadly divided into two types: i) cell-cycle regulators (CDK1, CDK2, CDK4, and CDK6) and ii) transcription-associated kinases (CDK7, CDK8, and CDK9). Abnormal regulation of cell-cycle CDKs is a well-established hallmark of tumorigenesis, and increasing evidence highlights transcriptional CDKs, particularly CDK9, as playing a crucial role in driving oncogenic transcriptional programs2,3. Acting as the catalytic core of the positive transcription elongation factor b (P-TEFb) complex, CDK9 phosphorylates the Ser2 residue within the heptapeptide repeats (YSPTSPS) of the RNA polymerase II carboxy-terminal domain, thereby releasing promoter-proximal paused polymerase into productive elongation. Abnormal activation of CDK9 sustains transcription of short-lived, anti-apoptotic proteins such as myeloid cell leukemia-1 (MCL-1), promoting malignant transformation and tumor cell survival. Consequently, pharmacological inhibition of CDK9 results in rapid MCL-1 depletion and suppression of tumor growth, establishing CDK9 as a compelling therapeutic target in cancer and other diseases, including HIV infection4–7. Early pan-CDK inhibitors, such as flavopiridol and dinaciclib, demonstrated potent CDK9 inhibition with robust antitumor activity but were hindered by limited selectivity and dose-limiting toxicities, complicating clinical translation (Fig. 1). These efforts yielded additional nonselective inhibitors, including AT7519, roniciclib, R547, and BMS-387032, some of which advanced into late-stage clinical evaluation. However, these limitations spurred the rational design of selective CDK9 inhibitors, which validated transcriptional repression as a viable therapeutic strategy8. It is worth highlighting that preclinical agents such as the amidopyridine-based AZ5576 induced rapid loss of RNAPII Ser2 phosphorylation, profound MCL-1 suppression, and caspase-dependent apoptosis in hematological malignancies, translating into significant antitumor efficacy in vivo (Fig. 2). Further optimization led to additional selective scaffolds, including LDC000067 and LY2857785, reinforcing CDK9 as a druggable transcriptional node. Importantly, the recent clinical progression of selective CDK9 inhibitors, such as NVP-2, BAY 1,143,572 and BAY 1,251,152, represents a major milestone in CDK9-targeted drug discovery9,10. Nevertheless, challenges related to selectivity, toxicity, and therapeutic window persist, underscoring the urgent need for continued discovery and development of next-generation CDK9 inhibitors with improved pharmacological profiles.Fig. 1Chemical structures of pan-CDK inhibitors.Fig. 2Chemical structures of CDK9 inhibitors.

  Recent advances in CDK9-targeted drug discovery demonstrate that integrating modern in silico methodologies with experimental validation markedly enhanced the efficient identification of potent and selective CDK9 inhibitors11–15. For example, Hirokawa et al. combined molecular dynamics simulations, cheminformatics-based screening, and structure-based docking to uncover compounds I and II (Fig. 3) that exploit a cryptic allosteric cavity encompassing the CCII and CCIII regions of CDK9; these ligands were experimentally validated and demonstrated additive inhibitory effects when co-administered with the ATP-competitive inhibitor BS-18116. Complementarily, large-scale docking followed by extended 500 ns molecular dynamics simulations of a natural product library identified FL_72 (III) and PH_435 (IV) as selective CDK9 inhibitors, supported by stable conformational dynamics, favourable binding free energies, and persistent interactions with key catalytic residues17. In a data-driven approach, Dong et al. employed a hybrid virtual screening strategy integrating machine learning based prediction with structure-based docking, leading to the discovery of a novel CDK9 inhibitor for acute myeloid leukaemia, exemplified by compound V with nanomolar potency (IC50 = 31.28 nM) and robust antiproliferative activity in hematologic tumor cell lines18. Additional structure-based workflows revealed that ligand 006 (VI) undergoes adaptive conformational rearrangement within the CDK9 binding pocket, enabling sustained hydrogen-bonding interactions with the catalytic Asp109 residue19. Likewise, computer-assisted drug design approaches combining predictive QSAR modelling along with synthesis identified compounds T1 (VII) and T2 (VIII) as promising CDK9 inhibitors, exhibiting enhanced binding affinity, dynamic stability, and favorable ADMET profiles relative to reference ligands20. Integrated multiscale in silico pipelines encompassing virtual screening, molecular docking, molecular dynamic simulations, 3D-QSAR modelling, MM/PBSA free energy calculations, and ADME assessment further identified compound IX with 71% inhibitory activity against CDK921. Finally, a comprehensive computational framework incorporating 3D-QSAR, fingerprint analysis, docking, molecular dynamic simulations, MMGB/PBSA calculations, and ADMET evaluation highlighted compound D98 (X) as a high-affinity CDK9 inhibitor stabilized by critical interactions with Ala153 and possessing a pharmacokinetic profile indicative of strong in vivo translational potential22. Taken together, these studies establish pharmacophore-based and 3D-QSAR driven strategies, particularly when coupled with molecular docking and rigorous free energy analyses, as indispensable tools for the rational design of next-generation CDK9 inhibitors. Given this background, we developed a new series of structurally novel CDK9 inhibitors employing an integrated computational framework encompassing atom-based 3D-QSAR analysis, pharmacophore-based virtual screening, molecular docking, prime MM/GBSA binding free energy calculation, MD simulation and in silico ADME profiling. The top-ranked hits were identified, and the emerging from this workflow were successfully synthesized and experimentally evaluated against a panel of human cancer cell lines to validate their antiproliferative efficacy and translational potential.Fig. 3Recently reported CDK9 inhibitors via in silico techniques.


## Materials and methods

### Dataset
  To generate a statistically robust pharmacophore model, a dataset of 55 structurally diverse small-molecule CDK9 inhibitors was compiled from the literature, encompassing a wide activity range with pIC50 values between 7.06 and 10.4023–25. The dataset was rationally divided into training and test sets based on activity distribution and key physicochemical attributes to ensure balanced representation of chemical space. The training set was employed for pharmacophore hypothesis generation and 3D-QSAR model construction, whereas the test set was reserved exclusively for internal validation. Compounds with pIC50 > 8 were designated as active, while those with pIC50 ≤ 8 were classified as inactive to enable stringent feature discrimination. The pIC50 values, binding energies, and pharmacophore fitness scores of all compounds are summarized in Tables S1-S4 (Supplementary Information).


### Ligand preparation
  Accurate three-dimensional ligand representations are essential for reliable pharmacophore model development. Owing to the conformational flexibility of the ligands, extensive sampling of thermodynamically accessible low-energy conformers was performed to capture potential bioactive geometries. All compounds were initially sketched in the Maestro build panel, converted to 3D structures, and prepared using the LigPrep module26. The resulting low-energy conformers, together with their activity annotations, were imported into the PHASE workflow. Ligands were classified as active or inactive using predefined activity thresholds to enable statistically meaningful pharmacophore hypothesis generation.


### Pharmacophore model generation
  Ligand pharmacophores are defined as spatial arrangements of key interaction features that mediate noncovalent recognition by the target receptor. To create pharmacophore hypotheses, PHASE utilizes six basic pharmacophoric features: hydrogen-bond donor (D), hydrogen-bond acceptor (A), hydrophobic group (H), aromatic ring (R), negative (N), and positive (P) ionizable centers27. Potential pharmacophoric sites are identified based on shared structural and interaction patterns across the ligand dataset and are mapped onto three-dimensional geometries. Ligand conformations exhibiting identical feature compositions and spatial arrangements are clustered into common pharmacophore models using a tree-based partitioning approach that evaluates inter-feature distances and geometric similarity28.


### Scoring hypotheses
  Pharmacophore scoring enables the identification of the most predictive hypothesis by quantitatively assessing how effectively active ligands map onto key interaction features. The generated hypotheses provide insight into the binding characteristics of active ligands at the receptor interface. PHASE scoring incorporates contributions from site-point and vector alignment, volumetric overlap, selectivity, number of matched ligands, and correlation with biological activity. The best pharmacophore hypothesis was identified based on a combined score analysis and the mapping fidelity of active compounds.


### Building a 3D-QSAR model
  The 3D-QSAR models in PHASE were developed using the biological activity of ligands mapped to the selected pharmacophore hypothesis. Atom-based 3D-QSAR modelling was performed using PLS regression with a binary spatial descriptors, and model quality was assessed based on the alignment consistency of the training set ligands. Using the best five-point hypothesis, 3D-QSAR models for CDK9 inhibitors were constructed from 43 training set compounds with a grid spacing of 1.0 Å. Models incorporating one to three PLS factors were constructed and validated using both training and test sets.


### Virtual screening
  Virtual screening was employed to identify potential CDK9 inhibitors from large chemical databases. The best atom-based 3D-QSAR pharmacophore models were used as three-dimensional queries for screening, requiring a minimum match of four out of five pharmacophoric features. Application of the optimized ADHRR pharmacophore in PHASE enabled the identification of pharmacophore-matched compounds from the Zinc database comprising 11,719 molecules with no reported CDK9 inhibitory activity29.


### Molecular docking and binding free energy calculations
  Molecular docking was performed to investigate the binding modes and interaction profiles of ligands within the CDK9 active site using the glide module of the Schrodinger software. The crystal structure of CDK9 (PDB ID: 4BCF) was retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank and prepared using the Protein Preparation Wizard in Maestro, followed by restrained minimization with the OPLS-2005 force field. Receptor grids were generated in Glide using the co-crystallized ligand to define the binding site, with a grid size of 10 Å × 10 Å × 10 Å and van der Waals scaling of 0.9. Low-energy ligand conformers were docked using the XP protocol. Binding free energies of docked complexes were further evaluated using MM/GBSA calculations implemented in Prime30.

### In silico ADME prediction
  ADME and drug-likeness properties are key determinants of lead viability and are among the major contributors to late-stage drug attrition31. In this study, in silico ADME profiling of hit compounds and newly designed molecules was performed using the QikProp module. Predicted parameters included octanol/water partition coefficient (QP logPo/w), aqueous solubility (QP log S), polar surface area (PSA), hydrogen-bond donor and acceptor counts, human serum albumin binding (QP log Khsa), Caco-2 cell permeability (QPP Caco), human oral absorption (%HOA), and blood–brain barrier partition coefficient (QP log BB) were computed.


### MD simulations
  The docked complexes of the ligands D3 and D6 were used to determine the stability of binding by subjecting the complexes to molecular dynamics for a duration of 100 ns. The TPI3P solvent model was used to generate the boundary conditions of dimensions 10 Å3. Using an NPT ensemble class, the temperature was maintained at 310 K, and the constant pressure was 1.013 bar. The constant temperature and pressure for the entire simulation were maintained via the Nose–Hoover chain thermostat and Martyna-Tobias-Klein barostat respectively. Post MD simulations, the simulation interaction diagram was used to interpret the RMSD of the protein–ligand, followed by protein–ligand contacts, thereby evaluating the stability of the protein–ligand complex.


## Results and discussion

### Determination of pharmacophore model
  Three five-feature pharmacophore variants i.e. ADHRR, AHRRR, and DHRRR comprising hydrogen-bond acceptor (A), hydrogen-bond donor (D), hydrophobic (H), and aromatic ring (R) features, were generated during common pharmacophore identification. Systematic evaluation of these variants resulted in the generation of 28 unique five-point pharmacophore hypotheses. The hypotheses were then ranked using PHASE survival scores, incorporating contributions from active and inactive ligand mapping, vector alignment, and volumetric overlap. Based on comprehensive score analysis and mapping consistency of active ligands, the five-point ADHRR pharmacophore hypothesis was selected for further investigation. The three-dimensional geometry of the optimized ADHRR model is illustrated in Fig. 4, wherein spheres A correspond to the hydrogen-bond acceptor feature, H indicates the hydrophobic feature, D denotes the hydrogen-bond donor feature, and R represents aromatic ring interaction sites.Fig. 4Obtained pharmacophore model ADHRR and representation with potent molecule 23.


### Validation of 3D-QSAR model
  Atom-based 3D-QSAR models were developed for the top three pharmacophore hypotheses using partial least squares (PLS) regression analysis. In this approach, experimentally determined pIC50 values were employed as dependent variables, while the PHASE-derived atom-based descriptors and corresponding PLS factors served as independent variables. A comprehensive statistical summary of the resulting 3D-QSAR models for CDK9 inhibitors, based on both training and test set compounds, is presented in Table 1. Among the evaluated hypotheses, the ADHRR based 3D-QSAR model exhibited superior statistical performance, characterized by a high coefficient of determination (R2 = 0.96), low standard deviation (SD = 0.14), a strong variance ratio (F = 357.8), robust internal predictivity (Q2 = 0.84), low root-mean-square error (RMSE = 0.21), and a favorable Pearson correlation coefficient (R = 0.92). These statistical indices demonstrate the high predictive accuracy, robustness, and reliability of the developed model, underscoring its suitability for prospective lead optimization and rational design efforts. ### 3D-QSAR visualization of the best active compound
  Atom-based 3D-QSAR models emphasize steric compatibility between ligands and the receptor, whereas pharmacophore-based 3D-QSAR models predict activity based on the spatial distribution of key pharmacophoric features. Visualization of 3D-QSAR contour maps aids in elucidating structure–activity relationships. Figure 6 depicts the 3D-QSAR contour visualization of the most potent compound, molecule 23 (Expt. pIC50 = 10.40). In Fig. 6a, blue contour cubes indicate regions favourable for hydrogen-bond donor interactions, whereas red cubes denote favourable hydrogen-bond acceptor regions, indicating contributions from the NH group as well as acceptor features associated with the pyridine and pyrimidine rings. In Fig. 6b, blue cubes represent hydrophobic-favored regions, while red cubes denote hydrophobic-disfavored areas. Favourable hydrophobic contributions were observed in proximity to the aromatic benzene ring, and piperazine moieties, indicating that enhanced hydrophobic interactions at these positions are beneficial for CDK9 inhibitory activity. In conjunction, these observations suggest that the presence of additional hydrogen-bond donor, hydrogen-bond acceptor, and hydrophobic features beyond the core pharmacophore significantly enhances ligand potency, providing valuable guidance for further lead optimization.Fig. 63D-QSAR model visualization of the best active compound 23, illustrating the effect of (a) H-bond donor and H-bond acceptor features and (b) hydrophobic features.


### Pharmacophore-based virtual screening
  Virtual screening represents a cornerstone of modern medicinal chemistry, enabling the efficient identification of chemically tractable lead candidates from large molecular libraries. In this study, a pharmacophore-based virtual screening strategy was employed to identify potential CDK9 inhibitors from the Zinc database comprising 11,719 compounds. A validated ADHRR pharmacophore model was used as the primary screening filter. Application of this model resulted in the identification of 1,641 compounds that fully satisfied all five pharmacophoric constraints (Fig. 7). The strict feature-mapping criteria ensured high selectivity toward ligands with optimal spatial and functional complementarity to the CDK9 active site. The resulting hit set is chemically diverse and enriched with scaffolds possessing a high likelihood of CDK9 inhibitory activity, thereby providing a robust foundation for further structure-based refinement and experimental validation.Fig. 7Alignment of resulted hits (grey in color) and most potent compound 23 (green in color).


### Molecular docking-based virtual screening
  Molecular docking studies were subsequently conducted on the pharmacophore-derived hit set to evaluate their binding modes and affinities within the CDK9 (PDB ID: 4BCF) active site. Docking simulations were performed using a hierarchical protocol comprising Standard Precision (SP) followed by Extra Precision (XP) docking. Initially, all 1,641 hits obtained from PHASE screening were subjected to SP docking, from which the top-ranked 2% of compounds (35 molecules) exhibiting favourable docking scores and interaction profiles were advanced to XP docking. This successive filtering strategy enabled stringent discrimination of high-affinity binders. From the XP docking results, 10 compounds displaying optimal binding orientations and superior docking scores were selected for binding free energy estimation using Prime MM/GBSA calculations. Figure 8 schematically illustrates the entire virtual screening and selection workflow.Fig. 8Workflow chart for identification of hit molecules.


### Interaction studies of screened hits
  Ligand-interaction diagram (LID) analysis was performed using the Schrödinger suite to elucidate the key binding interactions of the screened hits within the CDK9 active site. At first, the most potent compound 23 exhibited a well-defined hydrogen-bonding network comprising two interactions, both involving the hinge-region residue Cys106: (i) a hydrogen bond between the pyrimidine ring nitrogen and the backbone of Cys106, and (ii) a hydrogen bond between the pyrimidine –NH group and Cys106, highlighting the importance of the Cys106 residue for stabilization within the CDK9 binding pocket (Fig. 9). Similarly, the top-ranking hits (C1–C10), selected on the basis of favorable MM/GBSA binding free energies, consistently formed one or more hydrogen-bonding interactions with key active-site residues (Fig. 10). Compound C2, which exhibited a binding free energy of − 81.72 kcal/mol, formed multiple hydrogen-bonding interactions, including a hydrogen bond between the pyrimidine ring nitrogen and Cys106, as well as hydrogen bonds involving the amine –NH of the pyrimidine and the pyridine ring nitrogen with Cys106 and Lys48, respectively. In addition, a prominent π–π stacking interaction with Phe103 was observed. Similarly, compound C6 displayed a binding mode comparable to that of compound 23, characterized by two hydrogen-bonding interactions with Cys106, thereby underscoring the critical role of this residue as an anchoring hotspot for high-affinity CDK9 inhibition.Fig. 9Ligand interaction diagrams (2D & 3D) of the most potent compound 23.Fig. 10Ligand interaction diagrams (2D & 3D) of the top ranking hit molecules C2 (a) and C6 (b).

## Designing of new indole-based biphenyl amide hybrids (IBA’s)
  Virtual screening of the Zinc database identified multiple hit compounds exhibiting favourable binding free energies and well-defined interaction patterns within the CDK9 catalytic domain. Among these, compounds C2, C5, and C6 displayed superior binding affinities and key molecular interactions comparable to those of the reference inhibitor 23, indicating a high degree of structural and energetic similarity in CDK9 engagement. Of note, ten hit compounds demonstrated more favourable binding free energy values than the reference compound (> − 68.39 kcal/mol), suggesting enhanced stabilization of the ligand–protein complexes. Interaction analysis revealed that all prioritized hits (C1–C10) formed two to three conserved hydrogen bonds with residues lining the ATP-binding pocket, interactions that are critical for anchoring the ligands within the hinge region and for effective CDK9 inhibition. Pharmacophore elucidation from these active hits yielded a five-feature model (ADHRR), defining the essential molecular determinants for CDK9 binding. A notable structural convergence among the resulting hits was the presence of a pyrrole or fused pyrrole motif, or pyrrole-derived heterocycles often integrated with a hydrophobic benzene ring, underscoring the importance of this heteroaromatic framework in mediating π–π stacking and hydrophobic interactions within the CDK9 active site. These observations highlight the role of fused heteroaromatic systems in achieving optimal complementarity with the kinase binding pocket. Guided by these pharmacophoric and structural insights, three series of novel indole-based derivatives (300) were rationally designed. The indole scaffold, as a fused pyrrole-benzene system, intrinsically satisfies the identified pharmacophore requirements while offering structural versatility for fine-tuning steric and electronic properties. Accordingly, these newly designed indole derivatives are expected to establish enhanced intermolecular interactions and improved binding free energies, thereby representing promising lead candidates for further optimization as CDK9 inhibitors. A total of 300 newly designed indole-based biphenyl amide hybrids (IBAs) were screened against the validated ADHRR pharmacophore model, resulting in the identification of 32 compounds that fully satisfied all five essential pharmacophoric features (Fig. 11).Fig. 11Alignment of IBA’s in the ADHRR.

### In silico ADME properties of IBA’s
  The predicted ADME parameters of the IBA’s are summarized in Table 5. The IBA’s largely conform to Lipinski’s rule of five, with acceptable parameters and minor deviations in lipophilicity (QP log Po/w < 5). Predicted PSA values (48.3–94.8 Å) fall within the optimal range for oral absorption. Aqueous solubility (QP log S < 0.5) supports favorable intestinal permeability. Blood–brain barrier penetration (QP log BB, − 1.4 to 0) remained within acceptable limits. Human serum albumin binding and Caco-2 permeability predictions were satisfactory.   The molecular dynamic simulations were performed for the protein–ligand complexes i.e. compounds D3 and D6 with CDK9 (4BCF) for a period of 100 ns to analyze their stability. This deviation in the RMSD plot may be attributed to the consistent π-π interactions with Phe30 up to 75 ns which later decreased until the end with an overall contribution of 64%. Additionally, Cys106 contributed moderately via H-bonding with an overall contribution of 41%. There were a few residues such as Ile25, Ala46, Phe103, Ala153 and Leu156 that partially interacted (< 15%) via water-mediated H-bonding. This stability may be attributed to the consistent interaction of the D6 ligand with Phe30 (50% via π-π interactions), Cys106 (97% via H-bonding), and Asp167 (85% via H-bonding). The RSMD and RMSF analysis of both D3 and D6 complexes indicated minimal fluctuations in the active site residues, corroborating the stable ligand binding within the binding pocket. ## Conclusion
  In conclusion, a comprehensive pharmacophore-based virtual screening strategy was employed to identify novel and potent CDK9 inhibitors. The optimized five-point pharmacophore model, ADHRR, was developed using a curated dataset of 55 structurally diverse CDK9 inhibitors and systematically applied to screen the Zinc database. The robustness and predictive reliability of the generated hypothesis were validated using standard statistical protocols, yielding a high correlation coefficient (R2 = 0.98). Pharmacophore-matched hits were further subjected to molecular docking within the CDK9 active site, followed by Prime MM/GBSA binding free energy calculations to refine hit selection. From an initial pool of screened hits, ten compounds (C1–C10) exhibiting favourable pharmacophoric alignment and binding profiles were identified as potential CDK9 inhibitors. Guided by insights derived from the optimized pharmacophore hypothesis, a focused library of indole-based biphenyl amide hybrids (IBA’s) was rationally designed and evaluated through ADHRR, molecular docking and binding free energy analysis. Among these, seven compounds (D1–D7) demonstrated significantly improved binding free energies (− 70.20 to − 94.05 kcal/mol) compared with the reference active compound 23 (− 68.39 kcal/mol). It is evident that most of the newly designed compounds established key hydrogen-bond interactions with Cys106, a critical residue implicated in CDK9 inhibition. MD simulation results supported the docking findings, indicating that the lead compounds D3 and D6 form stable complexes with the target protein. Additionally, DFT analysis supported the electronic stability and favourable reactivity profiles of the IBA’s. The resulted IBA’s (D1–D7) were synthesized and in vitro antiproliferative evaluation revealed that compounds D3 and D6 exhibited pronounced cytotoxicity against malignant cell lines while displaying minimal toxicity toward normal human embryonic kidney cells (HEK-293t), indicating a favourable therapeutic index. In silico ADME profiling further suggests that all the resulting compounds possess promising oral bioavailability and acceptable pharmacokinetic characteristics. Taken together, the integrated computational experimental workflow combining pharmacophore-based virtual screening, molecular docking, MM/GBSA free energy calculations, ADME prediction, and DFT analysis demonstrates a robust and effective strategy for the discovery of novel CDK9 inhibitors. The lead compounds D3 and D6 emerge as particularly promising candidates and warrant further biochemical validation using in vitro CDK9 assays and advanced mechanistic studies.
