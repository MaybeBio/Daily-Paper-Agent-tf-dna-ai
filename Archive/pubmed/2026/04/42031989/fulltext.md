# A novel behavioral paradigm for odor-induced voluntary urination in mice


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-49754-y.


## Introduction
  The lower urinary tract serves two critical functions: urine storage and periodic voiding1. These functions rely on a complex neural control network that coordinates sensory input, central processing, and motor output, which is distributed across many parts of the central and peripheral nervous systems2. Disruption of this intricate control system—commonly caused by conditions such as stroke, spinal cord injury, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson’s disease, or Alzheimer’s disease—results in neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction (NLUTD)3. Hallmark symptoms of NLUTD include urgency, frequency, hesitancy, incontinence, nocturia, and urinary retention3, all of which markedly impair patients’ quality of life4,5. NLUTD imposes considerable healthcare costs and therapeutic challenges6. Unfortunately, our current understanding of NLUTD remains limited. Gaining deeper insights into how the central nervous system regulates the switch between urine storage and voiding is essential for elucidating the pathophysiology of NLUTD and developing targeted therapeutic strategies.

  Urination can be divided into reflexive and voluntary categories, each governed by distinct neural pathways1. Reflexive urination is an unconscious, automatic reflex process that prevents bladder overdistension, mediated by spinal and brainstem circuits7,8. The neural control mechanisms underlying reflexive urination have been extensively studied in anesthetized or decerebrate rodent models using techniques such as electrophysiological recordings, stimulation of brainstem nuclei, and c-Fos staining9,10. By contrast, voluntary urination involves conscious decision-making that enables initiation or delay of urination through higher-order forebrain regions integrating timing, environmental cues, and internal states11. Functional brain imaging studies in humans have identified key forebrain regions activated during voluntary voiding, including the prefrontal cortex, insular cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, and basal ganglia12–14. However, investigating the cellular and circuit-level mechanisms by which these regions regulate voluntary urination in animal models poses a significant challenge. A major obstacle is determining whether non-verbal animals are engaging in voluntary or reflexive urination.

  Urine scent-marking is a common social communication behavior observed in adult male mice15,16. When exposed to urine of estrous-phase females or to competing scent marks from other males, male mice perform intense scent-marking behavior17. Specifically, they deliberately deposit a large number of urinary scent marks in their territory to attract females or defend their space from intruders15,18,19. This innate capacity allows male mice to voluntarily choose when and where to release urine, thereby offering a promising naturalistic approach for assessing voluntary urination behavior. Previous studies have reported that individually housed male mice are more likely to exhibit scent-marking behavior and have been used as experimental subjects to study voluntary urination15,18,19. However, housing mice in social isolation requires more cage space and may introduce stress-related confounds20,21. Moreover, scent-marking behavior is susceptible to various environmental cues and internal states22,23. These considerations underscore the need for standardized paradigms that minimize environmental variability while preserving biological relevance.

  Strain background is a critical factor influencing scent-marking behaviors. Both BALB/c and C57BL/6J strains are widely used in neurobehavioral research. Although BALB/c males are known to display vigorous and stable urine scent-marking15,24,25, the limited availability of genetic tools on this background restricts its use for cell-type-specific mechanistic dissection26,27. Conversely, C57BL/6J mice are often preferred because of their well-defined genetic background and the wealth of available transgenic lines26,28, yet males of this strain typically exhibit weak urine scent-marking in short-duration paradigms and often require prolonged social isolation to elicit measurable responses18,29. To combine these complementary strengths, we utilized F1 hybrid males (C57BL/6J × BALB/c) as a strategy to integrate the robust marking phenotype of BALB/c with the genetic accessibility of C57BL/6J30,31. Therefore, in this study, we aim to establish a novel, simple, and highly reproducible paradigm for evaluating voluntary urination in F1 hybrid male mice.


## Results

### Hormone-based induction of estrus for efficient urine collection in ovariectomized (OVX) female mice
  Urine samples from estrous-phase females trigger olfactory-mediated scent marking behavior in male mice32. However, the transient nature of natural estrus (< 24 h) within the estrous cycle33, makes large-scale collection of stimulus urine samples labor-intensive. To enable efficient and controllable collection of estrus-phase urine samples, we established a hormone-based estrus induction protocol using ovariectomized (OVX) female mice of two different strains (Fig. 1a).

  In both C57BL/6J and BALB/c females, vehicle (corn oil) treatment resulted in persistent diestrous vaginal cytology throughout the experimental period, characterized by leukocyte predominance (Fig. 1b, c, top panels). In contrast, hormone treatment induced a consistent estrus-like cytological transition in both strains: vaginal smears shifted from leukocyte-dominant to anucleated, cornified epithelial cell-dominant profiles by day 2 post-treatment, indicative of successful estrus induction (Fig. 1b, c, bottom panels). Notably, the temporal dynamics and cytological characteristics were consistent between strains, with all hormone-treated OVX females exhibiting reliable estrus-like cytology by day 2. These results demonstrate that OVX females treated with hormone (estrogen and progesterone) reliably exhibited estrus-like cytology, allowing effective collection of estrus-phase urine for subsequent experiments.

### Differential scent-marking responses across mouse strains in a short-term assay
  Previous studies have shown that urinary scent-marking behavior varies substantially depending on experimental context, assay duration, and animal genetic background18,24,34. To test whether odor-evoked scent marking could be reliably captured within a short-term behavioral assay lasting ten minutes, we applied this paradigm to commonly used inbred male mouse strains (Fig. 2a–b). We first determined whether the genetic background of urine donors influences recipient marking behavior. Group-housed (GH) and socially-isolated (SI) BALB/c males were exposed to estrous urine from either OVX BALB/c or OVX C57BL/6J females (Supplementary Fig. 1). No significant differences were observed in urine spot number (GH: P = 0.80; Supplementary Fig. 1a–b; SI: P = 0.44; Supplementary Fig. 1d–e) or total urine-marked area across donor strains for either housing condition (GH: P = 0.22; Supplementary Fig. 1c; SI: P = 0.55; Supplementary Fig. 1f). These results indicate that the strain origin of estrous female urine does not significantly affect odor-induced voluntary scent-marking behavior across males of either genetic background. Thus, OVX C57BL/6J estrous urine was used as the standardized stimulus in subsequent experiments to ensure experimental consistency.

  In BALB/c males, OVX C57BL/6J estrous urine significantly increased both urine scent-marking number (white areas under ultraviolet (UV) illumination) and total urine-marked area compared with saline, regardless of housing condition (GH: both P < 0.001; SI: both P < 0.001; Fig. 2c–e). Notably, SI BALB/c males showed higher levels of baseline and stimulated marking than their GH counterparts (spot number: saline P < 0.001, estrous urine P < 0.001; area: saline P < 0.001, urine P < 0.01; Fig. 2c–e). By contrast, C57BL/6J males exhibited negligible marking responses within the 10-min assay. Neither GH nor SI C57BL/6J males showed significant increases in spot number or marked area following estrous urine exposure (GH: both P = 0.25; SI: both P = 0.06; Fig. 2f–h). For C57BL/6J males, no significant differences were observed between housing conditions under saline (spot number: P > 0.99; area: P > 0.99) or estrous urine stimulation (spot number: P = 0.32; area: P = 0.41; Fig. 2f–h).

  Finally, we examined F1 hybrid males (C57BL/6J × BALB/c) using standardized estrous urine. Estrous urine elicited reliable scent-marking in both GH and SI F1 males (Fig. 2i–k), with significant increases in spot number (GH: P < 0.001; SI: P < 0.001) and marked area (GH: P < 0.001; SI: P < 0.001). Similar to BALB/c males, SI F1 hybrids displayed higher marking levels than GH animals under both saline and estrous urine conditions (spot number: saline P < 0.01, urine P < 0.001; area: saline P < 0.01, urine P < 0.001; Fig. 2j–k). Taken together, these findings indicate that odor-induced urinary scent-marking behavior can be reliably detected within our 10-minute assay in BALB/c and F1 hybrid males, whereas C57BL/6J males do not display detectable odor-induced scent-marking under the same experimental conditions.

## Strain differences in odor-evoked urinary scent marking under distinct housing conditions
  To compare strain-dependent scent-marking responses, we analyzed BALB/c, C57BL/6J, and F1 hybrid males under GH and SI conditions (Fig. 3a–f). Under GH conditions, BALB/c males produced significantly more urine spots than C57BL/6J males (P < 0.001), whereas F1 hybrid males exhibited intermediate spot numbers, significantly lower than BALB/c (P < 0.05) but higher than C57BL/6J (P < 0.01; Fig. 3b). Total urine-marked area showed a similar pattern (Fig. 3c): BALB/c males marked larger areas than both C57BL/6J (P < 0.001) and F1 hybrid males (P < 0.05), with F1 hybrids also surpassing C57BL/6J males (P < 0.05; Fig. 3c). Under SI conditions, strain differences in spot number were more pronounced (Fig. 3d–e): BALB/c males exceeded both C57BL/6J (P < 0.001) and F1 hybrid males (P < 0.05), with SI BALB/c males producing significantly more spots than their GH counterparts (86.8 ± 5.7 vs. 46.8 ± 6.9, P < 0.05), consistent with the housing effect observed in Fig. 2d. F1 hybrids remained significantly higher than C57BL/6J males (P < 0.01; Fig. 3e). Total marked area followed comparable statistical patterns (BALB/c > F1 > C57BL/6J; Fig. 3f). Overall, these findings demonstrate that F1 hybrid males retain the odor-evoked scent-marking phenotype of the BALB/c parent while maintaining the C57BL/6J genetic background, thereby providing an optimal model for mechanistic dissection of voluntary urination.

### Different potencies of three urine samples in inducing voluntary urination in F1 hybrid mice
  To determine whether estrous urine from hormonally primed OVX females (OVX-E), natural estrous urine from intact females (Nat-E), and diestrus urine differ in their ability to elicit voluntary urination in F1 hybrid male mice, we implemented a modified 10-minute assay derived from the short-term scent-marking paradigm described above (Fig. 4a). Representative UV images showed robust and comparable scent-marking in response to both OVX-E and Nat-E stimuli, whereas minimal marking followed diestrus urine exposure in both GH and SI F1 hybrid males (Fig. 4b, c). In GH F1 hybrid males, urine spot number was significantly increased following exposure to OVX-E or Nat-E stimuli compared with diestrus urine (Fig. 4d, left; OVX-E vs. diestrus, P < 0.001; Nat-E vs. diestrus, P < 0.05), whereas no significant difference was detected between OVX-E and Nat-E (P = 0.54, ns). Consistently, total urine marked area was significantly greater under OVX-E or Nat-E than under diestrus urine (Fig. 4d, right; OVX-E vs. diestrus, P < 0.001; Nat-E vs. diestrus, P < 0.05), with no significant difference between the two estrous stimuli (P > 0.99, ns; Fig. 4d). In SI F1 hybrid males, urine spot number was likewise significantly elevated in response to both OVX-E and Nat-E compared with diestrus urine (Fig. 4e, left; OVX-E vs. diestrus, P < 0.01; Nat-E vs. diestrus, P < 0.05), with no significant difference between the two estrous conditions (P > 0.99, ns; Fig. 4e). Notably, total urine-marked area in SI males did not differ significantly among the three conditions (Fig. 4e, right; all pairwise comparisons, ns). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that estrous urine—whether from hormone-primed OVX or naturally cycling intact females—reliably and equivalently evokes voluntary urination in F1 hybrid males. This validates the OVX-hormone induction protocol as a standardized and practical alternative to natural estrous urine collection, enabling consistent large-scale experiments without the temporal constraints and variability of the natural estrous cycle.

### Automated detection of urinary scent marks using a YOLOv8-based deep learning framework
  To facilitate high-throughput and automated quantification of urinary scent marks following voluntary urination behavior, we developed a deep learning-based image analysis framework using the You Only Look Once, version 8 (YOLOv8) architecture (Fig. 5a–b). The model was trained on annotated ultraviolet images of urinary scent marks on filter paper. The trained YOLOv8-based detector processed each image in an average of 16.5 ms, representing a substantial increase in throughput compared to manual annotation. Quantitative analyses revealed close agreement between model-generated and manually annotated measurements for both scent mark number and total marked area, with no significant differences between automated and manual quantification (Fig. 5c–e). These results validate the analytical accuracy and reliability of the YOLOv8-based framework and demonstrate that it provides a scalable, objective, and high-throughput approach for quantifying urinary scent marks in short-term behavioral assays. By minimizing experimenter bias and improving analytical consistency, this automated system enables more accurate and reproducible assessment of urinary scent marks on filter papers obtained from voluntary urination behavior in mice.

### Whole-brain c-Fos mapping associated with estrous urine-evoked voluntary urination in F1 hybrid males
  To assess neural activity associated with odor-evoked voluntary urination, whole-brain c-Fos mapping was performed in GH F1 hybrid male mice following exposure to saline (control group, n = 6) or estrous urine samples (marking group, n = 6) (Fig. 6a). Mice exposed to estrous urine exhibited significantly higher urine mark counts than saline-exposed controls during the 10-min behavioral assay (saline: 2.7 ± 0.4; estrous urine: 15.2 ± 2.2; P < 0.01; Fig. 6b), with representative UV images confirming robust scent-marking (Fig. 6b). Regional c-Fos⁺ cell counts were quantified in 17 brain regions and comparisons between groups were performed using Mann–Whitney U tests with false discovery rate (FDR) correction (the two-stage step-up Benjamini–Krieger–Yekutieli procedure, FDR = 0.05). FDR-corrected analysis revealed significantly elevated c-Fos expression in the marking group across multiple cortical and subcortical regions (Fig. 6c).

  In cortical regions, the marking group showed higher c-Fos⁺ counts in prefrontal cortical regions, including the prelimbic cortex (PrL; P < 0.01), infralimbic cortex (IL; P < 0.01), and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC; P < 0.05) (Fig. 6d–e, i–k), as well as olfactory processing areas such as the anterior piriform cortex (APC; P < 0.05) and posterior piriform cortex (PPC; P < 0.01) (Supplementary Fig. 2a, e–f). Among subcortical regions, increased c-Fos expression was also observed in the supragenual nucleus (SuG; Supplementary Fig. 2a, i; P < 0.01), medial preoptic area (MPA; a key node for micturition control; Fig. 6f, l; P < 0.01), and dopaminergic/motivational circuits including the ventral tegmental area (VTA; P < 0.01; Supplementary Fig. 2a, h) and medial septum (MS; Fig. 6e; P < 0.05; Supplementary Fig. 2j). At the brainstem level, the pontine micturition center (PMC; P < 0.001; Fig. 6h, n) showed elevated activation, consistent with its established role in bladder–sphincter coordination. No significant differences were detected in the periaqueductal gray (PAG; Fig. 6g, m), accessory olfactory bulb (AOB), medial amygdala (MeA), lateral septum (LS), motor cortex (MO), anterior olfactory nucleus (AOD), or median preoptic nucleus (MnPO) after correction for multiple comparisons (Supplementary Fig. 2a–d, g, k–l). These results identify a distributed network encompassing prefrontal cortical control, olfactory processing, and pontine micturition centers as neural substrates underlying odor-evoked voluntary urination in F1 hybrid males.

## Discussion
  Reflexive urination is an innate physiological process that occurs involuntarily at birth7, whereas voluntary urination emerges through postnatal development and learning7,22. In humans, voluntary control over urination is gradually acquired and relies on higher-order neural processes1,35. Despite its importance, progress in understanding voluntary urination has been limited by the lack of reliable and experimentally tractable animal models. In this study, we developed a short-term, odor-induced behavioral paradigm using estrus-phase urine collected from hormonally primed ovariectomized females to evaluate voluntary urination behavior in male mice. Using this assay, F1 hybrid males derived from C57BL/6J × BALB/c strains exhibited reliable and reproducible voluntary scent-marking behavior under both group-housed and socially isolated conditions. In parallel, we implemented a YOLOv8-based automated image analysis pipeline to enable objective and high-throughput quantification of urinary scent marks. Furthermore, we performed whole-brain c-Fos immunostaining following odor-induced voluntary urination in GH F1 hybrid males to explore potential neural correlates associated with this behavior. Together, these findings establish a novel and scalable behavioral paradigm for studying odor-evoked voluntary urination in mice, and provide a foundation for future investigations into the neural substrates underlying this social communication behavior.

  Urinary scent marks convey individual identity, reproductive status, and social hierarchy in mammals and contribute to territorial delineation and conflict avoidance15. From an evolutionary perspective, the ability to store and selectively release urine likely emerged as a strategy to reduce predation risk by minimizing continuous scent dispersion36, and was later co-opted for complex, olfactory-guided social communication37,38. Although odor-based paradigms have been developed to study voluntary urination, they often involve environmental manipulations such as prolonged social isolation, which can introduce stress and reduce ecological validity29. Furthermore, the extended duration of these assays (1–2 h) limits their compatibility with real-time neural recording and manipulation techniques23,24,29,39. In this context, our short-term assay uses estrous-phase urine from hormonally primed females as an olfactory cue to evoke quantifiable scent marking without requiring social isolation. Our new paradigm fulfills the criteria as a brief, ethologically valid, and quantifiable model of voluntary urination in rodents. A potential concern is that strain-specific olfactory cues from the female donor could influence marking intensity. However, our data demonstrate that BALB/c males produced comparable urine spot numbers and total marked area when exposed to estrous urine from either BALB/c or C57BL/6J females (Supplementary Fig. 1). These results indicate that female donor strain does not significantly influence odor-evoked urination, validating the use of OVX C57BL/6J estrous urine as a standardized stimulus across experiments.

  Our study reveals striking strain-specific differences in scent-marking behavior between BALB/c and C57BL/6J male mice within our 10-minute testing paradigm. This discrepancy appears to stem from differences in baseline marking propensity and response dynamics rather than a true absence of isolation-induced enhancement in C57BL/6J mice. BALB/c males display high baseline scent-marking, allowing rapid responses that are further amplified by social isolation (Fig. 2c–e). In contrast, C57BL/6J males did not display detectable odor-induced scent marking within the same 10-minute assay (Fig. 2f–h), possibly reflecting strain-specific differences in response latency or territorial motivation rather than an absolute inability to mark. Notably, previous studies have reported that C57BL/6J males can exhibit robust scent-marking behavior under longer assay durations or prolonged social isolation23,29, indicating that the lack of detectable marking in our 10-minute paradigm reflects the temporal limitations of this brief assay rather than a fundamental deficit in this strain. Extended testing durations may be required for C57BL/6J males to manifest robust territorial behavior29, suggesting that social context effects need longer acclimation periods to emerge in this strain. Beyond response latency, BALB/c males produced more urine marks with greater spot widths, reflecting higher marking motivation and distinct scent dispersion strategies, potentially arising from differences in pheromone sensitivity, vomeronasal activation thresholds, or the evolutionary pressures shaping social communication. Collectively, these findings highlight that genetic background critically shapes both the expression and social modulation of territorial marking behavior.

  We adopted F1 hybrid males (C57BL/6J × BALB/c) to leverage the respective strengths of both parental strains: the robust scent-marking response of BALB/c and the well-established genetic toolkit available on the C57BL/6J background. As expected, F1 hybrids exhibited reliable, intermediate-level odor-evoked responses that combined BALB/c’s sufficient behavioral responsivity with C57BL/6J’s versatile genetic toolkit (Figs. 2 and 3). This phenotype provides both optimal quantification for short-term behavioral assays and compatibility with cell-type-specific neural interrogation tools—a balance essential to our experimental design. Consequently, F1 hybrids enabled subsequent experiments that integrated behavioral quantification with whole-brain c-Fos mapping (Fig. 6) and future circuit-level dissection. The stability of this intermediate phenotype is consistent with hybrid vigor, a phenomenon widely documented in behavioral research that confers enhanced genetic robustness and reduced inter-individual variability30,40,41. Thus, the F1 hybrid model offers a practical solution for quantifying odor-evoked voluntary urination under our time-constrained experimental conditions.

  The void spot assay (VSA) is a non-invasive method for assessing spontaneous urination patterns in rodents by placing them in an enclosure lined with absorbent filter paper and subsequently quantifying urine spots via ultraviolet fluorescence or ninhydrin labeling39,42. Its application is often limited by reliance on manual or semi-automated urine spot annotation, which is labor-intensive and susceptible to subjective bias43. Moreover, differences in experimental parameters, including cage configuration, assay duration, and housing conditions, may contribute to procedural heterogeneity and thereby affect reproducibility across laboratories44. Artificial intelligence-based methods have emerged as a promising solution to enhance the efficiency and objectivity of behavioral quantification in response to these challenges45,46. Among state-of-the-art computer-vision algorithms, YOLOv8 offers real-time object detection with high spatial precision47. We therefore hypothesized that YOLOv8 could reliably identify urinary scent marks in UV images. Accordingly, we developed an automated detection model that combines the YOLOv8 architecture with custom annotations created with LabelMe. The model processed each image in an average of 16.5 ms and produced results indistinguishable from manual analysis (Fig. 5). By eliminating observer bias and reducing labor intensity, our YOLOv8-based framework overcomes key limitations of manual annotation and provides a scalable, high-throughput tool for behavioral phenotyping.

  Human neuroimaging studies have identified a distributed supraspinal network involved in the control of urine storage and voluntary urination, including the medial prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, insula, thalamus, basal ganglia, periaqueductal gray, and pontine micturition center48,49. Functional MRI studies have shown that the desire to void, voluntary suppression, and initiation of urination are associated with activation of medial frontal and cingulate cortices, the insula, and brainstem centers50, suggesting that higher-order cortical regions provide contextual and motivational control over the brainstem micturition switch. Using the present odor-driven behavioral assay, we performed whole-brain c-Fos mapping to characterize neural activity associated with estrous urine-evoked voluntary urination in GH F1 hybrid males. Compared with control group mice, marking group mice exhibited increased c-Fos expression in the prelimbic, infralimbic, and anterior cingulate cortices, as well as in the posterior piriform cortex (Fig. 6c). These regions correspond to medial prefrontal and associative cortical areas that are thought to participate in decision-making, internal state evaluation, and context-dependent behavioral selection, consistent with the cortical activation patterns reported in human neuroimaging studies during voluntary control of micturition. No significant differences were detected in the accessory olfactory bulb or medial amygdala after correction, suggesting that higher-order cortical processing, rather than primary chemosensory structures, appears more closely associated with the initiation of voluntary urination in this paradigm51–53. In addition to cortical activation, increased c-Fos expression was observed in the medial preoptic area, ventral tegmental area, supragenual nucleus, and medial septum, indicating recruitment of subcortical regions that may contribute to motivational, social, or arousal-related modulation of the behavior54–56. At the brainstem level, the pontine micturition center showed greater activity in marking mice, consistent with its established role as a critical coordination center for bladder–sphincter function1,22,23,57,58. Notably, no significant change was detected in the PAG after multiple-comparison correction, suggesting that under odor-driven conditions, cortical and hypothalamic inputs may directly engage pontine circuits without strong recruitment of midbrain relay activity59,60. Together, these findings define a distributed cortical–subcortical–brainstem activation pattern associated with odor-evoked voluntary urination. Although c-Fos mapping does not establish causality, the regional specificity observed here provides an anatomical framework for future circuit-level studies of forebrain control of voluntary micturition.

  In conclusion, we established a short-term behavioral paradigm in which estrus-phase urine from hormonally primed OVX females evokes voluntary urination in F1 hybrid male mice. This assay enables quantifiable assessment of odor-induced scent marking under both group-housed and socially isolated conditions. In parallel, we implemented a YOLOv8-based deep learning pipeline for automated detection and quantification of urinary scent marks, facilitating objective and scalable analysis. Together, these approaches provide a practical framework for investigating the neural regulation of voluntary urination and its potential relevance to lower urinary tract dysfunction.


## Materials and methods

### Animals
  All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Guangxi University (Approval No. GXU-2025-150) and conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines. All animal experiments were reported in adherence to the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org). Adult BALB/c and C57BL/6J mice were obtained from the Guangxi University Laboratory Animal Center (Nanning, China) and maintained under standard laboratory conditions (12-hour light/dark cycle; temperature: 22 ± 2 °C; humidity: 35%–70%) with ad libitum access to standard rodent chow and water.

  For breeding, adult C57BL/6J males were housed with two C57BL/6J females (1:2 ratio), and adult BALB/c males were housed with two BALB/c females. F1 hybrid mice were generated by crossing BALB/c males with C57BL/6J females. Male offspring were weaned on postnatal day 21 (P21) and randomly assigned to either group-housed (GH, 5 males per cage) or socially isolated (SI, single-housed) conditions. GH males were maintained in stable social cohorts in standard cages until behavioral testing on postnatal day 91 (P91, approximately 13 weeks of age), a time point consistent with prior studies on scent-marking behavior in adult C57BL/6J males18. SI males were individually housed in identical cages from P21 to P91, with weekly bedding changes and minimal handling to avoid stress-related confounds. All behavioral assays were conducted using adult males weighing 25–30 g. A total of 201 adult mice were used in this study. For behavioral experiments, 174 male mice were used (F1 hybrids, n = 79; BALB/c, n = 59; C57BL/6J, n = 36). A subset of these F1 hybrid males (n = 12) was subsequently used for c-Fos mapping. Furthermore, 21 adult ovariectomized (OVX) female mice were used for hormone-induced estrus and urine collection (C57BL/6J, n = 15; BALB/c, n = 6). Additionally, 6 intact C57BL/6J females (without OVX surgery) were used for urine collection during the natural estrous cycle.


### Hormonal induction of estrus and urine sample collection in ovariectomized female mice
  Female C57BL/6J and BALB/c mice (6 weeks old, 16–18 g) underwent bilateral ovariectomy (OVX) under anesthesia with 1% sodium pentobarbital (i.p., 10 mL/kg; cat. no. P3761, Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). A ~ 1 cm dorsal skin incision was made caudal to the 13th rib, ovaries were exposed and excised, and the ovarian pedicles were ligated as described previously61. Following surgery, mice were allowed to recover for four weeks before being randomly assigned to either the hormone-treated group or the vehicle control group. For estrus induction, OVX females received daily intraperitoneal injections for three consecutive days as follows: β-estradiol (0.2 mg/mL, 100 µL; cat. no. E8875-5G, Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) on day 0, β-estradiol-3-benzoate (0.1 mg/mL, 100 µL; cat. no. E102160-5 g, Aladdin, Shanghai, China) on day 1, and progesterone (1 mg/mL, 100 µL; cat. no. P0130-25 g, Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) on day 2. All steroids were dissolved in corn oil (cat. no. C116025-500 mL, Aladdin, Shanghai, China). Control OVX females received equivalent volumes of corn oil daily. Vaginal cytology was assessed 4 h after each injection via lavage with 100 µL sterile saline, followed by air-drying and Giemsa staining (cat.no.BA4017, BaSO, Zhuhai, China)62. The estrous stage was determined by microscopic examination of epithelial cell morphology63. Females confirmed to be in estrus were transferred to metabolic cages (cat.no. LAS-Metabolic Cages, Tecniplast Group, Shanghai, China) for urine sample collection over an 8-hour period. Collected urine samples were transferred to sterile tubes and stored at − 80 °C until use.


### A short-term odor-induced assay for urine scent marking
  All behavioral assays involving C57BL/6J, BALB/c, and F1 hybrid male mice were conducted during the dark phase, beginning after 20:00. Animals were habituated to the behavioral chamber lined with filter paper for 10 min per day over two consecutive days. After each habituation trial, chambers were cleaned with 15% ethanol, air-dried, and relined with new filter papers to eliminate residual olfactory cues. On two subsequent test days, mice were subjected to the assay as described below. On each test day, mice were first transferred to the behavioral chamber using gentle handling procedures and allowed to move freely within the chamber for 30 s without any experimental intervention. If no urination occurred during this period, 100 µL of stimulus solution was applied to the center of the filter paper, and the 10 min behavioral assay was initiated. If urination occurred within the initial 30 s period, the filter paper was replaced, followed by an additional 30 s free exploration period before stimulus application. On the first test day, sterile saline was used as the stimulus. On the second test day, estrous urine samples collected from hormone-primed OVX females were used. Only urine marks deposited after exposure to the stimulus were included in the analysis. After each trial, mice were returned to their home cages, and the chambers were cleaned as described above. Throughout each trial, locomotion and urine deposition were simultaneously recorded using a dual-camera system positioned above and below the chamber. At the end of the assay, filter papers were collected and imaged in a dark room under ultraviolet illumination using an LED curing light (Model LED.B; Guilin Woodpecker Medical Instrument, Guilin, China; output intensity 1000–1200 mW/cm²) to visualize urinary scent marks. Under this illumination, urine residues exhibited yellow fluorescence, enabling clear identification of individual urine spots. All images were acquired under identical exposure settings and saved in TIFF format for subsequent quantitative analysis. For representative images shown in the figures, color information was removed during post-processing using Adobe Photoshop (Version 2019, Adobe Systems, San Jose, CA, USA) to generate grayscale TIFF images solely for visualization purposes; all quantitative analyses were performed using the original color images.


### Odor-induced voluntary urination paradigm in F1 hybrid mice
  Mice were habituated to the behavioral chamber lined with filter paper for 10 min per day on postnatal days (P) 91 and 92 in the absence of olfactory stimuli. On subsequent test days (P93–P95), mice were transferred to the behavioral chamber and allowed to move freely for 30 s prior to stimulus application, as described above. Following this period, 100 µL of the designated urine sample was applied to the center of the filter paper, and the 10 min behavioral assay was initiated. On P93, mice were exposed to estrous urine collected from hormone-treated OVX females. On P94 and P95, the same mice were exposed to estrous urine from naturally cycling females and diestrus urine, respectively, with diestrus urine serving as the negative control stimulus. Each session was recorded using a dual-camera system positioned above and below the chamber to capture locomotion and urine deposition. Chambers were cleaned and relined with fresh filter paper between trials. Urinary scent marks were imaged under ultraviolet illumination and saved in TIFF format for quantitative analysis. The threshold for voluntary urination was defined based on baseline responses from group-housed F1 hybrid males exposed to diestrus urine (n = 10). Under this condition, the mean urine spot number was 0.4 with a standard deviation of 1.3. The cutoff value, calculated as the mean plus two standard deviations, yielded approximately 2.9 urine spots. Because baseline observations included occasional higher values, a more conservative operational threshold was applied, and voluntary urination was defined as the deposition of more than five urine spots within a 10 min session. The same threshold was used across all strains and housing conditions.


### Immunohistochemical detection of c-Fos following odor-induced voluntary urination
  To examine neuronal activation associated with odor-induced voluntary urination, F1 hybrid male mice were assigned to experimental groups based on olfactory stimulus exposure during the behavioral assay. After two days of habituation to the behavioral chamber (postnatal days 91–92), mice were exposed for 10 min on postnatal day 93 to either 100 µL of estrus-phase urine collected from hormone-treated OVX females (marking group) or an equal volume of saline (control group). Ninety minutes after stimulus exposure, mice were deeply anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (1%, i.p., 10 mL/kg) and transcardially perfused with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Brains were post-fixed overnight at 4 °C, cryoprotected in graded sucrose solutions (15% and 20%), and coronally sectioned at 40 μm using a cryostat. Free-floating sections were permeabilized in 1% Triton X-100 in PBS, blocked with 10% goat serum, and incubated at 4 °C for 48 h with rabbit anti-c-Fos primary antibody (1:400; Abcam, ab190289). After PBS washes, sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:500; Invitrogen, A21206) for 2 h at room temperature and counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were acquired using fluorescence or confocal microscopy. Anatomical annotation was performed according to the Allen Mouse Brain Atlas (https://brain-map.org/atlases/anatomy#adult-mouse), and c-Fos positive cells were manually quantified using Fiji (ImageJ) (Version 2.9.0, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA)64.


### Manual and Automated Image Analysis of Urinary Scent Marks
  Manual Quantification. Raw TIFF images were cropped to the full filter paper area (15 cm × 27 cm; 72 pixels/cm) using Adobe Photoshop. Images acquired under ultraviolet illumination were converted to grayscale format in Photoshop by changing the image mode from RGB to grayscale to allow uniform intensity-based analysis of urine marks. Urinary scent marks were manually delineated as regions of interest based on their fluorescence intensity relative to the background. Both the number of marks and the total marked area were quantified in Photoshop using the Record Measurements function in the Analysis menu. The resulting measurements were exported to GraphPad Prism (Version 10.1.2, GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA) for subsequent statistical analysis.

  Automated Analysis. Urine spot images collected from filter papers as described above were used as the input dataset for automated detection and quantification. Individual urinary scent marks were manually annotated using polygon-based segmentation with LabelMe (version 5.0.1; https://github.com/wkentaro/labelme), and the annotated contours were exported as ground-truth labels for model training. A total of 100 annotated filter-paper images containing multiple urinary scent marks were included in the dataset. The dataset was divided into training, validation, and test subsets at a ratio of 7:2:1. Data augmentation, including random rotation, horizontal flipping, and Gaussian blurring, was applied exclusively to the training set to increase sample diversity and improve model robustness. A YOLOv8-based object detection and image segmentation model (version 8.0.20; Ultralytics; https://github.com/ultralytics/ultralytics) was then trained to automatically detect urinary scent marks, and the resulting predictions were used to derive mark counts and total marked area for each image. The hyperparameters were set as follows: batch size = 32, initial learning rate = 0.01, momentum = 0.937, and weight decay = 0.0005; all other parameters were kept at their default values in the Ultralytics framework. The optimized model was subsequently applied to all urine spot images for automated identification and segmentation of individual scent marks. The number of urine spots and the total marked area per image were quantified based on the segmented regions.


### Statistical analysis
  All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.1.2; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and nonparametric statistical tests were applied where appropriate. Data are presented as individual values with mean ± SEM, as indicated in the figure legends. For comparisons between two independent groups, the Mann–Whitney U test was used; for paired comparisons, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied. For comparisons involving more than two independent groups, the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc test was used. For repeated-measures comparisons across more than two conditions, the Friedman test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test was applied. All post hoc tests were two-tailed. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 unless otherwise specified, and all other comparisons used unadjusted P values. For c-Fos analyses, comparisons between Control and Marking groups were performed separately for each brain region using two-tailed Mann–Whitney U tests. For multi-region comparisons (Fig. 6c), P values were adjusted using the two-stage step-up procedure of Benjamini, Krieger, and Yekutieli to control the false discovery rate at 5%, with adjusted q values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.
