# S100P mediates IL-13/ATF4-driven epithelial barrier dysfunction in eosinophilic asthma: a potential biomarker and therapeutic target


## Background
  In allergic asthma, a detrimental cycle of epithelial barrier destruction, driven predominantly by type 2 inflammation, facilitates allergen exposure to deeper tissues. However, the precise mechanisms underlying this process remain poorly understood.


## Methods
  A bioinformatics analysis was performed using public datasets and an independent cohort of asthma patients. Single-cell profiling further elucidated the role of S100 calcium-binding protein P (S100P) in this condition. To investigate the impact of S100P on barrier integrity, human bronchial epithelial cells (16HBE) were stimulated with interleukin-13 (IL-13). The functional interaction between S100P and potential therapeutic compounds was evaluated by molecular docking and dynamics simulation.


## Results
  Our findings revealed significant alterations in the transcriptional regulation of the S100P, with protein levels correlating closely with lung function and eosinophilic inflammation. S100P was predominantly localized in airway epithelial cells (AECs), particularly in the goblet and club cells. It acts as a pivotal signal in maintaining epithelial barrier integrity by regulating tight junction proteins, such as ZO-1 and occludin, and altering transepithelial electrical resistance. Mechanistically, IL-13 markedly upregulated S100P expression in AECs, likely via activation of transcription factor 4 (ATF4). Molecular docking and dynamics simulations demonstrated that S100P interacts with the clinically established antihistamine astemizole and the immunosuppressant celastrol, both of which protect against S100P-induced barrier disruption.


## Conclusions
  Our work identified S100P as a key mediator of airway epithelial barrier dysfunction in asthma, shedding light on the crucial signaling of the IL-13/ATF4/S100P axis. These findings highlight the dual potential of S100P as both a biomarker and a therapeutic target, offering promising strategies for barrier protection in clinical asthma management.

## Introduction
  Asthma is one of the most prevalent chronic diseases worldwide, posing a significant public health challenge [1–3]. This condition is characterized by complex interactions between lung structural cells and immune cells, leading to airway inflammation, bronchial hyperresponsiveness, excessive mucus secretion, and airway remodeling [4, 5]. The epithelium, acting as the primary defensive line in lung against external stimuli, fulfills a pivotal function in the regulation of immune responses and maintaining airway homeostasis. Recent researches have emphasised its crucial role in asthma development, whereas the majority of current remedies primarily focus on the relief of airway hyperresponsiveness and inflammation [6]. To date, there is a paucity of reliable indicators to assess airway epithelial barrier damage, and clinical evidence concerning the efficacy of extant treatments for epithelial barrier rescue is sparse [7]. Therefore, a better understanding of the exact mechanisms underlying epithelial dysfunction involved in asthma may open up new avenues for clinical prevention and treatment.

  Emerging evidence indicates that the dynamic interactions among airway epithelial cells, immune cells, and their mediators are crucial events in the pathophysiology of asthma [8]. Allergens from pollen, insects, and fungi, disrupt the structure of epithelial tight junctions by virtue of their inherent active protease components, which facilitate the penetration of allergens into subepithelial layers. This structural compromise triggers the rapid release of alarmins (TSLP, IL-25, and IL-33) [9] and promote the maturation and differentiation of immunocytes. Consequently, cytokines such as interleukin-4, -5, and − 13 are overproduced, establishing a type 2 inflammatory microenvironment [10]. This process further impairs the junctional complex between epithelial cells, which in turn brings about prolonged exposure of allergens to deeper layers [11, 12]. Since the processes by which this cascade works and contributes to the collapse of the barrier remain obscure, the precise molecular mechanism underlying it needs to be further clarfied.

  S100 calcium-binding protein P (S100P) is a member of the S100 family of calcium-binding proteins. It is involved in diverse biological processes, including intracellular signal transduction, cell survival, and cytoskeletal dynamics. Furthermore, S100P can be secreted and act in a paracrine or autocrine manner to modulate cellular responses [13]. Research has demonstrated that S100P promotes tumor proliferation, migration, and invasion by regulating the cell cycle and the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [14]. Its expression correlates with clinical stage and poor prognosis in various cancers, including pancreatic and lung cancer [15, 16]. Despite its well-established role in oncology, the significance of S100P in asthma, particularly with regard to the potential profile involved in barrier dysfunction triggered by type 2 inflammation, remains entirely unexplored.

  In the present study, bioinformatics analyses were integrated with clinical samples from asthma patients, tissues from ovalbumin-challenged mice, and human bronchial epithelial cells. The primary objective of this investigation was to explore the role of S100P in asthma pathogenesis, with a specific focus on its contribution to the function of epithelial cells in the asthmatic airway.


## Methods

### Data acquisition
  Bulk RNA-seq datasets pertain to asthma (accession numbers: GSE256534, GSE137268, GSE147878, GSE85567, GSE179156, GSE67472, GSE63142) and transcriptomic profiles of airway epithelial cells stimulated in vitro with IL-4, IL-5, or IL-13 (accession numbers: GSE78914, GSE147341, GSE37693) were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) repository. Data were accessed by utilizing the GEOquery R package (version 2.68.0).


### Identification of DEGs
  Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified utilizing the GEO2R (NCBI) tool and the limma package (version 2.68.0), with identification criteria P < 0.05 and |log₂FC (fold-change)|≥0.6 [17–19]. Volcano and heatmap plots were generated using the OmicStudio tools (https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool) [20]. Overlapping DEGs were then visualized using Venn diagrams generated by the MicrobioMed Cloud Platform (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn) [21]. Gene expression levels were then compared across groups using transcripts per million (TPM) data.


### Enrichment analysis
  Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed on the identified DEGs using the ClusterProfiler package (version 4.8.1). In accordance with the established protocols, the GO analysis included the following components: biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF) [22]. To further explore the function of S100P in airway epithelial cells, the matrix was divided into two groups: low-expressed and high-expressed, based on the median expression of S100P. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA, version 4.1.0) was then conducted to investigate S100P-related signaling pathways. Pathways with False Discovery Rate (FDR) < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched [23].


### Analysis of scRNA-seq data
  Single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) data for human asthma [24] were obtained from the Human Cell Atlas (HCA) data portal at https://cells.ucsc.edu/?ds=teichmann-asthma. The expression distribution of S100P across airway epithelial cells and other cell populations was examined, with both the average expression levels and the proportion of S100P+ cells being quantified.


### Patients and samples collection
  Serum samples were collected from asthma outpatients at the First Affiliated Hospital with Nanjing Medical University and from healthy volunteers enrolled at the Health Examination Center. A detailed overview of the demographic and clinical characteristics is presented in Table 1. The study was approved by the Medical Ethical Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital with Nanjing Medical University (Approval No.: 2025-SR-677).

### Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
  Serum S100P levels were quantified using a commercially available ELISA kit (Absin Bioscience Inc., Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A standard curve was generated, and concentrations were calculated using ELISA Calc software.


### Construction of a mouse model of asthma
  Female wild-type BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old, 18–20 g) were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Centre of Nanjing Medical University (Nanjing, China). Mice were randomly assigned to control and asthma model groups. In the OVA model, mice received intraperitoneal sensitization on days 0, 7, and 14, followed by intranasal challenges on days 21 to 23, and were euthanized on day 24, following established protocols [25]. For the HDM model, mice were sensitized intranasally on day 0, followed by daily challenges from days 7 to 11, and sacrificed on day 14, as previously described [26, 27]. All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Nanjing Medical University (Approval No.: IACUC-2310019).


### Immunohistochemistry
  For immunohistochemical analysis, mouse lung tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 5-µm slices. After deparaffinization and rehydration, antigen retrieval was conducted using a standard protocol. Endogenous peroxidase activity was then inhibited by incubation with 3% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) for 15 min, followed by a 1 h blocking step with 10% goat serum at room temperature to reduce non-specific binding. The tissue sections were subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody against S100P (ThermoFisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA). The next day, sections were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Following thorough washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), the chromogenic reaction was performed using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB), with microscopic observation until optimal staining intensity was achieved. The sections were then extensively rinsed with distilled water, counterstained with hematoxylin for 40 s, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (75%, 95%, and 100%), cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral resin. The presence of positive staining was identified as brownish deposits.


### Immunoflurescence
  Following dewaxing and washing of the paraffin-embedded mouse lung tissue sections, both the tissues and 16HBE cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 15 min, and blocked with 10% goat serum. Primary antibodies were then applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C. For mouse lung tissues, anti-Uteroglobin (CC10) antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA), anti-S100P antibody, anti-Zona Occludens 1 (ZO-1) antibody (Proteintech, Wuhan, China), and anti-Occludin antibody (Proteintech, Wuhan, China) were used. In contrast, 16HBE cells were stained with anti-S100P antibody, anti-ZO-1 antibody, and anti-Occludin antibody (HUABIO, Shanghai, China). Subsequently, Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated anti-mouse IgG and Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, USA) were utilised as secondary antibodies, followed by nuclear counterstaining with DAPI (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) for 10 min. Finally, samples were mounted and imaged.


### Identification of potential transcription factors
  To investigate the regulatory mechanisms of S100P expression, the TF-Target Finder tool was used to predict potential transcription factors (TFs) (10.3390/biom14070749). The results obtained from CHIP_Atlas, FIMO_JASPAR, and TRRUST were then subjected to cross-analysis to identify key transcription factors [28]. The findings were presented using a Venn diagram.


### Cell culture and intervention
  The human bronchial epithelial cell line 16HBE was purchased from the Beijing Tumor Center (Beijing, China). The cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Invitrogen-Gibco, Paisley, Scotland) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, HyClone, Logan, UT, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S, Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA). Upon reaching 80%-90% confluence, the cells were passaged using 0.25% trypsin (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). For the intervention group, the cells were initially transfected with S100P-specific siRNA (GenePharma, Shanghai, China) (Table S1) for 8 h following the serum-free culture, after which they were stimulated with IL-13 (Novoprotein, Shanghai, China).


### Cell counting kit-8 assay
  The assessment of cell viability was conducted by means of a cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Inc., Kumamoto, Japan). Cells were seeded at a density of 5,000 cells/well in 96-well plates. Once ~ 70% confluence was attained, cells were subjected to a starvation for a period of 6–8 h. Thereafter, the cells were treated with IL-13 (Novoprotein, Shanghai, China), ATF4-IN-2 (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA), astemizole (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA), and celastrol (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) for 24 h. Subsequently, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent, mixed with 90 µL of complete medium, was added to each well, followed by incubation for 1–2 h incubation at 37 °C. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (CANY, Shanghai, China).


### Western blot analysis
  After culturing the 16HBE cells, total protein extraction was conducted utilising RIPA buffer (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), supplemented with a protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Roche). Protein samples (30 µg) were resolved by 12.5% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) via wet transfer. The membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, including anti-β-actin (Proteintech, Wuhan, China), anti-S100P (Proteintech, Wuhan, China), and anti-ATF4 (Proteintech, Wuhan, China). Following incubation of the samples with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies, protein signals were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). Imaging of the samples was conducted using a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP Imaging System.


### Measurement of transepithelial electrical resistance and epithelial permeability
  The 16HBE cells were seeded onto 24-well Transwell inserts at a density of 1 × 10^4 cells per well. Transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) of the cell layer was monitored using the Millicell-ERS system (Millipore Co., Bedford, MA, USA) at two-day intervals to assess barrier development. Once TEER values stabilized, indicating the establishment of a confluent airway epithelial monolayer, the cells were treated with recombinant S100P protein (Novoprotein, Shanghai, China), either alone or in combination with astemizole or celastrol. To evaluate the integrity of the barrier over time, it is necessary to measure TEER values at 0, 6, 12, and 24 h after treatment [29]. Paracellular flux across the epithelium was measured with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labeled 4-kD dextran.


### Molecular docking
  To identify potential targeted drugs for the S100P protein (see Table S2), small-molecule compounds that may bind to S100P were conducted using data from the DsigDB database (https://tanlab.ucdenver.edu/DSigDB). Molecular docking simulations were performed with AutoDock Vina 1.5.6 software to investigate interactions between S100P and a range of candidate small molecule drugs [30]. Protein and drug structures were preprocessed using Autodock tools, including hydrogenation and dehydration of proteins, hydrogenation of small molecules, and determination of torsional degrees of freedom. Subsequently, the docking box coordinates were defined based on the target protein’s active site. The optimal molecular conformation was determined by comparing docking scores. Discovery Studio software was used to generate 2D interaction diagrams illustrating the relationships between tested compounds and key residues, while PyMOL 2.1.0 was employed for 3D visualization and structural analysis [31]. A binding energy lower than − 5.0 kcal/mol is generally considered indicative of favourable binding activity between the protein and the compound. Lower binding energies correlate with stronger binding affinity and more stable conformations. These findings demonstrate that astemizole and celastrol can stably bind to S100P, supporting their potential as lead compounds.


### Molecular dynamics simulation
  The GROMACS version 2022.3 software was employed for the execution of molecular dynamics (MD) simulations [32, 33]. Small molecules were preprocessed with AmberTools22, which assigned the GAFF force field, and hydrogenation and RESP charge calculations were carriered out using Gaussian 16 W. The resulting potential data were incorporated into the topology file of the MD system. All simulations were conducted under isothermal-isobaric ensemble (NPT) conditions at 300 K and 1 bar pressure, using the Amber99sb-ildn force field. The system was solvated with the TIP3P water model, and Na+ counterions were employed to achieve neutralisation. Energy minimization process was executed using the steepest descent method, followed by equilibration within the isothermal-isochoric ensemble (NVT) and the NPT for 100,000 steps each, with a coupling constant of 0.1 ps and a total duration of 100 ps. A production MD simulation was then conducted, consisting of 5,000,000 steps with a time step of 2 fs, corresponding to a total simulation time of 100 ns.


### Statistical analysis
  All data were presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software v9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Inter-group comparisons of continuous variables were performed using the independent samples t-test for normally distributed data (Shapiro-Wilk test, P > 0.05) and the Mann-Whitney test for non-normally distributed data. One-way ANOVA was used for comparisions among multiple groups (for normally distributed data with homogeneous variances, as confirmed by Levene’s test, P > 0.05), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. For non-normally distributed data, the Kruskal-Wallis test was employed, with Dunn’s post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. Spearman’s correlation analysis was performed to ascertain the correlations between variables.


## Results

### Differential transcriptome analysis and enrichment analysis of various asthma sample types
  In preceding studies, the use of peripheral blood, induced sputum, and lung tissue biopsy samples were commonly used in transcriptomic research related to asthma [34, 35]. In order to systematically elucidate the molecular mechanisms of asthma, we retrieved transcriptomic datasets (GSE256534, GSE137268, GSE147878) covering the aforementioned sample types from the GEO database for differential expression and enrichment analyses (Fig. 1A-D, Fig. S1A-C). Notably, GO and KEGG analyses consistently indicated significant associations between asthma progression and T helper 2 (Th2) cell differentiation, T cell differentiation, and type 2 immune responses (Fig. 1D). To identify the DEGs common to different asthma sample types, a screening threshold of P < 0.05 and |log2FC|≥0.6 was applied. This analysis identified 8,467 significant DEGs in peripheral blood, compared to only 159 and 73 in induced sputum and tissue biopsy datasets, respectively (Fig. 1E). Venn diagram analysis to the intersecting genes revealed that S100P was the only gene differentially expressed in all three datasets (Fig. 1F). Furthermore, subsequent evaluation of the expression levels confirmed that S100P was consistently and significantly upregulated in different sample types of asthma (Fig. 1G).

### Single-cell mapping of S100P-expressing cell populations in asthmatic lung tissues
  In light of the considerable heterogeneity of cell types among the diverse samples, the single-cell dataset was further analyzed to examine the cellular localization of S100P. As shown in Fig. 2A, S100P was predominantly expressed in goblet, club, and mucous-ciliated populations. Notably, S100P expression was also detected in B cells, along with other populations, within the asthmatic airway. To achieve a more precise identification of the primary cell sources of S100P, quantitative and visual analyses were conducted on this single-cell dataset. The results revealed that the average expression level of S100P and the proportion of S100P+ cells in airway epithelial cells were significantly higher than those in other cell types. Moreover, in comparison with the control group, the expression level of S100P and the percentage of positive cells in the asthma group were also significantly increased (Fig. 2B-C).

### Differential expression of S100P in asthma severity stratification and endotype classification using GEO datasets
  Given the high level of S100P expression in airway epithelium, four GEO datasets (GSE85567, GSE179156, GSE63142, GSE67472) were selected for the evaluation of S100P expression levels in epithelial brush biopsy samples from asthma patients. A comparative analysis was conducted, revealing that the expression level of S100P in the asthma group was significantly higher than that in the control group (Fig. 2D). This finding provides further support for the hypothesis that there is an imbalance in S100P expression during the pathological process of asthma. Morever stratified analysis across datasets (GSE63142, GSE137268, GSE67472) indicated that the expression level of S100P was associated with the severity of asthma: compared with the control group, its expression was significantly increased in severe asthma, while there was no statistically significant difference in moderate asthma (Fig. 2E). The endotype classification analysis demonstrated that the expression level of S100P was the highest in eosinophilic asthma, yet there was no statistical difference in its expression between paucigranulocytic asthma and the control group (Fig. 2F). Given the eatablished correlation between eosinophils and type 2 inflammation, a subsequent analysis was conducted to examine the expression of S100P in varous T2 phenotypes. The results obtained were consistent with those earlier investigations. The expression of S100P in T2-high asthma was found to be signicantly higher than that observed in the control group and T2-low asthma (Fig. 2G).


### Serum S100P levels in asthma and correlation with clinical parameters
  To validate the reliability of the bioinformatics analysis, serum samples were collected from 15 healthy controls and 30 asthma patients (Fig. 3A). Serum S100P protein levels were measured using ELISA. The results demonstrated that serum S100P levels were significantly elevated in asthma patients compared with healthy controls (Fig. 3B). Correlation analysis revealed a significant negative correlation between serum S100P levels and pulmonary function parameters in asthma patients. More specifically, the study found a negative correlation between serum S100P levels and the forced expiratory volume in 1 s percentage of predicted (FEV1%pred) (R=-0.36, P = 0.04) and the forced expiratory volume in 1 s / forced vital capacity ratio (FEV1/FVC) (R=-0.42, P = 0.01) in asthma patients (Fig. 3E-F). In addition, serum S100P levels exhibited a significant positive correlation with peripheral blood eosinophil counts (R = 0.44, P = 0.02) and percentages (R = 0.46, P = 0.01) (Fig. 3H-I). However, no significant correlations were observed between serum S100P levels and other pulmonary function parameters, including FEV1, FVC, peak expiratory flow (PEF), or serum immunoglobulin E (IgE) levels (Fig. 3C-D, G and J). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis demonstrated that serum S100P yielded an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.80 for asthma diagnosis, suggesting its potential as an auxiliary diagnostic biomarker for asthma.

### Verification of S100P expression in mouse models of eosinophilic asthma mice
  To further investigate the direct relationship between S100P and asthma pathogenesis, both OVA- and HDM-challenged mouse asthma model were employed. Immunohistochemical analysis confirmed that, in comparison with the control group, the expression of S100P in the airway epithelium of asthmatic mice was significantly increased in both models (Fig. 4A-B and E). Immunofluorescence co-staining of S100P with CC10, a specific marker of airway epithelial club cells, was performed. This finding indicates that S100P is predominantly expressed in airway epithelial cells (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, the expression level of S100P in the airway epithelium of asthmatic mice was significantly higher than that in the control groups in both the OVA and HDM models (Fig. 4C-D and E). The OVA-challenged and HDM-challenged models primarily exhibits type 2 inflammation. As indicated by previous findings, S100P has been demonstrated to be associated with T2-high asthma (Fig. 2F-G). Consequently, the GSE179156 dataset was employed to investigate the potential associations between S100P and known T2 biomarkers. As demonstrated in [36], POSTN (periostin), CLCA1 (chloride channel accessory protein 1), and SERPINB2 (serpin B2) are key elements in this process. Our findings demonstrated a significant positive correlation between S100P and each of these biomarkers (Fig. 4F). Furthermore, analysis of human bronchial epithelial datasets (GSE78914, GSE147341, GSE37693) revealed the differential effects of type 2 cytokines. Additionally, both IL-4 and IL-13 were found to be the most significant, while the effect of IL-5 was not statistically significant (Fig. 4G).

### Functional role of S100P in airway epithelial cells
  The results from the above analysis suggest that the expression of S100P can be increased by the key asthma cytokine IL-13. In the subsequent stage of the study, the impact of elevated of S100P expression on the functionality of airway epithelial cells was investigated. Firstly, the median expression value was used to categorize asthma patients in the GSE85567 into a high and low S100P expression groups. Differential analysis was then performed (Fig. 5A-B). The GSEA demonstrated that elevated S100P expression was concomitant with the activation of the IL-4/IL-13 signalling pathway and disruption of tight junctions (Fig. 5C-D). Experimental data further revealed a significant downregulation (P < 0.05) of the tight junction proteins ZO-1 and occludin in the airway epithelial cells of asthmatic mice (Fig. 5E-F). In order to provide further corroboration the association between S100P and epithelial barrier integrity in the context of asthma, the expression correlation of these proteins was analysed in lung tissues from both OVA- and HDM-induced mouse models (Fig. 5E). In accordance with the hypothesis formulated, S100P expression exhibited a substantial negative correlation with ZO-1 levels. Interestingly, in the OVA-induced model specifically, no significant correlation was found between S100P and occludin (Fig. 5E). To directly verify this, the effect of recombinant S100P protein on bronchial epithelial cells was assessed. The results demonstrated that S100P, at a concentrations of 1 to 10 µM, significantly reduced the expression levels of the epithelial tight junction proteins ZO-1 and occludin (Fig. 5G), as well as the TEER value (Fig. 5H). As demonstrated in Fig. 5I, FITC-dextran permeability assays consistently revealed that S100P treatment increased paracellular flux in a dose-dependent manner. Asignificant elevation was observed at 10 µM compared to the control.

### Upstream regulatory mechanisms of S100P
  As demonstrated in the preceding functional analysis, S100P has been identified as a pivotal inducer of the impairment of epithelial barrier function, thus underscoring its critical regulatory role in this domain. The molecular mechanisms underlying this phenomenon have driven the focus of our research. Firstly, the prediction results from five bioinformatics databases were overlaid, which revealed that ATF4 was the sole common transcription factor (Fig. 6A). Subsequently, this finding was confirmed through the utilization of human bronchial epithelial cell models derived GEO datasets (GSE78914, GSE37693). The results indicated that IL-4 stimulation did not significantly change the expression level of ATF4 (P = 0.79), nor did it induce a correlation between ATF4 and S100P expression (R=-0.03, P = 0.90) (Fig. 6B). Conversely, IL-13 stimulation exhibited the capacity to markedly enhance the expression of ATF4 (P < 0.01) and S100P in bronchial epithelial cells (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, the expression of the two showed a significant positive correlation (R = 0.90, P < 0.01) (Fig. 6B). To demonstrate the direct binding of ATF4 to the S100P promoter, we further integrated the ChIP-seq data from the Cistrome database and performed a visual analysis using the UCSC Genome Browser (hg38 version) [37]. As demonstrated in Fig. 6C, the binding peaks of ATF4 were found to be significantly enriched in the promoter region of S100P.

  To further investigate the functional characteristics of IL-13, the CCK-8 assay was used to systematically evaluate its effect on the viability of 16HBE. As demonstrated in Fig. 6D, treatment with 0–50 ng/mL IL-13 did not result in significant alteration in cell viability. Western blot analysis displayed that, in response to IL-13 concentrations ranging from 0 to 20 ng/mL, the expression of ATF4 and S100P was enhanced in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 6E). To elucidate the direct regulatory effect of ATF4 on S100P, interventions were carried out using the well-eatablished inhibitor ATF4-IN-2, which suppresses the expression of ATF4. The experimental data demonstrated that the utilisation of ATF4-IN-2 does not evidently affect cell viability (Fig. 6F). Following the administration of the ATF4 inhibitor, a significant reduction in ATF4 expression was observed, thereby confirming that the inhibitory efficacy of the inhibitor had reached the anticipated level (Fig. 6G). Furthermore, when stimulated with IL-13 in combination with ATF4-IN-2, the immunofluorescence intensity of S100P in cells was significantly lower than that in the group stimulated with IL-13 alone (Fig. 6H). In order to directly test the hypothesis that endogenous S100P mediates IL-13-induced epithelial barrier dysfunction, 16HBE cells were used as a model system, with S100P expression being knocked down using specific siRNAs. As demonstrated in Fig. S3A, transfection with si-S100P led to a substantial decrease in S100P protein in comparison to controls, thereby confirming the efficacy of the knockdown. To ascertain whether S100P exerts a causal influence on barrier dysfunction, the functional consequences of its knockdown on epithelial integrity were assessed. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that the IL-13 induced downregulation of ZO-1 and occluding was substantially restored in S100P-silenced cells (Fig. 6I). Under basal conditions, TEER values were comparable between si-NC and si-S100P group (Fig. 6J). In the experiment, the presence of IL-13 led to a significant decrease in TEER in cells that had been subjected to si-NC transfection. This finding is indicative of a disruption in the integrity of the cell barrier. It is noteworthy that this reduction was significantly less pronounced in si-S100P cells, with TEER values remaining considerably higher that those observed in the IL-13 treated si-NC group (Fig. 6J). Consequently, FITC-dextran permeability assays consistently confirmed that IL-13 induced barrier leakiness was partially but significantly rescued by S100P silencing (Fig. 6K).


### Prediction of asthma treatment drugs targeting S100P
  In view of S100P’s regulatory function in airway epithelial barrier dysfunction in asthma, we investigated its potential as a targeted for therapeutic intervention. Screening the DisgDB database [38], we identified classic asthma drugs (salbutamol, terbutaline, formoterol, astemizole) and natural compounds (gamma-aminobutyric acid, gossypol, and celastrol) that potentially bind to S100P (Table S2). AutoDock molecular docking revealed that all candidate compounds, except terbutaline, had binding energies with S100P below − 5.0 kcal/mol. Astemizole and celastrol had the strongest binding, with docking scores of -7.2 kcal/mol and − 7.5 kcal/mol (Fig. 7A, Fig. S2A-D). We selected these two compounds for 100-ns molecular dynamics simulations to assess their binding stability. As shown in Fig. 7B-C, the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) curves of the S100P-astemizole and S100P-celastrol complexes reached equilibrium after 20 ns and 30 ns, with average RMSDs of 0.7 nm and 0.8 nm, respectively. The root-mean-square fluctuations (RMSF) of residues were within 0 to 1 nm, indicating minimal disruption to secondary structures. The radius of gyration (Rg) values were stable, with an average value of 2.85 nm for both, and the solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) values remained stable at around 65 nm2 and 60 nm2.

### Effects of astemizole and celastrol on S100P-induced airway epithelial barrier injury
  To evaluate the potential repair effects of astemizole and celastrol on S100P-induced epithelial barrier damage, the viability of 16HBE cells was first assessed using the CCK-8 assay. As demonstrated in Fig. 7D, within the designated concentration gradient range, neither astemizole nor celastrol exhibited a substantial impact on cell viability. In light of the aforementioned findings, the cells were treated with astemizole or celastrol, respectively, following stimulation with recombinant S100P protein (Fig. 7E). As demonstrated in Fig. 7F, compared to the group stimulated with S100P alone, the relative fluorescence intensity of ZO-1 protein was significantly increased in the groups treated with S100P in combination with astemizole or celastrol. Additionally, the TEER value also significantly increased (Fig. 7G). In addition, permeability assays employing FITC-dextran revealed that S100P treatment led to a decrease (Fig. S3B-C). This suggests that these two drugs can effectively alleviate airway epithelial barrier damage caused by S100P.


## Discussion
  Currently, standard asthma treatments, including short-acting β2-adrenergic agonists (SABAs) and inhaled corticosteroids (ICS), primarily focus on alleviating symptoms such as dyspnea, wheezing, and coughing through their bronchodilation and anti-inflammatory effects [39]. However, emerging evidence positions the epithelium in airway as a central orchestrator of asthma pathogenesis, spurring interest in the potential of epithelium-derived biomarkers for precision medicine. In the present study, we identified S100P as a potent molecule responsible for epithelial barrier disruption in asthma. It was significantly elevated in both human serum and OVA-challenged mice’s airway, acting as a downstream effector of the novel IL-13/ATF4 axis. Our findings revealed the importance of S100P as both a latent signaler and an executor in disease progression.

  Indeed, certain members of the S100 family have been found to play crucial roles in asthma pathophysiology. Serum levels of S100 calcium-binding proteins A4 (S100A4) and A9 (S100A9) are significantly elevated in asthma patients compared to healthy controls. Exposure to lipopolysaccharide (LPS) triggers an increase in S100A9 in airway epithelial cells, promoting the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps. This suggests that S100A9 may represent a promising therapeutic target for neutrophilic asthma [40, 41]. However, the role of S100P in asthma remains poorly characterized, with only one prior study reporting increased S100P transcriptional levels in asthmatic sputum following a high-fat diet [42]. Our current clinical data further extended this observation, revealing that serum S100P levels are remarkably higher in asthma patients than in healthy controls, particularly in those with eosinophilic asthma. In contrast to S100A9, which is linked to neutrophilic inflammation [40, 41], our findings delineated a distinct positive correlation between S100P levels and eosinophil counts and percentages. This eosinophil-specific association was further corroborated in vitro, with its expression being selectively induced by IL-13 (a pivotal type 2 cytokine) but not by IL-17. Collectively, these observations indicate that serum S100P may serve as a peripheral indicator of type 2 inflammation and epithelial barrier status in asthma. However, further studies in larger cohorts are warranted to evaluate whether serum S100P provides additive predictive value for identifying patients with active type 2 inflammation and impaired epithelial barrier integrity, and whether it could aid in stratifying patients for targeted therapeutic interventions.

  S100P has been proved to be predominantly expressed in intestinal epithelial cells, where it contributes to mucosal repair by regulating apoptosis and immune response [43]. Similarly, in the endometrium of women with recurrent miscarriage, S100P expression was observed to be significantly increased in ciliated cells [44]. All these imply that S100P may participate in the development of diverse diseases by regulating the fate of epithelial cells. Beyond its involvement in barrier tissues, S100P has also been proposed as a pro-metastatic factor in cancers, promoting invasion through processes like EMT [45]. Consistent with these clues, our single-cell analysis of asthmatic lung localized predominantly S100P expression to airway epithelial cells, with lower levels in B cells and macrophages. A direct link between S100P and epithelial barrier dysfunction was identified by our experimental model. Treatment of 16HBE cells with exogenous S100P [46] resulted in a decline in the fluorescence intensity of tight junction proteins (ZO-1 and occludin), alongside a substantial reduction in TEER. These results indicated that S100P directly compromises epithelial barrier integrity and function. With the supplementary data (Fig. S1D-G), it was as well showed that S100P stimuli caused concurrent activation of chemokine signaling and epithelial phenotype switching (particularly goblet cell hyperplasia). These changes may collectively contribute to the barrier disruption through two interrelated mechanisms: (I) the overgeneration of chemokines facilitates the recruitment of immunocytes to the airway, and (II) the overproduction of MUC5AC promotes their retention at the epithelial surface, potentially boosting allergen capture and perpetuating inflammatory responses. This S100P-driven “recruit-and-retain” paradigm could set up a self-perpetuating cycle that exacerbates epithelial barrier dysfunction in asthma. However, further researches are needed to establish the precise causal relationships underlying these interconnected events.

  As noted before, airway epithelial cells are considered to be crucial contributors to the disturbed airway microenvironment in asthma [47, 48]. These cells play a vital role in initiating a range of pathophysiological processes within the airway, encompassing structural remodeling, signal transduction, and inflammation evolvement. Accounting studies have demonstrated that impaired epithelial barrier integrity constitutes a core element in the onset and progression of asthma [49]. Nevertheless, the clinical data regarding the efficacy of existing therapies in restoring epithelial barrier remain limited [50]. Decades of research have established type 2 cytokines, particularly IL-13, play a central role in allergic diseases [9]. The role of this critical cytokine in asthma pathogenesis is well characterized, particularly through its regulation of B cell proliferation, goblet cell hyperplasia, excessive mucus secretion, and epithelial barrier disruption. The prevailing consensus is that IL-13 induces epithelial dysfunction in different ways, including reducing the levels of the actin-binding proteins such as Ezrin [25], altering the phosphorylation of tyrosine kinase 2 (TYK2) [51] and signal transducer and activator of transcription 6 (STAT6) in epithelial cells, and promoting the expression of histone deacetylases (HDACs) and sirtuins [52]. Using a combination of bioinformatic analysis and experimental vilidation, our present work unveiled a novel IL-13/ATF4/S100P signaling axis critical for barrier impairment, thereby advancing our understanding of the IL-13-mediated epithelial pathology in asthma.

  ATF4 is a multifunctional transcription factor that plays a central role in diverse cellular processes, particularly under stress conditions where its expression is induced and activated [53]. Previous studies have suggested that ATF4 is an upstream regulator of S100P [54]. However, the transcriptional influence of IL-13 on either ATF4 or S100P remains unclear. In our study. we found that IL-13 stimulation significantly upregulated ATF4 expression in 16HBE cells, thereby establishing a direct link between IL-13 signaling and ATF4 induction. To delineate the functional contribution of ATF4 within this signaling axis, we employed the selective inhibitor ATF4-IN-2, which has been reported to suppress ATF4 expression by ~ 70% in HeLa cells [55]. In 16HBE cells, pretreatment with ATF4-IN-2 markedly attenuated IL-13-induced upregulation of S100P, thereby supporting a regulatory role for ATF4 in this pathway. S100P exhibits context-dependent pleiotropic functions. It enhances tumor progression by suppressing ferroptosis in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [56]. Intriguingly, both ferroptosis and S100P have been implicated in the pathology of airway epithelial cells. As indicated by earlier research, IL-13 has been demonstrated to activate arachidonate 15-lipoxygenase (ALOX15), leading to ferroptosis-like phenotypic in airway epithelial cells [57]. Furthermore, ATF4 has been also shown to positively regulate ferroptosis in epithelial cells, primarily through the transcriptional control of genes such as DNA damage-inducible transcript 3 (DDIT3) and glutathione-specific gamma-glutamylcyclotransferase 1 (GGT1) [58]. Interestingly, several studies suggest that pharmacological inhibittion of ferroptosis can promote epithelial repair, which seems to contradict our view that S100P exacerbates epithelial damage [59]. Our data established S100P as a detrimental factor for epithelial barrier integrity in asthma, whereas in HCC it acts as a pro-survival factor by inhibiting ferroptosis [59]. This apparent discrepancy may reflect the context-dependent, pleiotropic nature of S100P across distinct tissue environments and pathological conditions [60].

  Natural compounds, prized for their structural diversity and multi-target potential, represent a promising source of innovative therapeutics for complex diseases. In our study, molecular docking revealed that celastrol (a natural compound) and astemizole (a recognised anti-allergic agent) both exhibited strong binding affinities to S100P, a key mediator of epithelial barrier disruption in asthma. While previous studies have indicated that celastrol can enhance tight junction proteins and suppress the expression of ferroptosis-related proteins, supporting its barrier-protective role, the potential of astemizole to restore epithelial integrity remains unexplored. Our findings bridge this gap by demonstrating direct binding of both compounds to S100P, thereby highlighting astemizole as a candidate for drug repurposing in barrier repair. Despite the absence of SPR and cell thermal shift assays, which could provide direct binding affinity data, our molecular docking and MD simulations offer robust theoretical evidence of their interactions with S100P, complemented by CCK-8 and functional assays that demonstrate their effects on cellular viability and function. While astemizole’s known cardiac toxicity (Q-T prolongation) limits its clinical translation, it remains a valuable experimental tool for exploring epithelial repair mechanisms. In contrast, celastrol showed no such toxicity and emerges as a more promising candidate for clinical application. Though SPR and thermal shift assays could further validate our findings, our current results lay a solid foundation for future exploration. Overall, both compounds show significant promise as S100P inhibitors, emphasizing S100P as a viable target for therapeutic intervention in asthma. Notably, celastrol demonstrates translational potential in ameliorating asthmatic epithelial injury, positioning it as a promising candidate for clinical application.

  In all, by integrating bioinformatic, clinical, and experimental evidence, our research comprehensively demonstrates the upregulation of S100P across multiple dimensions in asthma. We emphasizes a pivotal regulatory function of the IL-13/ATF4 axis in governing S100P expression and inducing airway epithelial barrier dysfunction. Importantly, our work provides the first evidence that S100P as a critical molecular switch, connecting Th2-type inflammation to direct epithelial barrier damage. This provides a theoretical foundation for the development of novel therapeutic approaches aimed at restoring epithelial barrier integrity in asthma.


## Conclusions
  To the best of our knowledge, the precise role and underlying mechanisms of S100P derived from airway epithelial cells in the pathogenesis of asthma remain poorly elucidated. The present study revealed a marked elevation of S100P expression in both the serum of asthma patients and the airway epithelial cells of asthma mouse models. A significant negative correlation was demonstrated between serum S100P levels and lung function in patients, specifically FEV1%pred and FEV1/FVC, while a positive association was observed with eosinophil count, hinting the potential of S100P as a clinical signal for eosinophilic condition. S100P was then proved to contribute to the disruption of epithelial tight junctions, a critical event in asthma pathophysiology. Given their direct binding to S100P, the classic antihistamine agent astemizole and the natural compound celastrol were proposed to exert protective effects in asthma by restoring the epithelial barrier integrity. Mechanistically, S100P was further confirmed to function as a downstream effector of the IL-13/ATF4 signal axis, initiating epithelial barrier dysfunction and thereby exacerbating asthma progression (Fig. 8). All these findings positioned S100P as a potential biomarker for compromised airway epithelial barrier function in asthma and is anticipated to be a promising therapeutic target for anti-inflammation interventions in asthma management.
