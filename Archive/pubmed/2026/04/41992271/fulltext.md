# LMO7-mediated ubiquitination of SIRT3 promotes osteoarthritis progression: an investigation using machine learning and molecular dynamics simulations


## Background
  This study aims to inform clinical decision-making by identifying metabolism-related biomarkers involved in the progression of osteoarthritis (OA). Four OA cartilage-related microarray datasets were downloaded from the GEO database. A metabolism-related differentially co-expressed gene signature (MDCGS) associated with OA was then identified through an integrative computational approaches of Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) and Linear Models for Microarray Data (LIMMA) in combination with machine learning tools. The optimal gene expression and functions were additionally explored in vitro and in vivo. Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations (MDs) were conducted to explore the mode of binding with the drug and its role in OA.


## Results
  An MDCGS comprising 30 upregulated genes and 29 downregulated genes was identified. The LMO7 gene was determined to exhibit the most prominent importance score within the MDCGS, and subsequent molecular analyses confirmed the ability of LMO7 to ubiquitinate SIRT3, leading to its degradation and subsequent OA progression. Molecular docking and MDs were employed to further investigate the binding mode and stability of LMO7 and its inhibitors. The effectiveness of LM-1685, which had the best binding stability with LMO7, was verified in both in vivo and in vitro experiments.


## Conclusions
  In summary the series of bioinformatics, machine learning, experimental, molecular docking, and MDs analyses performed in this study led to the identification of LMO7 as a promising target for treating OA progression.

## Background
  Osteoarthritis (OA) is an increasingly common form of degenerative joint disease [1], which the World Health Organization (WHO) estimates affects roughly 9.6% and 18% of men and women over the age of 60 throughout the world, respectively. Approximately one-quarter of all OA patients in developed countries suffer from disabilities [2, 3]. OA is characterized by pathologic characteristics that include the onset of synovial inflammation, knee osteophyte formation, subchondral bone thickening, progressive articular cartilage degeneration, and the overall narrowing of the knee joint space [4]. While current approaches to treating OA focus on the alleviation of knee pain and stiffness, they are not effective as a means of preventing overall OA progression. OA patients face chronic pain and dysfunction, and the disease poses a significant economic burden that is an increasing threat to the aging global population [5]. There is thus a pressing need to clarify the mechanisms governing the pathogenesis of OA and to identify promising biomarkers and signaling pathways that can be targeted to reliably diagnose and treat this debilitating condition.

  In recent years, damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) have been further identified in the context of OA research; these consist of small molecular moieties released from the ECM into the joint space during the degradation of the cartilage and initiating a deleterious cycle through interactions between the cartilage and synovial membrane [6, 7]. The identification of more biomarkers as therapeutic targets to disrupt this vicious cycle has attracted increasing attention [8]. In some studies, OA progression was monitored through early combined efforts to detect serum C-telopeptides of type II collagen (CTX-II) and cartilage oligomeric matrix protein (COMP) [9, 10]. Matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) levels have been reported to be upregulated, playing important roles in the process of early PCM degeneration [11]. Levels of α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) reportedly decline in the cartilage of human OA patients and in Interleukin-1β (IL-1β)-stimulated osteoarthritic chondrocytes such that supplemental α-KG administration can reverse these changes, highlighting its promise as a target for the alleviation of OA [12]. DDAH1 has been postulated to regulate ADMA as a means of controlling chondrocyte and overall cartilage senescence and degeneration [13]. Based on the aforementioned findings, the onset and progression of OA involve a multidimensional pathological network encompassing cartilage degradation, matrix remodeling, and immunoinflammatory processes [14]. However, the molecular processes that underlie the onset of OA remain only partially understood, and are thought to entail complex interactions among metabolic, biochemical, immunological, and other factors [15]. Current clinical biomarkers mentioned above predominantly focus on downstream damage markers indicative of cartilage tissue breakdown, while systematic studies targeting the holistic metabolic pathways underlying OA pathogenesis remain scarce. This research gap has constrained the development of early intervention strategies and precision medicine approaches for OA management.

  This study is the first reported multi-perspective characterization of how LMO7 functions in OA based on results from a range of machine learning-based models, molecular experiments, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics simulations (MDs) strategies. These data ultimately highlight the promise of LMO7 as a novel biomarker and candidate therapeutic target in OA.


## Results

### Met-DCGs identification

Pearson’s correlation coefficients were used for sample clustering, yielding a sample clustering tree (Additional file 1: Fig. S1C). ### Establishment and evaluation of the optimal MDCGS model
  Based on data from the compiled GSE51588 and GSE169077 cohorts, WGCNA combined with Linear Models for Microarray Data (LIMMA) was applied to identify 61 Met-DCGs. These Met-DCGs were then used to conduct integrated analyses, generating 97 diagnostic models via 13 distinct algorithms with a tenfold cross-validation framework. Consequently, an optimal consensus metabolism-related differentially co-expressed gene signature (MDCGS) model was obtained. Area under the curve (AUC), F-score, accuracy, and recall rate analyses were conducted for the compiled dataset and the validation datasets (Fig. 2A; Additional file 1: Fig. S2A-C). The models were then ranked individually based on four key performance indicators. Notably, the Support Vector Machine (SVM)-default model was found to be within the top rankings for each of these indicators, which was selected as an optimal consensus MDCGS model with an average AUC of 0.874, area under the precision-recall curve (AUPR) of 0.892, accuracy of 0.858, an F-score of 0.89, and a recall rate of 1 across the validation datasets (Fig. 2A and F; Additional file 1: Fig. S2A-C).Fig. 2Construction and validation of 97 diagnostic models. ### LMO7 is upregulated and promotes cartilage degradation during OA progression
  To establish an optimal cellular model of OA, cartilage cell viability was assessed with a CCK-8 assay in the human SW1353 and murine ATDC5 cell lines, while LMO7, MMP9, MMP13, and COL2A1 expression in these cells was detected by qPCR and Western immunoblotting. After treating SWI1353 or differentiated ATDC5 cells with IL-1β (0, 5, 10, or 20 ng/mL, mRNA and protein levels were detected after 4, 8, 16, 24, and 48 h. MMP9 and MMP13 were upregulated with time and mRNA dose, whereas COL2A1 levels exhibited the opposite trend in both of these cell lines (Additional file 1: Fig. S3A and B), and there was a gradual decline in cartilage cell viability (Additional file 1: Fig. S3C). Following treatment for 24 h with IL-1β (10 ng/m), mRNA and protein level stability was enhanced, and these IL-1β (10 ng/mL, 24 h)-stimulated cells were thus used in subsequent experiments. LMO7 mRNA and protein levels were examined, demonstrating its expression in vitro in the context of OA (Additional file 1: Fig. S3A and B). Next, to confirm the above results, qPCR and Western immunoblotting were first performed, leading to the detection of higher LMO7 protein and mRNA levels in 13 OA cartilage samples relative to control tissues (Additional file 1: Fig. S3D and E). This confirmed the reliability of the bioinformatics and model-based strategies implemented for this study, and provides a theoretical and experimental basis for further exploration of the molecular mechanisms.

### LMO7 ubiquitinates SIRT3 to promote OA progression
  To understand the mechanisms that allow LMO7 to facilitate the progression of OA, SW1353 cells were analyzed for proteins capable of binding LMO7 via IP performed with anti-LMO7 or control IgG followed by Mass spectrometry (MS). When IP samples were separated via SDS-PAGE and stained with Coomassie bright blue, significantly greater protein abundance in the ~ 30 kDa weight range was evident in anti-LMO7-precipitated samples relative to those precipitated with control IgG (Fig. 4A). MS analyses of proteins in this region revealed that SIRT3 was the predominant protein in this molecular weight range (Fig. 4B). A significant increase in SIRT3 protein levels was observed following LMO7 knockdown in SW1353 and ATDC5 OA cell lines treated with IL-1β, whereas no significant differences in SIRT3 mRNA levels were detected under the same conditions (Fig. 4C and D). This suggested that LMO7 may be capable of stabilizing SIRT3 at the protein level. To test this possibility, cycloheximide was used to suppress protein synthesis in these cells following LMO7 knockdown so that the dynamics of SIRT3 protein degradation were analyzed. Knocking down LMO7 was found to significantly reduce the SIRT3 half-life as compared to control cells (Fig. 4E), suggesting that the greater stability of SIRT3 observed after knocking down LMO7 may be linked to the inhibition of its degradation. To more fully clarify the underlying mechanisms, OA cells overexpressing LMO7 were treated using MG132 or chloroquine to respectively inhibit proteasomal and lysosomal activity. MG132 treatment was sufficient to reverse the reduced SIRT3 levels observed following the overexpression of LMO7 (Fig. 4F). The ubiquitination status of SIRT3 was examined using cell ubiquitination assays. Consistent with our hypothesis, silencing of LMO7 led to a notable reduction in the ubiquitination level of SIRT3, indicating that LMO7-mediated degradation of the SIRT3 protein was mediated by ubiquitination (Fig. 4G). ### Molecular docking

### MDs
  To better understand the dynamic interactions and stability characteristics of LMO7-ligand complexes, an MDs approach was next used to confirm the results of molecular docking analyses and to evaluate LMO7 molecular movement upon binding to these target ligands. The workflow is illustrated in Fig. 6A. The system was considered to be in a steady state if root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) variation was in the 1–3 Å range [17]. The RMSD of the LMO7 protein backbone prior to ligand binding ranged from 1.0 and 1.5 Å (Fig. 6B), whereas it was between 2.0 and 3.0 Å following binding to LM-1685 and flubendazole, consistent with reliable docking poses. However, based on the analysis of molecular motion trajectories, the interaction between LMO7 and LM-1685 demonstrated the most stable binding. An unstable interaction between LMO7 and orantinib, however, was detected, with RMSD values ranging from 4.0 to 8.0 Å. Root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) value fluctuations were also used to evaluate amino acid residue stability in the local conformation in LMO7-ligand complexes. These analyses revealed that the binding stability between LMO7 and LM-1685 surpassed that of flubendazole and was notably more robust than orantinib (Fig. 6C). Radius of gyration (Rg) values were further assessed, revealing that the overall molecular conformation of LMO7 was largely unchanged following binding to these three ligands (Fig. 6D). Energy analyses of the complex were computed with the Coulombic interaction energy (Coul-SR) and the Lennard–Jones energy (LJ-SR) methods, and LMO7 binding to LM-1685 was found to be more stable than flubendazole and oratinib binding with respect to electrostatic interaction energy and van der Waals interaction energy (Fig. 6E). RMSD and gyrate data were used to plot free energy topography to detect the lowest energy conformation for LMO7-ligand binding (Fig. 6F), with molecular conformations following MDs optimization. The low-free-energy landscape, analyzed via integral calculations, revealed that the area of stable binding regions for the LMO7-LM-1685 complex significantly exceeded that of flubendazole. In contrast, the LMO7-orantinib interaction exhibited two distinct low-energy basins, suggesting multiple metastable states during binding. ## Discussion
  Outcomes from the treatments of OA are currently less than satisfactory. The articular cartilage has long been a core focus of studies examining the pathogenesis of OA, but biomarkers associated with pathological changes in this tissue compartment are lacking. In an effort to bridge this gap, the present study adopted a multidisciplinary approach integrating bioinformatics, machine learning, molecular experiments, molecular docking, and MDs. This comprehensive approach enabled a detailed exploration of the molecular mechanisms underlying the role of LMO7 in OA progression from multiple angles, providing a novel direction for OA research.

  Here, integrated bioinformatics approaches were used for Met-DCG identification, after which these target genes were used to establish a consensus MDCGS model by utilizing 97 model types fitted to the training dataset. We have fully integrated the core strengths of diverse algorithms: linear models (Logistic Regression [LR], Simple Linear Regression [SLR], Ridge Regression [RR], Enet, and Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator [LASSO]) complement nonlinear models (Decision Tree [DT], Random Forest [RF], Gradient Boosting Machine [GBM], XGBoost, and SVM) by combining the simplicity of linear approaches with the flexibility of nonlinear ones; parametric methods (LR and Linear Discriminant Analysis [LDA]) collaborate with nonparametric methods (K-Nearest Neighbors [KNN] and DT) to adapt to varied data distributions while balancing model bias and variance; probabilistic models (Naive Bayes Algorithm [NBA] and LDA) paired with non-probabilistic models (SVM and GBM) ensure both interpretability and predictive accuracy. Algorithms like DT implicitly capture feature interactions through splitting rules, whereas Enet explicitly selects features via regularization, reflecting differing sensitivities to feature interactions. Additionally, algorithms such as RR, LASSO, and Enet inherently incorporate regularization, enabling multi-level constraints on model complexity to further mitigate overfitting. By randomly combining 13 mature algorithms to construct 97 benchmark models, we leverage algorithmic complementarity, enhance model diversity, and improve robustness. This integrative approach allowed for the fitting of a model with various machine learning algorithms and combinations thereof to reduce variable dimensionality, providing a simplified, more translational model, providing data-driven support for subsequent model selection. When evaluating the performance of MDCGS on different publicly accessible datasets, we found that the SVM-default model was the most consistent in terms of AUC, F-score, accuracy, and recall rate. Therefore, the SVM-default model was selected as the optimal model based on the mature models of existing applications mentioned above, and LMO7 was identified as scoring highest in importance in the SVM-default model. The results suggest that LMO7 has potential in assisting with the early diagnosis and targeted therapy of OA, as well as guiding our further verification of targets.

  LMO7 is an E3 ubiquitin ligase involved in the regulation of key biological processes including the immune response, signal transduction, and protein degradation. Preliminary research revealed the localization of LMO7 at adhesion junctions wherein it can maintain epithelial integrity, in addition to playing a role in cancer onset and progression [18, 19]. Karlsson et al. suggested that LMO7 was of clinical importance as it could reverse the positive effect of LRIG1 expression on patient survival in non-small cell lung cancer [20]. It has also been found that the expression of LMO7 varies among species, where it is involved in transcriptional regulation, signaling, and adhesion in muscle tissues, as well as in cancer [21]. Several researchers have also proposed that LMO7 plays a crucial role in vascular smooth muscle cells as a target gene of TGF-β and exerts negative feedback regulation on the TGF-β pathway [22]. Whether LMO7 is capable of modulating immune- and metabolism-related pathways in the context of OA progression, however, has not previously been documented. Here, in vitro and in vivo experiments were performed to explore the role of LMO7 in this setting. Initially, clinical samples were collected and used to validate LMO7 upregulation in OA, and cell- and animal-based models of OA were also established and used to confirm that LMO7 functions by driving inflammatory OA progression while delaying reductions in cellular viability and inhibiting OA chondrocyte apoptosis. To further explore the mechanism by which LMO7 promotes OA progression, MS and Co-IP were used to assess its potential for specific binding to SIRT3. SIRT3 is a mitochondrial deacetylase that plays a crucial role in regulating cellular glucose and fatty acid metabolism, antioxidant stress responses, and mitochondrial function [23]. Subsequent experiments confirmed that elevated levels of LMO7 promoted the ubiquitination of SIRT3, leading to the degradation of the latter and contributing to OA progression. Previous research has demonstrated that mitochondrial SIRT3 could rescue impaired functioning of the mitochondrial respiratory chain in OA and inhibit inflammatory activity and exert protective effects on the cartilage in OA [24, 25]. It has been proposed that SIRT3 protects against OA development by inhibiting the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway [26]. It has also been reported that SIRT3 functions as an effector gene in chondrocytes during diet-induced obesity, suggesting that SIRT3 suppresses glycolysis, enhances mitochondrial respiration and fatty acid metabolism, and accelerates the progression of high-fat diet-induced OA [27]. This is consistent with the present results that the interaction between LMO7 and SIRT3 influenced cellular metabolism, thereby promoting OA progression. These results suggest the potential of LMO7 as a biomarker for OA, as well as providing evidence for a synergistic pathogenic mechanism involving the modulation of ubiquitination and metabolism in a multi-dimensional interaction network.

  After using bioinformatics, machine learning, and molecular experiments to confirm the role that LMO7 plays in OA progression, the mechanistic role of LMO7 in OA was further explored from a disease-related target and drug treatment perspective through molecular docking and MDs analyses. The goal of these experiments was to provide guidance regarding OA pathogenesis and treatment through the establishment of a "disease–target–drug" network capable of informing drug discovery and therapeutic targeting [28]. Here, the integration and analysis of MDCGS-derived data yielded the LMO7 proteins and 10 candidate ligands (orantinib, LM-1685, mirin, flubendazole, hydroxycholesterol, sitagliptin, prostaglandin, parthenolide, scoulerine, and SC-19220) relevant to OA. The binding energy values for these 10 molecules when interacting as ligands with these protein receptors were analyzed, revealing that the total binding free energy of LMO7 interacting with orantinib, LM-1685, and flubendazole exhibiting the lowest binding free energy values. MDs was additionally used to evaluate the behavior of LMO7 when interacting with these three ligands in solution, calculating the molecular dynamics when the molecular system advances from an unbound to a bound state with type and providing confirmation of the stability of binding between LMO7 and these ligands. RMSD, RMSF, and the energy of interaction values were also calculated to facilitate the further optimization of molecular docking and to improve drug design and predictive accuracy. Of these three targets, LMO7 most stably interacts with the selective COX-II inhibitor LM-1685, which as subsequently verified in both cellular and animal models. Additional experimental analysis is thus warranted to examine how LM-1685 affects OA and compare the differences in efficacy and side effects with those of other drugs.

  Together, these findings support the value of LMO7 as a new biomarker in OA and a promising target for further clinical study. However, these findings are subject to some limitations. For one, these analyses were retrospective in nature and genes were screened and identified based on existing models. Thus, prospective multicenter studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate these findings. In addition, we will conduct a rigorous parameter sensitivity analysis of the linear SVM penalty coefficient to identify the optimal parameter setting that balances model fitting and generalization performance. We also aim to further develop and optimize a clinically applicable disease-associated predictive model [29–36], ensuring that the model captures true disease-related correlations rather than random associations. Secondly, clinical data and their corresponding expression data are limited in certain available online datasets, underscoring the necessity to further investigate and validate the association between LMO7 and specific clinical characteristics (e.g., pain, limited mobility, or restricted activities of daily living). Third, the binding site and the specific mechanism of the LMO7/SIRT3 signaling pathway in OA requires further clarifications, highlighting a need for additional in vitro and in vivo experimentation aimed at clarifying the underlying mechanisms and functions. These will form important directions for our future research.


## Conclusions
  In summary, LMO7 appears to hold promise as a novel OA progression-related biomarker. These results provide a foundation for further efforts to improve clinical decision-making and to support the targeted treatment of OA.


## Methods

### Data collection and preprocessing
  The overall study workflow is summarized in the Graphical Abstract. The GEO database was accessed to download four independent human knee articular cartilage tissue mRNA microarray datasets (GSE51588, GSE169077, GSE114007, and GSE57218). Data normalization was achieved using the Z-score and log2(x + 1) approaches. The GSE51588 and GSE169077 datasets were selected for training purposes, with the two remaining datasets being used for validation. For further details regarding these datasets, see Table 2. The GSE51588 and GSE169077 datasets were merged using the package inSilicoMerging in R, followed by batch effect removal and PCA. PCA was conducted on the uncorrected merged dataset to visualize the extent of batch effects. Subsequently, ComBat algorithm was applied to correct for these effects, ensuring adjustments targeted only non-biological variables while preserving biological differences. After correction, PCA was repeated to evaluate whether batch effects had been adequately mitigated. Ultimately, data from 46 OA patients and 15 healthy controls were obtained. The compiled dataset, including its detailed clinical and expression profile data, was then utilized for downstream analysis. Lists of metabolism-related genes were obtained from the MSigDB database using the keyword “Metabolism” to retrieve 10,203 relevant genes. ### WGCNA
  WGCNA approaches were employed for the identification of co-expressed genes and modules in the compiled dataset with the R WGCNA package. For each gene, median absolute deviation (MAD) values were calculated, and the genes with the top 4000 MAD values then being retained for further analysis. An undirected network was constructed based on the absolute correlations between genes to form co-expression networks. The deepSplit parameter was set to 2. A soft threshold of 15 was applied with a scale-free topology model fit of 0.9, enabling co-expression module identification. The expression matrix was then transformed into an adjacency matrix, and this, in turn, was transformed to generate a topological matrix. The topological overlap measure (TOM) was then used to cluster genes based on the average-linkage hierarchical clustering method. To preserve the biological plausibility of modules, the Partitioning Around Medoids algorithm was not forced to respect the dendrogram structure. A minimum number of genes per network module of 50 was selected using the hybrid dynamic shearing tree method standard, and the eigengenes for individual modules were subsequently identified, using modules for a cluster analysis. Modules situated closely to one another were merged and the detectCutHeight was set at 0.25 to obtain the final modules. Any genes that failed to fit with a particular module were assigned to the gray module. Pearson correlation coefficients were employed to assess relationships among the genes in each module. Hub genes involved in promotion/inhibition in each OA module were further selected based on a GS > 0.5 and an MM > 0.8.


### Met-DCG identification
  Differential gene expression analyses were conducted with the R limma package. DEGs were identified based on a |log2(FC)|> 1 and an adjusted p-value < 0.05. Upregulated and downregulated metabolism-related DEGs were identified by assessing the intersection among upregulated/downregulated DEGs, metabolism-related genes, and the upregulated/downregulated hub genes involved in OA promotion/inhibition using the VennDiagram package. Met-DCGs were further identified by the construction of boxplots with the respective R ggplot2 and ggpubr packages.


### Machine learning-based MDCGS development
  To facilitate the development of a highly accurate and stable consensus MDCGS, 13 machine learning algorithms and 97 combinations of algorithms were employed. The utilized algorithm types were as follows: LR, LDA, KNN, DT, RF, XGBoost, RR, Enet, SVM, GBM, SLR, NBA, and LASSO. The process for MDCGS development was as follows: (a) features that significantly impact response variables within the compiled GSE51588 and GSE169077 cohort were selected with a LASSO regression model; (b) 97 combinations of algorithms were implemented for significant Met-DCG figures, with diagnostic model fitting being achieved through tenfold cross-validation framework using the compiled dataset; (c) all three datasets (the compiled GSE51588 and GSE169077, GSE114007, and GSE57218) were then analyzed with the developed models; and (d) AUC, F-score, accuracy, and recall rate for each model were calculated in the training and validation datasets. The models were then ranked individually based on four key performance indicators. To prioritize clinical diagnostic efficacy, we evaluated and ranked models primarily by AUC, with accuracy, recall, and F-score as supplementary metrics. Furthermore, AUPR and DCA was used to evaluate the clinical utility of the diagnostic models.


### Cell culture
  SW1353 human chondrosarcoma cells were used for this study. This research cell line was initially isolated from a right humeral primary grade II chondrosarcoma in a 72-year-old woman of Caucasian ancestry. In addition, ATDC5 cells derived from undifferentiated murine mesenchymal cells capable of chondrocytic differentiation were used. These ATDC5 cells were differentiated into chondrocytes through the addition of insulin, transferrin, and sodium selenite (ITS) together with vitamin C for 2 ~ 3 weeks, yielding a population of cells similar to the state of chondrocytes in vivo. Appropriate concentrations of IL-1β were added to SW1353 and differentiated ATDC5 cells in order to establish an in vitro OA model, enabling mechanistic studies of the underlying pathological mechanisms involved in this disease.

  These cells were cultured in DMEM or RPMI 1640 (HyClone, UT, USA), containing fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gemini, CA, USA) with penicillin/streptomycin (100 U/mL, Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA) to enable the screening and establishment of stable OA cell lines. Cells were routinely incubated in a 37℃ 5% CO2 incubator.

  Cell growth media was changed at 2- to 3-day intervals, and cells were subcultured when confluent by removing the growth media, rinsing with PBS, collecting the cells with trypsin (HyClone), resuspending in fresh growth media, and seeding at a lower density in a new culture dish.


### Analysis of animal and human samples
  In total, 40 healthy male SD rats (6–10 weeks old, 150 ± 5 g) were purchased from Shenzhen Lingfu Top Biotechnology Company Limited and housed in the Laboratory Animal Department of Sun Yat-sen University were used for this study. After a 2-week acclimation period, rats were randomized into experimental groups. OA modeling was achieved via the transection of the anterior cruciate ligament, resulting in the DMM within the knee joint, thereby mimicking the pathology in a site that is commonly impacted in humans with OA. At 1-week post-surgery, treadmill-based exercise regimens were initiated, with 30 min of exercise per day for a 4-week period.

  Twenty rats were randomly assigned to the normal control group (n = 4), sham operation group (n = 4), DMM group (n = 4), DMM + LMO7Inh group (n = 4), and DMM + shLMO7 group (n = 4). A further 20 healthy male SD rats were randomly allocated to a normal control group (n = 4), sham operation group (n = 4), DMM group (n = 4), DMM + LMO7 group (n = 4), and DMM + LMO7 + SIRT3 group (n = 4). The rats in the DMM + LMO7Inh, DMM + shLMO7, DMM + LMO7, and DMM + LMO7 + SIRT3 groups were injected in the knee joint with the LMO7 inhibitor or an siRNA designed to knock down/overexpress LMO7 and overexpress SIRT3. After 14 days, these rats were euthanized, and intact knee joints were harvested for fixation, decalcification, and verification of knockdown or overexpression efficiency. Samples showing successful interference in expression were used for subsequent experiments. The Experimental Animal Care Commission of Sun Yat-sen University approved this study, with all experiments having been performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional ethics committee and the NC3Rs ARRIVE guidelines.

  Human articular cartilage samples were collected from 13 patients at the Seventh Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University and Shanghai Outdo Biotech Company. Samples corresponding to areas of tissue exhibiting severe damage to the articular cartilage were assigned to the OA group, while those from areas with articular cartilage that was relatively intact were selected as controls. The Medical Ethics Committee of the Seventh Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University approved this study, with all patients having provided informed consent.


### qPCR
  The 2−ΔΔCt method was used to analyze relative gene expression in qPCR analyses of collected samples, with β-Tubulin and GAPDH serving as reference controls for normalization purposes [41]. LMO7 primer sequences were as follows: Forward—TCACAGCGAGACCTTTTGGCAC (5’−3’), Reverse—CTCTGCCCAAATCGAGTTGCTC (5’−3’).


### Western immunoblotting
  Western immunoblotting was performed to validate the bioinformatics results. RIPA buffer was used to lyse SW1353 and ATDC5 cells, followed by SDS-PAGE separation and transfer onto PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad, CA, USA). Membranes were then probed overnight with primary antibodies (1:1000) at 4℃, incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (ABclonal, A3610, Wuhan, China), and detected with a hypersensitive chemiluminescence kit (Feienbio, ES-0006, Wuhan, China) [42].


### CCK-8 and flow cytometry assays
  A CCK-8 assay was used as directed by the manufacturer. Briefly, cells that had been transfected were plated in 96-well plates (2,000/well) and then 10 μL of CCK-8 solution (KeyGEN, Nanjing) was added per well, measuring absorbance with a microplate reader (model Spark 10M, Shenyang, China) at 0, 4, 8, 16, 24, and 48 h post-seeding [43]. Flow cytometry was used to analyze apoptotic cell death by harvesting cells with EDTA-free trypsin (C0201, Beyotime, China), washing with PBS, and using an Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (KGA108, Nanjing KeyGen Biotech) as directed. Briefly, cells were resuspended with binding buffer, followed by staining for 10 min using Annexin V-FITC and Propidium Iodide. Samples were then analyzed with a flow cytometer (model A00-1–1102, Beckman, USA).


### EdU uptake assay
  A Cell-Light EdU Apollo®567 In Vitro Imaging Kit (RiboBio) was used to assess the uptake of EdU by cells. Briefly, cells were added to 96-well plates (2,000/well) in 100 μL of medium with 20 μM EdU. Following a 2 h incubation, cells were fixed for 30 min with 4% paraformaldehyde, washed with PBS, permeabilized for 10 min with 0.5% Triton X-100, washed again, stained for 30 min with Hoechst 33,342, and the proliferation rate was then measured as directed by the manufacturer. After a final wash with PBS, positive cells were detected with a fluorescence microscop e(Leica, Germany) [44].


### Cycloheximide assay
  IL-1β-treated chondrocytes were transfected with shLMO7. Forty-eight hours after transfection, the medium was replaced with fresh medium supplemented with cycloheximide (10 μg/ml), and SIRT3 levels were analyzed using Western immunoblotting after incubation for a further 0, 4, 8, or 16 h.


### GST pull-down assay
  Cell lysates and glutathione-Sepharose 4B beads (Samgon Biotech, China) coupled to GST fusion proteins were incubated at 4 °C for 2 h, followed by washing with GST-binding buffer. The bound proteins were separated on SDS-PAGE, followed by immunoblotting with appropriate antibodies.


### Immunoprecipitation and proteomic analyses
  Primary chondrocyte proteins were extracted using RIPA buffer (P0013; Beyotime) containing PMSF (Beyotime) and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime) (100:1:1 v/v/v). The lysates were incubated overnight with 1 μg of normal rat IgG (A7016; Beyotime) or an anti-LMO7 antibody (sc-376807; Novus) at 4 °C. The samples were then incubated for 4 h with 40 μL of pre-washed protein A/G PLUS-Agarose beads (sc-2003; Santa Cruz) at 4 °C with gentle agitation. Proteins were separated on 10% SDS-PAGE gels which were then stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue (P0017F; Beyotime). MS (Novogene) and immunoblotting were used to analyze the samples.


### Ubiquitination assays
  For in vitro ubiquitination assays, HEK293T cells were transfected with plasmids encoding Myc-Ub and Flag-SIRT3. After incubation for 48 h, the cells were treated with MG132 (10 µM) for an additional 6 h. The cells were then lysed and the Flag-SIRT3 protein was purified from the lysates using anti-Flag magnetic beads. To investigate the role of LMO7 in the ubiquitination process, cell lysates from cells expressing shLMO7 were prepared. The purified Flag-SIRT3 protein was incubated with the shLMO7-containing cell lysates in a ubiquitination buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl, 5 mM MgCl₂, 2 mM dithiothreitol, 10 mM ATP, pH 7.5, 10 mM) for 2 h in a 37 °C water bath.


### Hematoxylin–Eosin staining
  Routine protocols were used for the hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of cartilage samples. Briefly, following deparaffinization and rehydration, longitudinal 5 μm sections were stained for 5 min with hematoxylin, rinsed in 1% acid ethanol five times, stained for 3 min with eosin, dehydrated with an alcohol gradient, and mounted slides were then imaged with a fluorescence microscope.


### Safranin-O-Fast Green staining
  Cartilage and bone tissue analyses were performed via Safranin-O-Fast Green staining. After deparaffinizing and rehydrating these samples, they were stained with the Fast Green dye for an appropriate period of time for bone tissue staining, after which the cartilage was stained by immersing samples in the Safranin-O dye. Excess dye was next removed with an acidic differentiation solution, followed by the dehydration of the obtained sections, which were mounted and analyzed.


#### IHC
  IHC staining was performed by incubating paraffin-embedded tissue sections with anti-LMO7 (sc-376807; Novus) for 1 h at 37 °C, followed by a 10 min incubation with HRP-labeled streptavidin for 10 min and staining with hematoxylin and DAB. The percentage of stained tissue area was scored with the following scale: 1 (1–25%), 2 (26–50%), 3 (51–75%), and 4 (76–100%). The intensity of staining was scored from 0(no color) to 3(light yellow, brown, and dark brown). Three individuals who were blinded to clinical parameters performed all tissue scoring, and the two scores were multiplied together to generate a final IHC score.


### Small molecule screening and molecular docking analyses
  The CMap database (https://clue.io/query), a tool for identifying connections among small molecules sharing a mechanism of action, chemicals and physiological processes, and diseases and drugs, enables predictive drug analyses based on gene differential expression. This facilitates the study of functional links among genes, small molecules, and diseases of interest [45, 46]. In total, LMO7, which exhibited the highest expression levels within the MDCGS context, along with other highly expressed genes, was inputted into the CMap, with the top 10 most highly ranked small molecule ligands associated with these targets then being selected for analysis. The 3D structures of these 10 ligands were drawn in ChemBio 3D software (v14.0.0.21), and the MM2 algorithm was used for ligand energy minimization. Protein structures were obtained from the Protein Data Bank and UniProt, followed by preprocessing to remove water molecules and ligands, add hydrogen, optimize amino acids, and perform appropriate patching. Molecular docking interactions between the LMO7 protein and the selected 10 ligands were assessed with the AutoDock Tools (v 1.5.7) with a semi-flexible molecular docking method. Binding affinity values for these small molecules and associated targets were assessed based on the docking energy values in the PyMOL package.


### MDs
  When molecular docking was complete, MDs was used to verify the stability of the selected docking model with the CHARMM36 force field and GROMACS (v 2021.4-Ubuntu-2021.4–2). The three small molecules that exhibited the lowest binding free energy values were selected for MDs analyses of their interactions with LMO7. Topology files were generated for each small molecule and LMO7, placing the ligands on the protein. An appropriate water box was subsequently defined, and the electrical neutrality of the system was achieved by adding essential ions. Energy minimization and ligand point restraint were then performed for the system, followed by temperature and pressure equilibration at 300 K. MDs was then performed for 100 ns at a fixed temperature and pressure, applying periodic boundary conditions. MDs trajectories were calculated based on the RMSD and RMSF, visualizing the results with Xmgrace and the PyMOL package. The gmx gyrate tool in GROMACS was used to compute the Rg, and energy analyses entailed the calculation of the average short-range Coul-SR and the short-range LJ-SR. Free energy topography plotting was also performed, and the PyMOL package was used to obtain and visualize the low-energy conformations.


### Statistical analyses
  R (v 4.4.0) and appropriate packages, Python (v 2.5.0, 3.8.0, and 3.10.0), and GraphPad Prism (v 8.0.1) were used to analyze all data. Results are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared with the unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests, Mann–Whitney U test, or one-way ANOVAs. Categorical data were compared with chi-square tests. Correlative relationships between continuous variables were analyzed with Pearson’s correlation coefficients. Significance levels were denoted as *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001; ns (not significant).
