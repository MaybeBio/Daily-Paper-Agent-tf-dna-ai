# Vitamin D deficiency in chronic hepatitis B across the disease spectrum: association with viral activity rather than hepatocellular carcinoma


## Background
  Chronic liver disease is often associated with vitamin D deficiency. However, the relationship between vitamin D deficiency and HBV disease severity, viral activity, and HCC remains unclear. We evaluated whether vitamin D deficiency is linked to the disease stage and HBV viral activity in chronic hepatitis B (CHB) and whether HCC is an independent predictor of vitamin D status.


## Methods
  This study at Ibn Sina Diagnostic Center, Dhaka, included 510 participants: chronic hepatitis B (CHB, n = 210), liver cirrhosis (LC, n = 150), hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC, n = 100), and healthy controls (HC, n = 50). Serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D and HBV DNA were measured using standard assays and real-time PCR. Group comparisons, regression analyses, and exploratory in silico analyses (STRING PPI and docking with VDR and STAT1) were performed.


## Results
  According to the findings of multivariable analysis, the levels of HBV DNA were found to be independently related to baseline vitamin D status. In contrast to this, the levels of HCC were not found to be independently related after adjustment. Univariate analysis of vitamin D and HBV DNA showed a weak correlation, suggesting the necessity to model for confounders. Enrichment of host-guiding PPI illustrated interferon-associated immune pathways, whereas docking substantiated compatibility of a calcitriol molecule with VDR and STAT1, by context hypothesis generating usefulness.


## Conclusion
  Chronic HBV infection suggests that vitamin D status relates more to viral activity in the host immune regulatory milieu than to HCC as an independent determinant. Making a statement about explanatory chronology is the first step.

## Introduction
  Chronic hepatitis B (CHB) is a longstanding viral infection of the liver that is a major global health issue. Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infects millions of people globally, and over time, it can progress to cirrhosis of the liver and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [1]. The viral activity and host-related factors contribute to the clinical course and outcomes of HBV infection. Serum HBV DNA level is a parameter commonly used to assess the viral replication and activity of hepatitis B virus (HBV) disease and its progression. This parameter is important in the clinical assessment of patients with hepatitis B [2].

  In the case of HBV infection, the persistence and replication of the virus are not mainly due to the direct cytopathic effect of the virus but to host immunity. The innate immune mechanisms, especially the interferon-mediated signaling pathway, are crucial for the control of HBV activity [3]. Viral replication can be suppressed by interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) and activated by the immune regulators STAT1, STAT2, and IRF9. An impaired function of the immune regulatory pathways may enhance the viral load and disease progression. Hence, HBV viral activity should be considered as a host immune-regulated event rather than a virus-driven event [4, 5].

  Ketosteroid vitamin D is an important immunomodulator with well-recognized roles extending beyond the calcium and bone metabolism. The vitamin D receptor (VDR), which is present in many immune cells like macrophages, monocytes, and dendritic cells, is responsible for the biological effects of vitamin D [6]. According to the previous studies, mechanisms through which vitamin D influences the innate immune response, antigen presentation, and cytokine regulation [7]. Vitamin D immune modulation is linked to interferon-mediated signaling pathways. The correlation suggests a possible regulatory role for vitamin D in antiviral immune responses [8].

  Vitamin D deficiency is often found in people who have chronic liver disease. In people infected with the hepatitis B virus (HBV), vitamin D deficiency may be due to hepatocellular dysfunctions, nutritional deficiency, or abnormal immune and inflammatory status [9, 10]. However, it is uncertain whether vitamin D deficiency in people with chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) is associated with liver disease severity only or is independently associated with HBV viral activity [11]. Some existing studies have investigated whether there is a relationship between vitamin D status and HBV DNA levels, with some reports suggesting an association while others show a weak or no association [12, 13].

  The existence of hepatocellular carcinoma complicates this relationship. Patients suffering from HCC can exhibit lower levels of vitamin D, which might be due to hepatic failure, metabolic changes, or systemic inflammation [14]. Therefore, whether the association between vitamin D deficiency and HBV viral activity is due to a virus replicating or the severity of the disease and HCC is unknown. In many previous studies, there was inadequate adjustment for HCC status, making it impossible to clarify if and to what extent vitamin D independently contributes to HBV viral activity [15, 16].

  In this context, in the present study, we aimed to evaluate the association between baseline vitamin D status and HBV viral activity assessed by HBV DNA levels in chronic hepatitis B patients. One of the aims was to find out whether hepatocellular carcinoma is an independent determinant of vitamin D deficiency or other determinants of vitamin D status and viral activity continue to be independent of HCC. Besides, this study also aimed to investigate the biological plausibility of this association in the context of host immune regulation with a focus on vitamin D–related immune modulation and interferon-mediated host response. The purpose of this study, which integrates a clinical and immunological perspective, is to clarify the role of vitamin D in HBV infection and to strengthen a host-centered view of HBV viral activity.


## Materials and methods

### Study design and population
  An observational cross-sectional study was carried out at Ibn Sina Diagnostic Center, Dhaka, Bangladesh. The research consisted of 510 cases who were consecutively enrolled from 20 April 2024 to 12 August 2025. The study included patients with chronic hepatitis B at different stages, including chronic hepatitis B, liver cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma, and healthy controls who were being evaluated for routine health.

  The Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Ibn Sina Diagnostic Center, Dhaka, Bangladesh (Ethical Approval No.: 13384-7/2024/Table (719/2024)) approved the study protocol. All training exercises were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Helsinki Declaration [17]. Before enrollment, all participants provided their written informed consent.


### Study groups and definitions
  The categorization of participants was done into four ranges as per established clinical, laboratory, and imaging criteria. Those who were CHB positive were defined as those who were HBsAg positive for the past six months, had HBV DNA positive, and had no clinical or radiological evidence of liver cirrhosis or HCC. Liver cirrhosis (LC) was diagnosed based on clinical manifestations, laboratory abnormalities, and characteristic findings on ultrasonography or other imaging modalities. Diagnosis of hepatocellular carcinoma according to current clinical practice guidelines based on characteristic imaging features with/without increased serum alpha-fetoprotein level.

  Individuals without a liver disease history, negative for hepatitis B surface antigen, and with normal liver biochemical parameters were defined as healthy controls (HC). These people were enrolled during routine health screening and were used as controls for comparison.


### Inclusion and exclusion criteria
  Inclusion criteria consisted of (i) adults (≥ 18 years) with chronic hepatitis B and (ii) baseline serum vitamin D and HBV DNA (viral load) availability. Information from clinical and laboratory examinations as routinely done during admission was collected to fairly compare groups and run multivariable analyses.

  The following conditions were excluded from the study. These included co-infection with hepatitis C virus (HCV) or human immunodeficiency virus (HIV). It also included other chronic liver diseases besides HBV. Chronic liver diseases included autoimmune diseases. Chronic liver diseases also included alcoholism. Other established chronic liver etiologies were also included in the exclusion criteria. Severe systemic illness was also included. Finally, it also included other conditions that would affect vitamin D level or clinical outcomes.


### Clinical and laboratory assessment
  Age and sex information was retrieved from routine clinical records and included in the data collection. For the current study, hematological evaluation was observed (WBC, RBC, and PLT). The liver injury was assessed biochemically by measuring the levels of serum aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), total and direct bilirubin, and albumin.

  Prothrombin activity was used to assess hepatic synthetic function. Furthermore, serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) levels will be determined to assess the HBV disease spectrum and detect hepatocellular carcinoma. All laboratory analyses were done with standardized methods according to routine clinical protocols at Ibn Sina Diagnostic Center.


### Measurement of HBV markers
  The routine clinical laboratory protocols were referred to for the real-time polymerase chain reaction (real-time PCR) assays to quantify serum HBV DNA (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). The concentrations of HBV DNA were computed as log10 copies/mL and served as quantitative measurements of HBV viral activity.

  The standard assays for the serological diagnosis were used to evaluate Hepatitis B antigen (HBeAg) status (Vitros 5600 origin from USA). The results were categorized as either positive or negative, serving as an additional virological maker that reflects HBV replication and immune activity.


### Measurement and classification of vitamin D
  The measurement of serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] concentrations which is recognized as gold standard method of measuring vitamin D status. The routine clinical laboratory techniques at the Ibn Sina Diagnostic Center were used to measure vitamin D in ng/mL.

  The vitamin D status was categorized based on the serum concentration of 25(OH)D into four cut-off groups: normal (≥ 30 ng/mL), insufficient (20–29.9 ng/mL), deficient (10–19.9 ng/mL), and severely deficient (< 10 ng/mL). These categories were used for the subsequent analyses of clinical and virological parameters associations [18].

### Literature-driven conceptual model
  A conceptual systems model was formed using nonquantitative literature as a reference, which helps to explore the possible biological relationship between vitamin D signaling, host immune regulation, and HBV viral activity. This model only utilized previously published original studies and review articles and did not encompass the generation of new experimental or computational data.

  Researchers conducted literature searches using PubMed, Google Scholar, and Web of Science to look for studies on vitamin D receptor (VDR) signaling, interferon-activated immune pathways, STAT signaling, and the ability of the host system to control HBV replication. In the conceptual model, vitamin D–VDR signaling was depicted as an upstream regulator as compared with host immune pathways of interferon-mediated signaling as intermediate regulators influencing viral activity of HBV as reflected with HBV DNA levels. The model does not quantify nor imply cause-effect or therapeutic action.


### Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI) network analysis
  In order to further study host immune regulatory interplay, a Protein–Protein Interaction (PPI) network analysis was performed using the STRING database (STRING: functional protein association networks). We selected seed proteins with established roles in the vitamin D signaling and the interferon-mediated antiviral immune response that included STAT1, STAT2, IRF9, IFNAR1, OAS1, MX1, and vitamin D receptor (VDR).

  Only proteins from Homo sapiens were used in this analysis, and high-confidence interactions (confidence score ≥ 0.7) were included. The interaction network was visualized by using STRING network tools, while Cytoscape was additionally employed for graphical purposes wherever appropriate. This study was designed to provide a supporting mechanistic context for host immune regulation in HBV infection, without any emphasis on targeting viral proteins or quantitative predictions.


### Molecular docking
  Molecular docking analysis was conducted to investigate the plausibility of vitamin D-related host immune modulation. The human vitamin D receptor (VDR) and STAT1 were selected as target proteins due to being involved in the signaling of vitamin D and the interferon-mediated antiviral immune response, respectively. Calcitriol was used as the ligand. The chemical structure of calcitriol, or 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D3, was obtained from PubChem.

  Crystallographic structures of human VDR and STAT1 were obtained from the Protein Data Bank (VDR: PDB ID 1DB1, STAT1: PDB ID 1BF5) (RCSB PDB: Homepage). Simulations of molecular docking were carried out using PyRx and AutoDock Vina. Before docking, standard protein and ligand preparation steps were performed, including the addition of hydrogen, energy minimization, and format conversion.

  Predicted binding poses and binding energies (kcal/mol) were produced, where binding energy can be interpreted as a comparative indicator of the stability of ligand–protein interaction. In this study, no viral protein was included. This was solely exploratory and hosted protein-focused. The dock results are not claimed to provide evidence of any antiviral, therapeutic, or causative effects. They do, however, provide a supportive molecular context for potential interactions between vitamin D signaling and interferon-regulated host immune pathways that are in line with the clinical association between vitamin D status and HBV viral activity (HBV DNA).


### Statical analysis and software
  Statistical analyses were conducted with GraphPad Prism (v9.0). Continuous variables are published as either mean ± SD or median (range), while categorical ones are published as frequency. Appropriate statistical tests were used for group comparisons. One-way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis tests were used for continuous variables. Chi-square or Fischer’s exact tests were used for categorical variables. Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients were applied for correlation analyses, and regression analyses were applied to evaluate the factors associated with serum vitamin D levels and vitamin D deficiency. The docking studies were performed in PyRx (AutoDock Vina). All tests were double-sided with a p-value < 0.05 defined as statistically significant.


## Results

### Clinical characteristics

#### Baseline characteristics of the study population
  The study consisted of 510 participants comprising patients with chronic hepatitis B (CHB, n = 210), liver cirrhosis (LC, n = 150), hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC, n = 100), and healthy controls (HC, n = 50). Significant differences at the baseline concerning demographic and laboratory characteristics were observed in all four groups (Table 1).

  Compared with the CHB and healthy control groups, the LC and HCC groups were significantly older (p < 0.001). The study found that there was a male preponderance across all HBV-related groups. Moreover, the sex composition significantly differed among groups (p < 0.001).

  Significant differences were seen among the groups in hematological parameters, including white blood cell count, red blood cell count, and platelet count. The LC group had the lowest platelet counts (p < 0.001). The markers of liver injury were higher in AST and ALT in patients with CHB but were significantly lower in the LC and HCC groups (p < 0.05).

  Both total bilirubin and direct bilirubin levels were significantly increased in the LC and HCC groups (p < 0.01), and serum albumin levels and prothrombin activity were significantly decreased in the LC patients (p < 0.001). The levels of HBV DNA in patients with CHB were found to be the highest. However, the levels in LC and HCC patients were found to be comparatively lower. In the HCC group, serum AFP levels were significantly greater than those of the other groups (p < 0.001).

  Upon statistical analysis of the four study groups, significantly different results were observed. The healthy controls had the highest median level of vitamin D of 23.5 ng/mL, followed by CHB patients with 21.3 ng/mL and LC patients with 20.4 ng/mL. The HCC patients had the lowest level, with 19.9 ng/mL.

  In multivariate analysis, only HBV DNA was maintained as an independent association with baseline vitamin D (p = 0.0004). ### Correlation of vitamin D with clinical and virological variables
  A correlation analysis was conducted to ascertain the association of vitamin D with other clinical and virological parameters (Fig. 1). In general, we found weak correlations between vitamin D and most hematological and biochemical variables.

  The correlation between vitamin D and HBV DNA was approximately zero (r ≈ 0), suggesting that baseline vitamin D levels and viral levels do not share a simple bivariate relationship. In a similar manner, there was a very weak association between vitamin D and alpha-fetoprotein, where no strong correlation was present between vitamin D and hepatocellular carcinoma.

  The albumin, prothrombin activity, and platelet count related to liver function showed moderate correlations with each other, although the correlations of these parameters with vitamin D levels were weak. A meaningful relationship was not found between vitamin D and total bilirubin or direct bilirubin. On the whole, vitamin D levels do not correlate strongly and in a linear way with a majority of clinical or virological parameters. Hence, multivariable analyses would be advantageous to explore any independent associations as well.

### Risk stratification model of HBV DNA vs. vitamin D in chronic hepatitis B
  The correlation analysis showed that there was an inverse relationship between vitamin D and HBV DNA in HBeAg-positive patients. There was statistically significant relationship between the treated and untreated groups (1 = -1.9, p = 0.04), which implies that the greater the level of HBV DNA in the body, the lower the level of Vitamin D. The 95% confidence limits of these groups were (-3.2, -0.6) and (-3.0, -0.5), respectively. Nevertheless, the correlation was lower and not significant in HBeAg-negative patients with p- values of 0.12 (treated) and 0.18 (untreated) which points to moderate but uncertainties in the relationship (Table 5).

  No significant correlation was observed in the healthy control group (0.02 = 0.85), indicating that the effect of HBV DNA does not have any effect on the Vitamin D levels of healthy people. These results further support the idea that HBV DNA levels are a more important determining factor in the status of Vitamin D in HBeAg-positive groups, whereas, the relationship is less evident in HBeAg-negative groups and the association is even non-existent in healthy controls.

### In silico study

#### Conceptual framework of vitamin D–immune–HBV interactions
  As shown in Fig. 3, a conceptual model that was developed based on literature, it highlights non-quantitative interactions between vitamin D signaling, the host immunity, and HBV viral activities. The model includes the VDR activation by calcitriol (active vitamin D) as an upstream signaling event that alters the innate immune responses through macrophages/monocytes and immature dendritic cells. At this stage, modifications to cytokines, antigen presentation, and macrophage formation occur, and this indicates an immunomodulatory environment [19, 20].

  The immune regulation layer of the host defense system has introduced the interferon-mediated signaling where the STAT1 pathway, through direct involvement of the central component, first effected the downstream functional immune response and regulatory cytokines, including IL-10, among others. Components of the adaptive immune system, inclusive of T-cell subsets (Treg, Th1, and Th17) and B-cell responses, are related to immune balance and inflammatory activity via the immune regulatory processes [21].

  HBV viral activity, downstream of the core model, is illustrated by HBV DNA levels. The model indicates no direct correlation between vitamin D and HBV viral activity; rather, they suggest a potential indirect link via host immune modulation. In sum, the conceptual framework suggests biological plausibility for the observed clinical link between vitamin D status and HBV DNA without indicating causality or therapeutic significance.

### Protein-protein interaction network analysis
  A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed with data from the literature to investigate host immune pathways that may link vitamin D–related biology with HBV viral activity (Fig. 4). Insights gained from high-confidence interactions (STRING score ≥ 0.7) depicted a highly connected immune regulatory hub that contains various interferon signaling components such as STAT1, STAT2, IRF9, and IFNAR1. Strong interactions were observed between these core regulators and various interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs), including OAS1, OAS2, OAS3, MX1, IFI6, and IFI27, showcasing a coordinated antiviral host response that relates to the control of HBV replication.

  Fig. 4Host immune protein-protein interaction network relevant to HBV viral activity. The high-confidence PPI network highlights interferon-mediated immune regulators (STAT1/STAT2/IRF9, IFNAR1) and associated interferon-stimulated genes involved in host control of HBV replication. Vitamin D–related components (CYP27B1) are shown as upstream contextual regulators, while the HBV entry receptor SLC10A1 remains peripheral. The network is literature-derived and does not imply causality or therapeutic effect

  Positioned upstream, vitamin D–related components, represented by CYP27B1, were indirectly associated with the interferon-mediated immune hub, promoting the notion that vitamin D signaling would have a modulatory rather than direct antiviral role. On the other hand, SLC10A1 (NTCP), the HBV entry receptor, was peripheral and did not integrate into the core immune interaction network, suggesting that the associations may not be driven by entry. Significantly, cancer-related pathways were not critical in the network architecture, adding weight to the idea that hepatocellular carcinoma is a stage of HBV disease rather than an independent driver of vitamin D–immune interactions. This PPI examination bolsters an immune-centered paradigm where vitamin D status may impact HBV viral activity through host interferon-regulated pathways.


#### Host immune pathway enrichment in vitamin D-HBV interaction
  Figure 5 presents functional enrichment of host immune pathways derived from the protein–protein interaction network, where bubble size represents the number of associated genes. The analysis shows predominant enrichment of interferon-mediated antiviral and innate immune response pathways, driven by STAT1, STAT2, IRF9, and multiple interferon-stimulated genes. These pathways are functionally linked to host regulation of HBV replication and HBV DNA levels. Vitamin D–related signaling is positioned as an upstream biological context influencing these immune regulatory pathways. Cancer-specific or hepatocellular carcinoma–related pathways are not prominently enriched, indicating that the association between vitamin D and HBV viral activity is primarily mediated through host immune regulation rather than HCC as an independent determinant.

#### Molecular docking analysis
  To investigate the molecular possibility of vitamin D-related host immune modulation, docking analysis was performed between calcitriol with its receptor, vitamin D receptor (VDR), and STAT1 protein. The docking results indicated that calcitriol may bind stably within the defined binding pockets of the two target proteins (Fig. 6).

  Fig. 6A and B show calcitriol bound to the vitamin D receptor (VDR) and STAT1, illustrating ligand orientation on each host protein, while C and D depict the corresponding hydrogen bond and van der Waals interactions. Together, the figure supports a host-centered, immune-modulatory role of vitamin D at the molecular level

  The orientation of the VDR–calcitriol complex revealed that the ligand was placed in the ligand-binding domain of the receptor and made several vans der Waals interactions together with normal hydrogen bonds (Figures A, C). Based on these interactions, a favorable binding orientation is deduced with an estimated binding energy of ∼−6.7 kcal/mol. This trend is in line with the known molecular compatibility of calcitriol with VDR as the primary mediator of vitamin D action.

  Calcitriol’s docking with the STAT1 protein placed it on a possible regulatory surface at which numerous non-covalent interactions were formed (Figures B, D). The complex’s stability, characterized by hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions, was slightly more favorable in binding energy (around − 7.2 kcal/mol) as compared to S. Owing to the centrality of STAT1 in the interferon-mediated anti-viral immuno-responses, this interaction provides molecular context supportive of potential vitamin D-related modulation at the level of host immunity.

  Noteworthy is the complete absence of viral proteins in the docking analysis, which was entirely focused on hosting proteins. The results do not show any direct antiviral or therapeutic effects but may conceptually highlight a possible connection between vitamin D signaling and interferon-regulated host immune pathways at the molecular level in agreement with the clinical association of vitamin D status with HBV viral activity (HBV DNA).


## Discussion
  According to the present study, vitamin D deficiency was frequently seen along the HBV disease spectrum, consistent with prior reports highlighting the high prevalence of low vitamin D levels in chronic liver diseases and HBV infection. Our analysis identified HBV DNA levels as an independent predictor of baseline serum vitamin D, suggesting that viral activity significantly influence vitamin D status, independently of disease stage and clinical confounders. These finding reinforce the idea that vitamin D status is more strongly with HBV replication or immune-viral activity, rather than merely reflecting disease stage, as suggested by observational study.

  Although univariate analysis showed an association between hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and vitamin deficiency, this association did not remain significant after multivariable adjustment, suggesting that HCC is not an independent predictor of vitamin D deficiency in this cohort. Rather, the observed unadjusted association may reflect the broader context of advanced HBV-related liver disease, where hepatic disfunction, impaired nutritional status, reduced sunlight exposure, and systemic inflammation may all contribute to lower vitamin levels [22–24]. In addition, correlation analysis showed a week or absent direct linear relationship between vitamin D and HBV DNA. The relationship between vitamin D status and viral activity is complex that showed association in multivariable modeling and indicates that with careful adjustment for potential confounders. Consequently, rather from being a direct reflection of HCC or an apparent linear correlate of HBV DNA, vitamin D status in chronic HBV infection may be most useful as a measure related with the host immune-viral milieu.

  Using the host immunity framework to interpret the association between vitamin D status and HBV DNA offers a more nuanced perspective than focusing solely on its antiviral effect. Vitamin D is primarily known as immune-modulating hormone, acting predominantly through the vitamin D receptor (VDR) [25–27]. VDR is expressed in various immune cell, including monocytes/macrophages, dendritic cells, and subsets of adaptive immune subsets [28, 29]. The ability of VDR signaling regulation cytokine producing, modulate antigen presentation, and activate innate immune responses is crucial in controlling viral infections and maintaining immune homeostasis [7]. Furthermore, host defense mechanisms, particularly those driven by interferon, play a key role in the persistence and replication of virous, including HBV, in chronic infection of [30, 31]. The connection between vitamin D and HBV activity is noteworthy; however, it is crucial to understand that the immune response involved, rather than any direct antiviral effects, likely account for the relationship observed.

  The interferon/STAT1 pathway is at the forefront of antiviral immunity because STAT1-utilizing signaling activates interferon-stimulated genes that help to create an antiviral state and suppress viral activity. Consequently, changes in vitamin D–VDR signaling may reflect or influence variations in immune regulatory capacity, linking vitamin D status to the overall immune–viral activity milieu captured by HBV DNA levels [32–34].

  These studies show that low serum vitamin D concentration levels are associated with higher HBV replication or viral load in chronic HBV patients. Our literature-driven conceptual framework structurally aligns with this model by positioning vitamin D–VDR signaling upstream of interferon-related host immune pathways, acting as an intermediate layer that modulates that modulates viral action [35, 36]. Moreover, STRING-base PPI analysis further reinforces our immune-centric interpretation through enrichment analysis, which highlights the interferon/innate antiviral response pathway [37, 38]. However, it is important to note that the observed relationships should be interpreted with caution, as confounding factors such as sunlight exposure, diet, and immune status may also contribute to the interplay between vitamin D and viral activity.

  Strong connectivity has been observed between core immune-related nodes, including STAT1/STAT2/IRF9, which is plausible and consistent with the role of immune regulation in viral infection [39, 40]. In conclusion, the docking studies of calcitriol, showing its compatibility with host targets like VDR and STAT1, provide a potential framework for understanding vitamin D -immune crosstalk [41–43]. Significantly, these in silico factors are meant to generate hypotheses and do not prove causation or treatment efficacy. However, these in silico finding should be considered as hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of causality or treatment efficacy.

  Although an HCC-related signal was seen in univariate comparisons, the association did not persist after multivariable adjustment, suggesting that HCC is not an independent predictor of vitamin D status in this cohort. The observed trend is more consistent with advanced HBV disease and the confounding effects associated with it, such as hepatic dysfunction, impaired metabolic, malnutrition, and inflammation, which are more prevalent in later stages of HBV infection [44–46].

  In chronic liver disease, low 25(OH)D levels are often associated with hepatic disfunction, reduced transport/metabolic capacity (e.g., reduced albumin and vitamin D-binding proteins), malnutrition, reduced sunlight exposure, and systemic inflammation, all of which contribute to vitamin D deficiency and are more common in advanced stages where HCC occurs [47]. The fact that the association between HCC and vitamin D disappeared after considering demographic and clinical factors suggests that HCC should be interpreted as a contextual factor within the HBV disease progression, rather than a direct driver of vitamin D dysfunction. There is extensive evidence that that vitamin D deficiency is common in HBV-related liver disease and is more strongly linked to disease severity and the host’s immune-metabolic state than to cancer status alone [48, 49].

  The analysis revealed a statistically significant inverse correlation (β = −1.9, p = 0.04) between HBV DNA and vitamin D levels in HBeAg-positive patients, indicating that higher HBV DNA levels are associated with lower vitamin D levels [10]. However, the correlation was weaker and not statistically significantly in HBeAg-negative patients (p = 0.12 for treated, p = 0.18 for untreated). These findings are consistent with the regulation of the tumor immune microenvironment, as discussed in recent studies on immune modulation networks, such as role in cancer progression and immune modulation [12]. These finding highlight the need for the establishment of a risk stratification model, specific vitamin D monitoring frequencies, and subgroup analyses based on HBeAg status and antiviral treatment history to better understand which patient populations would benefit most from vitamin D monitoring [34].

  A significant limitation in this study is the age difference the groups, which could introduce confounding. While the multivariable model controlled for age, the large age span between the healthy control (HC) group and the liver cirrhosis (LC) and HCC groups may still have influenced the observed associations. Age is a known factor affecting vitamin D metabolism and can influence the immune response [50, 51]. Given the median age of the HC group was 39 years, compared to 56 and 55 years for LC and HCC groups respectively, it is possible that this age disparity could have contributed to vitamin D deficiency, independent of HBV infection status. This issue could be mitigated by utilizing propensity score matching (PSM) or other methods to match the groups more effectively by age to reduce this potential bias. Without such adjustments, age-elated confounders could skew the relationship between vitamin D and HBV activity [52–54].

  The in-silico analysis should be viewed as exploratory and host protein-centric, rather than proof of a causal mechanism. These finding offer contextual support for generating hypothesis but should not be interpreted as conclusive evidence of biological action [55–57].

  The result suggest that vitamin D status may serve as a supportive marker of the host immune-viral activity milieu in chronic HBV infection, with the association between vitamin D and HBV DNA persisting in adjusted analysis [47, 54, 58]. Routine monitoring of vitamin D status in HBV patients, especially in those at higher risk and at advanced disease stages, may help in clinical profiling [32, 41, 59]. However, the current findings do not support the recommendation of vitamin D supplementation for antiviral activity. The therapeutic role of vitamin D supplementation, if any, should be confirmed by well-designed clinical trials. Therefore, this study should be considered as providing observational and hypothesis-supporting evidence, not treatment recommendations.

  Building upon the current findings, future research should explore the role of lymphatic vessel density and its integration with microanatomical features in understanding the interplay between vitamin D status and viral activity in chronic HBV infection. Additionally, a more comprehensive approach, such as precision medicine strategies that incorporate metabolic, virological, and microenvironmental parameters, should be considered [60].


## Conclusion
  Vitamin D deficiency is prevalent and varies according to clinical stage in this study of HBV patients. Multivariable analyses identified HBV DNA as an independent correlate for baseline vitamin D status. Although an association with HCC was observed in unadjusted comparisons, it did not persist after adjustment, suggesting that HCC is not an independent determinant of vitamin D status. These findings imply that vitamin D status is more closely associated with the immune-viral activity milieu than with cancer status. The in-silico analyses, including conceptual modelling, PPI enrichment, and exploratory docking, provide biological plausibility for this relationship, though they are hypothesis-generating and not causal. Further research is needed to explore this connection and validate the clinical relevance of these findings.
