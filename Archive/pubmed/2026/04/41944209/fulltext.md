# ROWVA: A Structure‐Based Metric for Predicting the Pathogenicity of Protein Variants Using Alphafold2


## ABSTRACT
  p53, an important tumor suppressor protein, functions as a tetramer. Therefore, malignant variants in the tetramer‐forming domain increase the likelihood of p53 dysfunction. Recent developments in genome analysis technology have expanded our understanding of malignant variants. However, variants of uncertain significance are also being increasingly identified. Hence, methods to assess the pathogenicity of these variants are required. In this study, we aimed to examine whether AlphaFold2 can be used to evaluate the functional impacts of p53 variants based on predicted three‐dimensional (3D) structural information. For each variant present in datasets of p53 functional score, we performed 3D structural prediction using AlphaFold2. We analyzed the correlations among multiple AlphaFold2‐derived scores to predict functional scores, such as protein stability and pathogenicity labels, for each dataset. The root‐mean‐square deviation obtained by comparing the 3D structures predicted by AlphaFold2 for the wild‐type and variant structures showed a high correlation with each functional score. Overall, these findings indicate that AlphaFold2 can be used to evaluate variants.


## Introduction
  p53 is a crucial tumor suppressor protein comprising 393 amino acids [1, 2]. As a tetrameric transcription factor that binds to DNA, p53 is involved in mechanisms that regulate cell fate [3, 4, 5]. p53 is activated in response to stress signals associated with cancer development and malignancy, suppressing tumors through cell‐cycle arrest or apoptosis [6]. Therefore, p53 dysfunction is closely associated with cancer onset and progression [7]. The TP53 gene encodes p53, and its domain‐specific genetic variations influence cancer onset and progression.

  Approximately 50% of human cancers exhibit p53 variants, with approximately 90% of oncogenic variants classified as missense variants [8, 9]. Malignant missense variants in p53 occur at different frequencies across domains. p53 comprises six domains [10]: two transcriptional activation domains (TAD1, TAD2), a proline‐rich domain (PRD), a DNA‐binding domain (DBD), a tetramerization (TET) domain, and a regulatory (REG) domain (Figure 1). Among these, the DBD has been the most extensively studied. DBD consists of around 200 amino acid residues. Approximately 86% of p53 mutations observed in cancer occur within the DBD [11, 12]. In contrast, approximately 17% of p53 germline mutations found in Li‐Fraumeni syndrome and Li‐Fraumeni‐like syndromes occur within the TET, although it comprises short amino acids (~30 residues) [13]. Given the frequency of malignant variants per residue, the TET domain is as crucial to p53 function as the DBD.

  Recent progress has been made in personalized cancer genomic medicine. This approach involves analyzing a patient's cancer tissue to identify genetic characteristics, enabling the selection of treatments tailored to the patient's condition and genetic profile. Factors, including p53, that can contribute to cancer development are evaluated in genetic testing for cancer genomic medicine. The results of genetic testing in the field of cancer genomic medicine can be classified into five categories based on the likelihood of benign or malignant potential: pathogenic (P), likely pathogenic (LP), likely benign (LB), benign (B), and variants of uncertain significance (VUS) [14]. VUS are genetic variants that cannot be functionally classified due to insufficient evidence [15]. As genomic technologies advance and genetic testing becomes more affordable, its adoption has grown [16]. This increase in genetic testing has led to the identification of additional VUS. VUS can cause anxiety for patients owing to the ambiguity in diagnosis, highlighting the need to determine whether VUS are associated with pathogenicity [15]. Each year, VUS in p53 are identified in genomic medicine, necessitating methods to assess their pathogenicity.

  In previous studies, the functional impact of VUS was measured using wet‐lab experimental methods, such as saturation genome editing [17]. However, variant evaluation via such wet‐lab methods is time‐consuming and costly, rendering wet‐lab experiments impractical as the number of VUS increases. This limitation has driven the need for more efficient evaluation techniques. To address this, dry‐lab methods employing in silico techniques that use information such as amino acid primary sequences have been developed to evaluate variants [18]. Nevertheless, conventional in silico methods have not effectively incorporated advanced information, such as the three‐dimensional (3D) structure of proteins.

  AlphaFold2 [19] (AF2), developed by DeepMind, is an artificial intelligence system that uses deep‐learning models to predict the 3D structures of proteins with high accuracy from their amino acid sequences. AF2 demonstrated substantially higher accuracy than other methods in the CASP14 protein structure prediction competition. AF2 can even predict the 3D structures of proteins with missense variants whose structures have not been experimentally determined, provided the amino acid sequence is known, making it potentially useful for VUS evaluation. However, whether AF2 can be applied for structural predictions of missense variants remains under debate. For instance, some studies, including research conducted by the developers of AF2, argue that the performance of AF2 decreases when predicting the structures of proteins with missense variants [20, 21]. In contrast, some studies claim that using AF2 to predict protein structures with missense variants facilitates effective assessment of variant pathogenicity based on the resulting structures [22]. Integrating these perspectives, although AF2 can successfully predict the structural impact of some missense variants, it fails to do so in other cases. Therefore, it is necessary to verify which missense‐variant proteins enable accurate structural prediction and to characterize them. If missense variant proteins can be successfully predicted structurally, this could also contribute to assessing their pathogenicity.

  Previous studies have measured various functional scores, such as protein thermal stability [23], tetramer formation ability [13], and pathogenicity labels [24], to evaluate p53 variants.

  This study examined the utility of AF2 in evaluating the functional implications of p53 variants using predicted 3D structural data. Specifically, four AF2‐derived scores were calculated from the TET domain structure of p53 as predicted by AF2. Among these, three—interface predicted template modeling plus predicted template modeling (ipTM + pTM) [25], predicted aligned error (PAE) [26], and per‐residue local distance difference test (pLDDT) [27]—are established metrics in the literature. Furthermore, we developed a new score based on the root mean square deviation (RMSD) between the wild‐type and variant structures predicted by AlphaFold2, termed ROWVA (root mean square deviation between wild‐type and variant structures predicted by AlphaFold2). ROWVA is determined by extracting sequences restricted to the domain of interest, predicting 3D structures for wild‐type and variant proteins with AlphaFold, and subsequently comparing the variant structures with their wild‐type counterparts. Through correlation and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses, we assessed the ability of each AF2‐derived score to predict p53 functional outcomes and pathogenicity classifications across multiple datasets. Our findings suggest that ROWVA demonstrates superior predictive performance. This comparative approach, leveraging AF2‐predicted structures of wild‐type and missense variants, has potential applicability to other multimeric proteins.


## Materials and Methods

### Study Overview
  First, we extracted and compiled a list of genetic variants within the TET domain of p53 that result in missense mutations and are linked to functional scores (protein stability, thermodynamic, and transcriptional activity scores) and pathogenicity labels, based on previous literature [13, 23, 24, 28]. Next, we predicted the 3D TET tetramer structures of wild‐type and each missense variant using AF2. From the predicted structures, we calculated four AF2‐derived scores, including ROWVA—the new RMSD‐based score developed in this study. Subsequently, we investigated the correlations between these scores and p53 functional scores from previous studies [13, 23, 24, 28] and from our newly conducted experiments. Finally, we evaluated the accuracy of these scores in predicting variant pathogenicity labels based on ClinVar data. In our correlation analyses between transcriptional activity scores and their predictive accuracy for pathogenicity labels, we also included scores from established pathogenicity prediction tools—AlphaMissense [29], SIFT [30], and PolyPhen‐2 [31]—as comparative references.


### Extraction of p53 Genetic Variants
  The genetic variants within the TET domain of p53 included in this study were obtained from ThermoMutDB [23], the results of a previous investigation assessing the thermal stability of tetramers using peptides [13], and the TP53 [28] and ClinVar [24] databases. For each source, only missense variants corresponding to residues 325–356 of p53 were considered.


### Tetramer Structure of TET for p53 Prediction Using AF2
  Protein structure prediction was performed using AF2 [19] v.2.3.2. The amino acid sequence of wild‐type p53 (393 residues) was retrieved from UniProt (ID: P04637), from which 32 residues corresponding to the TET domain (residues 325–356) were extracted (Figure 1). Sequences were identified based on the wild‐type sequence, introducing amino acid substitutions corresponding to each variant. Each amino acid sequence, including both the wild‐type and variants, was input into AF2 using its AlphaFold‐Multimer [32] to predict the tetrameric structures of the TET domain for p53. Among the 25 predicted structures generated for each sequence, the structure with the highest confidence score (ipTM + pTM [25]) was selected and further refined as the relaxed_model. This refined structure was adopted as the representative structure for each variant.


### Calculation of AlphaFold2‐Derived Scores
  The following AF2‐derived scores were calculated: ipTM + pTM, pLDDT, PAE, and ROWVA. The ipTM + pTM score is a metric that evaluates the confidence of the predicted protein structure [32]. The higher the ipTM + pTM score, the closer the predicted structure is to the actual structure. The ipTM + pTM score was obtained from the ranking_debug.json file generated by AF2. The pLDDT score represents the confidence level for each amino acid in the predicted structure [19]. The higher the pLDDT score, the more reliable the position of each amino acid in the predicted structure. The pLDDT values for all residues were extracted from the .pdb files representing each predicted structure. The average pLDDT value for all residues was calculated and defined as the pLDDT score for each protein.

  The PAE score is a matrix that computes the predicted positional error for all residue pairs in the predicted structure [33]. Higher PAE values indicate lower reliability in the positional relationship of those residue pairs. The PAE score was derived from the .pkl file of the model with the highest predicted confidence by calculating the PAE between each pair of residues and taking the average as the score.

  The ROWVA score is the RMSD between the wild‐type and variant structures. To calculate ROWVA, the amino acid sequence is first restricted to the relevant functional domains, and then the three‐dimensional structures of the wild‐type and mutant proteins are predicted using AF2. The resulting structures are then compared by RMSD. The higher the ROWVA score, the greater the difference between the predicted and wild‐type structures. The RMSD was calculated as the square root of the average squared distance between corresponding atoms in the superimposed proteins using the align command in PyMOL v.2.5.0 (www.pymol.org). RMSD is computed as follows: RMSD=1N∑i=1Ndi2 where N represents the number of atoms in the ligand, and d  i  indicates the Euclidean distance between the i‐th pair of corresponding atoms. A set of scripts for calculating ROWVA is available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.


### Extraction of p53 Variant Data
  Experimental data on the effects of variants on protein stability were obtained from ThermoMutDB [23] (version 1.3). The data on the effects of variants on peptide thermodynamic scores measured experimentally were obtained from a previously published study [13]. These details are described in Data S1. The final dataset and corresponding metrics are presented in Tables S1 and S2.


### TP53 Germline Variants
  All information, including amino acid mutations, regarding the TP53 germline missense variants was downloaded from The TP53 Database [28] (R20, July 2019 version; https://tp53.cancer.gov).


### Generation of TP53 Knockout hTERT‐HME1 Cell Line (hTERT‐HME1‐TP53KO)
  The TP53‐knockout hTERT‐HME1 cell line was generated as described in Data S1. All oligonucleotide primers are presented in Table S3.


### Luciferase Assay and Western Blot Analysis
  Luciferase assay and western blot analyses were carried out as described in Data S1. The complete dataset and corresponding metrics are summarized in Table S4.


### ClinVar‐Based Selection of p53 Missense Variants
  The dataset containing p53 TET domain missense variants, labeled according to pathogenicity, was obtained from ClinVar [24] as described in Data S1. The final dataset and corresponding metrics are summarized in Table S5.


### Machine Learning‐Based Pathogenicity Prediction Scores
  Scores from representative machine learning‐based pathogenicity prediction methods—AlphaMissense [29], SIFT [30], and PolyPhen‐2 [31]—were obtained as described in Data S1. The final dataset and corresponding metrics are summarized in Tables S4 and S5.


### Calculation of Distance Between TET Chain Residues
  The distance between each residue of interacting chains in the wild‐type conformation of the p53 TET domain was calculated using the distance command in PyMOL v.2.5.0 (www.pymol.org). As one strand consists of 32 residues, a total of 1024 different distances were calculated. The complete data set is shown in Table S6.


### Evaluation of ROWVA Performance in PTEN
  To examine whether the p53 validation approach is applicable to other proteins, we also performed analyses on PTEN. Details of the methods are provided in Data S1. The final dataset and corresponding metrics are summarized in Table S7.


### Statistical Analysis
  Correlation analysis between scores was conducted using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient using Python (version 3.11.2). A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To evaluate pathogenicity prediction performance for each score in the ClinVar dataset, ROC curves and the area under the curve (AUC) were calculated. The ROC curve illustrated the relationship between the sensitivity and specificity of each AF2‐derived score. The AUC served as an indicator of the overall discriminative ability of each AF2‐derived score. ROC curves were plotted, and AUC values were calculated using the scikit‐learn library in Python (version 3.11.2), specifically employing the roc_curve and auc functions.


## Results

### Correlation Between AF2‐Derived Scores and Protein Stability Scores
  We investigated the correlation between thermodynamic stability scores and each AF2‐derived score for the wild‐type and 28 genetic variants within the TET domain of p53. The correlations with protein stability score were negative and significant for PAE (r = −0.368, p < 0.05) and ROWVA (r = −0.368, p < 0.05; Figure 2). Hence, variants with higher PAE or ROWVA values exhibited decreased protein stability.


### Correlation Between AF2‐Derived Scores and Tetramerization Scores
  Correlation analyses were performed for AF2‐derived scores and tetramerization scores for genetic variants within the TET domain of p53 (Figure 3). The correlations for the five tetramerization scores were significant only for ROWVA (p < 0.05). ROWVA positively correlated with two metrics: ΔΔGu  Tm, which represents the difference in ΔG between wild‐type and mutant peptides at the Tm of the wild‐type peptide, and the K  d  value of the tetramer–monomer transition. The correlation coefficients with ROWVA were negative for three metrics: the tetramer score, which represents the proportion of tetramer formation, the transition temperature (Tm), and ΔHu  Tm, which indicates variation in the enthalpy of unfolding at Tm. These results suggest that variants with higher ROWVA values exhibited a more unstable structure compared to the wild‐type and were more prone to dissociation. They also exhibited a lower proportion of tetramer formation, a lower temperature at which 50% of the peptide undergoes denaturation, and a reduced amount of energy required for peptide denaturation.


### Correlation Between AF2‐Derived Scores and Post‐Tetramerization Expression Levels
  The TP53 protein forms a tetramer and induces transcriptional activity by binding to the promoter regions of target genes [34]. To investigate whether mutations in the TET domain of TP53 affect its transcriptional activity, we first generated TP53‐knockout normal mammary epithelial cells (HME1‐TP53KO) using the CRISPR/Cas9 method (Figure 4A). Next, we constructed plasmids expressing 24 different variants registered in the TP53 database and introduced them into HME1‐TP53KO cells, together with a reporter plasmid (pGL4.85) containing a TP53‐binding domain upstream of the luciferase gene (Figure 4B and Figure S1). The results were then compared with AF2‐derived scores and those obtained from pathogenicity prediction tools such as AlphaMissense, SIFT, and PolyPhen‐2 (Figure 4C). The correlation with the TP53 transcriptional activity score was significant for all AF2‐derived scores, while among the scores from pathogenicity prediction tools, only the AlphaMissense score showed a significant correlation. The largest correlation coefficient was observed for ROWVA (r = −0.698, p < 0.05).


### ROC Analysis of the ClinVar Dataset
  We performed ROC analysis to evaluate the performance of both AF2‐derived scores and scores from pathogenicity prediction tools in classifying wild‐type and 21 genetic variants within the TET domain of p53 as benign or pathogenic. Considering the characteristics of each AF2‐derived score, the ipTM + pTM and pLDDT scores were predicted to be pathogenic with smaller values, whereas the PAE and ROWVA scores were predicted to be pathogenic with larger values. Among the AF2‐derived scores, ROWVA showed the highest AUC (0.90, 95% CI: 0.76–1.00; Figure 5). Among the scores derived from pathogenicity prediction tools, AlphaMissense had the highest AUC (0.98, 95% CI: 0.92–1.00).


### Additional Analysis of Residue 347
  A347D showed particularly high ROWVA values in the ClinVar dataset. Figure 6A,B show the wild‐type and A347D 3D structures, respectively, displayed in PyMOL. It is clear that these two structures are very different. We performed additional analyses on A347D. First, we measured the distance between each residue of the interacting strand (Chain A and Chain D in Figure 6A) in the wild‐type conformation (Table S6) and found that the distance between residues 347 and 347 was the smallest. Next, ROWVA was calculated using variants with amino acid substitutions at residue 347, incorporating 19 different changes (Figure 6C); A347T and A347M showed higher ROWVA values than A347D.


## Discussion
  We employed AF2 to predict the 3D structures of each variant from its amino acid sequence. Subsequently, we calculated four scores—ipTM + pTM, PAE, pLDDT, and ROWVA—using the predicted structural data. These calculated scores were correlated with three p53 functional scores specific to each dataset: monomeric protein stability, tetramerization capacity, and post‐tetramerization expression levels. Variants with higher ROWVA values tended to exhibit reduced monomeric protein stability, diminished tetramerization capacity, and possibly lower gene expression driven by tetrameric proteins. Furthermore, we assessed the predictive accuracy of ClinVar pathogenicity labels based on AF2‐derived scores using AUC. We configured the analysis such that low scores on ipTM + pTM and pLDDT, and high scores on PAE and ROWVA, indicated a disadvantage. Among the AF2‐derived scores, ROWVA had the highest AUC. AF2‐based structural prediction of variants within the TET domain of p53 suggests the potential utility of AF2 for predicting p53 tetramer formation capacity and pathogenicity of missense variants.

  ROWVA demonstrated the best performance among the four AF2‐derived scores in terms of correlation with p53 functional scores and in predicting variant harmfulness. The superior performance of ROWVA among the AF2‐derived scores is likely attributable to its unique focus on evaluating predicted tertiary structures, as opposed to other metrics that primarily assess the reliability of structural predictions. The ipTM + pTM score combines two metrics: the pTM score, which reflects confidence in the predicted protein 3D structure, and the ipTM score, which indicates confidence in inter‐residue interactions within the predicted structure [25]. The PAE score measures the reliability of the predicted relative positions of specific residue pairs [26], whereas the pLDDT score indicates the confidence in the structural prediction for each residue [27]. These scores are thus primarily related to the reliability of the predicted structures. A possible explanation for the lack of significant correlation between ipTM + pTM, PAE, and pLDDT with p53 functional scores across datasets is that certain variants—although associated with high confidence in predicted structures—may adversely affect tetramer formation. Initially, we hypothesized that benign variants would adopt stable tertiary structures similar to those of the wild‐type, resulting in high prediction confidence, whereas malignant variants would have altered structures, leading to lower prediction confidence. However, the absence of a correlation between prediction confidence scores and functional scores suggests the presence of variants that contradict this initial assumption, namely malignant variants that still achieve high prediction confidence. This suggests that AF2 may be capable of predicting the tertiary structures of malignant variants with significant structural changes, without compromising accuracy, in addition to accurately predicting the structures of wild‐type proteins. Therefore, unlike the other three metrics, ROWVA, which was calculated by comparing the predicted tertiary structures of wild‐type and variant proteins, showed significant correlations with scores related to tetramer formation and other functional properties.

  In the correlation analysis of the scores for each dataset (protein stability scores, tetramerization scores, and post‐tetramerization expression scores), ROWVA exhibited the highest correlation coefficient among all AF2‐derived scores across all datasets. This suggests that variants with higher ROWVA values may exhibit greater structural changes in p53, potentially negatively affecting protein stability, tetramerization capacity, and gene expression driven by tetrameric proteins. Similarly, in predicting ClinVar pathogenicity labels, ROWVA demonstrated the highest accuracy. This indicates that variants with higher ROWVA values are more likely to be harmful variants. Hence, ROWVA derived from AF2‐predicted structural data can be used to predict variant functionality and evaluate VUS within the TET domain of p53. Additionally, our findings contribute to the ongoing debate regarding the applicability of AF2 for variant assessment [21, 22] by providing a case that supports the potential utility of AF2‐derived predictions in variant evaluation.

  To clarify the practical utility of ROWVA, we conducted a comparative analysis with established pathogenicity prediction tools, including AlphaMissense, SIFT, and PolyPhen‐2. ROWVA demonstrated the highest correlation with transcriptional activity scores among all evaluated methods (Figure 4C), suggesting that it may more accurately capture the effects of structural changes on p53 transcriptional function. In predicting ClinVar pathogenicity labels, ROWVA achieved a higher AUC than SIFT and PolyPhen‐2 but was marginally outperformed by AlphaMissense (Figure 5). However, given that AlphaMissense incorporates not only structural data but also population frequency data, whereas ROWVA relies solely on predicted structural data, the comparable performance of ROWVA is notable. Importantly, the analysis comparing correlation coefficients between each score and transcriptional activity included variants beyond those classified as benign or pathogenic in ClinVar, whereas the prediction of pathogenicity labels was restricted to variants annotated as benign or pathogenic in ClinVar. For the transcriptional activity subset, we evaluated the correlation between transcriptional activity scores and ROWVA or AlphaMissense after excluding variants with known benign or pathogenic annotations (Figure S2). Although none of the correlations were statistically significant, ROWVA exhibited a higher correlation coefficient than AlphaMissense. This highlights the potential utility of ROWVA for assessing variants with limited information, such as VUS.

  Structural predictions that focus only on the TET domain are considered essential for the accurate ROWVA calculations. In preliminary analyses, we performed structural predictions using the full‐length p53 sequence. These analyses suggest that the predicted structure of the region, excluding the DNA‐binding and TET domains, is unstable when using the full‐length sequence (Figure S3). When the predicted structures of the terminal regions vary among variants, differences in residue positions within these regions could significantly impact ROWVA calculations, potentially hindering proper comparisons of the TET domain. To address this, we limited the sequence used for AF2‐based structural predictions to the TET domain instead of the full‐length p53 sequence, enabling valid comparisons relevant to tetramer formation. We also performed structural alignment between the TET domain structure predicted by AF2 and the experimentally determined structure (PDB ID: 2J0Z); the resulting RMSD was 1.928 (Figure S4). This level of agreement is comparable to that reported in a previous study [19] comparing AF2‐predicted and experimentally determined structures, confirming that AF2 can make sufficiently accurate structural predictions for the TET domain. This RMSD value exceeds most ROWVA values from our study. Although it may appear to indicate notable structural variation, ROWVA compares AF2‐predicted wild‐type and variant structures, whereas this RMSD measures the difference between the experimental and predicted structures. Therefore, these values should not be directly compared. However, the possibility that variant structures predicted by AF2 do not represent the true structures cannot be excluded. Nevertheless, ROWVA showed the highest correlation coefficients across nearly all protein stability, thermodynamic, and transcriptional activity datasets examined in this study. Taken together, these observations suggest that although AF2‐based variant predictions may differ from the actual structures, comparing predicted variant structures with their corresponding predicted wild‐type structures may enable the detection of structural changes induced by variants. Therefore, rather than interpreting ROWVA as a measure of differences between the true wild‐type and true variant structures, it is more appropriate to interpret it as a metric that reflects differences between wild‐type and variant proteins as mediated through AlphaFold predictions. To emphasize this interpretation, we designate the term ROWVA, rather than RMSD, as the metric representing interatomic distances between two structures. The “A” in ROWVA refers to AlphaFold2, the structure prediction tool employed in this study. Nonetheless, advancements in structure prediction may yield tools with greater accuracy than AlphaFold2. In such instances, the “A” in ROWVA may be construed as denoting AI‐based structure prediction methods. This approach enables continued use of the term ROWVA while enhancing its general applicability, without necessitating modification of the term itself.

  The accuracy of ROWVA in predicting pathogenicity labels may vary by region within the TET domain. The ROWVA values in malignant variants such as E326V, I332N, and G334W were small, which negatively affected the prediction accuracy of the pathogenicity label (Table S5). A common feature of these variants is their occurrence in the early segment (residues 325–334) of the p53 TET domain (residues 325–356). This segment reportedly adopts the following secondary structures: a β‐sheet spanning residues 325–333, a turn at residue 334, and an α‐helix spanning residues 335–356 [35]. Thus, ROWVA evaluation can predict malignant variants in the α‐helices but may have difficulty predicting malignant variants in the β‐sheet and turn regions. Additionally, p53 forms tetramers through hydrophobic interactions between α‐helical segments of the TET domain [36]. This suggests that ROWVA could serve as a metric to assess interactions between these helices.

  A particularly notable finding was the distinct ROWVA for the A347D variant. Calculating the inter‐residue distances of the wild‐type structure predicted by AF2 revealed that residue 347 is the closest point of contact between the interacting α‐helices. The substitution of alanine with aspartic acid at position 347 likely increases steric hindrance, disrupting the interaction. To verify this prediction, we compared the wild‐type and A347D structures (Figure 6A,B). In the wild‐type, the dimer's α‐helices intersect perpendicularly. The A347D variant forms a dimer with parallel α‐helices, indicating impaired tetramer formation. To further investigate the impact of missense variants at position 347 on tetramer formation, we calculated the ROWVA for 19 possible amino acid substitutions (Figure 6C). Among these, variants A347S, A347G, A347C, and A347L had a ROWVA score below 1 Å. These variants have minimal steric hindrance, allowing α‐helical interactions to persist and the structure to closely match the wild‐type. These results indicate that ROWVA can effectively evaluate the impact of missense variants at residue 347 on tetramer formation, particularly given this residue's role in α‐helix interactions. Additionally, these results suggest that the variants exhibiting notably large ROWVA values are likely indicative of actual structural changes induced by the variants, rather than artifacts from AF2‐based predictions. Nevertheless, as these conclusions rely on AF2 structural predictions, further experimental investigation, such as direct structural analysis of A347D and A347T, is warranted in subsequent research.

  Previous studies have investigated the correlation between structural differences in wild‐type and mutant proteins and their pathogenicity [22, 37]. However, such studies have typically focused solely on the relationship between structural alterations and the final phenotype, namely pathogenicity. Our study offers two significant contributions: the development of ROWVA, a metric designed to efficiently evaluate structural variants using AlphaFold, and an in‐depth analysis of multiple factors contributing to pathogenicity, including protein stability, oligomerization potential, transcriptional activity as a multimer, and overall pathogenicity. ROWVA distinguishes itself from existing methods, such as AlphaMissense [29] and combinations of AlphaMissense with other scoring metrics [38]. Previous research has demonstrated that integrating multiple metrics—such as AlphaMissense scores, ΔΔG, and RSA—can enhance the accuracy of pathogenic variant predictions by leveraging structural and sequence‐derived features [38]. Notably, while other approaches generate scores for entire proteins, ROWVA restricts its calculation to specific domains. This domain‐focused approach, based on comparative analysis of AlphaFold‐predicted 3D structures, facilitates the identification of structural features responsible for variant effects. Incorporating ROWVA with additional scoring strategies in future work may not only increase the accuracy of pathogenicity prediction but also enable evaluation of the underlying structural characteristics associated with variant effects.

  The results of this study show that ROWVA may evaluate missense‐variant multimeric proteins. For example, this approach could potentially be extended to cancer‐related multimeric proteins such as Ras, EGFR, VEGF, and HER2. Indeed, many cancer‐associated proteins also harbor pathogenic variants, further emphasizing the need for broadly applicable evaluation methods [39, 40]. This approach may apply to other essential human proteins that function as multimers—for example, hemoglobin, collagen, actin, tubulin, and immunoglobulins.

  As an initial step to demonstrate the broad applicability of ROWVA, we performed supplementary analyses using PTEN as the target protein, given its involvement in hereditary diseases and the availability of functional scores for multiple variants previously reported [41]. For this analysis, we focused on the phosphatase domain, where malignant variants have been documented. Functional scores for PTEN variants were extracted from a previously published study [41], in which PTEN stability was evaluated in HEK293 cells. We investigated the correlations between the PTEN stability score [41] for the wild‐type and 60 variants within the phosphatase domain, and the scores derived from AF2‐based methods and AlphaMissense (Figure S5). Only ROWVA's results were statistically significant and exhibited the largest absolute correlation coefficient among all evaluated metrics. Hence, ROWVA may be applicable to proteins other than p53.

  A limitation of our method for evaluating missense‐variant proteins with ROWVA is the constrained generalizability. Our findings suggest that ROWVA has the potential to detect missense variants that affect interactions. However, it may be less effective for evaluating pathogenic variants that do not involve interactions. In fact, variants deemed malignant by ClinVar but unrelated to multimer formation (E326V, I332N, and G334W) were challenging to distinguish using ROWVA. Therefore, the evaluation may be restricted to variants within regions that require interactions, such as multimer‐formation domains.

  The evaluation of missense variants using ROWVA requires further validation. In the cases we examined, ROWVA appeared to reflect the differences in interactions between alpha‐helices. Moving forward, investigating other types of interactions will be essential to determine which interactions can be detected by ROWVA. By clarifying these details, we can further refine and establish appropriate application methods for this approach.

  In conclusion, our study suggests that ROWVA, among the AF2‐derived scores, may be particularly useful for protein function prediction and pathogenicity assessments. ROWVA demonstrated a strong correlation with p53 functional scores in both existing datasets and our experiments, and achieved higher accuracy than other AF2‐derived scores in predicting pathogenicity labels. Upon detailed analysis, we found that ROWVA can effectively evaluate protein interactions. This method holds potential for evaluating the pathogenicity of missense variants in multimeric cancer‐related proteins and other multimers that play essential roles in human physiology.
