# Glioblastoma radiomics can delineate systemic immune activity states like blood abundance of T cell populations or transcription factors


## Background
  Glioblastoma, the most frequent and most malign brain cancer, not only cultivates a local immunosuppressive milieu but also causes systemic immunological dynamics. Radiomics is an advanced, automated imaging analysis approach that harnesses data point patterns not readily visible for the human eye. It has been shown that radiomics can differentiate glioblastoma from other tumors, that it can recognize molecular features and that it can identify local immune infiltration in the tumor. However, whether radiomics can also indicate systemic, i.e. peripheral blood, immune states has not been investigated so far.


## Methods
  Therefore, we retrospectively analyzed magnetic resonance images of a comprehensively immunophenotyped clinical cohort (n = 34) and performed radiomics feature extraction from three morphological segments of the tumor: the necrotic core, the contrast-enhancing margin and the T2/FLAIR hyperintensive peritumoral zone. 321 radiomics dimensions were then integrated with 67 peripheral blood immunology markers (from flow cytometry and PCR). Via machine learning methods like t-SNE dimensionality reduction and hierarchical clustering, as well as regression modelling, we integrated the highly multidimensional data.


## Results
  A radiomics variable of the T2 hyperintensity zone seemed to predict T helper 17 blood levels. Radiomics variables of the necrotic core were apparently correlated with blood immune cell RORγT levels and CD15 + myeloid cell abundance. Major immune activation parameters like the number of naïve and activated CD8 + T cells, early-differentiated CD8 + T cells, CD56 + natural killer cells or levels of the T helper 1-polarizing transcription factor T-bet could be delineated by integrated multivariable modelling of radiomics features.


## Conclusions
  In an exploratory study on a modestly-sized but immunologically well-characterized glioblastoma cohort we provide first hypothesis-generating evidence that data-driven radiomics approaches could delineate systemic immune states. In the future, non-invasive, radiomics-based blood immunology prediction could e.g. be helpful for patient stratification or immunotherapy research. Before that, however, additional confirmatory studies are needed given the inherent limitations of this work.

## Introduction
  Glioblastoma is the most frequent and most aggressive primary brain cancer. Despite intensive multimodal treatment with surgery, chemotherapy and radiotherapy, survival is typically limited to less than two years. Novel approaches for treatment as well as for a better understanding of the underlying disease biology represent an urgent, as-of-yet unmet clinical need. One current frontier in advancing glioblastoma science is the automated, machine-based interpretation of tumor imaging via “radiomics” approaches. Radiomics harnesses the information represented in the imaging data that is not visible with or detectable by the human eye. Via various statistical algorithms, a plethora of data dimensions that are deducted from the raw, digital image information of individual bit patterns are computed. This allows a comprehensive characterization of imaged tissues that vastly surpasses what would be determinable via traditional image interpretation. Radiomics analyses have been applied to glioblastoma imaging – mostly magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) – in a number of scientific investigations [1–3]. One early application was to harness the power of radiomics for differentiating between glioblastoma and other lesions that equally show a contrast-enhancing rim on MRI, like e.g. lymphomas [4–6]. Then, research followed that aimed to predict survival of glioblastoma patients based on radiomics imaging analysis, often via investigating the peri-tumoral infiltration zone [7–10]. Apart from that, radiomics was also used to delineate the molecular composition of the tumor, mostly with a focus on major histopathological markers like Ki-67, MGMT or IDH1 [11–17]. Radiomics approaches and artificial intelligence are also explored for therapy response assessment [18, 19]. Recently, a focus of glioblastoma radiomics research has increasingly been to decipher the local immunology of the tumor. For instance, the immune infiltration pattern of the tumor with respect to tumor-associated macrophages or cytotoxic T cells has been associated with certain radiomics characteristics in recent research [20–22]. And indeed, it makes sense to examine glioblastoma immunology with all means available. For glioblastoma is a deeply immunology-driven disease. Even though it is highly immune-infiltrated it is regarded as an immunologically “cold” tumor as it cultivates an immensely immunosuppressive milieu and micro-environment [23, 24]. Interestingly, glioblastoma is not only altering the immunological milieu in its immediate vicinity but can also have distant immunological effects. For example, it has been shown that glioblastoma-associated CD8 + cytotoxic T cells are present in the peripheral blood or that it can induce systemic immunosuppression [25, 26]. It is thus surprising that one major aspect of glioblastoma immunology has so far not been studied with respect to radiomics technologies: potential relations of radiomics variables and systemic, i.e. blood-based, immunology. Given that radiomics has shown predictive ability related to local tumor immune infiltration and given the known systemic immunological effects of glioblastoma, it is well thinkable that radiomics protocols can also delineate systemic immune states. Hitherto, however, this assertion has not been formally investigated.

  With the present work we are thus aiming to close that gap in glioblastoma science. We build on a recently performed introductory study where we had conducted a first, elementary exploration of connections between systemic immunology and glioblastoma imaging [27]. It encompassed a basic form of MRI description relying on qualitative and semi-quantitative analysis by human assessors but did not include advanced, automated radiomics analyses. In terms of patient origin, it had used an immunologically well-characterized cohort from an immunotherapy study [28, 29]. We now examined this cohort again with a more elaborate, data-driven and comprehensive radiomics approach and mapped in how far tumor radiomics descriptors can correlate with blood immunology.


## Methods

### Patients
  This retrospective analysis includes participants from a national, randomized, multicenter, open-label phase II trial evaluating a dendritic-cell vaccine (Audencel) for newly diagnosed WHO grade 4 glioblastoma (EudraCT 2009-015979-27) [29]. All individuals provided written informed consent prior to enrolment. The study protocol had been approved by the local institutional ethics committee (approval number TRX 2/P-II-018).


### Variable characteristics
  A total of 388 features were evaluated, consisting of 321 radiomics features, and 67 immunological markers (see Supplementary Table 1). Radiological parameters included volumetric measurements (contrast-enhancing tumor region, necrotic tumor core, and T2 hyperintensive peritumoral area) and 24 semantic, morphological descriptors based on the VASARI feature set (see below). Data-driven radiomics were extracted from segmented regions on post-contrast T1-weighted MRI, including the contrast-enhancing tumor region, the necrotic tumor core, and the T2 hyperintensive peritumoral area. Immunological variables were obtained from baseline peripheral blood samples.


### Immunological data
  The detailed immunological methods used on peripheral blood from the trial patients have elsewhere already been delineated [28]. In short, blood immune cell populations at baseline were measured via flow cytometry of specific surface markers on a BD LSR-II cytometer (BD Bioscience, Heidelberg, Germany). Polarization towards a T helper 1 (Th1), T helper 2 (Th2), T helper 17 (Th17) or regulatory T cell (Treg) phenotype was identified via qRT-PCR of the respective cytokines or transcription factors (IFNγ, T-bet, IL4, GATA3, IL10, FOXp3, TGFβ, IL17A, RORγT) using the Taqman® 7500 Violet PCR system (Applied Biosystems). Furthermore, cell fraction quantification was also performed on peripheral blood cells after they had undergone elutriation for manufacturing of the cell therapy product [30]. These cell populations are labelled as “elutra”.


### Magnet resonance imaging acquisition
  Due to the multicenter structure of the original clinical trial, imaging was conducted on various MRI scanners, all operating at a magnetic field strength of 1.5 Tesla. At each site, a standardized 3D T1-weighted imaging protocol was applied, utilizing either magnetization-prepared rapid acquisition gradient echo (MP-RAGE) sequences or contrast-enhanced T1-weighted spin-echo (SE) sequences (0.1 mmol/kg gadopentetate dimeglumine). The resulting images had a consistent spatial resolution with a minimum slice thickness of 1.5 mm. In addition, all participants underwent axial T2-weighted imaging with a slice thickness of at least 2 mm. For the present study, only preoperative MRI scans, performed before surgical resection or adjuvant treatment, were included in the analysis.


### Postprocessing and volumetry on MRI
  Imaging preprocessing was performed in analogy to a method already published by Kickingereder et al. [31]. First, all MR images were registered to the post-contrast T1-weighted images by using the linear image registration tool with a mutual information algorithm and a 6-degree of freedom transformation from the Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging of the Brain (FMRIB) software library (FSL-FLIRT; http://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/fslwiki/FSL) [32–36].

  Intensity non-uniformity correction was applied using N4 bias field correction [37, 38], followed by intensity normalization using the WhiteStripe [39] method (packages implemented in Python v. 3), which rescales voxel intensities on a per-image basis using normal-appearing white matter as a reference. This procedure transforms arbitrary MR signal intensities into comparable, standardized intensity ranges while remaining fully unsupervised and independent of downstream analyses. All preprocessing steps were performed independently for each subject, thereby preventing information leakage between patients or between imaging features and immunological readouts. Tissue segmentation was performed on the post-contrast T1-weighted and T2 sequences to delineate three distinct tumor compartments: (1) the contrast-enhancing tumor region (excluding dura, large vessels, resection cavity, and central necrosis), (2) the necrotic tumor core, and (3) the T2 hyperintensive peritumoral area (excluding contrast-enhancing and necrotic regions). Segmentation and volume quantification were conducted using the semi-automatic active contour model known as “snake evolution” in ITK-SNAP version 3.4.0 (www.itksnap.org) [40]. This algorithm evolves a 3D contour based on the anatomical target and iteratively refines it using a velocity-based mathematical model. The method has demonstrated strong reproducibility and efficiency in 3D tumor volumetry across multiple studies [27, 40, 41] While ITK-SNP segmentation is automatic in principle, post-processing segmentation quality control for anatomical plausibility is performed by a human. Here, these assessments were independently performed by two trained raters following a standardized protocol. To ensure consistency, all segmentations were visually inspected for anatomical plausibility. Inter-observer reproducibility was assessed using the Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) to evaluate spatial overlap and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) to assess agreement in voxel counts. A two-way random-effects model with absolute agreement and single measures (ICC(2,1)) was applied. ICC values were interpreted as poor (< 0.50), moderate (0.50–0.75), good (0.75–0.90), and excellent (> 0.90).


### Data-driven radiomics feature extraction
  Radiomics feature extraction was performed using the open-source package PyRadiomics (version 3.0.1) in Python (https://pyradiomics.readthedocs.io/en/latest/) [42]. Radiomic features were extracted from the normalized images that undergone N4 bias field correction and WhiteStripe normalization (see above) but no additional explicit feature harmonization across patients or scanners (e.g., ComBat) was applied. We extracted radiomic features from (1) the contrast-enhancing tumor region mask, (2) the necrotic tumor core mask and (3) the T2 hyperintensive peritumoral area mask on post-contrast T1-weighted images. Extracted radiomic features included 14 shape, 18 first order, and 75 s order features subdivided to gray level cooccurrence matrix (GLCM), gray level dependence matrix (GLDM), gray level run length matrix (GLRLM), gray level size zone matrix (GLSZM), and neighboring gray tone difference matrix (NGTDM). In total 321 radiomic features were extracted. In Supplementary Table 1, all extracted radiomic features are fully listed.


### Semantic imaging features
  A total of 24 qualitative imaging descriptors were obtained from baseline MRI scans in accordance with the VASARI (Visually AcceSAble Rembrandt Images) feature set [43]. These included a mix of variable types: seven ordinal, 13 binary, five categorical, and three continuous descriptors. These features capture a wide range of tumor morphology and anatomical characteristics. A complete list of the extracted VASARI parameters is provided in Supplementary Table 1. Comprehensive definitions and documentation of the VASARI criteria can be accessed through the Cancer Imaging Archive’s official VASARI project page: https://wiki.cancerimagingarchive.net/display/Public/VASARI+Research+Project.


### Statistical analysis
  Statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.2.1 and GraphPadPrism version 9.5.0. Machine learning computations were performed with Orange Data Mining version 3.39.0. P-values less than 0.05 were considered significant.

  For radiomic features a feature selection was performed in order to exclude redundant features. The similarity of all extracted features was computed using the Pearson correlation and all highly correlating features (cut off r > 0.95) were excluded for further statistical analysis. In Supplementary Table 2 all radiomic features after feature selection are summarized. Pearson’s analysis was used for correlations between quantitative immunologic parameters and semantic features as well as radiomics (with a normal distribution). Spearman test was used for correlations between quantitative immunologic parameters and ordinal, i.e. also semiquantitative, VASARI features. Point-biserial correlation was used for correlations between quantitative immunological parameters and binary VASARI features. After an initial broad screening for exploratory purposes, all results of correlation analyses were corrected for multiple comparison (Bonferroni’s adjustment). Differences among categorical/binary VASARI features and quantitative immunologic parameters were evaluated with one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey test. To quantify uncertainty, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for key Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated using Fisher’s z-transformation. To contextualize sensitivity given the available sample size and pairwise missingness, we estimated the minimum detectable correlation magnitude for 80% power (two-sided α = 0.05) based on Fisher’s z approximation.

  Multivariable linear regression models were constructed with each immunologic parameter as an individual outcome. Model performance was evaluated using adjusted R² and F statistics. Variable selection was performed using bidirectional stepwise regression based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), with predictors iteratively added or removed until the model with the lowest AIC was identified. Internal model stability and generalizability were assessed using leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV). Cross-validated performance was quantified using R², root mean squared error (RMSE), and mean absolute error (MAE). As a sensitivity analysis to mitigate potential overfitting in this high-dimensional setting, regularized regression (LASSO and Elastic Net) was conducted, with penalty parameters optimized via LOOCV and performance evaluated using the same cross-validated metrics.

  For unsupervised, data-driven discovery of patterns in the multidimensional variable space, principal component analysis (PCA) followed by t-distributed stochastic neighbour embedding (t-SNE) was performed. Onto the t-SNE map, clusters derived from unsupervised hierarchical clustering were plotted.


## Results

### Evaluation of tumor segmentation and sample size power indicated an appropriate foundation for the planned analyses
  In terms of tumor segmentation, inter-observer agreement was excellent for all tissue regions. The contrast-enhancing tumor demonstrated near-perfect spatial agreement (mean Dice = 0.995 ± 0.006) and excellent volumetric agreement (ICC(2,1) = 1.00, 95% CI 1.00–1.00). Similarly, the necrotic core (Dice = 0.968 ± 0.067; ICC = 0.999, 95% CI 0.998–1.00) and T2-hyperintense peritumoral region (Dice = 0.993 ± 0.009; ICC = 1.00, 95% CI 1.00–1.00) demonstrated excellent reproducibility. These findings indicate highly robust segmentation and render a potential impact of inter-observer variability on downstream radiomics feature extraction quasi negligible.

  Pairwise complete sample sizes were similar for VASARI numeric features and radiomic features when correlated with immunological parameters (median n = 34; mean 32.7; range 8–34). Accordingly, the analyses were powered to detect moderate-to-large correlations (|r|≥0.47) for most comparisons (n = 34), whereas for comparisons with substantial missingness (minimum n = 8), only very large correlations (|r|≥0.85) could be detected with 80% power at α = 0.05.


### Three hundred twenty-one radiomics MRI variables were integrated with 67 peripheral blood immunology markers
  On the one hand, through using radiomics feature extraction algorithms and semantic glioblastoma description (see Methods section), we could successfully measure 321 single specific radiomics variables. This was based on dividing the tumor formation present on T1-weighted contrast-enhanced and T2/FLAIR-weighted images into three areas: the contrast-enhancing rim, the necrotic core and the peritumoral T2/FLAIR area (i.e. what is often referred to as the “edema” surrounding the tumor). Figure 1 illustrates the tumor segmentation and automatic feature extraction process. In addition to this purely computational radiomics analysis, the images also underwent structured semantic description following the VASARI protocol in order to fully capture all potential radiomics dimensions.

  Fig. 1Algorithmic radiomic feature extraction methodology. Tumor magnetic resonance images were automatically segmented and intensity values were normalized. Then radiomics features were extracted algorithmically and finally tumor radiomics values were integrated with peripheral blood immunology via standard statistics as well as machine learning

  On the other hand, via harnessing the set of immunological data available to us from a previous study [27], we had 67 peripheral blood immunology markers at our disposal for the analysis. They were derived from flow cytometric phenotyping of the peripheral immune system as well as PCR-based quantification of major immune-modulatory transcription factors. The full set of imaging variables and immunology data was in the end available for 34 patients. All of these had glioblastoma as the final histological diagnosis based on post-surgical neuropathological reporting. In terms of isocitrate dehydrogenase mutation (IDH) status, all patients under investigation were IDH wildtype (100%).


### Semantic glioblastoma MRI features were re-analyzed as indicators of systemic immune states
  As a first step of analysis, we investigated whether the cohort under investigation would exhibit similar features as in a prior study with respect to semantic image interpretation. For we had previously elucidated that qualitative or semi-quantitative metrics, like the extent and shape of the contrast-enhancing tumor margin, can go hand-in-hand with peripheral, systemic immunological variables. Even though the present cohort was derived from this initial investigation cohort, it was now a slightly smaller subset of it. We hence explored such patterns in the present dataset via repeating the previous computations. And indeed, we arrived at generally similar findings (see Suppl. Figure 1). Again, several features of the contrast-enhancing margin were connected to e.g. the CD3 + T cell count or several CD8 + T cell subsets in the blood. For instance, the “definition” of the contrast-enhancing rim (i.e. “well-defined” or “poorly-defined”) was positively correlated with the number of activated CD8 + T cells in the blood. After strict multiple testing correction, these effects, however, were not detectable anymore – probably due to the smaller sample size here.


### Data-derived radiomics variables of glioblastoma regions were related to semantic MRI features
  Next, we aimed at another basic assessment, namely whether the human-centered, structured, semantic description of tumor imaging would be associated with the data-driven, computer-derived radiomics features. For this question had not been explored so far – neither in our cohort nor by others in the literature. Thus, we computed an exploratory correlation matrix with the goal of mapping every possible pair of semantic VASARI variable and data-derived radiomics variable (see Fig. 2). Here, we observed that a plethora of semantic features were indeed connected to data-derived features. For example, T2/FLAIR hyperintensity (“edema”) volume was positively correlated with radiomic features extracted from the necrotic core, including “grey level dependence matrix non-uniformity” (r = 0.55, 95% CI 0.26–0.75; n = 34) and “grey level run length matrix high grey level emphasis” (r = 0.49, 95% CI 0.18–0.71; n = 34). Similarly, necrosis volume showed positive correlations with several radiomic features, including “grey level run length matrix run length non-uniformity“ derived from the T2/FLAIR hyperintensity region (r = 0.52, 95% CI 0.22–0.74; n = 32). In addition, the semi-quantitative necrosis proportion was positively associated with necrosis-derived “grey level dependence matrix non-uniformity” (r = 0.50, 95% CI 0.17–0.73; n = 30). Importantly, nine semantic–radiomic feature pairs remained statistically significant after correction for multiple testing (Fig. 2). Together, these findings provide exploratory evidence for systematic associations between semantic and data-derived imaging features in glioblastoma.

### Unsupervised automatic hierarchical clustering yielded necrosis-based phenotypes
  Subsequently, we asked whether the full dataset available would allow the automatic delineation of glioblastoma subtypes – based on a purely data-driven, unsupervised machine learning approach. Because it could well be that considering and integrating all data dimensions measured – radiomics MRI features as well as blood immunology features – would lead to hitherto undiscovered glioblastoma subgroups. Thus, we performed a hierarchical clustering analysis and plotted it onto a t-SNE map, where the multidimensional variable space is condensed into two interpretable dimensions. When focusing on reaching two such data-driven, separable groups in hierarchical clustering, we noticed that they also separated on the t-SNE plot (Fig. 3A). Then, when investigating their most-distinguishing features, we found that of the top ten features, eight were necrosis-related (Table 1). They also mapped well to the t-SNE groups (Fig. 3B-D). Apparently, necrosis-based variables are most relevant for sub-grouping of glioblastoma based on the present dataset. Importantly, peripheral blood immunology variables were not among the top-defining characteristics.

### Radiomics of the T2 hyperintensive peritumoral area was inversely correlated with blood T helper 17 cell abundance
  After that, we turned to the main question, i.e. in how far radiomics features are potentially connected to systemic immune status. First, we investigated the radiomics variables of the T2 hyperintensive peritumoral area. When mapping all potential radiomics-immunology-connections in an exploratory fashion, we could identify 133 such correlations (Fig. 4). For instance, “neighbouring gray tone difference matrix (NGTDM) busyness” was positively associated with various immunopolarizing transcription factors in the blood, like GATA3 (r = 0.82, 95% CI 0.66–0.91; n = 32) or RORγT (r = 0.59, 95% CI 0.30–0.78; n = 31). Further, “grey level dependence matrix (GLDM) dependence entropy” was negatively correlated with the abundance of T helper 17 cells in the peripheral blood (r = − 0.49, 95% CI − 0.71 to − 0.18; n = 34). Importantly, when narrowing down the findings via stringent multiple testing correction, this was also the one variable that remained detectable as statistically significant.

### Radiomics of the contrast-enhancing margin did not exhibit robust immunology relations
  Following that, we turned to the contrast-enhancing margin and similarly assessed whether radiomics features of it are connected to peripheral blood immunology. In the broad screening phase, we arrived at 42 potential radiomics-immunology-associations (Suppl. Figure 2). Among them were e.g. a possible relation between the “shape surface volume ratio” and the abundance of activated CD8 + and CD4 + T cells in the blood. Though, after rigorous multiple testing correction, none of the putative connections remained.


### Radiomics of the necrotic tumor core was associated with RORγT in peripheral blood immune cells
  Next, we analysed the necrotic tumor core the same way, i.e. via mapping all potential correlations between radiomics variables and peripheral immune system variables. Here, we arrived at a matrix of 72 presumable such connections. Of note, many radiomics dimensions seemed to be connected to IL17 mRNA levels in peripheral blood immune cells (Fig. 5). However, after strict multiple testing correction, two other variable pairs robustly showed statistical significance: “gray level run length matrix (GLRLM) run length non uniformity” was positively correlated with RORγT intensity in blood immune cells (r = 0.46, 95% CI 0.13 to 0.70; n = 31) and “gray level dependence matrix dependence (GLDM) non uniformity” was negatively correlated with the number of CD15 + myeloid cells (as measured in elutriated blood, see Methods) (r = -0.36, 95% CI -0.64 to 0.01; n = 30).

### Integration of radiomics variables via regression modelling could predict blood parameters like activated CD8 + T cells
  Finally, we aimed at developing integrated prediction models that would consider many radiomics variables at once in order to identify which blood immunology variables can be delineated via such an approach. Thus, we performed multiple regression analyses for every single immunological marker, where each of it served as the dependent variable and all radiomics features served as the independent predictor variables. This allowed us to delineate the immunology variables where radiomics-based prediction is possible and to list the actual radiomics predictors. As a result, we could identify 12 blood immunology variables that can be predicted based on tumor radiomics (Suppl. Table 3). Interestingly, among them were various cytotoxic T cell and general T cell populations (e.g. CD3 + cells, activated CD8 + cells, naïve CD8 + cells, early-differentiated CD8 + cells), immune-polarizing transcription factors (e.g. T-bet and FoxP3) and further cell populations like monocytes (CD14 + cells) and natural killer cells (CD56 + cells). In terms of predictive power, it was stemming from parameters related to all three tumor areas (the contrast-enhancing margin, the necrosis area, the T2 peritumoral area). For instance, prediction of the number of activated CD8 + cells in the blood was significantly driven by the “definition” of the enhancing margin (i.e. well- or poorly-defined). Blood immune cell levels of the transcription factor Tbet were significantly driven by “first order energy” of the necrosis. And, abundance of naïve CD8 + cells in the blood was e.g. significantly driven by the “quality” of the contrast-enhancement (mild or avid) or the “neighbouring gray tone difference matrix coarseness” of the necrosis.

  To illustrate potential future clinical use cases, we derived explicit multivariable radiomics-based prediction equations (Suppl. Table 4). In more detail, we here show them for three peripheral blood immunologic variables characterized by low-dimensional radiomic signatures and comparatively strong AIC-selected model performance (Table 2). The final AIC-selected prediction equation for naïve CD8 + levels was: naïve CD8 = 58.15 − 4.13 × glrlm run entropy (T2/FLAIR) − 13.71 × enhancement quality + 16.05 × ngtdm coarseness (Necrosis) + 7.46 × glcm correlation (Necrosis), with leave-one-out cross-validated performance of R² = 0.17, RMSE = 4.16, and MAE = 2.98. For early differentiated CD8 + levels, the corresponding model was: early differentiated CD8 = 44.88 + 0.38 × glrlm long run low gray level emphasis (T2/FLAIR) − 10.43 × enhancement quality − 1.56 × gldm dependence entropy (Necrosis), yielding cross-validated R² = 0.14, RMSE = 3.91, and MAE = 2.94. For blood T helper 17 cells, the final model was: blood T helper 17 cells = − 2.26 + 69.85 × gldm dependence non-uniformity normalized − 0.09 × glrlm run variance (T2/FLAIR), with cross-validated R² = 0.27, RMSE = 1.37, and MAE = 0.98. For early differentiated CD8+, LASSO retained a single predictor and Elastic Net retained four predictors with comparable cross-validated performance. Full results of regularized regressions for all 12 blood immunology variables are given in Suppl. Table 5. Overall, regularized regression methods with their strict approach to variable selection confirmed the fundamental concept of the study that radiomics variables can delineate blood immune parameters. Interestingly, they did not materially improve predictive accuracy compared with the AIC-selected models, supporting the robustness of the already observed associations within this cohort.


## Discussion
  In this paper, we present a comprehensive integration of glioblastoma radiomics with peripheral blood immunology. We first show that human-interpreted semantic radiomics features can be correlated with purely data-derived, machine-interpreted radiomics features. We then use unsupervised methods to automatically segment the patient cohort into subgroups and elucidate that necrosis-related radiomics variables might be main drivers for sub-phenotyping. We next observe that a radiomics variable of the T2 hyperintensive region seems to be correlated with blood Th17 cell abundance. Likewise, we describe that radiomics variables of the necrotic core appear to be correlated with blood immune cell RORγT levels and with CD15 + myeloid cell blood abundance. And finally, we present indications that 12 different systemic immunology variables could be predicted via regression modelling based on tumor radiomics – among them major immune activation parameters like the number of activated CD8 + T cells, early-differentiated CD8 + T cells, the number of CD56 + natural killer cells or the levels of the T helper 1-polarizing transcription factor T-bet. Lastly, we corroborate the overall study hypothesis via additional regularized regression methods.


### Comparison with literature data
  To the best of our knowledge, this investigation is the first project that specifically connects glioblastoma radiomics with peripheral blood, and thus systemic, immunology. While we are not the only ones to use radiomics technologies on glioblastoma MRI datasets, we look at it from a so-far overlooked angle. For instance, Fan et al. used glioblastoma radiomics to gauge the amount of local macrophage infiltration – but not systemic macrophage counts [20]. Similarly, Zhou et al. predicted local glioblastoma infiltration levels of tumor-associated macrophages via radiomics [44]. Hsu et al. performed a related analysis for other immune cells like tumor-resident cytotoxic T cells, as did Liu et al. [22, 45]. Additionally, Zhou et al. integrated radiomics and local tumor immune infiltration for survival prediction [46]. Wei et al. harnessed glioblastoma radiomics for mapping changes in the local tumor microenvironment in response to immunotherapy – in a murine model and without reference to blood immunology [47]. Luan et al. analysed archival glioblastoma MRI data via radiomics and could predict the amount of tumor-infiltrating immune cells – without investigating systemic immune cells [21]. Saidak et al. followed a similar approach [48]. Badani et al. discussed in how far glioblastoma radiomics could be used to predict checkpoint-inhibitor responses – again focusing on local tumor immunology [49]. George et al. actually used radiomics-based machine learning to predict clinical outcomes after checkpoint-inhibition – but not studying immune system outcomes [50]. Aslan et al. followed a similar approach, while Turco et al. worked with a likewise concept in a murine model [51, 52]. Roesler et al. discussed how radiomics can help in differentiating glioblastoma from inflamed, immune-infiltrated brain tissue – hence again investigating local phenomena [53]. Taken together, the link to systemic immune states is a novel perspective that had apparently been missing.


### Immunological interpretation
  When interpreting our findings from an immunological point of view, three main observations can be made. First, it is of interest that a radiomics variable of the T2/FLAIR hyperintensive peritumoral area (“GLDM dependence entropy”) was indirectly, i.e. negatively, correlated with T helper 17 cell abundance in the blood. T helper 17 cells have been shown to have a complex and paradoxical role in tumor immunology, at times being pro-tumorigenic and at times being anti-tumorigenic [54]. The T2/FLAIR hyperintensive peritumoral region is often seen as a mixture of infiltrating tumor- and immune cells, leukencephalopathy and “edema” around the tumor, which implies that it is a space where immune reactions take place. Apparently, the radiological configuration of the peritumoral immune reaction can indicate how frequent T helper 17 cells are in the body. This might delineate the intensity of their involvement in tumor immunology – however, without clarifying their respective functional mode. Likewise, it fits that peripheral blood cell levels of the transcription factor RORγT were associated with the necrosis radiomics feature “GLRLM run length non uniformity”. Because, interestingly, RORγT is a master regulator of T helper 17 cells [55, 56]. Thus, taken together, especially T helper 17-related variables seem to be going along with radiomics features.

  Second, it is remarkable that regression modelling – which integrates multiple radiomics variables at once – led to a list of predictable immunovariables that is largely composed of features relevant for anti-tumor immunity. Among the peripheral immunology markers were e.g. monocytes that can present tumor antigens themselves or give rise to tumor antigen-presenting dendritic cells [57]. CD56 + natural killer cells as major anti-tumor immune cell population were also identified [58]. And, most importantly, activated CD8 + T cells as well as the T helper 1-polarizing transcription factor T-bet were on the list – they are both hallmarks of anti-tumor immunity [25, 59, 60]. Likewise, the blood abundance of early-differentiated CD8 + T cells could be predicted with glioblastoma radiomics – they are especially effective at anti-tumor cytotoxicity [61, 62]. Even that the immunoregulatory FoxP3 was on the list could be seen as of relevance as knowing its levels is crucial for a comprehensive understanding of the systemic tumor-related immune state [63]. Third, in terms of deciphering the underlying immunobiology that is driving e.g. the fact that peripheral blood RORγT and T-bet were both related to necrosis radiomics in various analyses, one could speculate that systemic release of danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) from the necrotic area over the blood-brain-barrier might be the mechanism [64].


### Biological interpretation of radiomics features
  In terms of providing a general interpretative framework for the actual biological meaning of the various radiomics features, one could speculate that they describe texture, shape or other characteristics at the molecular, cellular and tissue level – i.e. the microenvironment. The underlying drivers could be features of the tumor biology like heterogeneity of necrotic areas or extracellular matrix regions, or different patterns of immune infiltration or vessel formation. It is indeed well thinkable that exactly such features then drive systemic immune states via the following mechanisms: e.g. higher vascularity could mean more leakage of antigens or DAMPs into the circulation. Similarly, more or less necrosis could result in more or less immune stimulation. And, varying extracellular matrix features could influence extravasation or infiltration properties of signalling molecules and cells from the systemic circulation. Additional studies should shed more light onto this aspect – e.g. via the direct inclusion of histomorphological analyses. One could even imagine constructing tissues in 2D or 3D cultures with specific properties in vitro and then performing MRI on them to dissect the radiomics effects of varying tissue compositions. Nevertheless, a certain “black box” element will naturally prevail in all radiomics research, which remains a limitation to consider (see below).


### Potential utility for glioblastoma research and treatment
  When deducting potential practical implications from the here presented findings, many such are possible. A better understanding of connections between tumor radiomics and systemic immunology has a basic science merit in itself. It could serve as the starting point for continuative research. Further, knowing immunological profiles simply from imaging could enable rapid and easy patient selection based on desired immunophenotypes. In the end, this could even lead to “precision immunotherapy” – as immunotherapies in development could be tailored to specific immune profiles like high levels of T-bet or great abundance of activated CD8 + T cells. Likewise, tumor radiomics could be used for capturing longitudinal, temporal immune system dynamics without the need for repeated invasive venipunctures. This would be helpful for mapping not only the course of disease but also for mapping system-wide responses to immunotherapies or other treatments.

  To illustrate that these clinical use cases could be already implemented with the insights generated here, we provide the specific radiomics-based prediction formulas, also for three especially interesting systemic immune variables (naïve CD8 + cells, early differentiated CD8 + cells, T helper 17 cells). Theoretically, these formulas could be readily applied to other clinical data sets. For example, a concrete practical setting would be to design an immunotherapy trial where a novel agent (e.g. a checkpoint inhibitor) is only given to patients with a “high” count of early-differentiated CD8 + cells (e.g. in the “upper third” as predicted by the radiomics-based formula provided above). With this immunotherapy stratification, patients most likely to benefit from the novel therapy could be selected. A concrete treatment monitoring example could then be to continuously assess the early-differentiated CD8 + cell levels during the course of the study – based on the routine MRIs done, without the need for repeated venipuncture. A rising level of the cells could e.g. signal desirable immunological activity.

  While such specific application scenarios are already possible, for now, it is advisable to use the provided formulas in the setting of prospective, confirmatory clinical trials to validate their robustness rather than for immunotherapy trials or routine patient management scenarios.


### Strengths and limitations
  The present work has several strengths worth mentioning. Mainly, it is of legitimate value that we ventured into a novel field of crossing comprehensive tumor radiomics with systemic immunology – an innovative approach that had not been pursued so far. Further, we not only relied on deep, thorough radiomics but also on advanced machine learning methods like tSNE to interpret the multidimensional data. Also, we describe potential radiomics-immunology connections that seem to make sense from an immunobiological point of view. And, finally, we performed the analysis with the aim of future clinical utility in mind.

  Nevertheless, as usual, certain limitations have to be discussed. Importantly, we faced a considerable “overfitting” challenge [65] as the number of data dimensions vastly exceeded the number of subjects under investigation – i.e. 321 radiomics dimensions plus 67 immunology dimensions amounting to a total of 388 dimensions versus only 34 patients. We mitigated that circumstance via meticulous, strict multiple testing correction and only considered findings after correction as relevant. These, however, can then be regarded as relatively robust in our view. Further, while an external validation was not possible given the lack of a second, similar dataset cohort, we still performed rigorous internal validation (e.g. leave-one-out cross-validation). Here, the overall study hypothesis that radiomics variables can delineate systemic immune parameters was well corroborated. Nevertheless, given the exploratory design, multiple comparisons, and modest sample size, all findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and require validation in larger, harmonized cohorts. Accordingly, the models should be interpreted as internally derived predictive signatures rather than final clinically deployable tools. They are provided for additional external studies for confirmation and only then they are ready for routine clinical use. Of note, the consistent identification of explanatory radiomic features across multiple immune parameters suggests a biologically meaningful association between radiomics-visible tumor (microenvironment) heterogeneity and systemic immune profiles.

  Another important limitation to mention is the fact that MRI data were acquired across multiple centers using different 1.5T scanners and acquisition protocols, which may introduce variability in radiomic feature values. To mitigate such effects, all images underwent standardized preprocessing including linear registration, N4 bias field correction, and WhiteStripe intensity normalization, which have been shown to reduce intensity-related variability across scans. However, these steps do not fully eliminate scanner- or protocol-specific effects, and no explicit batch harmonization (e.g., ComBat) was applied. Consequently, some radiomic features, particularly those sensitive to intensity distribution and texture may be influenced by residual technical variability. Importantly, this would be expected to attenuate true biological associations rather than generate spurious correlations, suggesting that the reported associations represent conservative estimates. Future studies with larger cohorts should explicitly assess inter-site variability and incorporate harmonization strategies to improve feature robustness and reproducibility. A further point to consider is that MRI and blood analysis were not performed at exactly the same time but within a window of a few days. This minor limitation has been extensively discussed already in the preceding paper and should not constitute a bias as it is anyways desirable to capture lasting, stable immune states that persist over days. Finally, another drawback of the current approach is that the data-derived radiomics dimensions remain challenging to interpret content-wise. It is difficult for the human investigator to finally define what e.g. “neighbouring gray tone difference matrix busyness” denominates even if mechanistic interpretative frameworks can be useful tools for discussion (see above). In the end, a certain aspect of radiomics variables necessarily remains a “black box” [66]. This does not necessarily reduce their usefulness, though.


## Conclusion
  Altogether, we here provide a first exploration of integrating comprehensive glioblastoma radiomics with systemic immunology. Even though additional confirmatory studies are needed given limitations like the modest sample size, we provide first illustrative evidence for the concept of using glioblastoma radiomics for systemic immune analyses. In doing so, we aim to inspire future precision immunotherapy approaches based on non-invasive, imaging-based patient stratification. Also, we generate first hypotheses, which systemic immune system parameters are most likely predictable via glioblastoma radiomics – including immune variables with relevance for tumor immunology and immunotherapy like the abundance of activated or early-differentiated CD8 + T cells in the blood.
