# Exploring the potential targets and mechanisms of malachite green induced lung adenocarcinoma through network toxicology and molecular docking


## Abstract
  Malachite green (MG), a synthetic dye, has turned into a major risk to human health, because of its toxicity of teratogenic, genotoxic, carcinogenic, and immunosuppressive properties. And the result of ADMETLAB 2.0 platform prediction shows that MG is highly toxic to the respiratory system in our study. However, no study has conducted to verify and explain the association between MG and lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD). In the current investigation, a total of 34 candidate target genes that might be involved in MG-mediated modulation in LUAD were identified and screened through integrated machine learning algorithms, including LASSO regression and random forest analysis. Subsequent functional annotation revealed that these genes were predominantly enriched in biological processes associated with the cell cycle. Meanwhile, pathway analysis indicated that the majority of these genes were closely linked to the p53 signaling pathway. Furthermore, molecular docking simulation validated that MG could stably and specifically bind to TP53, CCNB1, and MAPK1 proteins. In summary, our results demonstrated that MG may participate in the tumorigenesis and progression of LUAD, mainly by regulating the cell cycle via the p53 and MAPK signaling pathways. Among these, TP53, CCNB1, and MAPK1 could act as crucial candidate targets in MG‑mediated LUAD development. These findings provide novel insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying the role of MG in LUAD, and lay a theoretical basis for the future prevention and targeted therapy of LUAD.


## 1. Introduction
  According to the World Health Organization estimates, lung cancer represents a major global public health challenge. It ranks as the second most prevalent malignant tumor worldwide and is responsible for roughly 18.0% of all cancer-related mortalities.[1] Lung cancer is a complex disease driven by the interplay between environmental exposures and genetic susceptibility. Over the past few decades, the extensive application of industrial chemicals has given rise to a variety of environmental contaminants, many of whose toxicological characteristics remain poorly understood. These pollutants have contaminated various media such as water, air, soil, and food, and mounting evidence has linked such environmental pollution to the development of lung cancer.[2-4] Therefore, the risk of lung cancer associated with environmental and food contaminants should not be overlooked. Furthermore, accumulating evidence has indicated that environmental risk factors are responsible for 70% to 90% of all cancer cases.[5] Accordingly, the relationship between environmental and food pollutants and tumor development has emerged as a critical research focus.

  Malachite green (MG) is a synthetic dye that is widely utilized as a fungicidal and antiseptic agent in aquaculture, fishery production, and the food processing industry.[6-8] Despite these restrictions, the application of MG in aquatic food animals has been strictly restricted or even banned in many countries. However, owing to its low cost, easy accessibility, and favorable antimicrobial effects, the illegal utilization of MG in aquaculture remains on the rise.[9-11] Moreover, accelerated industrialization has led to elevated levels of MG released from urban, agricultural, and industrial activities, thereby posing substantial risks to environmental safety. MG can be easily absorbed and accumulated in fish and other aquatic organisms, and further biomagnified along the food chain into human tissues. Given its teratogenic, genotoxic, carcinogenic, and immunosuppressive toxicity, the application of MG in aquaculture has consequently become a prominent threat to human health.[6,12,13] Currently, evidence indicates that most synthetic dyes exert varying degrees of toxic effects on organisms. These adverse impacts include damage to organs such as the liver, spleen, and kidneys, impaired immune and reproductive functions, disturbed intestinal microbiota and intestinal barrier permeability, as well as developmental disorders in certain mammals.[9,14,15] In vitro investigations have demonstrated that high-dose MG exposure can suppress cell viability, trigger DNA damage, and elevate the expression of genes associated with cell apoptosis.[16,17] In addition, MG has been reported to induce respiratory dysfunction via suppressing mitochondrial respiratory enzymes and damaging both cancerous and non‑cancerous pulmonary epithelial cells, thereby classifying it as a respiratory toxicant.[15,18,19] Although mounting evidence has associated MG exposure with multiple adverse health effects, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying MG-mediated lung cancer initiation and progression remain largely unclear. To date, limited research has been performed to explore and validate the causal relationship between MG and lung cancer.

  Against this background, we performed an integrated analysis using network toxicology and molecular docking to identify the key molecular targets involved in MG-related lung carcinogenesis. The approach adopted in this study offers a novel strategy for evaluating the carcinogenic risk of environmental contaminants, which may provide a theoretical foundation for the early detection and prevention of their carcinogenic effects.


## 2. Material and methods

### 2.1. Flow of research
  As illustrated in Figure 1, the overall workflow of this study is described as follows. We first comprehensively retrieved genes related to MG and LUAD from multiple public databases. Subsequently, Venn diagram analysis was applied to screen the overlapping candidate target genes of MG associated with LUAD. The obtained genes were further analyzed using Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses, and machine learning algorithms were utilized to identify hub genes. Finally, molecular docking was performed to verify the binding affinity between MG and the identified hub genes.


### 2.2. Identification of MG toxicity
  The chemical structure of MG was retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov), and its toxicological properties were further validated via the ADMETLAB 2.0 platform (https://admetmesh.scbdd.com/service/evaluation/cal).


### 2.3. Screening genes of related to MG
  To accurately screen for target genes regulated by MG, 3 well-recognized drug target-related online databases were utilized in this study, namely CTD (http://ctdbase.org/), Swiss Target Prediction (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/) and PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). Candidate target genes were then identified by cross-verifying the data from these databases, with only those genes present in at least 2 of the 3 databases being selected.


### 2.4. Screening genes of related to LUAD
  Two gene expression datasets from the GEO database were retrieved for analysis: GPL570-based GSE31210, which included 20 healthy controls and 226 LUAD patients, and GPL6884-based GSE75037, comprising 83 LUAD tissue samples and their 83 matched adjacent non-tumor lung tissues. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were screened from these datasets using the GEOR2 tool with the cutoff criteria of P < .01 and |log fold change| > 1. Moreover, 5 authoritative online databases – GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org), CTD (http://ctdbase.org/), TTD (http://db.idrblab.net/), DisGeNET (https://www.disgenet.org/) and PharmGKB (https://www.pharmgkb.org/) – were utilized to mine genes associated with LUAD pathogenesis. Additionally, the GEPIA database (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn) was employed to identify DEGs in The Cancer Genome Atlas-LUAD datasets with the same screening thresholds of P < .01 and |log fold change| > 1. For the sake of ensuring the reliability of predictive outcomes, only genes that were annotated in at least 2 of the aforementioned databases were defined as LUAD-related genes.


### 2.5. GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis
  To explore the biological functions and signaling pathways underlying MG-mediated regulation in LUAD, we conducted KEGG pathway enrichment analysis and GO enrichment analysis – encompassing biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF) categories – via the online DAVID analysis tool (https://davidbioinformatics.nih.gov/). The outcomes of these enrichment analyses were subsequently visualized by means of the online analytical tools hosted at http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn.


### 2.6. Construction of a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network and screening of hub genes
  A PPI network was constructed via the STRING database (http://string-db.org) with a threshold of combined score > 0.4 applied for screening. The interaction data were then downloaded, and the PPI network was optimized for enhanced visualization using Cytoscape software (Version 3.8.0). CytoHubba plugin was subsequently utilized to identify core hub genes within the constructed network, with the degree algorithm employed to calculate and select the top 15 hub genes. LASSO regression analysis was conducted via the “glmnet” package in R software on the CNSknowall platform (http://cnsknowall.com/index.html#/HomePage), with a 10-fold cross-validation approach adopted as the cross-validation strategy for this analysis. This method exhibits superior performance to conventional regression analyses in the assessment of high-dimensional datasets. Additionally, a random forest algorithm was applied to classify the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) among the target genes; the random forest model identifies the optimal number of variables by computing the mean error rate of the candidate genes involved. The ggplot2 (v3.4.4) and randomForest (v4.7.1.1) R packages were utilized to perform random forest analysis. We calculated the error rate corresponding to decision tree numbers ranging from 1 to 1000, and the optimal tree number was identified based on the minimum error rate obtained. A random forest tree model was then constructed after the determination of the above parameters. Finally, the feature importance score of each candidate core gene was calculated, and the genes with the highest importance values were selected for subsequent analyses. The overlapping genes identified by the 2 machine learning algorithms and the CytoHubba degree algorithm were defined as the hub genes of MG associated with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD).


### 2.7. Molecular docking
  The 3-dimensional (3D) crystal structures of TP53, CCNB1, and MAPK1 were retrieved from the RCSB PDB database (http://www.rcsb.org/) and designated as the receptor proteins for molecular docking. The 2-dimensional (2D) chemical structure of MG was downloaded from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and used as the ligand. Molecular docking assays were subsequently performed via the CB-Dock2 online platform (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2/). For the docking results, lower Vina scores represent stronger binding affinity between the receptor and ligand, while a cavity size that is approximate to or larger than that of the ligand corresponds to higher docking accuracy.


## 3. Results

### 3.1. The structure and toxicity assessment of MG
  The chemical structure of MG was acquired from the PubChem database (Fig. 2A). Toxicity evaluation of MG was subsequently carried out using the ADMETLAB 2.0 toxicity prediction platform, and the results demonstrated that MG exerts potent toxic effects on the respiratory system (Fig. 2B).


### 3.2. Collection of MG target genes
  Target genes of MG were identified by mining 3 specialized online databases for chemical-target associations. Screening results revealed 41, 48, and 88 MG-associated target genes from CTD, PubChem and Swiss Target Prediction, respectively. To ensure the reliability of predictive outcomes, only genes annotated in at least 2 of these databases were defined as bona fide MG target genes. A total of 35 MG target genes were finally obtained in accordance with this screening criterion (Fig. 2C, Supplementary File 1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R708).


### 3.3. Identification results of MG for LUAD related genes
  A total of 2699 and 9902 DEGs were identified from the GSE31210 and GSE75037 datasets, respectively. Additionally, 9288, 37,986, 7, 139, and 4245 LUAD-associated DEGs were retrieved from GeneCards, CTD, DisGeNET, PharmGKB and The Cancer Genome Atlas-LUAD, respectively (Fig. 2D, Supplementary File 2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R709). In the present study, DEGs that were annotated in at least 2 of the aforementioned databases were defined as LUAD-related genes, which yielded a total of 14,208 LUAD-related genes (Fig. 2D, Supplementary File 2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R709). Venn diagram analysis was subsequently performed, and the results identified 34 potential target genes of MG associated with LUAD (Fig. 2E, Supplementary File 3, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R708).


### 3.4. GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis MG for LUAD related genes
  To investigate the functional roles of MG in regulating LUAD-related genes, we conducted bioinformatic analyses on the 34 identified potential target genes. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that the biological process (BP) terms were primarily enriched in cell cycle phase transition, mitotic cell cycle G1/S transition, response to reactive oxygen species, and response to metal ions (Fig. 3A). For cellular component (CC) terms, the enrichment results were mainly associated with the cyclin-dependent protein kinase holoenzyme complex, serine/threonine protein kinase complex, protein kinase complex, phosphorus-containing group transferase complex, nuclear envelope, mitochondrial matrix, nuclear membrane, chromosome, telomeric region, chromosomal region, and pseudopodium (Fig. 3B). Molecular function (MF) enrichment analysis indicated that these genes were predominantly implicated in ubiquitin-like protein ligase binding, MAP kinase activity, ubiquitin protein ligase binding, protein serine/threonine kinase activity, RNA polymerase II CTD heptapeptide repeat kinase activity, phosphatase binding, cyclin-dependent protein serine/threonine kinase regulator activity, histone kinase activity, MHC class I protein binding and MHC protein binding (Fig. 3C). Moreover, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the target genes were mainly enriched in the p53 signaling pathway, endocrine resistance, cellular senescence, human T-cell leukemia virus 1 infection, cell cycle, pancreatic cancer, colorectal cancer, AGE-RAGE signaling pathway in diabetic complications, hepatitis B and prolactin signaling pathway (Fig. 3D). The results of the top 10 enriched terms for BP, CC, MF and KEGG, along with their corresponding genes, were visualized as circos plots (Fig. 4A–D, Supplementary File 4, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R708).


### 3.5. PPI network construction and identification of hub genes
  To investigate protein–protein interactions among the products of these target genes, the STRING database was utilized to construct a PPI network for the 34 candidate target genes (Fig. 5A). The degree algorithm of the CytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape software was then employed to screen the top 15 hub genes from this network (Fig. 5B, Supplementary File 5, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R708). Additionally, 2 machine learning algorithms were applied to further identify hub genes within the 34 target genes: LASSO regression analysis selected 17 hub genes corresponding to lambda.1se (Fig. 5C, D, Supplementary File 5, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R708), while random forest analysis identified and visualized the top 10 feature genes ranked by relative importance (Fig. 5E, F, Supplementary File 5, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R708). For more accurate identification of core hub genes, Venn diagram analysis was performed on the hub gene sets obtained from Cytoscape, LASSO and random forest analyses. Ultimately, TP53, CCNB1, and MAPK1 were identified as the key hub genes mediating the effects of MG in LUAD (Fig. 5G).


### 3.6. Expression vand receiver operating characteristic curve of 3 hub genes in LUAD samples
  The expression levels of the 3 hub genes were assessed using the GSE31210 dataset. As illustrated in Figure 6A–C, the mRNA expression levels of TP53 and CCNB1 were upregulated in LUAD tissues, whereas MAPK1 was downregulated. Compared with healthy control samples, these 3 hub genes exhibited favorable diagnostic value in LUAD samples, with area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.8190 for TP53, 0.8829 for CCNB1, and 0.7600 for MAPK1 (Fig. 6D–F). Consistent results were obtained in the validation dataset GSE75037: the expression levels of TP53 and CCNB1 were significantly higher in the LUAD group than in the healthy control group, while MAPK1 was significantly lower (Fig. 6G–I). The corresponding AUC values were 0.6757 for TP53, 0.9427 for CCNB1, and 0.9410 for MAPK1 (Fig. 6J–L).


### 3.7. Molecular docking between MG and 3 hub genes
  Molecular docking assays were performed to investigate the binding interactions between MG and the 3 identified hub genes. The results demonstrated that MG bound to all 3 hub gene-encoded proteins with high affinity, yielding docking scores of −6.4 kcal/mol for TP53, −6.7 kcal/mol for CCNB1, and −8.2 kcal/mol for MAPK1 (Fig. 7A–C, Table 1). Notably, MAPK1 showed the strongest binding affinity with MG (Fig. 7C). These findings suggest that MG may directly interact with these key genes, thereby potentially modulating LUAD-associated biological processes.


## 4. Discussion
  Malachite green (MG) is a synthetic dye widely used as a biological tissue stain and as an antiparasitic agent in aquaculture. Multiple studies have detected MG residues in food colorants and farmed fish products intended for human consumption. Given its documented toxicological characteristics, such contamination poses potential health risks to both end consumers and individuals occupationally exposed to this compound.[20,21] Therefore, MG represents a potential hazard to aquatic organisms, human health, and the surrounding environment. It is easily absorbed by fish and other animals, and can gradually accumulate in human tissues via the food chain. Besides, it has been observed that MG causes respiratory disorders both cancerous and noncancerous. It has also been confirmed in our study (Fig. 2) that MG is highly toxic to the respiratory system. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying this association have not been fully elucidated. In recent years, integrative transcriptomic and single-cell analyses have validated the utility of multi-omics approaches for identifying key regulatory targets in cancer.[22] Given the carcinogenicity of MG and its current research status, this study was designed to reveal the effects of MG on the molecular pathogenesis of lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) by integrating network toxicology and molecular docking. Network toxicology is an interdisciplinary method combining bioinformatics, systems biology, and chemical informatics, which enables systematic exploration of how chemical substances interfere with biological networks, impair cellular functions, and thereby promote disease progression. Molecular docking further provides a computational strategy to simulate and characterize the atomic-level interactions between MG and its target genes, helping to elucidate the underlying pathogenic mechanisms.

  Bibliometric evidence supports the growing application of computational and systems-level analyses in tumor-immune research.[23] In this study, network toxicology and molecular docking were combined with datasets from GEO, GeneCards, CTD, TTD, DisGeNET, and PharmGKB databases to systematically identify 34 potential target genes linking MG exposure and LUAD. A PPI network was constructed using the STRING database and Cytoscape software, from which 15 core hub genes were screened; these genes are thought to serve as key components in the molecular mechanisms of MG‑mediated LUAD.Bioinformatic functional enrichment further revealed that the biological effects of MG in LUAD are likely associated with cell cycle phase transition, mitotic G1/S transition, and the p53 signaling pathway. Finally, using LASSO regression and random forest algorithms, we identified TP53, CCNB1, and MAPK1 as the 3 pivotal hub genes underlying MG‑induced LUAD.

  Previous studies have demonstrated that the tumor suppressor p53, encoded by the TP53 gene, plays an essential role in regulating normal cell proliferation and suppressing tumorigenesis. Nevertheless, mutant p53 not only loses its canonical tumor‑suppressive function but also often gains oncogenic properties that promote tumor development.[24] TP53 was abnormally upregulated in LUAD and had a good affinity with MG that were also verified in our research. This may be due to the fact that the core function of p53 is to regulate the cell cycle, and its G1 phase arrest is dependent on the transcriptional activation of p21, which is indispensable for the maintenance of gene composition. However, this important tumor suppressor function is lost in cancers expressing mutant p53.[25] And aberrant cell cycle progression represents one of the fundamental mechanisms underlying tumorigenesis. Accumulating evidence suggests that the cell cycle regulatory pathway is closely integrated with other cancer hallmarks, such as metabolic reprogramming and immune escaping.[26] A recent study reported that MG exerts dose‑dependent cytotoxicity in ACP02, L929, MNP01, and MRC-5 cell lines, with a genotoxic profile characterized by elevated frequencies of micronuclei, nucleoplasmic bridges, nuclear buds, and DNA fragmentation.[19] Moreover, MG-exposed cells were arrested at the G2/M phase, implying a prolonged cell-cycle checkpoint repair process.[27] However, the limitation of this result is that as the current analysis does not distinguish between wild-type and mutant TP53, functional assumptions based solely on expression levels is not cautious. The experimental validation is necessary.

  Cyclin B1 (CCNB1) is a key cyclin family member that regulates the G2/M and metaphase/anaphase transitions.[28] Recent studies have shown that CCNB1 is overexpressed in lung cancer and correlated with poor prognosis,[29] which is consistent with our observation that CCNB1 was highly expressed in LUAD and exhibited strong binding affinity to MG. Functional enrichment analysis indicated that CCNB1 participates in the cell cycle and p53 signaling pathway. Together, these findings imply that MG may participate in cell cycle regulation. We therefore hypothesize that MG contributes to LUAD progression by modulating the cell cycle via the p53 signaling pathway.

  Mitogen-activated protein kinase 1 (MAPK1), also referred to as extracellular signal‑regulated kinase 2 (ERK2), is one of the most extensively investigated members of the MAP kinase family.[30] As a core signaling component, MAPK1 participates in multiple intracellular signaling cascades and regulates a wide range of cellular processes, including immune responses, cell proliferation, differentiation, and development.[31] Accordingly, MAPK signaling pathways also serve crucial roles in stress‑ and inflammation‑related responses, and their dysregulation is closely associated with diabetes, inflammatory diseases, and tumorigenesis.[32] Numerous exogenous and endogenous factors exert biological functions via the MAPK signaling pathway. For instance, NFs can directly target the IGF2 receptor and activate MAPK signaling, thereby promoting the transformation of NFs into cancer‑associated fibroblasts (CAFs) with enhanced proliferation, migration, and proinflammatory gene expression, ultimately facilitating melanoma growth.[33] In lung cancer, vascular cell adhesion molecule‑1 (VCAM‑1) secreted by cancer‑associated fibroblasts (CAFs) activates the AKT and MAPK signaling pathways in tumor cells by binding to integrin α4β1, thereby enhancing cancer cell proliferation and invasion.[34] The MAPK signaling pathway exerts an inhibitory effect on glucose metabolism in NSCLC,[35] and MAPK-related mitochondrial adaptive changes have been demonstrated to be closely associated with the invasiveness of glioma progression.[36] The importance of the MAPK pathway has been well-established in lung cancer development, particularly for NSCLC.[37] The MAPK family comprises multiple subfamilies, among which the stress‑activated protein kinases – represented by the p38 subfamily in mammals – mediate a wide array of cellular processes, including DNA repair, cell fate determination, and cell cycle arrest.[38] Notably, low concentrations of MG have been shown to upregulate the expression of genes associated with apoptosis and oxidative stress in cultured cells, likely through lipid peroxidation. This process promotes the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which at high levels induce DNA damage, trigger metabolic pathway activation, and ultimately lead to apoptosis.[39,40] In accordance with the above findings, our study demonstrated that MAPK1 was significantly downregulated in LUAD and exhibited strong binding affinity with MG. Collectively, these results suggest that MG may participate in lung adenocarcinoma progression by modulating the MAPK signaling pathway.

  Although the present study provides extensive theoretical insights, further experimental validation is required to verify these computational predictions. Future investigations should employ large‑scale epidemiological studies and animal models to confirm the key molecular targets and signaling pathways identified in this work. In the present study, 2 independent GEO datasets (GSE31210 and GSE75037) were employed for expression validation, a strategy that initially augmented the reliability of the identified gene expression patterns by mitigating cohort-specific biases. Nevertheless, potential inter-dataset discrepancies cannot be disregarded. Furthermore, unmeasured confounding variables across datasets – including ethnic disparities, socioeconomic factors, or prior treatment regimens – may further exacerbate heterogeneity, thereby imposing limitations on the direct generalizability of our results to diverse LUAD patient populations. To overcome this limitation and enhance the external validity of future research, we will incorporate larger, more heterogeneous cohorts – encompassing prospective multicenter studies with standardized clinical, pathological, and molecular annotations – to comprehensively capture the full spectrum of lung adenocarcinoma heterogeneity. And docking results provide supportive structural evidence rather than definitive proof of molecular mechanisms, and that experimental validation is required. In addition, it is necessary to conduct longitudinal studies to track long-term exposure and assess disease progression for establishing a clearer causative relationship.


## 5. Conclusions
  In summary, this study is the first to systematically investigate the potential molecular mechanisms by which MG may contribute to LUAD using network toxicology and molecular docking. Through multi‑database integration, PPI network construction, and machine learning approaches including LASSO and random forest analysis, we identified 3 key targets: TP53, CCNB1, and MAPK1. Our results indicate that these 3 genes may serve as pivotal targets underlying MG‑related LUAD, likely by regulating the cell cycle via the p53 and MAPK signaling pathways. This work provides a theoretical basis for the prevention and intervention of LUAD and other health risks associated with MG exposure.
