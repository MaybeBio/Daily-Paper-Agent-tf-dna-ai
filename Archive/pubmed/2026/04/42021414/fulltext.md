# Integration of multi-omics and network toxicology reveals TLR4-mediated nephrotoxicity induced by arecoline


## Background
  Arecoline, the primary alkaloid in areca nut, induces renal injury, yet its underlying molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood. This study aimed to identify arecoline’s molecular targets and elucidate the subsequent pathological signaling cascade in renal cells.


## Methods
  We integrated network toxicology, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to predict arecoline’s targets. In vitro validation in human kidney (HK-2) cells assessed target engagement via a cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA). Downstream signaling, inflammatory, and fibrotic markers were evaluated using Western blotting, ELISA, and immunofluorescence.


## Results
  Computational models predicted a stable arecoline-Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) complex. CETSA demonstrated that arecoline enhanced TLR4 thermal stability in HK-2 cells, strongly indicating target engagement. This binding activated the PI3K/AKT/NF-κB signaling pathway. Consequently, arecoline induced NF-κB-dependent upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β), elevated the renal injury marker KIM-1, and promoted the accumulation of fibrotic proteins (α-SMA, Collagen III).


## Conclusions
  Arecoline instigates nephrotoxicity by targeting TLR4 and activating the PI3K/AKT/NF-κB axis, driving an inflammatory and fibrotic phenotype in renal tubular cells. The arecoline-TLR4 interaction represents a critical event in areca nut-associated nephropathy, offering a novel therapeutic target.

## Introduction
  Arecoline, a naturally occurring alkaloid in areca nut (Areca catechu), is widely consumed across South and Southeast Asia as part of a long-standing cultural practice that often involves betel leaf, slaked lime, and tobacco [1]. While traditionally valued for its mild stimulant effects, chronic areca nut chewing has been associated with severe health consequences, including oral submucous fibrosis, oral and esophageal cancer, cardiovascular disorders, and systemic organ injury such as cardiovascular disease and renal injury [2]. The primary bioactive compound in areca nuts, arecoline, is implicated in these toxicities due to its metabolic activation and interaction with biological systems [3].

  Nephrotoxicity, or renal injury, is an underrecognized but clinically significant consequence of areca nut consumption [4]. The kidneys, responsible for filtering and concentrating xenobiotics, are particularly vulnerable to toxic insults, which can manifest as acute renal injury or progress to chronic kidney disease—a major public health burden worldwide [5]. Epidemiological studies have suggested an association between habitual areca nut chewing and renal dysfunction, with some evidence pointing to arecoline as a potential nephrotoxic agent [6, 7]. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying arecoline-induced nephrotoxicity remain poorly elucidated, necessitating advanced approaches to uncover the pathways involved.

  Network toxicology provides a systems-level framework to interrogate complex chemical–biological interactions by integrating multi-database target mining, protein–protein interaction (PPI) topology, and functional enrichment to reveal key nodes, pathways, and transcriptional regulators [8, 9]. Unlike traditional toxicology, which often focuses on isolated endpoints, network toxicology provides a holistic view of how a compound disrupts cellular homeostasis, making it an ideal tool for studying complex toxicities like nephrotoxicity. By identifying key nodes, pathways, and potential biomarkers, this approach facilitates the discovery of therapeutic targets and preventive strategies [9]. This methodology has been proven effective in delineating the nephrotoxicity of other compounds, such as melamine and cyanuric acid co-exposure, and holds promise for revealing the renal effects of arecoline [8]. Among the potential molecular mediators of such injury, Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) has emerged as a central player in renal pathology. TLR4 is widely expressed in renal cells, particularly in tubular epithelial cells, where it serves as a critical sensor for innate immunity. Upon activation by endogenous damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) or exogenous toxins, TLR4 triggers robust intracellular signaling cascades, initiating sterile inflammation and promoting the transition from acute tubular injury to chronic renal fibrosis [10–13]. Despite its well-established role in driving various nephropathies, whether TLR4 serves as a direct upstream target in arecoline-induced renal injury remains unknown.

  Despite the well-established carcinogenic risks of areca nut chewing, its nephrotoxic effects remain poorly characterized. Most studies have emphasized oral carcinogenesis, leaving systemic toxicity largely unexplored [14]. Conventional toxicological methods may fail to capture the complex and dynamic interplay of molecular events underlying renal injury. To address this gap, we combined computational toxicology with in vitro validation to investigate the nephrotoxic potential of arecoline. By integrating multi-omics data—including gene expression profiles, protein-protein interaction networks, toxicological databases, and transcription factor predictions—we constructed a comprehensive molecular network model, combined with molecular biology experiments to elucidate the mechanism of arecoline’s impact on renal function. Our study aims to comprehensively characterize the nephrotoxic effects of arecoline, identify key pathways and molecular targets, and provide mechanistic insights that may inform intervention strategies to mitigate renal injury in populations at risk from areca nut consumption. ## Method

### Comprehensive network toxicology analysis of arecoline
  To holistically evaluate the biological activity and toxicological potential of arecoline, we implemented a tiered computational workflow initiated by retrieving its canonical SMILES notation as well as 2D and 3D structural expressions from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) [15]. This structural representation was subsequently subjected to pharmacokinetic profiling using SwissADME (http://www.swissadme.ch) [16], which predict the absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) properties of compounds. SwissADME employs Lipinski’s Rule of Five to evaluate oral bioavailability, utilizing five physicochemical parameters: hydrogen bond donors, hydrogen bond acceptors, molecular weight, rotatable bonds, and octanol-water partition coefficient. Complementary toxicological profiling of arecoline was conducted via ADMETlab 3.0 (https://admetlab3.scbdd.com) [17], providing mechanistic insights for subsequent toxicity risk assessment.


### Identification and screening of potential targets of arecoline
  Arecoline (SMILES: CN1CCC = C(C1)C(= O)OC) was selected as the compound of interest for this study. Putative protein targets of arecoline were screened by consolidating prediction outputs from seven computational platforms: BATMAN-TCM (http://bionet.ncpsb.org.cn/batman-tcm/) [18], SwissTargetPrediction (www.swisstargetprediction.ch) [19], SEA (https://sea.bkslab.org/) [20], HIT (http://hit2.badd-cao.net.) [21], SuperPred (http://bioinformatics.charite.de/superpred) [22], TargetNET (http://targetnet.scbdd.com) [23], and PharmMapper (http://lilab.ecust.edu.cn/pharmmapper/) [24]. These platforms facilitate systematic exploration of arecoline’s potential biological interactions and toxicological significance, enabling comprehensive characterization of its pharmacological profile.


### Selection and analysis of nephrotoxicity and renal injury-related target networks
  Genes related to nephrotoxicity were identified by searching three disease-associated databases: the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD, http://ctdbase.org/) [25], the GeneCards database (http://www.genecards.org/) [26], and the Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) database (https://omim.org/) [27]. The search strategy used five standardized nephrotoxicity keywords: “nephrotoxicity”, “renal toxicity”, “kidney toxicity”, “renal injury”, and “kidney injury” to identify associated genes. Results from each database were aggregated, and duplicates were eliminated to compile a unified list of nephrotoxicity and renal injury-related genes.


### Screening of core targets and construction of protein interaction networks
  The STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) [28], which integrates protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks from public repositories and literature evidence, was employed for network construction. Potential arecoline targets were intersected with nephrotoxicity and renal injury-related targets using Venny 2.1 (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/index.html). The resulting overlapping targets were imported into STRING to generate a PPI network under the “multiple proteins” search mode with Homo sapiens as the designated species. The network data were subsequently visualized in Cytoscape 3.9.1. An “arecoline-target-nephrotoxicity and renal injury” interaction network was then constructed. Core hub genes were identified via CytoHubba plugin, followed by KEGG and GO enrichment analysis and transcription factor (TF) prediction prediction for these top-ranked targets.


### Functional analysis and pathway enrichment of target proteins
  The core hub genes were subjected to KEGG and GO enrichment analysis using the “org.Hs.eg.db”, “clusterProfiler” [29], “enrichplot” and “ggplot2” R packages. This analysis elucidated the biological processes (BP), molecular functions (MF), cellular components (CC), and signaling pathways associated with the core targets. Significantly enriched terms were selected based on a threshold of p < 0.05 for subsequent interpretation.


### Prediction of core co-transcription factors
  The identified common genes were submitted to the ChEA3 database (https://amp.pharm.mssm.edu/chea3/) [30], a tool for predicting TF regulation based on integrated gene set libraries. This analysis aimed to identify core co-transcription factors that may regulate the expression of both arecoline targets and nephrotoxicity and renal injury-related genes, shedding light on the transcriptional regulatory mechanisms involved in arecoline-induced nephrotoxicity and renal injury.


### Molecular docking and visualization
  Molecular docking was performed to evaluate the binding affinity between arecoline and the identified core targets. The 3D structures of the core targets, including TLR4 (PDB ID:2Z62)、HSP90AA1(PDB ID:5J2X)、HSP90AB1(PDB ID:6NY8)、IL2(PDB ID:8SOZ) and IL6(PDB ID:1ALU), were downloaded from the Protein Data Bank (PDB, https://www.rcsb.org/), and the structure of arecoline was obtained from PubChem. Docking simulations were executed using AutoDock Vina (version 1.5.7) [31], with binding energies calculated to assess interaction strengths. Docking results were visualized using PyMOL (version 2.5.2) to generate 3D models of the arecoline-target complexes. Key interactions, such as hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic contacts, were highlighted to illustrate the molecular basis of binding.


### Molecular dynamics simulations
  Molecular Dynamics Simulations were performed using Gromacs 2022. Force field parameters were generated with the pdb2gmx tool and the AutoFF web server. The AMBER14SB force field was assigned to the receptor protein, while the GAFF2 force field was applied to the ligand. The system was solvated in a cubic box of TIP3P water molecules with a 1 nm extension [32]. Ions were added to neutralize the system using the gmx genion tool. Long-range electrostatic interactions were treated with the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method using a 1 nm cutoff. Bond constraints were applied with the SHAKE algorithm, and integration was carried out with a 1 fs time step via the Verlet leap-frog algorithm. Prior to production simulation, the system was energy-minimized through 3000 steps of steepest descent followed by 2000 steps of conjugate gradient minimization. The minimization protocol included: constraining the solute while minimizing waters, constraining counterions during minimization, and finally minimizing the entire system without constraints. Simulations were conducted under NPT conditions at 310 K for 100 ns. The gmx rms, gmx rmsf, gmx hbond, gmx gyrate, and gmx sasa tools were employed to calculate root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), hydrogen bonds, radius of gyration (Rg), and solvent accessible surface area (SASA), respectively.


### Cell viability assay
  The cytotoxicity of arecoline on human kidney proximal tubular epithelial (HK-2) cells was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; HY-K0301; MCE) assay. Arecoline (CAS No.63-75-2; A111573; Aladdin) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and diluted in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/Ham’s F-12 K (DMEM/F12K; Thermo Fisher, 11320033) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; S711-001 S; Lonsera, Suzhou Shuangru Biology Science & Technology Co., Ltd) to achieve final concentrations of 0, 5, 15, 30, 60, 120, 240, 480, 960, and 1920 µM. The final concentration of DMSO in all experimental groups was strictly maintained below 0.1% (v/v) to ensure no vehicle-induced cytotoxicity. HK-2 cells were seeded in 96-well plates (Guangzhou Jet BioFiltration Co., Ltd) at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well and incubated for 24 h at 37℃ in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. Cells were then treated with the prepared arecoline solutions for 24 h. Subsequently, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and cells were incubated for an additional 2 h at 37℃. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, USA). The GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0) was used for analyzing and processing the data.


### ELISA for the identification of the levels of inflammatory factors
  HK-2 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well. After 24 h of culture, cells were treated with varying concentrations of arecoline for an additional 24 h. Supernatants were collected, centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 10 min, and analyzed for IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α levels using commercial ELISA kits (Proteintech, China) according to manufacturer protocols. Optical density was measured at 450 nm using a Thermo Scientific microplate reader.


### Western blotting
  Western blotting (WB) was conducted to evaluate the expression levels of proteins related to renal injury and renal fibrosis, as well as the expression levels of key signaling pathway proteins, as previously described [33]. HK-2 cells were treated with selected arecoline concentrations for 24 h and lysed in ice-cold radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (R0020; Solarbio) supplemented with 1% protease inhibitor cocktail (CW2200; Cwbio) and 1% phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (CW2383; Cwbio). Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (PC0020; Solarbio). Equal amounts of heat-denatured protein (30 µg per lane) were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated overnight at 4℃ with primary antibodies against KIM-1 (83221-2-RR; Proteintech;1:5000), α-SMA (AF1032; Affinity;1:1000), Collage Ⅲ (AF5457; Affinity;1:1000), AKT (YP-mAb-14652;UpingBio;1:1000), p-AKT (YP-mAb-14292;UpingBio;1:1000), PI3K (YP-mAb-14915;UpingBio;1:1000), p-PI3K (YP-mAb-14485; UpingBio;1:1000), NF-κB (AF5006; Affinity;1:1000), p-NF-κB (AF2006; Affinity;1:1000), TLR4 (YP-mAb-13172; UpingBio;1:1000), and β-actin (AF7018; Affinity;1:8000) as a loading control. Following three washes with TBST, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (S0001 or S0002; Affinity;1:10000) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using a chemiluminescent HRP substrate (wbkls0500; Millipore) and quantified with ImageJ software (version 1.53).


### Immunofluorescence staining
  Cells were grown in a confocal dish, fixed in 4.0% formaldehyde at room temperature for 15 min, permeated with 0.25% Triton X-100 at room temperature for 10 min, and sealed with 5.0% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h. Then the cells were incubated with primary antibodies: anti-IL-1β (GB12115-50; Servicebio), anti-IL-6 (GB11117-50; Servicebio), and anti-KIM-1 (PA5-79345; Thermo Fisher) overnight at 4℃. Anti-rabbit IgG fragment (CY3) (GB21403; Servicebio)-labeled, anti-mouse (Alexa Fluor 488) (GB25301; Servicebio)-labeledand anti-rabbit (Alexa Fluor 488) (GB25303; Servicebio)-labeled with secondary antibody were incubated for 1 h, and nuclei were stained with DAPI. Images were analyzed by laser confocal microanalysis system (Nikon DS-Qi1MC, Nikon Corporation).


### Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)
  Specifically, when cell density reached about 95%, proteins were extracted using cell lysis buffer. Arecoline (120 µM) was then added and the mixture was incubated for 3 h. After incubation, the protein solution was aliquoted into eight microcentrifuge tubes and heated at gradient temperatures (42, 46, 50, 54, 58, 62, 66 and 70℃) for 3 min. The samples were subsequently centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 20 min, and the supernatants were collected. Then, 5× loading buffer was added to the supernatants, followed by boiling for 5 min. Finally, the denatured protein samples were used to perform Western blotting.


### Statistical analysis
  All data are presented as standard deviation (SD) ± mean (n = 3). Comparisons between the two groups were performed using the T-test, and comparisons among more than two groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software.


## Results

### Preliminary arecoline toxicity network assessment
  Based on SwissADME predictions, we evaluated the pharmacokinetic properties of arecoline. Its molecular structure formula is shown in Fig. 2A. As summarized in Table 1, arecoline satisfies the molecular size criterion for orally active compounds with a molecular weight of 155.19 g/mol (below the 500 g/mol threshold). Furthermore, arecoline exhibits favorable absorption and distribution characteristics: three hydrogen-bond acceptors (within the ≤ 10 limit), zero hydrogen-bond donors, and two rotatable bonds (below the ≤ 10 threshold). The Moriguchi Log P (MLogP) value of 0.58 (< 4.15) indicates high gastrointestinal absorption potential. Collectively, arecoline demonstrates full compliance with Lipinski’s Rule of Five parameters (Fig. 2B), suggesting promising bioavailability and inherent bioactivity potential upon oral administration [34].

  Toxicological risk assessment via ADMETlab 3.0 indicated significant nephrotoxic potential of arecoline in simulated biological systems, with a drug-induced nephrotoxicity probability of 0.590 (Table 2). This value represents a statistically significant moderate-to-high risk threshold (p < 0.05), suggesting that chronic arecoline exposure from habitual areca nut consumption may pose substantial human health hazards. Implementation of rigorous risk assessment protocols and preventive measures is urgently warranted to mitigate potential public health impacts. Further experimental validation of these computational predictions is essential, alongside exploration of toxicity-mitigation strategies.

### Network toxicology analysis of arecoline targets in nephrotoxicity and renal injury
  Putative targets of arecoline were predicted by consolidating outputs from seven computational platforms, yielding 249 unique protein targets after deduplication. Nephrotoxicity-related targets were then systematically curated from GeneCards, CTD, and OMIM, resulting in 4,221 nephrotoxicity targets, 17,821 renal injury-associated genes, and 4,066 disease-intersection genes. To elucidate connections between arecoline exposure and renal pathology, a Venn diagram was constructed identifying 146 intersecting target genes (Fig. 3A). These overlapping targets were subsequently imported into the STRING database to generate a PPI network (Fig. 3B). Finally, an integrated arecoline-target-nephrotoxicity and renal injury network was reconstructed using Cytoscape 3.9.1 (Fig. 3C), comprehensively mapping interrelationships between the compound, disease phenotypes, and core biological targets.


### KEGG and GO analysis of arecoline targets in nephrotoxicity and renal injury
  To systematically elucidate the mechanistic basis of arecoline-induced nephrotoxicity and renal injury, we performed GO/KEGG enrichment analyses on core targets using R, revealing significant enrichment in plasma membrane signaling receptor complexes (GO:0098802), serine/threonine protein kinase complexes (GO:1902554), and cyclin-dependent protein kinase holoenzyme complexes (GO:0000307) for CC; protein tyrosine kinase activity (GO:0004713), transmembrane receptor protein tyrosine kinase activity (GO:0004714), and protein serine/threonine kinase activity (GO:0004674) for MF; and cell death in response to oxidative stress (GO:0036473), regulation of oxidative stress-induced cell death (GO:1903201), positive regulation of oxidative stress-induced cell death (GO:1903209) for BP, with KEGG analysis further indicating PI3K/AKT signaling pathway activation (hsa04151) as a key nephrotoxicity mechanism (Fig. 3D-E). Collectively, arecoline-induced nephrotoxicity and renal injury result from convergent actions across multiple pathological pathways, including oxidative stress-mediated cell death, dysregulated kinase signaling, and PI3K/AKT pathway hyperactivation, as evidenced by our integrated network pharmacology and functional enrichment analyses.

To further predict the key pathways involved in arecoline-induced nephrotoxicity and renal injury, we integrated KEGG analysis results with pathway-specific information from the KEGG database and consolidated disease- and drug-target associations. This revealed the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway as a potentially central mediator, likely facilitating arecoline’s nephrotoxic effects via activation of NFKB1 (Fig. 4C).

### Arecoline targets TLR4 and stabilizes its structure
  To identify specific targets underlying arecoline’s action, we intersected the core drug-disease targets—specifically referring to the top-ranked hub genes previously identified via the CytoHubba plugin and described in Fig. 4—with the PI3K/AKT pathway, yielding eight overlapping targets (Fig. 5A). From these, five upstream hub targets—IL-6, IL-2, TLR4, HSP90AA1, and HSP90AB1—were selected for molecular docking with arecoline (Fig. 5B). The results showed binding energies below − 5 kcal/mol for TLR4 (-5.31 kcal/mol), HSP90AA1 (-5.24 kcal/mol), and HSP90AB1 (-5.10 kcal/mol), with TLR4 exhibiting the strongest binding affinity. Three-dimensional interaction models of the docking poses are presented in Fig. 5C.

  To further validate the binding stability of the TLR4-arecoline complex, molecular dynamics simulations were performed. RMSD serves as a reliable indicator of the conformational stability of proteins and ligands, reflecting the deviation of atomic positions from their initial coordinates. Lower RMSD values indicate greater conformational stability. Accordingly, RMSD was used to evaluate the equilibration of the simulated system. As shown in Fig. 5D, the complex system reached equilibrium after 5 ns, fluctuating around 1.2 Å thereafter, indicating high stability upon binding between the small molecule and the target protein. The Rg describes the overall structural compactness and is used to characterize the tightness of a protein structure. The relatively stable fluctuations in Rg during the simulation suggest no significant expansion or contraction of the small molecule–target protein complex (Fig. 5E). SASA was used to evaluate the protein surface area. The SASA between the target protein and the small molecule was calculated (Fig. 5F). The results show no significant change in SASA upon ligand binding, suggesting minimal impact on the protein structure. Hydrogen bonds play a crucial role in ligand–protein binding. The number of hydrogen bonds between the small molecule and the target protein during the simulation is shown in Fig. 5G. The number fluctuated between 0 and 2, with one hydrogen bond being most frequently observed. These results indicate favorable hydrogen-bond interactions within the complex. The RMSF reflects the flexibility of individual amino acid residues in the protein. As illustrated in Fig. 5H, the complex exhibited relatively low RMSF values (mostly below 1.8 Å), indicating low flexibility and high stability. In summary, the complex system demonstrated stable fluctuations throughout the simulation, suggesting strong binding between the small molecule and the target protein.

  To further investigate the direct interaction strength between arecoline and TLR4, we performed validation using the CETSA. The CETSA results demonstrated that TLR4 protein gradually degraded under progressively increasing temperatures, while arecoline delayed TLR4 degradation and enhanced its stability (Fig. 5I-J). Collectively, these findings suggest that arecoline promotes nephrotoxicity through binding to TLR4 and activating the PI3K/AKT/NF-κB signaling axis.

### Arecoline promotes the secretion of inflammatory factors in HK-2 cells
  To investigate the genuine impact of arecoline on HK-2 cells, we initially conducted a CCK-8 assay (Fig. 6A). The results demonstrated that arecoline concentrations below 240 µM were non-cytotoxic, whereas concentrations exceeding 240 µM exhibited significant cytotoxicity in a dose-dependent manner. Consequently, a safe concentration range of 0 to 120 µM was selected for subsequent molecular biology experiments. To further validate the mechanism of arecoline-induced nephrotoxicity, we measured the expression levels of inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β in cell supernatants post-treatment using ELISA. The results indicated that arecoline treatment significantly upregulated the secretion of inflammatory cytokines in HK-2 cells (Fig. 6B-D). Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining results also demonstrated that arecoline upregulated the expression of IL-1β and IL-6 in HK-2 cells (Fig. 6E).

### Arecoline regulates the expression of nephrotoxicity-related proteins and key signaling pathway proteins
  Immunofluorescence staining revealed that arecoline treatment significantly upregulated the expression of KIM-1, an early renal injury biomarker [35], in HK-2 cells (Fig. 7A-B). Simultaneously, WB analysis was performed to assess the expression of nephrotoxicity-related proteins and key signaling pathway proteins. Consistent with previous research results, the fibrosis indicators α-SMA and COL Ⅲ showed increased expression as the concentration of arecoline increased (Fig. 7C and E-F) [14]. Notably, the expression level of KIM-1 and TLR4 and the phosphorylation levels of key signaling proteins, including PI3K, AKT, and NF-κB, were positively correlated with increasing concentrations of arecoline (Fig. 7C, D and G-J).

## Discussion
  Renal injury represents a significant and often underrecognized complication of chemical exposure, with growing evidence linking environmental toxins and natural compounds to progressive nephropathy [36, 37]. Within this context, our study demonstrates that arecoline—a primary alkaloid of areca nut—induces nephrotoxicity and renal injury through multi-pathway dysregulation. These findings provide a critical mechanistic basis for clinical observations linking areca nut consumption to renal dysfunction.

  This study integrates bioinformatics predictions with in vitro experimental validation to uncover the molecular mechanisms by which arecoline, a major alkaloid in areca nuts, induces nephrotoxicity and renal injury. Toxicity prediction platforms indicate that arecoline exhibits significant hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, neurotoxicity, and reproductive toxicity. Animal and cellular studies further demonstrate that arecoline can induce epithelial-mesenchymal transition and fibrosis in renal tubular cells, contributing to the progression of chronic kidney disease [14, 38]. Furthermore, epidemiological data have established a positive association between betel nut chewing and an increased risk of chronic kidney disease [4, 39]. Thus, our investigation into the nephrotoxic and renal injury effects of arecoline is grounded in a solid theoretical and empirical foundation.

  TLR4, the first identified member of the mammalian Toll-like receptor family, is widely expressed in renal cells, particularly in renal tubular epithelial cells [40]. TLR4 serves as a critical molecule bridging innate immunity and inflammatory injury. It is a well-established regulator of innate immune responses and has been implicated in various models of renal injury, such as ischemia-reperfusion injury and diabetic nephropathy [41, 42]. Molecular docking analysis, molecular dynamics simulations, and CETSA experimental results collectively demonstrate that TLR4 serves as a high-affinity target for arecoline, indicating a robust molecular interaction. It is worth noting that our CETSA data demonstrate that arecoline binds to and physically stabilizes the existing pool of TLR4 protein, while a marked increase in total TLR4 expression is observed following arecoline exposure. Binding of natural small molecules can induce conformational stabilization of proteins or promote their proper folding and maturation in the endoplasmic reticulum [43, 44]. Together, these mechanisms may contribute to the accumulation of TLR4 protein following arecoline exposure, although further studies are needed to validate this hypothesis. This engagement likely initiates a cascading activation of the PI3K/AKT/NF-κB signaling axis, a pathway well-established in mediating inflammatory and apoptotic responses in renal tissues [45]. Mechanistically, previous studies have established a direct link wherein TLR4 activation recruits adaptor proteins (such as MyD88), which subsequently activate Class I PI3K, leading to downstream AKT phosphorylation and NF-κB nuclear translocation [46–48]. Our findings perfectly align with this established paradigm. The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway participates in various cellular activities and mediates key physiological processes including glucose homeostasis, lipid metabolism, autophagy, apoptosis, inflammation, and oxidative stress. It plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of diabetic nephropathy (DN) and renal injury [49]. NF-κB signaling is a critical pathway in tissue inflammation, as NF-κB regulates the expression of inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and adhesion molecules—all of which play essential roles in modulating inflammatory responses [50]. In previous studies, the activation of NF-κB protein and elevated levels of TNF-α and IL-1β have been positively correlated with renal inflammatory injury and extracellular matrix deposition in DN rat models and high glucose-treated HK-2 cells. Inhibition of NF-κB has been shown to effectively alleviate renal inflammation and fibrosis induced by persistent hyperglycemia in DN [51]. Our network analysis suggests that upstream signaling pathways of NF‑κB, such as PI3K/AKT and TLR4, may represent key mechanisms through which arecoline induces inflammatory renal injury. Consistent with this perspective, arecoline significantly upregulates key inflammatory cytokines, TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β in HK-2 cells. It is noteworthy that in our cellular model, while significant cytotoxicity (cell death) was observed at 240 µM, robust inflammatory and injury marker (KIM-1) elevations occurred at sub-lethal concentrations (60–120 µM). This discrepancy is expected in toxicological models, highlighting that arecoline induces profound functional impairment and molecular initiating events well before reaching the threshold of overt cell death. Furthermore, to rigorously capture this inflammatory cascade, we utilized ELISA to quantify the extracellular secretion of the primary initiator TNF-α, complementing this with immunofluorescence of IL-1β and IL-6 to confirm intracellular pro-inflammatory activation. These findings indicate that beyond the “EMT–fibrosis” axis, arecoline may also inflict renal injury through TLR4-mediated inflammatory pathways, suggesting that its pathogenic profile is not limited to a purely fibrosis-prone trajectory, but rather encompasses an “inflammation–fibrosis” continuum.

  While our study provides compelling evidence for the role of the TLR4/PI3K/AKT/NF‑κB pathway in vitro, it has certain limitations. First, the use of HK-2 cells, while valuable for mechanistic studies, does not fully capture the complexity of kidney tissue in a living organism. Future research should employ animal models of arecoline exposure to validate these findings in a physiological context. Second, our focus on the TLR4/PI3K/AKT/NF‑κB axis may overlook other pathways contributing to arecoline’s nephrotoxicity. Beyond inflammatory activation, our network analysis suggests that arecoline exposure perturbed essential biological processes related to oxidative stress and kinase activity. Furthermore, transcriptional regulator prediction highlighted NFKB1 and MYRFL as central orchestrators of this toxicological network, offering novel targets for future therapeutic intervention. Exploring these pathways could yield a more comprehensive picture of arecoline’s effects on the kidney.

  From a clinical perspective, the relevance of our findings to human health remains to be established. Given the widespread consumption of areca nut products in regions like South Asia, epidemiological studies are needed to quantify the burden of kidney disease among users and correlate it with arecoline exposure. Therapeutically, inhibiting TLR4 (for example, by using TAK-242) may be a strategy to alleviate the renal toxicity damage caused by arecoline. Moreover, future in vitro and in vivo studies employing TLR4 antagonists like TAK-242 in the presence of arecoline will provide crucial functional data to further support TLR4 as the direct binding target. However, the feasibility and safety of such intervention measures still need to be further investigated in preclinical and clinical settings. In this context, exploring natural products presents a highly promising therapeutic alternative. Recent evidence strongly highlights the renoprotective effects of natural compounds; for instance, wogonoside has been shown to significantly protect against podocyte and tubular injury in diabetic nephropathy [52]. This underscores the broad therapeutic potential of natural products in mitigating complex inflammatory and fibrotic kidney diseases [53].


## Conclusion
  In conclusion, this study demonstrates that arecoline induces nephrotoxicity and renal injury by regulating the TLR4/PI3K/AKT/NF‑κB signaling pathway, offering a novel mechanistic insight into its nephrotoxic effects. By integrating bioinformatics and experimental approaches, we have identified a molecular framework that enhances our understanding of arecoline-induced nephrotoxicity and renal injury and points to potential therapeutic targets. These findings emphasize the need to address the renal risks of areca nut consumption and lay the foundation for future research into preventive and therapeutic strategies for affected populations.
