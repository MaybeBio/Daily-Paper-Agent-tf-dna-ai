# GC-MS guided phytochemical profiling of Bauhinia tomentosa flowers: molecular docking against HPV-E6 and p53 in cervical cancer


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-40361-5.


## Introduction
  Cervical cancer is a major global health concern, ranking as the fourth most common cancer in women and one of the leading causes of mortality in low- and middle-income countries. Its incidence is particularly high in South Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa, where barriers to early screening, HPV vaccination, and adequate treatment contribute to its prevalence. According to the National Cancer Registry Programme of India, cervical cancer remains the second most common cancer among Indian women, with projections suggesting more than 85,000 new cases annually by 20251. Persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) types, especially HPV-16 and HPV-18, underlies nearly all cases of cervical carcinogenesis, primarily through viral oncoproteins E6 and E7. Although p53 is widely implicated across cancers, in HPV‑positive cervical cancer it is specifically inactivated through HPV-E6–driven ubiquitin‑mediated degradation, making the E6–p53 axis a defining hallmark of HPV‑driven cervical carcinogenesis2–4. These oncoproteins inactivate tumor suppressors such as p53 and Rb, thereby promoting genomic instability and malignant transformation5. Approaches that inhibit E6 or rescue p53 activity have been shown to restore p53-p21 signalling and trigger apoptosis in HPV‑positive cervical cancer models6,7. High‑risk HPV-E6 promotes cervical carcinogenesis by recruiting E6AP to ubiquitinate and degrade the tumour suppressor p53, thereby disabling DNA‑damage‑induced cell‑cycle arrest and apoptosis2–4. Despite the availability of chemotherapeutic drugs such as cisplatin and cyclophosphamide, their clinical success is limited by systemic toxicity, drug resistance, and non-specific cytotoxicity8,9. Thus, the search for safer, plant-derived anticancer agents capable of modulating HPV-driven oncogenesis remains an urgent biomedical priority10.

  The genus Bauhinia (family: Caesalpiniaceae), widely distributed in tropical and subtropical regions, encompasses more than 300 species traditionally valued for their medicinal properties. Numerous Bauhinia species are documented in Ayurveda, Siddha, and other folk systems of medicine for treating microbial infections, inflammation, diabetes, and cancer11,12. Phytochemical studies across Bauhinia species have identified a repertoire of bioactive metabolites, including flavonoids (kaempferol, quercetin, rutin), phytosterols (β-sitosterol, stigmasterol), terpenoids (lupeol), tannins, and saponins, many of which exhibit antioxidant, immunomodulatory, and antitumor activities13,14. Among these, Bauhinia tomentosa Linn., locally known as “Mantharai” in Tamil Nadu, is a perennial ornamental shrub distributed across South India and Sri Lanka. Traditionally, it has been used for treating wounds, ulcers, diarrhea, microbial infections, and inflammatory disorders15. The plant species is rich in secondary metabolites with diverse pharmacological effects, including antidiabetic, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, antioxidant, and anticancer properties. Experimental studies have demonstrated its ability to scavenge free radicals, modulate cytokines, and regulate inflammatory mediators16–18. Phytochemical screening of B. tomentosa flowers, leaves, and buds has revealed the presence of multiple flavonoids, phenolic acids, phytosterols, and triterpenes. Radha et al.19reported the isolation of novel compounds from the flowering buds, including hydroxy-methoxy phenyl derivatives, 5-hydroxyflavone, and pentahydroxyflavones. These findings highlight the structural diversity of the metabolites and their potential contribution to bioactivity. Recent investigations have further confirmed that the methanolic extracts of B. tomentosa contain alkaloids, glycosides, saponins, steroids, tannins, and coumarins20.

  In addition to conventional phytochemistry, nanoparticle-based studies have exploited the bio-reductive properties of B. tomentosa. Silver nanoparticles biosynthesized from its flower extracts were found to possess significant antibacterial, antifungal, and antioxidant activity, attributed to the functional groups of flavonoids and phenolics acting as stabilizing and capping agents20. Such data emphasize that B. tomentosa flowers harbor multifunctional metabolites capable of biomedical applications, making them promising candidates for anticancer exploration. Multiple in vivo studies have validated the pharmacological potential of B. tomentosa. Kannan et al.16demonstrated its nephroprotective activity against cisplatin-induced renal toxicity in rats. Methanolic extracts significantly restored antioxidant enzymes (SOD, CAT, GSH), reduced lipid peroxidation, and lowered serum creatinine and urea, suggesting both protective and antioxidant functions. Similarly, its extracts ameliorated acetic acid-induced ulcerative colitis in animal models by suppressing inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α, iNOS, and MPO, comparable to sulfasalazine15. Notably, B. tomentosa has shown distinct anticancer potential. An in vivo study using Dalton’s ascites lymphoma (DAL) model revealed that methanolic extracts prolonged lifespan by 52% and reduced tumor volume by 73% when co-administered with cyclophosphamide, indicating potent synergistic and chemoprotective effects21. The antitumor activity was attributed to immune modulation, enhancement of antioxidant defense, and suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and VEGF. Together, these findings substantiate the plant’s anticancer relevance and provide a scientific rationale for further mechanistic evaluation. Despite these promising pharmacological studies, the specific phytocompounds responsible for the anticancer effects of B. tomentosa flowers remain poorly characterized. Traditional approaches often rely on crude extracts, which limit mechanistic understanding. Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) offers a powerful analytical platform to identify volatile and semi-volatile secondary metabolites, while in silico molecular docking provides insights into the binding interactions of these compounds with key cancer-related targets22.

  In cervical cancer, the oncoprotein HPV-E6 is a central driver of malignancy due to its ability to bind and degrade p53, the guardian of the genome. Targeting HPV-E6 directly, or restoring p53 activity, represents a dual therapeutic strategy to halt oncogenesis5. Therefore, evaluating the binding potential of B. tomentosa flower metabolites against HPV-E6 and p53 provides a rational route to uncover plant-derived anticancer candidates. Recent GC-MS analyses of B. tomentosa extracts have identified major bioactive compounds such as stigmasterol, γ-sitosterol, lupeol, and phenolic derivatives23. These compounds are well-documented for their cytotoxicity, apoptosis-inducing properties, and ability to interfere with signaling pathways in cancer models. For example, γ-sitosterol has been reported to induce apoptosis via caspase activation, while lupeol has demonstrated anti-proliferative effects in multiple cancer cell lines24. By systematically integrating phytochemical profiling with molecular docking and ADMET screening, it is possible to bridge the gap between ethnopharmacology and modern cancer drug discovery. This study aims to profile the methanolic extract of B. tomentosa flowers using GC-MS, identify the phytochemical constituents, and evaluate their molecular docking interactions with HPV-E6 and p53. The ADMET and drug-likeness analyses further provide predictive insights into pharmacokinetic behavior and toxicity risks, thereby assessing their suitability for oral drug development. By combining chemical profiling and computational pharmacology, the present study provides the first comprehensive account of B. tomentosa flowers as a potential natural source of anticancer agents targeting cervical cancer.


## Materials and methods

### Sample collection and preparation of extract
  Fresh flowers of Bauhinia tomentosa Linn. (Mantharai) were collected from Madipakkam, Chennai, Tamil Nadu, India (Fig. 1). The plant species was authenticated by a taxonomist (Dr. K. Devanathan, Botanist, Department of Botany, The American College, Madurai-625002, Tamil Nadu, India) and a voucher specimen (Specimen ID: BT-2025-MT-018) has been deposited at the Herbarium of Saveetha University, Saveetha Medical College, SIMATS, Thandalam, Chennai, Tamil Nadu, India. The collected flowers were washed with tap water and rinsed with distilled water to remove surface contaminants. They were shade-dried at ambient temperature (28 ± 2 °C) for 10 days and pulverized into a fine powder using a mechanical grinder. For extraction, 10 g of powdered flower sample was soaked in 100 mL of analytical-grade methanol (1:10 w/v) in a conical flask. The flask was sealed and incubated at 37 °C in a rotary shaker for 48 h to facilitate cold maceration. The extract was filtered using Whatman No. 1 filter paper, and the filtrate was concentrated under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator at 40 °C. The crude methanol extract was stored at 4 °C in amber vials for subsequent GC-MS profiling and molecular docking studies.

### Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analysis
  The phytochemical constituents of the methanol extract of Bauhinia tomentosa flowers were analyzed using GC-MS. The analysis was performed on an Agilent GC 7890 A system equipped with a 5975 C inert XL EI/CI MSD mass selective detector. The chromatographic separation was achieved using a DB-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm ID × 0.25 μm film thickness). The injection volume was 1 µL, administered in splitless mode. The oven temperature was initially set at 30 °C, then ramped at 10 °C/min to 350 °C and held for 4 min. Helium (99.99%) was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1 mL/min. The injector and transfer line temperatures were maintained at 280 °C. Electron ionization was conducted at 70 eV, with the mass scan range set between 45 and 380 m/z. The acquired mass spectra were analyzed using the NIST 20 Mass Spectral Library to identify the components based on similarity index scores. Compounds were characterized by their retention time (RT), name, molecular formula, CAS number, match score, and relative abundance. The percentage composition of each compound was calculated based on peak area normalization.


### Molecular docking
  Molecular docking studies were carried out to assess the binding affinity of bioactive phytocompounds identified from the methanolic extract of Bauhinia tomentosa flowers against cervical cancer-associated protein targets. The docking workflow was conducted using Schrödinger Suite 2021 (Schrödinger LLC, New York, USA).


### Ligand preparation
  The chemical structures of the selected phytocompounds, identified through GC-MS profiling, were retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Ligand structures were prepared using the LigPrep module, which generated three-dimensional conformations with accurate stereochemistry, ionization states (within the pH range 2.0 to 7.0), and up to 32 stereoisomers per ligand. Geometry optimization and energy minimization were performed using the OPLS_2005 force field.


### Target protein selection and preparation
  Two protein targets with established roles in cervical carcinogenesis were selected based on their therapeutic relevance: HPV-E6 (PDB ID: 4XR8), the viral oncoprotein responsible for E6‑mediated degradation of p53, and human p53 (PDB ID: 1TUP), the central tumour suppressor frequently inactivated in HPV‑positive cervical cancer4,6,25. While p53 is a common tumour suppressor across malignancies, its direct functional inactivation by HPV-E6 in cervical epithelium and its use as a readout of restored tumour‑suppressor activity in recent E6‑inhibition studies support its selection here as a disease‑relevant host target al.ongside HPV-E64,7,26. The 3D-crystal structures of all protein targets were downloaded from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/). Protein preparation was carried out using the Protein Preparation Wizard of the Schrödinger Suite, which included the assignment of bond orders, addition of hydrogen atoms, optimization of the hydrogen bonding network, correction of metal ion geometries, and removal of water molecules using the Epik module. Protonation states were assigned at physiological pH, and the protein structures were subjected to restrained energy minimization using the OPLS_2005 force field to ensure structural stability before molecular docking.


### Receptor grid generation and docking
  Receptor grids were generated at the active sites of each protein using default van der Waals scaling factors and partial charge cut-off parameters. Molecular docking was conducted using the Glide module. Preliminary screening was performed in High Throughput Virtual Screening (HTVS) mode to filter potential binders. The top-ranking ligands were further refined using the Extra Precision (XP) mode. Binding affinities were assessed based on Glide G-scores, and the most favorable poses were selected for subsequent visualization and molecular interaction analysis27.


### Molecular dynamics simulation
  The top-ranked protein-ligand complexes obtained from molecular docking were further subjected to Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulations using Desmond v3, integrated within the Schrödinger Suite. Each complex was embedded in an orthorhombic simulation box and solvated using the Single Point Charge (SPC) water model. System neutrality was achieved by adding appropriate numbers of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ counterions. Prior to simulation, energy minimization was performed to relax the system and remove steric clashes. MD simulations were then conducted for a duration of 100 nanosecond (ns) under NPT ensemble conditions, with a constant temperature of 300 K and pressure of 1.013 bar. Temperature and pressure were maintained using the Nosé-Hoover chain thermostat and Martyna-Tobias-Klein barostat, respectively. The OPLS_2005 force field was applied throughout the simulation for both protein and ligand. Post-simulation trajectory analyses were carried out to assess the structural stability and flexibility of the protein-ligand complexes. Key parameters such as Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD) and Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF) were computed to evaluate the conformational dynamics and binding stability of the ligands within the active sites of the target proteins.


### Principal component analysis (PCA) and free energy landscape (FEL)
  Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was conducted to further explore the conformational space and dominant motion patterns of the protein-ligand complexes during molecular dynamics simulations. PCA was performed on the Cα atoms of the protein backbone to extract the essential dynamics by reducing the dimensionality of atomic motions. The covariance matrix of atomic positional fluctuations was calculated and diagonalized to obtain eigenvalues and eigenvectors, which represent the amplitude and direction of motion, respectively. The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) were used to construct a two-dimensional projection plot, thereby visualizing the dominant conformational transitions. Subsequently, a Free Energy Landscape (FEL) analysis was performed using the same PC1 and PC2 data to map the thermodynamically stable states of the protein-ligand complexes. The free energy was calculated based on the probability distribution of conformations using the Boltzmann equation: ΔG = -RT In (P). Where ΔG is the free energy, R is the gas constant, T is the temperature, and P is the probability of the system adopting a particular conformational state. The FEL plots highlighted energy minima, corresponding to stable conformational basins, which offer details about the structural stability, flexibility, and binding-induced shifts in protein dynamics upon ligand interaction. These analyses validated the conformational convergence and thermodynamic stability of the selected protein-ligand complexes, strengthening the molecular docking and MD simulation findings.


### Pharmacokinetics and drug‑likeness prediction
  The pharmacokinetic behavior and drug-likeness properties of the top-scoring phytocompounds identified from the methanol extract of Bauhinia tomentosa flowers were evaluated using in silico tools. Researchers employed two widely used web servers, SwissADME28and admetSAR29, to predict key ADME (Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Excretion) characteristics. To assess drug-likeness, Lipinski’s Rule of Five was applied. According to this rule, a compound is considered to exhibit excellent oral bioavailability if it meets at least four of the following criteria: LogP (lipophilicity) ≤ 5, molecular weight ≤ 500 Da, hydrogen bond donors ≤ 5, hydrogen bond acceptors ≤ 10, and number of rotatable bonds ≤ 10. SwissADME also computed additional pharmacokinetic parameters, including topological polar surface area (TPSA), water solubility (Log S), saturation (fraction of sp³-hybridized carbons), and the number of rotatable bonds (an indicator of molecular flexibility). These descriptors offer details about a compound’s bioavailability, permeability, and structural drug-likeness. Furthermore, blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeability, human intestinal absorption (HIA), P-glycoprotein (PGP) substrate/inhibitor status, and cytochrome P450 (CYP450) inhibition profiles were evaluated to estimate metabolic interactions and transport characteristics. Toxicological risk assessments were performed using the admetSAR and OSIRIS Property Explorer30. Parameters such as LD₅₀ (median lethal dose), toxicity class, mutagenicity, tumorigenicity, reproductive toxicity, and irritant potential were predicted to assess the safety profile of each compound. These predictions provide a comprehensive understanding of the pharmacokinetic suitability and potential toxicity of the bioactive phytocompounds as promising anticancer agents.


## Results and discussion

### Identification of phytochemicals from B. tomentosa flowers
  GC-MS analysis of the methanolic extract of B. tomentosa flowers identified 45 phytochemical compounds (Table 1; Fig. 2). The extraction and profiling of phytoconstituents are critical steps in drug discovery pipelines, especially for herbal-based therapeutics. Belonging to the family Caesalpiniaceae, B. tomentosa is reported to be a rich source of bioactive secondary metabolites, including flavonoids, phytosterols, terpenoids, phenolics, tannins, and saponins19,20. These compounds are well known for their antioxidant, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer activities, and they thus contribute to the ethnomedicinal applications of the plant. The major constituents detected in the methanolic flower extract were stigmasterol (18.28%), γ-sitosterol (12.28%), 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (14.01%), and 4 H-pyran-4-one, 2,3-dihydro-3,5-dihydroxy-6-methyl- (13.68%) (Table 1). These three major constituents are also highlighted on the GC‑MS chromatogram for visual reference (Fig. 2). Minor but pharmacologically significant compounds included lupeol, octadecanoic acid, phytol, and other terpenoids (Fig. 2).

  Many of these identified metabolites have been previously reported in other medicinal plants for their roles in modulating oxidative stress, apoptosis induction, and inhibition of tumor progression23,24. Bis(2‑ethylhexyl) phthalate (DEHP) was also detected in the chromatogram; however, DEHP is a well‑known plasticizer used in PVC and related materials and has been widely reported as an environmental and analytical contaminant in plant and herbal preparations due to leaching from plastic consumables31–33. As there is no evidence that DEHP is biosynthesized by Bauhinia species, the present signal is most plausibly attributable to exogenous plasticizer contamination during sample handling, ground water contamination in low land areas or storage rather than an endogenous metabolite. The detection of phytosterols (stigmasterol and γ-sitosterol) is particularly important, as these molecules are well recognized for their cytotoxic and pro-apoptotic properties in cancer cells. Similarly, the triterpene lupeol has been extensively documented for its ability to suppress NF-κB signaling and induce apoptosis, while 5-hydroxymethylfurfural and pyran derivatives contribute to antioxidant defense mechanisms. Collectively, the GC-MS findings confirm that B. tomentosa flowers are a chemically diverse reservoir of pharmacologically relevant compounds that may synergistically contribute to anticancer potential.

### Molecular docking of protein-ligand system using autodocktools
  Molecular docking was performed to investigate the interaction between phytochemicals identified from B. tomentosa flowers and cervical cancer-associated protein targets HPV-E6 (PDB: 4XR8) and p53 (PDB: 1TUP). The analysis of 45 phytocompounds revealed that several metabolites displayed high binding affinity, outperforming the reference chemotherapeutic agents cisplatin and bicalutamide (Table 2).

  Against HPV-E6, the top-ranking compound was γ-sitosterol, which exhibited a binding energy of -7.8 kcal/mol, followed closely by stigmasterol (-7.5 kcal/mol) and lupeol (-7.6 kcal/mol). These phytosterols demonstrated stronger binding than cisplatin (-4.0 kcal/mol) and even surpassed bicalutamide (-7.1 kcal/mol) (Fig. 3A and 3C). The interactions of γ-sitosterol with HPV-E6 involved stabilization of residues implicated in the degradation of p53, suggesting its potential to disrupt viral oncoprotein-host protein interactions. Phytosterols such as γ-sitosterol are known for their pleiotropic pharmacological actions, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anticancer activities, with several studies reporting their ability to induce apoptosis through caspase activation and modulation of Bcl-2 family proteins34–36. The structural similarity of γ-sitosterol and stigmasterol to cholesterol also allows them to integrate into cell membranes, altering membrane dynamics and potentially influencing signaling cascades involved in oncogenesis.

  Docking studies with the p53 protein, which acts as a tumor suppressor, highlighted γ-sitosterol (-7.5 kcal/mol), lupeol (-7.3 kcal/mol), and stigmasterol (-7.2 kcal/mol) as the most favorable ligands (Fig. 3D and 3F). These binding affinities were superior to cisplatin (-4.2 kcal/mol) and comparable to or stronger than bicalutamide (-6.9 kcal/mol). The identified compounds interacted with the DNA-binding core domain of p53, stabilizing regions critical for transcriptional activity and apoptotic signaling. Such interactions suggest that B. tomentosa metabolites could potentially restore or enhance p53-mediated tumor suppressor functions, which are often compromised in cervical cancer through HPV-E6-mediated degradation37,38. The triterpene lupeol is particularly notable, as it has been documented to inhibit proliferation and induce apoptosis in a variety of cancers, including cervical carcinoma, via modulation of PI3K/Akt, NF-κB, and Wnt/β-catenin pathways39. Similarly, phytosterols such as γ-sitosterol have been reported to suppress tumor growth in breast, prostate, and colon cancers through regulation of cell cycle checkpoints, induction of oxidative stress, and inhibition of metastasis-associated enzymes34,36.

  Overall, docking results strongly support the notion that γ-sitosterol, stigmasterol, and lupeol are the key phytochemicals of B. tomentosa flowers with dual binding potential to HPV-E6 and p53. Their superior docking scores relative to cisplatin highlight the therapeutic promise of these naturally occurring compounds as potential anticancer leads for cervical cancer. To provide an overview of the most promising ligands, the top‑docking phytocompounds and reference drugs are summarized in Table 3, while the complete docking dataset is presented in Table 2. Beyond docking scores, the key protein-ligand interactions provide additional insight into the predicted potency of the lead compounds. For HPV-E6, γ‑sitosterol formed stable hydrogen bonds with residues such as Cys51 and Val62 within the E6 binding groove. These specific contacts are depicted in the 2D ligand-interaction diagram of the HPV-E6-γ-sitosterol complex (Fig. 3A and 3C), which highlights the hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions that contribute to the high docking score. In addition to multiple hydrophobic contacts, which together are likely to enhance binding strength and interfere with the E6-p53 degradation interface. For p53, lupeol and stigmasterol engaged residues in the DNA‑binding core region, including Arg248 and Arg147, helping to stabilize conformations associated with transcriptional activation and apoptosis (Fig. 3D and F; Table 3). The presence of these specific hydrogen‑bond networks and non‑polar contacts supports the higher binding energies observed for γ‑sitosterol, stigmasterol, and lupeol relative to cisplatin, and suggests that these phytocompounds may exert stronger functional inhibition of HPV-E6 while favouring p53 stabilization.

### Pharmacokinetic behavior and ADMET predictions of bioactive compounds
  To assess the pharmacokinetic suitability and drug-likeness of the phytocompounds identified from B. tomentosa flower extract, ADMET predictions were carried out using SwissADME and pkCSM. The detailed in silico ADMET predictions for all 45 GC‑MS identified phytocompounds are summarized in Table 4. The lead compounds γ-sitosterol, stigmasterol, and lupeol complied with Lipinski’s Rule of Five, confirming favorable oral bioavailability. Their molecular weights (412.7–426.7 g/mol) were below the 500 Da threshold, and LogP values ranged from 5.8 to 7.2, consistent with high lipophilicity. This property contributed to moderate gastrointestinal absorption (GIA) for γ-sitosterol and stigmasterol, while lupeol demonstrated slightly higher absorption due to balanced polarity. Compounds such as 5-hydroxymethylfurfural and 4-H-pyran-4-one derivatives, with lower molecular weights (126–144 Da) and optimal TPSA values, showed high GIA and solubility, suggesting that smaller phenolics may act as supportive bioactives. In contrast, sterols displayed limited aqueous solubility, which represents a key barrier to efficient systemic exposure and oral bioavailability. This limitation underscores the need for formulation strategies such as nano‑emulsions, solid lipid or polymeric nanoparticles, and liposomal systems to enhance dissolution, gastrointestinal absorption, and tissue delivery of γ‑sitosterol and stigmasterol24,35.

  Blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration was predicted for γ-sitosterol, stigmasterol, and lupeol, while polar phenolics were non-permeant. P-glycoprotein (P-gp) substrate prediction was positive for γ-sitosterol and stigmasterol, suggesting possible efflux-mediated clearance, whereas lupeol and smaller metabolites were non-substrates, favoring systemic retention. No major cytochrome P450 inhibition was observed for most compounds, although weak inhibitory interactions with CYP3A4 were predicted for phytosterols, indicating a low but manageable risk of drug-drug interactions. Bioavailability scores were moderate for sterols (0.17-0.24) and higher for phenolic metabolites (0.55).

  Importantly, bis(2‑ethylhexyl) phthalate (DEHP), although detected in the GC-MS profile, is a ubiquitous plasticizer contaminant rather than a plant‑derived metabolite and has been widely reported to leach from laboratory plastics and herbal packaging materials32,33. Consistent with its documented hepatotoxic and endocrine‑disrupting properties31, DEHP was therefore excluded from docking‑based lead selection and drug‑likeness/ADMET prioritization in this study.

  Taken together, the ADMET predictions support γ-sitosterol, stigmasterol, and lupeol as promising anticancer candidates with acceptable oral absorption, non-mutagenic profiles, and minimal systemic toxicity. These results are consistent with earlier reports describing phytosterols and triterpenes as safe, drug-like scaffolds for cancer therapy development24,28,35. From a safety perspective, it is important to interpret the superior docking scores of γ‑sitosterol, stigmasterol, and lupeol relative to cisplatin in the context of their distinct pharmacology. Cisplatin and related platinum drugs act primarily through DNA cross‑linking and generation of reactive oxygen species, leading to non‑specific cytotoxicity in rapidly dividing normal tissues and clinically significant nephrotoxicity, neurotoxicity, and myelosuppression. By contrast, phytosterols and triterpenes are endogenous components of the human diet and have been reported to induce apoptosis, cell‑cycle arrest, and suppression of pro‑tumorigenic signaling pathways in cancer cells while exhibiting relatively low systemic toxicity in preclinical models34–36. The non‑mutagenic profiles and absence of severe organ toxicity predicted by the present ADMET analysis therefore suggest that Bauhinia tomentosa‑derived sterols might offer a more favorable therapeutic window than cisplatin, although this must be confirmed by dedicated in vitro and in vivo toxicity studies.

### Molecular dynamics simulation, principal component analysis (PCA), and free energy landscape (FEL)
  Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed to evaluate the structural stability and binding dynamics of the top-ranked phytocompounds from B. tomentosa flowers in complex with cervical cancer-associated proteins. The two protein-ligand systems subjected to 100 ns simulations were HPV-E6-γ-sitosterol and p53-lupeol. The root mean square deviation (RMSD) of the protein backbone indicated early equilibration with minimal fluctuations across the trajectory. The HPV-E6-γ-sitosterol complex reached equilibrium after ~ 10 ns, maintaining an average RMSD of 0.19–0.22 nm, while the p53-lupeol complex stabilized around ~ 15 ns with an average RMSD of 0.18–0.21 nm, suggesting robust ligand binding and negligible deviation from native conformations (Fig. 4A), consistent with other in silico docking-based approaches developed for cervical cancer40.

  Importantly, ligand RMSD values remained below 0.22 nm for both systems, further confirming sustained active-site engagement throughout the simulation. Root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) analysis demonstrated reduced mobility of residues within the binding pockets, with most RMSF values between 0.08 and 0.14 nm, indicating strong stabilization by the ligands (Fig. 4D). Elevated fluctuations were observed only in flexible loop regions and terminal residues, a typical feature of protein dynamics. The radius of gyration (Rg) values remained consistent, reflecting preserved structural compactness (Fig. 4B). The HPV-E6-γ-sitosterol complex maintained an Rg of 1.88–1.92 nm, while the p53-lupeol complex ranged from 2.01 to 2.07 nm, confirming that ligand binding did not induce unfolding or destabilization of the protein structures. Similarly, solvent accessible surface area (SASA) values were stable, averaging 12,800 − 13,200 Å² for HPV-E6 and 13,300 − 13,700 Å² for p53 (Fig. 4C), suggesting consistent solvation and structural integrity during the 100 ns simulation.

  Further insights into the global conformational landscape were obtained from Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Free Energy Landscape (FEL) mapping (Fig. 5). PCA revealed that the first two principal components accounted for the majority of structural variance, highlighting dominant and stable conformational states. FEL plots identified well-defined low-energy basins, confirming that both protein-ligand complexes occupied thermodynamically favorable conformations throughout the trajectory. Taken together, the MD simulation results validate that B. tomentosa phytocompounds, particularly γ-sitosterol and lupeol, form stable and energetically favorable complexes with HPV-E6 and p53, respectively. The retention of structural compactness, low flexibility in binding pocket residues, and persistent low-energy states confirm the potential of these natural metabolites as promising lead candidates for further in vitro and in vivo evaluation in cervical cancer therapeutics.

  Despite their favorable docking and toxicity profiles, the high lipophilicity and limited aqueous solubility of γ‑sitosterol and stigmasterol predicted in this study indicate that formulation optimization will be essential for successful translation. Nano‑delivery platforms, including phytosterol loaded solid lipid nano-particles, polymeric nano-particles, nano-emulsions, and liposomes, have been reported to improve the solubility, membrane permeability, and antitumor efficacy of structurally related sterols in preclinical models24,35. Integrating Bauhinia tomentosa derived sterols into such advanced delivery systems will therefore be a logical next step in future in vitro and in vivo investigations.


## Conclusion
  In this study, the methanolic flower extract of B. tomentosa was systematically investigated for its anticancer potential using GC-MS profiling, molecular docking, ADMET predictions, and molecular dynamics simulations. GC-MS analysis identified 45 phytochemicals, among which γ-sitosterol, stigmasterol, and lupeol demonstrated the strongest binding affinities toward the oncogenic HPV-E6 protein and the tumor suppressor p53. These interactions suggest their potential to inhibit HPV-mediated oncogenesis while stabilizing p53 functions. Pharmacokinetic and ADMET predictions confirmed that the lead compounds largely comply with Lipinski’s Rule of Five, exhibit moderate to high gastrointestinal absorption, and display minimal risks of CYP450-mediated metabolism or systemic toxicity. Molecular dynamics simulations further validated the structural stability and compactness of the HPV-E6-γ-sitosterol and p53-lupeol complexes, while PCA and FEL analyses revealed well-defined low-energy basins, confirming their thermodynamic stability. Taken together, the findings establish B. tomentosa flowers as a promising natural source of phytochemicals with dual-target activity against HPV-E6 and p53. Among these, γ-sitosterol and lupeol emerge as strong lead candidates with favorable drug-like properties and binding stability. Nonetheless, in vitro and in vivo validation will be crucial to translate these in silico insights into clinically relevant, plant-based interventions for cervical cancer therapy. The present study is limited to GC‑MS‑based phytochemical profiling and in silico evaluation, and does not include MTT or other cell‑based assays on cervical cancer cell lines due to funding and facility constraints. In subsequent work, the authors plan to test the methanolic crude extract and lead compounds such as γ‑sitosterol and lupeol in HPV‑positive cervical cancer cell lines using MTT and apoptosis assays to validate the computational predictions.
