# An integrated analysis of lactylation signature reveals PRDX1 as a therapeutic target of keloid pathogenesis


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45451-y.


## Introduction
  Keloids represent a pathological fibroproliferative disorder characterized by aggressive dermal overgrowth extending beyond original wound margins, exhibiting high population prevalence1–5. The clinical burden manifests in three primary dimensions: functional impairment manifesting as restricted mobility from joint-area scarring, psychosocial distress arising from disfigurement caused by facial or neck lesions, and recalcitrant recurrence6–10. Therapeutic approaches for keloids are primarily categorized into surgical and non-surgical methods. While surgical intervention delivers immediate effects, it carries a high recurrence rate exceeding 50% as documented in clinical studies11. Current mainstream non-surgical therapies such as intralesional corticosteroid injections, radiotherapy, and topical 5-fluorouracil application exhibit significant limitations including restricted eligibility criteria, limited therapeutic sustainability and stability, and risks of skin pigmentary alterations12–14. Advanced treatment techniques for keloids include several novel modalities such as laser therapy and botulinum toxin therapy; however, their efficacy and safety profiles require further validation through rigorous clinical observation15,16. There is an urgent clinical need for precision therapeutic strategies that directly target the underlying pathogenic mechanisms, making it imperative to further elucidate the molecular pathways driving keloid initiation and progression.

  The pathogenesis of keloids is complex, which is a confluence of multiple contributing factors. Keloid pathogenesis involves complex interactions among multiple pathological factors including persistent inflammatory stimulation, aberrant TGF-β signaling activation, continuous mechanical tension, and hypoxic microenvironments17,18. Chronic inflammation drives abnormal fibroblast proliferation and excessive extracellular matrix deposition through sustained release of profibrotic cytokines, while dysfunctional immune cells such as regulatory T cells further exacerbate inflammatory cascades19–21. TGF-β serves as a core regulatory factor whose pathway hyperactivation directly induces dysregulated collagen synthesis18,22; recent studies reveal that endoplasmic reticulum stress promotes TGF-β1 expression, establishing a vicious cycle23. Concurrently, mechanical tension contributes to pathogenesis in high-stress regions, such as cervical and anterior thoracic region, by altering skin biomechanical properties24,25. Notably, hypoxic microenvironments within keloid tissue induce metabolic reprogramming that enhances fibroblast glycolysis and lactate accumulation, with hypoxia-inducible factor HIF-1α further promoting angiogenesis and glycolytic gene expression26–28. Lactate accumulation derived from hypoxic environments may epigenetically regulate fibrotic progression through lactylation modification, a mechanism that remains systematically unexplored in keloids.

  The conventional biochemical paradigm classified lactic acid as a terminal waste metabolite in anaerobic glycolysis. However, a groundbreaking discovery of histone lysine lactylation modification revealed a new paradigm for lactate-mediated gene transcription regulation29. Subsequent studies confirmed widespread lactylation occurrence in non-histone proteins, modulating protein functions to participate in critical biological processes including immune responses exemplified by MCT1-mediated IL-17 secretion in T cells and DNA damage repair illustrated through MRE11 propionylation influencing chemotherapy resistance30,31. In fibrotic diseases, accumulating evidence has substantiated that lactylation actively promotes fibrotic progression across multiple disease contexts32–34. However, keloid research lacks a systematic framework for investigating lactylation modifications, with the regulatory mechanisms of lactylation-associated genes governing fibroblast phenotypic transformation and disordered collagen metabolism remaining imperative to elucidate.

  In this study, we applied an integrated analysis of multi-dataset transcriptomics and machine learning to identify lactylation hub genes in keloid pathogenesis. This work opens a new avenue for targeting lactatylation-related genes with identified compounds and provides foundational insights for future research on lactylation-regulated fibrotic mechanisms.


## Materials and methods

### Data sources
  Publicly available mRNA expression profiles were downloaded from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) using the GEOquery package. The included datasets comprise three RNA‑seq datasets (GSE158395, GSE188952, and GSE173900) and one microarray dataset (GSE92566). Detailed information on sample sizes and tissue types for each dataset is summarized in Supplementary Table 1.


### Data analysis
  To address platform-specific characteristics, RNA-seq and microarray datasets were preprocessed separately: RNA-seq data were log2-transformed (log2(x + 1)) to stabilize variance, while microarray data underwent background correction and normalization. All datasets were then converted to a common log2 scale and quantile-normalized with the preprocessCore package (https://bioconductor.org/packages/preprocessCore). For integrated multi-cohort analysis, inter-dataset batch effects arising from platform or study origin were adjusted using ComBat from the sva package35. Differential expression analysis was carried out using the limma package36, which fits a linear model and applies empirical Bayes moderation to estimate expression changes. Statistically significant genes were defined as those with an adjusted P‑value (Benjamini–Hochberg method) < 0.05 and an absolute log₂ fold change > 0.3. Heatmaps were generated with the pheatmap package (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=pheatmap) based on z‑score normalized expression values, and other graphical plots were produced using the ggplot2 R package (https://ggplot2.tidyverse.org).


### Screening of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)
  A linear model for microarray data (LIMMA) package from R language was used for DEG screening36. To improve the reliability of differentially expressed genes, probe sets for which the adjusted P was < 0.05, and |logFC| was > 0.25 between mild and advanced fibrosis were defined as significantly differentially expressing.


### Functional analyses of DEGs and gene set enrichment analysis
  To investigate the biological functions of the DEGs, functional analysis was performed using the ClusterProfiler package. These analyses combined Gene Ontology (GO) and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), with the GO categories categorizing functional pathways into three aspects including Biological Process (BP), Molecular Function (MF), and Cellular Component (CC). The P-values underwent multiple testing correction using the Benjamini-Hochberg method or FDR, with the threshold set at FDR less than 0.05. For the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) of the core DEGs, the ClusterProfiler package was utilized to calculate the enrichment scores for given gene pathways. For single-gene GSEA of the five hub genes, we performed a pre-ranked GSEA approach. Specifically, for each hub gene, Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated between its expression and that of all other genes in the dataset. All genes were then ranked in descending order based on their correlation coefficients to generate a pre-ranked gene list. These pre-ranked lists were used as input for GSEA against the Reactome pathway database using the clusterProfiler package in R. It should be noted that the GSEA was performed using the full pre-ranked list of all genes, not limited to the top correlated genes shown in the visualization.


### Identification of lactylation‑related hub genes
  A set of 336 lactylation-related genes was compiled from published literature37,38. After intersecting with this list, candidate lactylation-associated DEGs underwent feature selection using three complementary machine-learning algorithms to reduce method-specific bias: Random Forest (ranked by Gini-index-based importance), XGBoost (ranked by Gain-based importance), and LASSO regression (implemented via the glmnet package with the optimal penalty parameter λ determined through 10‑fold cross‑validation). To enhance robustness, genes consistently identified by at least two algorithms were considered key candidates, and the intersection across all three methods yielded five high-confidence hub genes. The discriminative ability of each hub gene was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis on the same integrated dataset that served as the training cohort. ROC curves and area under the curve (AUC) values were calculated using the pROC package in R (version 4.2.2), with clinical status (control = 0, keloid = 1) as the true state variable. The 95% confidence intervals for AUCs were estimated via non-parametric bootstrapping: 2,000 bootstrap resamples were generated from the original dataset while preserving group labels, the AUC was recalculated for each resample, and the distribution of these 2,000 AUC values was used to derive the 95% CI (lower and upper bounds). It should be noted that no cross-validation or independent external validation was employed in this exploratory analysis; therefore, the results reflect the performance of the genes within the training dataset and warrant validation in future studies. Correlations among the hub genes were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient, calculated with the cor.test function in R. Only statistically significant gene pairs (p < 0.05) were retained for visualization. The chord diagram was generated using the circlize package in R. In the diagram, each sector represents a gene, with arc length proportional to the sum of the absolute correlation coefficients between that gene and all others.


### Immune cell infiltration analysis
  This study comprehensively evaluated the immune cell infiltration levels in each sample of the research using the ssGSEA algorithm through the R package “GSVA”; subsequently, this study analyzed the correlation between hub genes and infiltrating immune cells using Spearman correlation analysis.


### Regulatory network construction
  Potential upstream miRNAs and transcription factors of the target genes were predicted using the RegNetwork database (https://regnetworkweb.org/)39, retaining only interactions that were experimentally validated in the database.


### Compound–Gene interaction profiling and therapeutic drug prediction
  Bioactive compounds associated with the core genes were identified via NetworkAnalyst40, based on its integrated chemical–gene interaction repositories. Drug–gene targeting relationships were further predicted using the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD, https://ctdbase.org/)41, with a focus on clinically actionable compounds that interact with key genes.


### Human samples
  Human scar tissues and normal skin tissues were voluntarily donated by patients undergoing surgical resection. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects and/or their legal guardian(s) prior to the collection of tissue samples. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Ethics Committee of Shanghai 9th Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (number: SH9H-2023-T200-1) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.


### Western blot
  rotein lysates were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against PRDX1 (1:1000, Abcam, ab15571) and β-actin (1:2000, Abcam, ab8227) as loading control. HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000, Abcam) were applied if necessary for 1 h at room temperature, and protein bands were visualized using ECL substrate.


### Quantitative real-time PCR
  Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol reagent, reverse-transcribed into cDNA using PrimeScript RT Master Mix. qPCR reactions were performed with SYBR Premix Ex Taq on a QuantStudio 6 system. Gene expression was normalized to GAPDH and calculated via the 2 − ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed as follow: GAPDH forward, 5′-CTGGGCTACACTGAGCACC-3′; reverse, 5′-AAGTGGTCGTTGAGGGCAATG-3′; PRDX1 forward, 5′-CATTCCTTTGGTATCAGACCCG-3′; reverse, 5′- CCCTGAACGAGATGCCTTCAT-3′;


### Small interfering RNA (siRNA)-mediated knock-down assay
  Keloid fibroblasts were transfected with 50nM PRDX1-targeting siRNA or scramble control using Lipofectamine 3000. After 36 h incubation, knockdown efficiency was validated by qPCR/Western blot. Functional assays including CCK-8, transwell, and ROS detection were subsequently performed. siRNA sequences: si-1, sense, 5′-CCAGUUCACUGACAAACAUTT-3′; anti-sense, 5′-AUGUUUGUCAGUGAACUGGTT-3′; si-2, sense, 5′-GAACAUUCCUUUGGUAUCATT-3′; anti-sense, 5′-UGAUACCAAAGGAAUGUUCTT-3′.


### Plasmid transient transfection
  Using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Thermo Fisher, L3000015), cells in good growth condition were transfected with the PRDX1 recombinant plasmid (WZ Biosciences, CH885311) and the corresponding empty vector control, respectively, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Transfection efficiency was assessed 36 h post-transfection, followed by functional analysis.


### ROS detection
  Intracellular ROS levels were measured using DCFH-DA probe (Beyotime, S0033S). Cells were incubated with 10µM DCFH-DA for 30 min at 37 °C, washed with PBS. Next, fluorescence intensity was quantitatively assessed under microscopic observation and statistically analyzed. For quantification, fluorescence images were captured from at least three random fields per sample using a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss LSM 900). Mean fluorescence intensity per field was measured using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). Relative fluorescence intensity was calculated by normalizing to the control group. Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using unpaired Student’s t-test.


### Wound healing and transwell assay
  For wound healing, confluent monolayers were scratched with pipette tips and migration distance was measured at 0/12 h. For transwell, 5 × 10⁴ cells in serum-free medium were seeded into upper chambers (Corning) with 8-µm pores; lower chambers contained 10% FBS medium. Migrated cells were fixed/stained after 24 h and counted.


### CCK-8 assay
  Cells (3 × 10³/well) were seeded in 96-well plates. At designated time points, 10% CCK-8 reagent (MCE, HY-K0301) was added and incubated for 2 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.


### Immunofluorescence staining
  Cells fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, blocked with 1% BSA, and incubated with primary antibody, including PRDX1 (1:500, Abcam, ab15571), α-SMA (1:1000, Abcam, ab242395), and anti-L-lactyl lysine (Pan-Kla) (1:100, PTM BIO, PTM-1401RM) overnight at 4 °C. The corresponding secondary antibody was applied for 1 h. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were captured by confocal microscopy (Zeiss LSM 900).


### Statistical analysis
  All data were analyzed by GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad Software, USA) and performed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All functional assays were independently repeated at least three times. Comparisons between the two groups were performed by unpaired t-test while comparisons between multiple groups were analyzed by one-way ANOVA. For all comparisons, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001).

### The overall expression profile of keloid patients

Subsequent normalization was performed, with principal component analysis (PCA) and boxplots demonstrating improved data uniformity post-normalization (Fig. 1A-D). Differential expression analysis via ANOVA identified 810 upregulated and 1,139 downregulated genes in keloid samples (Fig. 1E, F).

### The expression feature of lactylation-related genes in keloids
  By intersecting these DEGs with 336 lactylation-related genes (Supplementary Table 2)37,38, based on the screening of the literature and the extraction of protein lactylation targets identified by mass spectrometry in these studies, we identified 15 lactylation-related genes among the upregulated DEGs and 11 genes at the intersection of downregulated genes and lactylation-related genes (Fig. 3A). A volcano plot comparing the expression of these 26 lactylation-associated differential genes revealed substantial fold changes and statistically significant p-values (Figure S1A). Their expression patterns differed markedly between keloid and control tissues (Figure S1B,C), suggesting potential critical roles in keloid pathogenesis. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of these 26 genes revealed distinct patterns due to the complementary annotation frameworks of the two databases. The analysis identified significant GO terms associated with processes such as response to oxidative stress, activation of immune response, positive regulation of response to biotic stimulus, and structural constituent of skin epidermis (Fig. 3B–E), highlighting their potential roles in oxidative stress and immune processes relevant to keloid development. In contrast, KEGG pathway enrichment yielded a more limited set of significant results, consistent with its focus on larger, predefined signaling and metabolic pathways. Protein-protein interaction predictions further indicated robust and interconnected relationships among their encoded proteins (Figure S2A, B), supporting potential synergistic effects in modulating keloid progression.

### Identification of hub genes of lactylation
  To objectively and robustly identify hub genes among the lactylation-associated candidates, we employed a multi-algorithm feature selection strategy to mitigate bias from any single method. First, the eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) algorithm was applied to the 26 lactylation-related DEGs, identifying eight candidate genes based on feature importance (Fig. 4A), with PRDX1 and CSRP1 ranking highest. Second, Random Forest analysis independently ranked the genes by importance, again identifying PRDX1 and CSRP1 as the top contributors (Fig. 4B). Third, LASSO regression analysis with 10‑fold cross‑validation selected nine key genes (Fig. 4C). To integrate these results, we focused on genes reproducibly identified across methods. The intersection of genes from all three approaches yielded five high‑confidence lactylation hub genes: PRDX1, CSRP1, IFI16, CALD1, and VIM (Fig. 4D), which showed strong mutual correlations (Fig. 4E). And in the Fig. 4E, each sector represents a gene, and the arc length of the sector is proportional to the sum of the absolute correlation coefficients between that gene and all other genes. The chords represent correlations between genes, with colors gradient-mapped based on the correlation values (green for negative correlations, red for positive correlations), and the width of each chord is proportional to the absolute value of the correlation coefficient. Among these five genes, PRDX1 and CSRP1 showed lower expression in keloid tissues compared to normal tissues, while IFI16, CALD1, and VIM were relatively upregulated in keloid tissues. Finally, to evaluate the overall discriminative capacity of the identified hub gene set rather than to filter individual genes, we performed ROC analysis. Of note, this ROC analysis was conducted on the same dataset used for feature selection, and the results should therefore be interpreted as exploratory. The AUC values and their confidence intervals provide an initial estimate of discriminative potential, but independent validation is required to confirm the generalizability of these findings. The results demonstrated reasonable discriminatory power for PRDX1, CSRP1, IFI16, and CALD1 in distinguishing keloid from normal skin (Fig. 4F, G), with PRDX1 exhibiting the highest predictive efficacy. The error bars in Fig. 4G represent these confidence intervals and reflect the sampling variability of the AUC estimates.

### The expression of hub genes correlated with immunological characteristics in keloids
  To characterize the immune microenvironment in keloids, we first assessed the overall coordination of immune infiltration by analyzing pairwise correlations among the 28 immune cell types (Fig. 5A). This correlation matrix reveals clusters of immune cells that tend to co-infiltrate (positively correlated) or exhibit mutually exclusive infiltration patterns (negatively correlated), providing a systematic view of the immune contexture. Subsequently, by comparing infiltration scores between keloid and control tissues, we identified nine specific immune cell subsets that were significantly elevated in keloids (Fig. 5B). Given that the five lactylation hub genes (PRDX1, CSRP1, IFI16, CALD1, and VIM) were robustly identified as consensus drivers from our multi-algorithm screening (Fig. 4) and exhibit differential expression in keloids, we next sought to investigate their potential mechanistic role in shaping the observed immune landscape. We therefore analyzed the correlations between the expression levels of these five key genes and the infiltration abundance of the dysregulated immune cells (Supplementary Table 3). Further analysis of correlations between these immune cells and lactylation-modified gene expression demonstrated that CALD1 expression significantly positively correlated with infiltration levels of five immune cell types: memory B cell, plasmacytoid dendritic cell (pDC), effector memory CD4 T cell, natural killer T cell, and central memory CD4 T cell. Among these, four cell types (excluding Effector memory CD4 T cell) exhibited significantly increased infiltration in keloids (Fig. 5C). CSRP1 expression showed significant negative correlations with nine immune cell types, seven of which demonstrated substantial keloid infiltration, with the most pronounced negative correlation observed for plasmacytoid dendritic cell (Fig. 5D). Correspondingly, IFI16 expression positively correlated with five immune cell types, four of which were significantly infiltrating subtypes (Fig. 5E). Correlations of PRDX1 expression were observed with only two immune cell types: a positive correlation with γδ T cells and a negative correlation with pDCs (Fig. 5F). Finally, VIM expression exhibited significant positive correlations with nine immune cell types, most belonging to subtypes with prominent keloid infiltration (Fig. 5G). These findings demonstrate substantial immune cell infiltration in keloids and reveal significant correlations between the expression of these core genes and the abundance of specific immune subsets, suggesting potential associations between lactylation-related gene expression and immune landscape alterations in keloid pathogenesis.

### Prediction of regulatory networks and targeted drugs for key genes
  Utilizing the Regnetwork database to predict upstream miRNAs and transcription factors, we constructed a regulatory network for lactylation-modified core differentially expressed genes in keloid. The five hub genes were regulated by shared transcription factors, suggesting potential biological synergy: KLF12 targeted VIM and CALD1; NFKB1, MYB, and SP1 co-regulated VIM and CSRP1; MYC modulated CSRP1 and PRDX1; MAX jointly regulated PRDX1 and IFI16 (Fig. 7A). NetworkAnalyst database further identified 25 bioactive chemicals significantly associated with these hub genes, potentially modulating target-related pathways (Fig. 7B). Multiple substances including arsenic trioxide, phenylmercuric acetate, trichostatin A, and valproic acid influenced several hub genes concurrently. Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD) database analysis predicted drug-target interactions for key genes (Fig. 7C; complete data in Supplementary Table 4). As established previously, given PRDX1’s highest feature importance and AUC score, we prioritized screening compounds targeting this gene. Screening criteria (≥ 2 scientific reports documenting increased abundance/expression) yielded 11 chemicals (Supplementary Table 5), with exclusion of toxic agents narrowing to three candidates: acetaminophen (a common analgesic and antipyretic), valproic acid (an anticonvulsant and mood stabilizer), and vorinostat (a histone deacetylase inhibitor approved for cutaneous T-cell lymphoma)—potential therapeutics for inhibiting keloid progression via PRDX1. These results establish a foundation for elucidating regulatory mechanisms and developing targeted interventions.

### Experimental validation of PRDX1 knockdown and overexpression in regulating ROS and fibroblast function
  To investigate the biological functions of lactylation-related differentially expressed genes in keloid, we first analyzed PRDX1 in the integrated GEO database and found that its expression was lower in keloid tissues compared with matched normal skin controls (Figure S4A). We further examined PRDX1 mRNA levels in human keloid and normal skin tissues, which confirmed the downregulation of PRDX1 in keloid (Figure S4B). Next, immunofluorescence co-staining with PRDX1, pan-lactylation (pan-Kla), and alpha-SMA antibodies revealed elevated α-SMA expression in keloids, indicating increased activated fibroblasts (Fig. 8A, B). PRDX1 expression was significantly reduced in keloid tissues compared to normal controls and exhibited co-localization with lactylation modifications and α-SMA in both tissue types (Fig. 8A, B). Subsequent PRDX1 knockdown using siRNA in BJ fibroblast cells was validated by reduced mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 8C, D). Following PRDX1 knockdown, we observed enhanced proliferative capacity in fibroblasts (Fig. 8E). Furthermore, quantification of DCFH-DA fluorescence revealed a significant increase in intracellular ROS levels upon PRDX1 knockdown, as measured by mean fluorescence intensity per field (Fig. 8F, unpaired t-test, p < 0.01). Beyond ROS alterations, PRDX1 silencing markedly enhanced fibroblast migration capacity, demonstrated by an approximately 1.5- to 2-fold higher migrated cell numbers in transwell assays and an increased wound healing percentage (Fig. 8G, H). On the other hand, after transfection of BJ cell lines with PRDX1-overexpression plasmids (Figure S4C), CCK‑8 assays showed that their proliferative ability was inhibited (Figure S4D), and ROS levels decreased (Figure S4E). Transwell and wound healing assays further demonstrated that fibroblast migration was attenuated upon PRDX1 overexpression (Figure S4F, G). These cellular experiments substantiate that PRDX1 exerts functional inhibitory effects in fibroblasts. Reduced PRDX1 expression in keloids elevates ROS production and potentiates fibroblast activity, thereby promoting keloid pathogenesis.

## Discussion
  Keloids exhibit tumor-like properties and depend on anaerobic glycolysis, namely the Warburg effect42,43. Under hypoxic conditions, keloid cells increase glycolysis, leading to elevated lactate production, which is analogous to lactate metabolism in the tumor microenvironment43–45. In the tumor microenvironment, lactate can influence the function of immune cells, such as the activity of CD8 + T cells and dendritic cells46. While direct evidence is currently lacking regarding lactate’s effect on immune cells in keloids, it is speculated that it may influence the inflammatory response and immune regulation in keloids through similar mechanisms. In other fibrotic diseases, lactate has been found to affect disease onset and progression through lactylation modification via multiple mechanisms, including regulating cell metabolism, gene expression, and inflammatory responses47–49. Lactylation is a newly discovered post-translational protein modification involving the binding of lactate molecules to lysine residues on proteins, thereby altering the structure and function of proteins, affecting their interactions with other molecules, and consequently regulating intracellular signaling pathways and gene expression50–52. In fibrotic diseases such as pulmonary, hepatic, and renal fibrosis, lactylation, as an emerging post-translational protein modification, has been found to play a key role in driving the fibrotic process45,53–55. Its role is closely related to metabolic reprogramming, the regulation of pro-fibrotic gene expression, and the remodeling of the immune microenvironment. Lactylation forms a core positive feedback loop driving mechanism in fibrosis: metabolic reprogramming characterized by enhanced glycolysis in fibrotic tissues leads to massive lactate accumulation56; lactate serves as a precursor modifying histones and other proteins57; histone lactylation significantly activates pro-fibrotic gene expression programs including TGF-β27; the activated pro-fibrotic factors further induce metabolic reprogramming and fibroblast activation, producing more lactate. This vicious cycle of “lactate accumulation-lactylation modification-pro-fibrotic gene expression-more lactate production” continuously drives the initiation and progression of fibrosis. This study, for the first time, systematically reveals the core regulatory role of lactylation modification in the pathological progression of keloids by integrating multi-omics analysis and machine learning approaches. The study found that lactate-derived lactylation modification synergistically regulates fibroblast activation, collagen metabolic dysregulation, and immune microenvironment remodeling by targeting 26 differentially expressed genes including 15 upregulated and 11 downregulated genes (Fig. 3). These genes are significantly enriched in pathways related to oxidative stress, immune response, and extracellular matrix remodeling.

  Current explorations into the function of lactylation modification in keloid have already reported that lactate can stimulate TGF-β1 secretion and promote the activity of keloid fibroblasts, affecting their proliferation, collagen synthesis, and migratory capacity, through H3K18 lactylation-dependent transcriptional upregulation of LTBP327. However, from a global perspective, to achieve a more thorough and comprehensive understanding of lactate’s role in keloids, the specific signaling pathways and epigenetic mechanisms through which lactate regulates fibroblasts or other cells still require further in-depth research. Through the cross-application of XGBoost, random forest, and lasso regression, we successfully identified 5 lactylation hub genes (PRDX1, CSRP1, IFI16, CALD1, VIM) (Fig. 4). Among them, PRDX1, as a key antioxidant enzyme, is significantly downregulated in scar tissue; experimental validation showed that its knockout leads to elevated ROS levels and significantly enhances fibroblast proliferation and migration abilities, directly confirming that PRDX1 deficiency promotes fibrosis by exacerbating oxidative stress (Fig. 8). The expression of CALD1 and VIM positively correlates with pro-fibrotic pathways such as collagen synthesis and extracellular matrix organization, while the decreased expression of CSRP1, an ECM negative regulatory factor, may release the inhibition on collagen deposition. Notably, these hub genes show significant correlations with abnormally elevated infiltration of various immune cells in scars such as plasmacytoid dendritic cells and effector memory T cells: CALD1, IFI16, and VIM are positively correlated with pro-fibrotic immune cells, while CSRP1 is negatively correlated with immunosuppressive cells (Fig. 5), suggesting that lactylation modification in keloid tissue may potentially amplify an inflammation-fibrosis vicious cycle through regulating immune-stromal interactions.

  The relationship between PRDX1 and immune cell infiltration is complex and context-dependent. In colorectal cancer, PRDX1 overexpression is closely associated with poor prognosis and reduced T-cell infiltration. PRDX1 promotes an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment in colorectal cancer by driving M2-like tumor-associated macrophage polarization via metabolic reprogramming58. Moreover, high PRDX1 expression correlates with poor prognosis in multiple cancers. However, PRDX1 undergoes a function switch—from tumor suppressor in early malignancy to oncogenic driver in advanced cancers—dictated by its redox-dependent oligomerization59. In acute liver injury, elevated circulating PRDX1 derived from hepatocytes regulates macrophage phenotypes by activating TLR4, thereby promoting inflammatory responses and immune cell recruitment60. These studies collectively highlight the pivotal role of PRDX1 in inflammation and immune suppression. In the present study, PRDX1 expression was found to be positively correlated with γδ T cells. As a key antioxidant enzyme, PRDX1 may influence the survival, proliferation, or chemotactic ability of γδ T cells by modulating the local redox state, leading to their increased infiltration. On the other hand, the negative correlation between PRDX1 and pDCs is also noteworthy. pDCs are the primary producers of type I interferon (IFN-I), and IFN-I production is typically associated with the initiation of inflammatory responses. This negative correlation may reflect the contribution of PRDX1 to immune suppression or inflammation resolution in keloids.

  Our immune infiltration analysis revealed a distinct immune-profile in keloids, characterized by a significant increase in nine specific immune cell subsets, including pDCs, memory B cells, natural killer T cells, and central memory CD4⁺ T cells (Fig. 5B). The enrichment of pDCs is particularly notable, as they are major producers of type I interferons and potent initiators of inflammatory responses61. Their elevated abundance, coupled with strong positive correlations with the lactylation hub genes CALD1 and VIM (and a negative correlation with PRDX1), suggests a potential link between lactylation-driven gene expression and sustained innate immune activation in keloids. Furthermore, the significant infiltration of memory B cells and effector memory CD4⁺ T cells point to an adaptive immune component in keloid pathology. These findings collectively extend the current understanding of keloid immunopathology beyond chronic inflammation. They suggest that lactylation modification may orchestrate a specific immune microenvironment—featuring heightened innate sensing, adaptive memory cell involvement, and stromal-immune crosstalk—that collaboratively drives excessive fibroblast activity and ECM deposition. This integrated view connects the enriched molecular pathways, such as immune activation, with the observed cellular players, providing a more nuanced mechanism for how lactylation could fuel the immune-fibrotic axis in keloids.

  This study not only discovered and investigated five lactylation hub genes in keloid but also revealed through regulatory network analysis that these hub genes are co-regulated by common transcription factors including KLF12, NFKB1, and MYC, providing key nodes for targeted intervention (Fig. 7). Based on a drug prediction model, this study screened 25 potential active compounds; in-depth analysis targeting the core hub gene PRDX1 identified three drugs with clinical translation potential: acetaminophen, valproic acid, and vorinostat. These drugs are already clinically approved, with well-documented safety data in their original indications, which may facilitate their repurposing. According to the analysis, they can modulate PRDX1 expression, which we demonstrate regulates ROS accumulation and fibroblast activation, offering new strategies to overcome current treatment dilemmas for keloids (Supplementary Table 5). In summary, this study not only explored the role of lactylation-modified genes in keloids but also, through hub gene identification and targeted drug prediction, laid the theoretical and experimental groundwork for developing precise anti-fibrotic therapies for keloids.


## Conclusion
  This study establishes that lactylation modification-related genes are critical drivers of keloid progression and can serve as novel therapeutic targets. Through integrated analysis of multi-dataset transcriptomics and machine learning algorithms, we identified 26 lactylation-related differentially expressed genes and converged on five hub genes (PRDX1, CSRP1, IFI16, CALD1, VIM) central to keloid fibrosis. Among these, PRDX1 showed the highest diagnostic potential in our model and was experimentally validated as a functional modulator of fibroblast activity. Experimental validation confirmed significantly reduced PRDX1 expression in keloid tissues, and its knockdown in fibroblasts elevated ROS levels, enhanced proliferation, and increased migration – directly linking PRDX1 deficiency to fibroblast hyperactivation. Furthermore, hub genes exhibited significant correlations with dysregulated immune cell infiltration, suggesting lactylation modulates the immune-fibrotic crosstalk. Regulatory network analysis predicted shared transcription factors (KLF12, NFKB1, MYC) governing the hub genes, while drug screening prioritized three clinically actionable compounds (acetaminophen, valproic acid, vorinostat) targeting PRDX1. Collectively, these findings provide the first systematic evidence of lactylation’s role in keloids, identify PRDX1 as a promising therapeutic target, and offer novel, clinically translatable strategies to disrupt the fibrotic cascade.
