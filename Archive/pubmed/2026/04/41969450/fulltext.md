# Integrated bulk RNA-seq and scRNA-seq identification of a novel “PET-SPI1-MYL9” transcriptional axis in lung adenocarcinoma driven by polyethylene terephthalate exposure


## Background
  Airborne microplastic polyethylene terephthalate (PET) accumulates in human lungs and is linked to respiratory pathologies; however, its molecular role in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) remains unclear. This study aims to explore the potential carcinogenic mechanisms of PET exposure in LUAD.


## Methods
  We integrated single-cell RNA sequencing, machine learning algorithms [including Classification and Regression Trees (CART), Naïve Bayes (NB), random forest (RF), and support vector machine (SVM)], molecular docking, survival analysis, and multi-omics data. Through differential expression screening across datasets combined with Venn analysis, we identified seven PET-associated oncogenic targets. Seven PET-associated oncogenic targets were identified via differential gene screening and Venn analysis.


## Results
  MYL9 was validated as a downregulated, LUAD-protective biomarker associated with significant survival benefit [hazard ratio (HR) =0.59, 0.16, 0.23; all P<0.05]. These findings were consistent across the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database, co-expression networks, and three independent LUAD datasets. SPI1 was identified as a key transcriptional regulator, showing strong co-expression with MYL9 (R=0.556, P<0.05) and concurrent downregulation in LUAD. Molecular docking revealed that PET bound to the DNA-binding pocket of SPI1 (ΔG =−5.30 kcal·mol−1), suggesting its transcriptional inhibition of MYL9.


## Conclusions
  Our integrated bioinformatics approach supports a novel “PET-SPI1-MYL9” transcriptional axis, revealing a potential non-genotoxic carcinogenic pathway for PET. While the computational evidence is robust, further wet-lab experiments are needed to validate the binding and transcriptional inhibition mechanism. This model provides a framework for understanding airborne microplastic toxicity in LUAD. We propose that PET promotes LUAD by disrupting the SPI1-MYL9 transcriptional axis, highlighting a potential environmental trigger and candidate targets for diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.


## Introduction
  Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is a common malignancy worldwide in terms of both incidence and mortality (1). Its development is influenced by a combination of genetic, lifestyle, and environmental factors (2). In addition to established risk factors such as smoking, environmental pollutants, including airborne particulate matter 2.5 (PM2.5) and chemical carcinogens, have been implicated in LUAD (3,4). In recent years, microplastics have emerged as novel environmental contaminants of global concern (5). Their accumulation in the human body and potential health risks have attracted increasing attention from the scientific community (6,7). Among various microplastics, polyethylene terephthalate (PET) is of particular concern because of its pervasive use and environmental persistence.

  PET is a plastic polymer widely used in packaging, textiles, and consumer products (8). During use and disposal, PET undergoes physical abrasion, weathering, and ultraviolet degradation, generating micro- and nanosized particles that enter the atmosphere and can be inhaled (9). Studies have detected PET particles in human lung tissues, with a higher abundance observed in pathological regions, such as pulmonary ground-glass nodules, suggesting a possible link with pulmonary pathogenesis (10). Owing to its high chemical stability and resistance to biodegradation, PET can persist in tissues for prolonged periods and may enhance biological toxicity through mechanisms commonly associated with microplastics such as cellular internalization, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses, potentially promoting lung tissue damage and even malignant transformation (11).

  Although environmental pollutants such as PET have been increasingly implicated in LUAD, the mechanistic pathways linking PET exposure to carcinogenesis remain poorly understood. This knowledge gap stems from three specific challenges. First, the core signaling pathways and effector molecules during PET-driven phenotypic acquisition are unknown. Second, current methodologies cannot comprehensively track the dynamic relationships among PET exposure dosage, transcriptomic heterogeneity, and phenotypic alterations. Third, experimental validation of direct physicochemical interactions between PET and critical cellular regulators is lacking, creating a discontinuity in the “exposure-to-effect” causal chain (12). To address these gaps, we employed an integrative multi-omics approach. We combined bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data from clinical LUAD specimens, transcriptomes from in vitro PET-exposed cellular models, and lung cancer single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets, and analyzed them using machine learning algorithms. This strategy aimed to enable the systematic identification of shared molecular targets and upstream regulators that are critical in PET-associated LUAD. Furthermore, to bridge the third knowledge gap, we performed molecular docking simulations to investigate potential direct interactions between PET and key cellular regulators identified in our analyses, providing mechanistic insights into the initial molecular events of the exposure-to-effect cascade. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-1-2630/rc).


## Methods

### Data acquisition and bulk RNA-seq differential gene expression analysis
  This study was an integrated bioinformatic analysis based on publicly available datasets. As the study utilized exclusively pre-existing, de-identified public data, no additional ethical approval or informed consent was required for this specific analysis, in accordance with the data use policies of the respective databases. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

  Gene expression data used in this study were sourced from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) of the National Center for Biotechnology Information (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Specifically, this included: bulk RNA-seq data from LUAD tissues [GSE116959 (13,14)], GSE75037 (15,16), GSE31908 (17), GSE102287 (18,19) and GSE68465 (19,20); bulk RNA-seq data from PET-treated epithelial cells [GSE269348 (21,22)]; and a lung cancer scRNA-seq dataset [GSE131907 (23,24)]. All data analyses were performed using R programming language (v.4.3.0, RRID:SCR_001905). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the limma package (v.3.56.2, RRID:SCR_010943) (25), with filtering criteria set as |fold change| >1.5 and P value <0.05. The visualization results of volcano and heatmap were plotted by ggplot2 package (v.3.4.2, RRID:SCR_014601).


### scRNA-seq processing and malignant-cell DEG analysis
  The scRNA-seq dataset GSE131907 was processed using the Seurat software (v.4.3.0, RRID:SCR_007322). Quality control was performed to exclude low-quality cells based on the following thresholds: nFeature_RNA between 200 and 9,000, nCount_RNA ≥3,500, mitochondrial gene percentage ≤10%. Normalization was performed using NormalizeData, and the top 2,000 highly variable genes were selected using FindVariableFeatures. Scaled expression values were obtained using ScaleData. Dimensionality reduction was performed using principal component analysis (PCA), and significant principal components were identified using ElbowPlot and JackStrawPlot. Cell clusters were visualized using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). Cell types were annotated using established marker genes and literature references. Malignant cells were identified by copy number variation (CNV) analysis using inferCNV (v.1.14.0, RRID:SCR_021140), with normal epithelial cells serving as controls. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using FindMarkers, applying thresholds of |fold change| >1.5 and P value <0.05.


### Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis
  GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed on the identified DEGs using the clusterProfiler R package (RRID:SCR_016884). Homo sapiens was selected as the target species with a significance threshold of P<0.05. Ultimately, 10 significantly enriched KEGG pathways were identified. Additionally, the top 10 significantly enriched GO terms within each category, biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF), were selected. All enrichment results are visualized using bars and circular chord diagrams generated with R visualization packages.


### Venn analysis of overlapping targets
  DEGs from the GSE269348 dataset were subjected to human-mouse homology conversion using the biomaRt R package (v.2.56.1, RRID:SCR_019214). Subsequently, identify the intersection genes GSE269348, GSE75037, GSE116959, and GSE131907 on four datasets, and visually represent the results using VennDiagram package (RRID:SCR_002414) to draw Venn diagrams.


### Core target identification based on machine learning
  Using the seven candidate genes obtained from the intersection, machine-learning models were constructed for the GSE116959 and GSE75037 datasets. After Z-score standardization of the expression matrix, the training and test sets were partitioned via stratified sampling in a 7:3 ratio. Four types of machine learning models were built: the Classification and Regression Trees (CART) (rpart package v.4.1.19, RRID:SCR_021200), Naïve Bayes (NB) (e1071 package v.1.7.13, RRID:SCR_001199), random forest (RF) (randomForest package v.4.7.1.1, RRID:SCR_015718), and support vector machine (SVM) (kernlab package v.0.9.32, RRID:SCR_010505). Hyperparameters were optimized through 10-fold cross-validation and key genes were screened based on feature importance (CART: variable splitting gain; RF: decrease in Gini index; SVM: weight vector; NB: absolute log-transformed conditional probabilities). Screening results from the models of the two datasets were intersected using Venn diagrams. The pROC package (v.1.18.0, RRID:SCR_000412) was used to generate receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and calculate the area under the curve (AUC) values to evaluate model performance. Combined with the differential expression trends of genes across datasets, core targets were identified.


### Evaluation of localized expression patterns for core targets
  The HPA database (http://www.proteinatlas.org/, RRID:SCR_006710) was used to assess localized expression patterns of core targets in both normal lung and LUAD tissue samples.


### Construction of gene co-expression correlation networks
  A gene co-expression network was constructed using data from the cBioPortal database (http://www.cbioportal.org/, RRID:SCR_014555), and the top-20 positively and negatively correlated genes were identified using Cytoscape software (v.3.8.2, RRID:SCR_003032).


### Survival analysis of core targets
  Survival analysis was performed using the LUAD datasets GSE68465, GSE31908, and GSE102287 from GEO. The survival of patients with LUAD against MYL9 expression was estimated using Kaplan-Meier plotter (RRID:SCR_018753) (26).


### Identification of upstream transcription factors (TFs)
  Based on annotations of gene TF regulatory relationships from the Human Transcription Factor Target Database (hTFtarget; https://guolab.wchscu.cn/hTFtarget/#!/, RRID:SCR_017402) and the Cistrome database (Cistrome DB; https://cistrome.org/db/, RRID:SCR_015965), upstream regulatory TFs for MYL9 were retrieved, yielding 74 candidate TFs. These 74 candidate TFs were then intersected with the DEGs identified in the GSE116959 and GSE75037 datasets. Venn diagrams were generated using the VennDiagram R package (RRID:SCR_022329) to visualize overlaps and core TFs were identified based on these intersections.


### TF correlation analysis
  For the GSE75037 and GSE116959 datasets, the gene expression matrices of the core TFs and core targets were extracted. Pearson’s correlation analysis was performed separately for each dataset using the R psych package (RRID:SCR_021744). This analysis calculated the correlation coefficient and significance P value for the expression levels of each TF and core target. The P values were subsequently transformed using −log10. Scatter plots with fitted lines were generated to visualize the relationship between representative TFs and core targets. In addition, dot plots displaying correlation coefficients were generated. These visualizations were used to illustrate correlation patterns across the datasets.


### Molecular docking
  Molecular docking analysis was conducted to assess the potential binding affinity between the core chemical structure of PET and key TFs. For this computational assessment, the PET monomer (ethylene terephthalate) was employed as a theoretical proxy to probe baseline molecular interactions. Crystal structure files were sourced from the Research Collaboratory for Structural Bioinformatics Protein Data Bank (RCSB PDB; https://www.rcsb.org/, RRID:SCR_012820), and the monomer structure was obtained from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/, RRID:SCR_004287). Protein preparations were done using PyMOL software (RRID:SCR_000305).

  Molecular docking was conducted using AutoDock software (RRID:SCR_012746), with binding energy (kcal·mol−1) serving as the core evaluation metric. The conformation with the lowest binding energy was selected as the optimal binding position. This monomer-based docking serves as a first-step screening to formulate testable hypotheses regarding PET-TF binding, prior to investigations with more complex polymeric or particle models.


### Statistical analysis
  All analyses were performed using R (v.4.3.0, RRID:SCR_001905). Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless stated otherwise. Survival curves were compared using log-rank tests, hazard ratios (HRs), and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were derived using Cox proportional-hazards models. Machine learning hyperparameters were tuned via 10-fold stratified cross-validation and model performance was assessed using AUC. Two-tailed tests were used, and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.


## Results

### Transcriptomic profiling of PET-exposed lung cells and LUAD cells
  To systematically investigate the impact of PET exposure on pathogenesis, we first analyzed LUAD tissues (GSE116959 and GSE68465), PET-exposed epithelial cells (GSE269348), and lung cancer scRNA-seq data (GSE131907). By comparing DEGs across these datasets, we applied four machine learning algorithms to further screen hub genes. Based on gene expression and prognostic analyses, we selected MYL9 for subsequent analyses. Potential TFs of MYL9 were predicted and applied to co-expression analysis. Therefore, SPI1 was chosen for molecular docking using PET. The workflow of the analysis is shown in Figure 1.

  RNA-seq analysis of the PET exposure dataset GSE269348 identified 494 DEGs (337 upregulated and 157 downregulated; Figure 2A). A heatmap of the top-20 DEGs clearly distinguished the PET-treated samples from normal samples (Figure 2B). KEGG analysis revealed 15 significantly enriched pathways (Figure 2C,2D) with the Epstein-Barr virus infection pathway exhibiting the greatest enrichment. Immune-related (e.g., antigen processing and presentation) and virus-related (e.g., herpes simplex virus 1 infection and viral carcinogenesis) pathways were also enriched (P<0.05), suggesting that DEGs may regulate immune processes and are linked to viral infection and tumorigenesis. GO analysis of PET-exposed cells revealed significant enrichment in immune-related processes, including the immune response and pathogen defense (BP, Figure 2E), as well as MFs such as peptide antigen binding (MF, Figure 2F).

  In LUAD tissue GSE116959, we identified 4,419 DEGs (1,924 upregulated and 2,495 downregulated; Figure 3A). A heatmap of the top-20 DEGs clearly distinguished tumor material from normal samples (Figure 3B). GO terms were enriched in BPs such as development and morphogenesis (Figure 3C) and MFs including protein binding (Figure 3D). Similarly, the GSE75037 dataset exhibited 5,897 DEGs (3,048 upregulated and 2,849 downregulated; Figure 3E), and a heatmap of the top-20 DEGs confirmed normal tumor segregation (Figure 3F). GO enrichment was observed in stress responses and development (BP, Figure 3G) and enzyme-binding activities (MF, Figure 3H). Collectively, these transcriptomic profiles highlight a stark contrast: PET-exposed cells primarily modulate immune and viral pathways, whereas LUAD tissues are predominantly enriched in developmental and stress-related pathways.


### ScRNA-seq analysis identified DEGs in malignant LUAD cells
  A preliminary analysis of the single-cell dataset GSE131907 was performed using the Seurat package. Following quality control, UMAP clustering was used to identify 19 major cell clusters (Figure 4A). The cell clusters were annotated as T cells, natural killer (NK) cells, myeloid cells, epithelial cells, B cells, fibroblasts, mast cells, endothelial cells, plasma cells, and lymphatic endothelial cells (Figure 4B). This annotation integrated information from the CellMarker database (http://117.50.127.228/CellMarker/) and marker gene expression profiles, including epithelial cell markers (e.g., EPCAM and KRT19) (22), T/NK cell markers (e.g., CD3D and NKG7), and myeloid cell markers (e.g., LYZ and CD68) (Figure 4C) (23,24).

  To distinguish malignant from non-malignant epithelial cell populations in tumor samples, CNV features were analyzed (25). Using epithelial cells from normal lung tissue (nLung) as a reference, CNV inference was performed on epithelial cells from tumor lung tissue (tLung), utilizing raw gene expression counts combined with chromosomal location data. The inferCNV signaling heat maps revealed distinct patterns; the reference group (normal epithelial cells) exhibited uniform gene expression, predominantly blue (low expression). In contrast, tumor-derived epithelial cells displayed marked expression heterogeneity, with alternating red and blue blocks reflecting CNV fluctuations across chromosomal regions. A non-malignant epithelial cell population within the tumor (marked by black boxes) showed an expression pattern similar to that of the normal reference, indicating no significant CNV alterations (Figure 4D).

  Based on the malignant and non-malignant cell populations defined by their CNV profiles, we performed differential expression analysis to explore their molecular differences. By applying the same threshold, 348 DEGs were identified (197 upregulated and 151 downregulated; Figure 4E). A heatmap of the top-20 DEGs clearly distinguished the expression profiles of malignant and non-malignant cells (Figure 4F).

  KEGG analysis (Figure 5A) showed significant enrichment in multiple pathways. The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) and ribosome pathways had high enrichment scores and low P values, indicating their pivotal roles. Other enriched pathways included those involved in infection responses, immune regulation, cell metabolism, and tumor-related processes, reflecting the diverse biological roles of DEGs.

  GO enrichment analysis further clarified functions of the DEGs. In GO-BP (Figure 5B), enrichment was observed for translation, tissue development, and stress responses. At the CC level (GO-CC; Figure 5C), the DEGs were associated with vesicles, extracellular exosomes, and secretory granules. The enriched MFs (GO-MF; Figure 5D) included protein binding, oxidoreductase activity, and identical protein binding. This functional analysis provided insights into disease mechanisms. Together, these data uncovered 348 malignant epithelium-specific DEGs that were enriched in viral infections, immune regulation, and metabolic pathways, highlighting their roles in tumorigenesis.


### Key candidate genes identified through machine learning-based screening
  To identify genes differentially expressed in LUAD cells, we compared DEGs in the above three GEO datasets and DEGs in LUAD cells from the single-cell dataset. As illustrated in the Venn diagram, seven common genes were identified: MDK, ISG15, TNFSF10, C3, MYL9, ID2 and NUPR1 (Figure 6A). Genes preliminarily associated with the studied phenotype were identified using differential expression analysis. Subsequently, for the GSE116959 and GSE75037 datasets, these genes were used as features and independently analyzed using four machine learning algorithms: CART, NB, RF, and SVM (26). The intersections of key genes identified by the different algorithms were visualized using Venn diagrams. The core genes determined through this approach included MYL9 and MDK (from analysis of GSE116959) (Figure 6B) and ID2, MYL9, MDK, B2M, and NUPR1 (from the analysis of GSE75037) (Figure 6C). Notably, MYL9 was consistently downregulated in both GSE116959 and GSE75037 datasets, whereas MDK was consistently upregulated. The consistent identification of these core genes across different datasets and algorithms suggests their potentially significant biological relevance and provides a foundation for subsequent research.

  For each dataset, the core genes were selected based on each algorithm and an optimal model was determined. The performance of the models on the training sets is summarized as follows: in the GSE116959 dataset, NB, RF, and SVM all demonstrated exceptional performance (AUC =1.000), while the CART model exhibited relatively good performance (AUC =0.948) (Figure 6D). In the GSE75037 dataset, NB (AUC =0.988) and RF (AUC =1.000) achieved outstanding results; both CART and SVM exhibited comparable performance (AUC =0.976 for both), indicating good proficiency (Figure 6E). A comprehensive analysis revealed significant performance variations among the models during the training phase, and the NB algorithm consistently maintained stable and superior predictive efficacy across both datasets, demonstrating robust cross-dataset adaptability. In contrast, the CART exhibited a relatively weaker performance for certain evaluation metrics. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) analysis (corresponding to the ROC curves in the figures) further revealed that models, including NB and RF, also performed robustly on the test sets of both the GSE116959 and GSE75037 datasets. Specifically, the test set AUCs for NB were 0.682 for GSE116959 and 0.927 for GSE75037. The machine learning screening of four key datasets pinpointed MYL9 and MDK as core, consistently dysregulated genes across algorithms, with MYL9 downregulated and MDK upregulated.


### Machine learning and multi-dimensional validation reveal MYL9 as a protective core gene in LUAD
  Cross-screening across the GSE116959 and GSE75037 datasets using multiple machine learning models identified MYL9 as a core DEG. To confirm the reduced expression of MYL9 at the protein level in LUAD compared to normal lung tissue, we used immunohistochemistry (IHC) results from the HPA database (Figure 7A). The results demonstrated a distinct brown positive staining for MYL9 in normal lung tissues, indicating baseline protein expression. In contrast, LUAD tissues exhibited significantly attenuated or absent MYL9-positive staining, reflecting markedly reduced protein expression. MYL9 protein expression was substantially lower in LUAD tissues than in normal lung tissues. This histological evidence further supports the hypothesis that downregulation of MYL9 may contribute to LUAD pathogenesis, providing direct validation for mechanistic investigations. Gene co-expression network analysis (Figure 7B) revealed a significant correlation between MYL9 expression and multiple functional genes. HSPD1, MARS2, and EFEMP2 were positively co-expressed with MYL9, whereas RBM47, L2HGDH, and GSE1 were negatively co-expressed. This suggests that MYL9 participates in the regulation of LUAD pathogenesis through complex molecular interaction networks.

  For the independent validation of MYL9 as a core gene identified in this study, we used three additional LUAD cohorts from the GEO database (GSE68465, GSE31908, and GSE102287). Survival analysis across these cohorts confirmed the clinical prognostic value of MYL9, with Kaplan-Meier curves consistently demonstrating significantly higher survival probabilities in patients with high MYL9 expression compared to low-expression groups (GSE68465: HR =0.59, 95% CI: 0.45–0.78, P<0.05; GSE31908: HR =0.16, 95% CI: 0.03–0.75, P<0.05; GSE102287: HR =0.23, 95% CI: 0.05–1.02, P<0.05) (Figure 7C-7E). These consistent findings across independent cohorts indicated that MYL9 exhibited protective effects in LUAD. Survival analysis further confirmed its significant association with patient prognosis, demonstrating a protective effect across the independent cohorts (GSE68465, GSE31908, and GSE102287). Concordantly, protein expression levels were consistently reduced in the tumor tissues. Collectively, these multidimensional findings robustly support MYL9 as a core candidate gene for LUAD research and establish a critical target foundation for subsequent functional mechanism validation and clinical translation studies. Together, the integrative machine learning, IHC results, and multi-cohort survival analyses converged on MYL9 as a consistently downregulated, clinically protective core gene in LUAD.


### Differential TF screening and cross-dataset correlation validation reveal SPI1 as a core upstream regulator of MYL9
  To elucidate the upstream regulatory mechanisms of MYL9 in PET-driven LUAD, this study systematically predicted and validated upstream MYL9 TFs to identify and verify its core transcriptional regulators. First, we leveraged the hTFtarget and Cistrome databases. Based on potential interactions mediated by TF binding elements within gene regulatory regions, 74 candidate upstream regulatory TFs for the MYL9 gene were identified. To precisely identify the core TFs closely associated with LUAD development, candidate TFs were further intersected with DEGs from the GSE116959 and GSE75037 LUAD datasets. Venn diagrams visually depicted the intersections among the candidate TF set, GSE116959 DEGs, and GSE75037 DEGs (Figure 8A). This analysis yielded 12 TFs present in both candidate TF and DEG datasets. Notably, SPI1 was differentially downregulated in both datasets, highlighting its significance for subsequent investigations.

  To validate regulatory relationships between the differentially expressed TFs and MYL9, correlation analyses were performed on the GSE75037 dataset. Correlation heat maps and scatter plots revealed significant associations between multiple TFs and MYL9 expression (Figure 8B). Notably, the TF SPI1 exhibited a R value of 0.556 with MYL9 (P<0.05) (Figure 8C), strongly suggesting SPI1’s potential key regulatory role in MYL9 expression. Simultaneously, analysis of SPI1 distribution based on the hTF target database (Figure 8D) provided further support for the potential involvement of SPI1 in LUAD pathogenesis and its potential regulatory role involving MYL9. Collectively, the evidence obtained through this integrated workflow established SPI1 as a core TF that regulates MYL9. Collectively, cross-dataset TF screening and correlation analysis identified SPI1 as a key upstream regulator of MYL9 in PET-driven LUAD.


### Molecular docking reveals PET binding to SPI1 DNA-binding domain and impairs its transcriptional regulation of MYL9 in LUAD
  Based on a multidimensional analysis, SPI1 was identified as a core upstream TF regulating MYL9, suggesting its potential involvement in PET-driven molecular pathways during LUAD development. To computationally explore the possibility of a direct interaction, SPI1-PET molecular docking simulations were performed. The docking results predicted that the PET monomer could stably bind within the DNA-binding domain of the SPI1 protein, with a calculated binding free energy (ΔG) of −5.30 kcal/mol, supporting the possibility of a direct molecular interaction (Figure 8E). Analysis of the predicted binding mode revealed that PET could primarily form hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonds with amino acid residues in SPI1, including Tyr-235, Lys-217, Asn-219, and Leu-172. We hypothesize that interactions involving these key residues might impair SPI1 transcriptional regulatory activity.

  The DNA-binding domain of SPI1 is essential for transcriptional regulation. If confirmed experimentally, the predicted interactions at these critical residues could alter the spatial conformation of SPI1, thereby potentially diminishing its capacity to bind to the promoter region of the MYL9 gene. This would, in turn, impair SPI1’s ability to transcriptionally regulate MYL9. Collectively, these computational findings propose a novel molecular model for how PET may modulate the SPI1-MYL9 pathway and potentially drive LUAD (Figure 8F).


## Discussion
  The widespread presence of PET microplastics in the environment has raised increasing concerns regarding their potential health impacts, particularly through inhalation and accumulation in the respiratory system. In this study, we integrated multi-omic analyses and machine learning approaches to investigate the role of PET exposure in LUAD. We found that PET may promote LUAD progression by directly binding to the TF SPI1, thereby inhibiting the expression of its downstream target MYL9, a gene whose elevated expression correlates significantly with improved patient survival. Therefore, we explicitly frame our study within the “hazard identification” paradigm. Within this framework, the use of higher, non-environmental doses is designed to elucidate potential mechanistic pathways of toxicity, serving as a necessary first step prior to risk assessment. We acknowledge the clear differences between our experimental conditions (specifically in dose, particle size, and duration) and realistic human exposure. Below, we contextualize these findings within the broader landscape of environmental carcinogenesis and LUAD pathogenesis.

  PET, a major constituent of plastic products and textiles, enters the atmosphere as micro- and nano-particles via wear and degradation, thereby posing an inhalation risk. Numerous studies have documented the presence of PET particles in human lung tissues (27). Our results align with the growing body of evidence that microplastics are not merely inert particles but can also interact with biological systems and potentially influence tumorigenesis. Unlike genotoxic carcinogens, PET may promote cancer through non-genetic mechanisms, such as chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, or transcriptional interference, as proposed here. However, the environmental relevance of our computational exposure model warrants critical consideration. A seminal review of microplastic research underscores a common methodological gap: environmental microplastics are heterogeneous and weathered, whereas most laboratory studies—for practical reasons—rely on simplified models like pristine spheres (Rozman and Kalčíková, 2022) (28). Our study aligns with this reductionist approach by employing a defined PET monomer. The explicit objective was not to mimic complex airborne particles, but to isolate and test whether the fundamental chemical motif of PET could structurally engage with a target like SPI1—thus generating a testable, mechanistic hypothesis. Therefore, our docking predictions represent an initial molecular-level insight, not a direct risk assessment. Extrapolation to real-world health implications requires caution. Bridging this gap necessitates future work with environmentally representative particles (e.g., weathered fibers), relevant exposure concentrations, and experimental validation to confirm the biological significance of the predicted PET-SPI1 interaction.

  A key finding of our study is the identification of MYL9 as a protective factor against LUAD. Notably, MYL9, as a member of the myosin light chain family (29), is known to participate in processes such as cell contraction and migration (30), and has been reported to be associated with invasion and metastasis in various tumors including glioblastoma, colorectal cancer (CRC), and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (31-33). For example, Wen et al. found that MYL9 is upregulated in squamous cervical cancer and that knockdown of MYL9 can inhibit the migration and invasion of cancer cells. This mechanism is related to enhanced aerobic glycolysis and activation of the JAK2/STAT3 pathway (34). Deng et al. analyzed CRC tissues and cells using various experimental techniques [such as scRNA-Seq, reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR), western blotting, and animal models] and found that MYL9 was predominantly expressed in cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) in CRC. It regulates the secretion of CCL2 and TGF-β1 from CAFs by binding to IQGAP1 through the ERK1/2 pathway, thereby affecting the immune microenvironment in CRC and promoting tumor progression as well as epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) (35). However, we are the first to reveal in LUAD that its high expression is significantly associated with favorable patient prognosis (HR =0.59, P<0.05). To reconcile these seemingly disparate findings, we propose a context-dependent and stage-specific model for MYL9 function. In this model, within the epithelial structure of primary LUAD, MYL9 likely contributes to maintaining cellular adhesion and tissue homeostasis, which correlates with the observed better prognosis. Conversely, in advanced or metastatic settings, aggressive subclones may co-opt MYL9’s cytoskeletal regulatory function to drive invasion. This dual role highlights that gene function is not binary but is dynamically shaped by disease stage and microenvironment. This paradoxical tissue-specific function may stem from the uniqueness of the pulmonary epithelial microenvironment: the integrity of the epithelial barrier is highly dependent on myosin-mediated cellular junctions (36,37). Downregulation of MYL9 is associated with reduced integrity of the lung epithelial barrier, suggesting it may promote microenvironmental disruption and malignant transformation by compromising this barrier. This finding indicates a significant context-dependency of the myosin pathway in tumorigenesis, necessitating further investigation to elucidate the regulatory mechanisms that control MYL9. The identification of the TF SPI1 is crucial for this regulatory chain.

  Furthermore, we identified SPI1 as an upstream regulator of MYL9. SPI1 (PU.1), a classical immunomodulatory TF (38,39). Notably, similar to many TFs, SPI1 functions are context-dependent, with potential roles beyond its canonical immune-related activities (40,41). It regulates the differentiation and maturation of immune cells such as macrophages and B cells in the hematopoietic system and maintains immune homeostasis by modulating pro- and anti-inflammatory factors. Additionally, it functions in the early stages of T cell development, influencing the rhythm of cell proliferation and differentiation, as well as lineage fate determination (42). Beyond its well-established roles in hematopoiesis and immunity, emerging evidence suggests that SPI1 exerts context-specific functions in non-hematopoietic tissues. In this study, multi-omics cross-validation revealed that SPI1 was significantly co-expressed with MYL9 (R=0.556, P<0.05) and concurrently downregulated in LUAD tissues. Molecular docking simulations further predicted that PET can stably bind to SPI1’s DNA-binding domain (ΔG =−5.30 kcal/mol), with the binding site encompassing core amino acids within this domain (e.g., Tyr-235 and Lys-217). Furthermore, it is important to emphasize that the proposed direct interaction between PET and the TF SPI1 is based solely on computational docking. This model provides a detailed and mechanistically plausible hypothesis by identifying a specific binding interface and key interacting residues, which include Tyr-235, Lys-217, Asn-219, and Leu-172. However, it remains a prediction that requires experimental confirmation. We therefore explicitly present this finding as a working hypothesis, which establishes SPI1 as a high-priority target for further experimental scrutiny. The precise residues and binding mode identified here offer a clear and testable framework for future validation. This SPI1-PET interaction may inhibit SPI1 transcriptional activity by interfering with its ability to bind to target gene promoters. This discovery extends SPI1’s functional role in the regulation of pulmonary epithelial homeostasis and offers a new perspective on the functional plasticity of TFs across tissues. Furthermore, the potential direct interaction between PET and SPI1 provides a molecular anchor for the “non-genomic carcinogenic effects” of environmental microplastics. This mechanism aligns with the established paradigms of environmental pollutant action, such as benzo[a]pyrene binding to the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) (43) and heavy metals inhibiting zinc finger (ZnF) TFs (44), collectively highlighting TFs as a core group of targets for environmental exposure intervention.

  Based on the above evidence, we propose a working model: “PET-SPI1 inhibition-MYL9 downregulation-LUAD progression”. This model integrates a multi-scale evidence chain spanning environmental exposure to clinical phenotypes. However, a key limitation of this study is that the proposed “PET-SPI1-MYL9” regulatory axis remains a computational and correlative hypothesis, requiring direct experimental confirmation that SPI1 transcriptionally regulates MYL9 in lung epithelial cells. Its speculative nature must be emphasized: the molecular docking results require validation of binding affinity via surface plasmon resonance (SPR) measurements; the direct regulation of MYL9 by SPI1 needs confirmation of binding and transcriptional control through chromatin immunoprecipitation-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (ChIP-qPCR) and dual-luciferase reporter gene assays; future work should therefore employ chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) and dual-luciferase reporter assays in relevant cell models to test SPI1’s binding and regulatory activity on the MYL9 promoter, thereby translating this predictive model into validated mechanism. Additionally, the tumor-suppressive phenotype of MYL9 necessitates establishing causal links in animal models.

  There are certain limitations in this study. A key limitation stems from our use of public transcriptomic datasets, which lack detailed clinical annotations such as smoking history and comprehensive treatment records. Therefore, our survival analysis could not adjust for these potential confounders. While this analysis successfully established the clinical relevance of the MYL9-associated pathway, its independent prognostic value remains to be confirmed. Future studies utilizing prospective cohorts with high-quality, annotated clinicopathological data are needed to perform rigorous multivariable analyses for this purpose. First, reliance on public datasets may introduce confounding factors (e.g., smoking history and tumor stage differences). Future studies should rigorously control for these variables in prospective clinical cohorts and validate the expression patterns and prognostic values of SPI1 and MYL9 using IHC and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). Second, the regulatory relationships within the PET-SPI1-MYL9 axis have not been validated in cellular experiments. Subsequent research should delineate the hierarchical regulatory effects of PET exposure, combined with gene knockout/overexpression experiments. Third, the downstream effectors that mediate MYL9 regulation in LUAD progression remain unclear. Key downstream target genes must be identified using transcriptome sequencing coupled with functional assays.

  While this approach provides novel mechanistic insights, it consequently underscores the necessity for future research to employ exposure scenarios that more closely mirror real-world conditions. Such scenarios include, but are not limited to, the use of environmentally relevant concentrations, long-term low-dose paradigms, and complex mixtures. The goal of this future work is to validate the molecular mechanisms identified here and, ultimately, to more accurately assess potential human health risks.

  In summary, this study revealed that PET disrupts pulmonary epithelial homeostasis and drives LUAD by directly binding to SPI1 and inhibiting MYL9 transcription. The discovery of this regulatory axis not only deepens our understanding of carcinogenic mechanisms involving environmental microplastics but also provides novel insights for risk warning and targeted intervention strategies in PET-associated LUAD.


## Conclusions
  Environmental PET microplastics downregulate the protective gene MYL9 and its upstream regulator SPI1 in LUAD. Molecular docking simulations showed that PET binds to the SPI1 DNA-binding pocket, disrupting SPI1-MYL9 transcriptional control and driving tumorigenesis. Our study suggests that PET microplastics contribute to LUAD by disrupting the SPI1-MYL9 transcriptional pathway. These findings provide a novel perspective on the environmental carcinogenesis of microplastics and highlight potential diagnostic and therapeutic targets for PET-associated LUAD. Future studies should focus on experimental validation and elucidation of the detailed molecular cascades involved.
