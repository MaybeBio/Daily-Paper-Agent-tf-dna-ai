# Potential of FGF2 and GIPR as immunological biomarkers and therapeutic targets in recurrent pregnancy loss


## Abstract
  Recurrent pregnancy loss (RPL) is a complex obstetric complication whose immunological mechanisms remain incompletely understood. This study aimed to identify immune-related biomarkers for RPL by integrating the GSE165004 dataset with immune-related genes from the MSigDB database. A total of 234 immune-related differentially expressed genes (IDEGs) were identified, which were enriched in pathways such as natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity, JAK-STAT signaling, and chemokine signaling. Using machine learning algorithms (LASSO, SVM-RFE, RF), four hub genes—FGF2, TINAGL1, CKLF, and GIPR—were screened. Among them, FGF2 and GIPR were significantly downregulated in the RPL group (P < 0.05). Diagnostic efficacy was evaluated using ROC curves, and a nomogram was constructed. Immune cell infiltration analysis via CIBERSORT revealed that FGF2 showed a negative correlation with monocyte infiltration, whereas GIPR was positively correlated with resting dendritic cells (P < 0.05), suggesting their involvement in modulating the immune microenvironment. Upstream regulator analysis using NetworkAnalyst predicted that FGF2 may be regulated by miRNAs such as miR-15a-5p, while GIPR was associated with transcription factors SP1/SP3. Validation using single-cell transcriptomics and preliminary clinical samples (RT-qPCR/WB) confirmed the downregulation of FGF2 and GIPR in RPL (P < 0.05). In conclusion, our findings suggest that altered FGF2 and GIPR expression may be associated with immune dysregulation in RPL and warrant further functional investigation. These findings provide preliminary insights for potential immunotherapeutic strategies in RPL, although larger-scale validation and functional studies are needed.


## Introduction
  Pregnancy loss before fetal viability is defined as miscarriage, whereas recurrent pregnancy loss (RPL) is commonly defined as two or more consecutive pregnancy losses prior to 20–24 weeks of gestation, although some guidelines still consider three or more losses as the classical criterion. Unlike sporadic miscarriage, RPL is recognized as a distinct clinical condition often requiring specialized medical intervention [1]. RPL affects approximately 2.5% of women of reproductive age and is a common pregnancy complication [2]. Established etiologies include chromosomal abnormalities, uterine anatomical defects, autoimmune disorders, diminished ovarian reserve, environmental factors, and male factors [3]. However, a definitive cause is identified in only about 50% of cases, making early screening and accurate diagnosis challenging [4]. Therefore, discovering specific diagnostic markers and therapeutic targets is crucial for addressing this devastating condition.

  Accumulating evidence suggests that immune dysregulation may be a key factor in idiopathic RPL [5]. Approximately 50% of RPL cases are closely associated with immune dysfunction. For instance, an imbalance between T helper 1 (Th1) and T helper 2 (Th2) cell responses has been implicated in RPL pathogenesis [6–8]. Endometrial immune dysregulation is also considered a contributing factor [4]. Furthermore, autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus and antiphospholipid syndrome negatively impact pregnancy outcomes and can lead to RPL [9]. RPL likely arises from a complex interplay among maternal, embryonic, and environmental factors [10–12]. Maternal immune homeostasis is essential for establishing and maintaining a successful pregnancy. Dysregulation of these finely tuned immune mechanisms can result in pregnancy failure, positioning maternal immune dysfunction as a pivotal element in RPL etiology [9, 13]. Pregnancy induces decidualization, a unique immunological process that activates decidual natural killer (dNK) cells, contributing to maternal immune tolerance. Altered interactions between fetal HLA-E and dNK cells may provoke immunological abnormalities, disrupt maternal immune homeostasis, and lead to RPL [14].

  Recent studies have underscored the importance of tissue-specific immune biomarkers and pathways in female infertility, highlighting the need for cell-type-resolved analyses in reproductive disorders [15]. Additionally, genetic variants in steroidogenesis pathways have been implicated in polycystic ovary syndrome pathogenesis, further emphasizing the role of molecular pathways in reproductive health [16].

  Collectively, immune factors play a significant role in RPL pathogenesis. This study aimed to identify immune-related diagnostic biomarkers for RPL through genomic analysis of patient data. We established a diagnostic model and validated potential markers to explore new predictive indicators and elucidate underlying mechanisms in RPL development.


## Materials and methods

### Data acquisition and sample collection
  Gene expression data were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The GSE165004 dataset (24 RPL patients and 24 healthy controls, derived from endometrial tissue) served as the training set. The GSE26787 (5 RPL patients and 5 healthy controls) dataset was used for validation. Detailed clinical characteristics of these datasets, including gestational age, tissue type, and inclusion/exclusion criteria, were obtained from the original GEO submissions.


### Identification and functional analysis of RPL-related immune differentially expressed genes (IDEGs)
  Raw data from each GEO dataset were preprocessed using the “limma” R package, including background correction, normalization, and gene symbol conversion. Batch effects were corrected using the “Combat” function from the “SVA” R package. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between RPL and control samples in GSE165004 were identified using the “limma” package, with thresholds set at |logFC| ≥ 2.0. Immune-related genes were obtained from the MSigDB database. Immune-related differentially expressed genes (IDEGs) were identified by intersecting DEGs with immune-related genes using a Venn diagram. Visualization was performed via volcano plots and heatmaps using “ggplot2”. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were conducted using the “clusterProfiler” package (v4.10.0) [17], with results presented as bar plots. For GO/KEGG enrichment analysis, significance was determined using an adjusted P-value < 0.05.


### Screening of hub genes
  Three machine learning algorithms were applied to the IDEGs to identify key RPL-related genes: Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression, Support Vector Machine Recursive Feature Elimination (SVM-RFE), and Random Forest (RF). For LASSO regression, 10-fold cross-validation was used to select the optimal penalty parameter λ, with features having non-zero coefficients retained. The SVM-RFE algorithm was implemented using the “e1071” package with 10-fold cross-validation to refine gene selection. The RF algorithm was run with 500 trees and assessed gene importance based on the Mean Decrease Gini index, retaining genes with a value > 2. No additional train/test split was performed within the training dataset; instead, the entire GSE165004 dataset was used for feature selection, and independent validation was performed using the separate GSE26787 dataset. Genes common to all three methods were designated as hub genes.


### Validation of hub genes
  Expression levels of hub genes were compared between RPL patients and controls in the training and validation sets using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Diagnostic performance was evaluated by calculating the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) using the “pROC” package. For each ROC analysis, 95% confidence intervals were calculated using the DeLong method, and exact AUC values are reported in the Results section.


### Construction of a nomogram
  A diagnostic nomogram incorporating the hub genes was constructed using the “rms” R package. The model’s performance was assessed via calibration curves, decision curve analysis (DCA), and clinical impact curves (CIC).


### Immune microenvironment analysis
  Immune cell infiltration was estimated using the CIBERSORT algorithm to quantify the relative proportions of 22 immune cell types. As CIBERSORT was originally developed for bulk transcriptome data from peripheral blood, its application to endometrial or decidual tissue samples may have limitations, which we acknowledge in the Discussion. Differences in immune cell fractions between groups were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and visualized via violin plots. Spearman correlation analysis was performed to assess associations between hub gene expression and levels of immune cell infiltration.


### Construction of regulatory networks
  To explore upstream post-transcriptional regulation, a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) network was constructed. Potential miRNAs targeting the hub genes were predicted by intersecting results from the miRanda, miRDB, and TargetScan databases. Subsequently, long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) predicted to sponge these miRNAs were identified using the spongeScan database. An integrated lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA network was visualized to hypothesize potential regulatory axes.


### Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis
  The GSE214607 dataset (derived from human decidual tissue) was analyzed using the “Seurat” and “harmony” R packages. Quality control excluded cells with < 200 or > 7500 detected genes, > 20% mitochondrial gene expression, or > 3% ribosomal gene expression. Harmony algorithm was used for batch effect correction during principal component analysis (PCA). Cell clusters were identified using the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions (resolution = 0.5), and cell types were annotated using SingleR with the Human Primary Cell Atlas as a reference. Marker genes were identified with thresholds of log2FC > 1 and adjusted p-value < 0.05. Results were visualized using bubble plots and heatmaps.


### Clinical sample collection
  Discarded abortal tissue samples were collected from 3 RPL patients (mean age ± SD: 35.0 ± 5.3, gestational weeks: 9.7 ± 1.5) and 3 healthy women of reproductive age (mean age ± SD: 33.3 ± 7.4, gestational weeks: 10.3 ± 1.5) at The Affiliated Southwest Hospital of Youjiang Medical University For Nationalities. Inclusion Criteria for the RPL Group: Pregnant women with a history of two or more previous spontaneous abortions who meet the RPL criteria established by the American Society for Reproductive Medicine. Exclusion criteria included chromosomal abnormalities, uterine anatomical defects, autoimmune disorders, and endocrine abnormalities. Inclusion criteria for the control group: (1) No history of adverse pregnancy outcomes; (2) Good health; (3) Voluntary termination of pregnancy. Samples were immediately placed in labeled cryotubes and transferred to -80 °C within one hour for long-term storage. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Affiliated Southwest Hospital of Youjiang Medical University For Nationalities (Approval No. 2026011601). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the relevant laws and regulations and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.


### Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)
  Total RNA was extracted from samples using TRIzol reagent (AC1712, Sparkjade) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (22208, TOLOBIO) on a real-time PCR system. The primers used in this study are listed in Table 1. Primers for GIPR (HQP154325, iGenebio) and FGF2 (HQP005403, iGenebio) were validated for specificity. GAPDH served as the internal control. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method. All reactions were performed in triplicate.

### Western blotting
  Proteins were extracted using RIPA buffer (PC101, Epizyme Biotech) and quantified with a BCA assay (Pierce). Equal amounts of protein (30 µg per lane) were separated by 12% SDS‑PAGE, electrotransferred onto PVDF membranes, and probed with the antibodies listed in Table 2. Signals were detected using an ECL chemiluminescent reagent and analyzed with ImageJ software. β‑Actin served as the loading control. All experiments were performed in triplicate. Quantification data are presented as mean ± SD from three independent experiments, with effect sizes and 95% confidence intervals reported where applicable. The original Western blot images are provided in Supplementary Figure S1.

### Statistical analysis
  All analyses were performed using R version 4.0.2. Differences between two groups were analyzed using the unpaired t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test based on data distribution. Spearman correlation was used to explore relationships between gene expression and immune cell infiltration. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d or rank-biserial correlation) and 95% confidence intervals were calculated for key comparisons where applicable. All numerical values are reported to two decimal places. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Western blot quantification was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.2.


## Results

### Screening and pathway enrichment analysis of RPL-related IDEGs
  From the MSigDB database, 1793 immune-related genes were obtained (Supplementary Table S1). Comparison with DEGs from GSE165004 yielded 234 IDEGs (90 upregulated, 145 downregulated). Their expression patterns are visualized in the volcano plot (Fig. 1A) and heatmap (Fig. 1B), demonstrating clear separation between RPL and control groups.

  Fig. 1Identification of differentially expressed immune-related genes in the GSE165004 dataset. A Volcano plot showing the distribution of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between recurrent pregnancy loss (RPL) and control groups. B Heatmap displaying the expression profiles of immune-related DEGs (IDEGs) in RPL versus control samples

  GO enrichment analysis revealed that IDEGs were primarily involved in biological processes such as cell adhesion, cell activation, and chemotaxis (Fig. 2A). KEGG analysis indicated significant enrichment in pathways including Natural killer cell mediated cytotoxicity, JAK-STAT signaling pathway, and Chemokine signaling pathway (Fig. 2B), highlighting their potential roles in RPL immunopathology.

  Fig. 2Functional enrichment analysis of immune-related differentially expressed genes (IDEGs). A Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of IDEGs. B Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis of IDEGs


### Identification and screening of hub genes
  Three machine learning algorithms were applied to the 234 IDEGs. LASSO regression selected 17 feature genes (Fig. 3A), RF identified 10 (Fig. 3B-C), and SVM-RFE selected 12 (Fig. 3D-E). The intersection of these methods yielded four hub genes: FGF2, TINAGL1, CKLF, and GIPR (Fig. 3F). It should be noted that the limited sample size of the training dataset (n = 48) may increase the risk of overfitting; independent validation using GSE26787 was therefore performed to mitigate this concern.

  Fig. 3Screening of hub genes using machine learning algorithms. A LASSO regression analysis for hub gene selection. B, C Random forest (RF) algorithm for hub gene selection. D, E Support vector machine recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) algorithm for hub gene selection. F Venn diagram showing the overlap of genes selected by LASSO, SVM-RFE, and RF algorithms


### Diagnostic performance evaluation and nomogram construction based on hub genes
  In the GSE165004 training set, expression of CKLF, FGF2, and GIPR was significantly downregulated in RPL, while TINAGL1 was upregulated (Fig. 4A-D). ROC analysis showed AUC values of 0.98 (95% CI: 0.96–1.00) for FGF2, 0.96 (95% CI: 0.89–1.00) for GIPR, 0.97 (95% CI: 0.91–1.00) for CKLF, and 0.98 (95% CI: 0.94–1.00) for TINAGL1, indicating excellent diagnostic potential (Fig. 4E-H).

  Fig. 4Diagnostic value of hub genes in the GSE165004 dataset. A–D Differential expression of hub genes between the RPL and control groups. E–H Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves evaluating the diagnostic performance of each hub gene

  In the GSE26787 validation set, CKLF, FGF2, and GIPR remained significantly downregulated in RPL. However, CKLF showed significant expression differences among RPL in the validation set. Whereas TINAGL1 showed no consistent differential expression (Fig. 5A-D). Corresponding AUC values in the validation set were 0.76 (95% CI: 0.40–1.00) for FGF2, 1.00 (95% CI: 1.00–1.00) for GIPR, 0.76 (95% CI: 0.40–1.00) for CKLF, and 1.00 (95% CI: 1.00 ‑1.00) for TINAGL1 (Fig. 5E-H).

  Fig. 5Diagnostic value of hub genes in the GSE26787 dataset. A–D Differential expression of hub genes between the RPL and control groups. E–H Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves evaluating the diagnostic performance of each hub gene

  A logistic regression model incorporating the four hub genes was used to construct a nomogram (Fig. 6A). The calibration curve demonstrated high agreement between predicted and observed outcomes (Fig. 6B). Decision curve analysis (Fig. 6C) and clinical impact curve analysis (Fig. 6D) confirmed the model’s favorable net benefit and clinical utility for RPL prediction.

  Fig. 6Construction and evaluation of a nomogram for predicting recurrent pregnancy loss (RPL) based on four hub genes. A Nomogram incorporating the four hub genes. B Calibration curve assessing the agreement between predicted and observed outcomes. C Decision curve analysis (DCA) evaluating the net benefit of the nomogram. D Clinical impact curve (CIC) demonstrating the clinical utility of the nomogram


### Immune cell infiltration analysis and correlation with hub genes
  CIBERSORT analysis revealed the relative proportions of 22 immune cell types in RPL and control groups (Fig. 7A). Differential analysis showed a significant increase in monocyte proportion in the RPL group (P < 0.05; Fig. 7B). Correlation analysis indicated that CKLF and FGF2 expression were negatively correlated with monocyte infiltration, while GIPR was positively correlated with resting dendritic cells (P < 0.05; Fig. 7C). These findings suggest that hub genes may modulate immune cell composition within the microenvironment, although computational deconvolution methods like CIBERSORT have inherent limitations that should be considered.

  Fig. 7Correlation between hub gene expression and immune cell infiltration. A Distribution of immune cell subtypes in RPL and control groups. B Comparative analysis of differentially infiltrated immune cells between RPL and control groups. C Spearman correlation analysis between hub gene expression and immune cell infiltration levels


### Construction of non-coding RNA and transcription factor regulatory networks for hub genes
  Using NetworkAnalyst, five miRNAs (miR-877-3p, miR-302a-5p, miR-15a-5p, miR-545-3p, miR-186-5p) were predicted to target FGF2 (Fig. 8A). Ten transcription factors (TFs) were predicted to regulate the two key hub genes. FGF2 was associated with seven TFs (EGR1, ERG, HDAC5, HIF1A, HOXB7, LMO2, PTTG1), while GIPR was linked to SP1 and SP3 (Fig. 8B). No significant miRNA/TF interactions were predicted for CKLF and TINAGL1 under the applied thresholds.

  Fig. 8Construction of ceRNA and transcription factor regulatory networks for hub genes. A ceRNA network (lncRNA–miRNA–mRNA) of hub genes. B Transcription factor (TF) regulatory network of hub genes


### Single-cell transcriptomic analysis of hub genes
  scRNA-seq analysis of dataset GSE214607 identified five major cell subpopulations: epithelial cells, fibroblasts, lymphocytes, myeloid cells, and endothelial cells (Fig. 9A). Hub gene expression was cell-type-specific: FGF2 was highly expressed in fibroblasts, TINAGL1 in epithelial and endothelial cells, and CKLF and GIPR in lymphocytes (Fig. 9B). Notably, FGF2 expression was predominantly detected in fibroblasts, while GIPR was primarily expressed in lymphocytes, suggesting that these genes may exert their effects through distinct cellular compartments at the maternal-fetal interface. Their distribution across the cellular landscape is visualized in Fig. 9C.

B Heatmap illustrating the expression levels of hub genes in different cell subpopulations. C UMAP plots visualizing the distribution of hub gene expression across individual cells


### Expression analysis of biomarkers in clinical samples
  RT-qPCR and Western blotting confirmed significant downregulation of FGF2 and GIPR in RPL patient samples compared to controls (P < 0.05; Fig. 10), consistent with bioinformatics findings. These results provide preliminary validation of the potential of FGF2 and GIPR as RPL biomarkers, although the small sample size (n = 3 per group) limits the statistical power and generalizability of these findings.

## Discussion
  RPL is a multifactorial disorder with incompletely understood etiology. Growing evidence underscores the significance of immune-related factors [5]. Our study integrated bioinformatics and preliminary experimental validation to identify four immune-related hub genes (FGF2, TINAGL1, CKLF, GIPR), with FGF2 and GIPR emerging as key players. Immune cell infiltration analysis, single-cell sequencing, and regulatory network exploration provided a comprehensive view of their roles in RPL immunopathology. However, as this study is largely based on bioinformatics analyses with limited experimental validation, the findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Furthermore, genetic variations in the vitamin D receptor (VDR) have been implicated in infertility and may modulate immune tolerance during pregnancy [16]. Importantly, The polymorphisms in CYP11A1, CYP17A1, and CYP19A1 affect steroidogenesis and are associated with infertility in women, highlighting the importance of genetic background in reproductive disorders. These genetic factors may also influence the immune microenvironment at the maternal‑fetal interface, and future studies should explore whether such variants are linked to the altered FGF2/GIPR expression observed in RPL [17].

  Our study extends previous machine learning-based biomarker discovery efforts in RPL by integrating multiple layers of evidence: (1) we combined three machine learning algorithms for robust gene selection; (2) we validated findings in an independent GEO dataset; (3) we performed single-cell RNA-seq analysis to resolve cell-type-specific expression patterns; and (4) we provided preliminary experimental validation using RT-qPCR and Western blotting. Notably, FGF2 has been previously identified as a potential RPL biomarker in a machine learning study by Lin and Pang, and our study independently confirms this finding while also identifying GIPR as a novel candidate. This convergence across independent studies strengthens the evidence for FGF2’s involvement in RPL and highlights GIPR as a newly implicated gene warranting further investigation.

  FGF2 (basic fibroblast growth factor) is a pleiotropic cytokine involved in various immune processes. In inflammatory diseases like asthma, FGF2 modulates immune responses, potentially via the FGFR/MAPK/NF-κB pathway, influencing cytokine release (e.g., IL-1β, IL-18) [18]. In cancer, FGF2 promotes tumor progression and immune evasion by activating pathways such as Ras/MAPK and PI3K/AKT [18]. In melanoma, FGF2 synergizes with PGE2 to induce immunosuppressive cytokines (e.g., IL-10, TGF-β) and M2 macrophage polarization, inhibiting CD8 + T cell function [19]. FGF2 can also activate fibroblasts via the FGFR1/JAK2/STAT3 pathway, shaping the immune microenvironment [20]. Our findings of downregulated FGF2 in RPL tissues and its negative correlation with monocyte infiltration suggest that aberrant FGF2 expression may disrupt immune homeostasis at the maternal-fetal interface, contributing to RPL. However, it is important to note that our study does not establish a causal relationship between FGF2 downregulation and RPL pathogenesis.

  GIPR, primarily known for its role in glucose metabolism via interaction with glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) [21–23], is also expressed on immune cells like macrophages. Loss of GIPR activity can enhance tissue inflammation by upregulating macrophage-derived pro-inflammatory factors [24, 25]. The maternal-fetal interface requires a state of immune tolerance, maintained partly by low cytotoxicity of decidual NK cells (dNK), macrophages, and γδ-T cells [26–28]. GIPR signaling may contribute to this balance by modulating immune cell activity, such as suppressing pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages or overactive CD16 + NK cells. In mouse models, GIPR deficiency in immune cells disrupts type 2 immune networks in white adipose tissue, altering expression of mediators like IL-33 and IL-5 [29]. This could indirectly impair metabolic and immune homeostasis during pregnancy. Furthermore, dual knockout of GLP-1R and GIPR exacerbates early type 2 innate immune responses to allergens [30], a mechanism potentially relevant to maternal hyperreactivity against fetal semi-alloantigens. Although direct evidence linking GIPR to RPL is lacking, its metabolic and immunomodulatory functions warrant further investigation. Although direct evidence linking GIPR to RPL is currently lacking, its metabolic and immunomodulatory functions make it an intriguing candidate for future investigation.

  To integrate FGF2 and GIPR into a broader immunological framework of RPL, we propose the following interconnected mechanisms: (1) Th1/Th2 imbalance --- FGF2 may influence T cell polarization through its effects on cytokine production; (2) Treg/Th17 dysregulation --- both FGF2 and GIPR signaling could modulate regulatory T cell function; (3) Decidual NK-cell dysfunction --- altered cytokine signaling may affect dNK cell activity; and (4) Macrophage polarization --- FGF2’s negative correlation with monocytes suggests a role in macrophage phenotype regulation. Future studies are needed to test these hypotheses directly.

  Pathway enrichment analysis revealed IDEG involvement in NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity, JAK-STAT, and chemokine signaling, aligning with existing literature on RPL immunology [31, 32]. NK cell dysfunction is a well-documented feature in RPL [33, 34], and JAK-STAT pathway dysregulation may perturb Th1/Th2 balance at the maternal-fetal interface [7, 35].

  Single-cell analysis highlighted cellular heterogeneity in RPL, with hub genes showing distinct expression patterns across epithelial cells, fibroblasts, lymphocytes, myeloid cells, and endothelial cells [36, 37]. Specifically, FGF2 was predominantly expressed in fibroblasts, while GIPR was primarily expressed in lymphocytes. This cell-type-specific expression suggests tailored immune responses and offers potential for targeted therapies [38]. Immune infiltration findings—increased monocytes and decreased resting CD4 + memory T cells in RPL—support the notion of innate immune overactivation coupled with adaptive immune suppression in RPL [36]. Specifically, elevated monocytes may differentiate into pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages, which are implicated in trophoblast injury and RPL [39]. The positive correlation between GIPR and resting dendritic cells hints at a role in antigen presentation and T cell tolerance induction [40, 41].

  The use of computational immune infiltration methods such as CIBERSORT has inherent limitations. These algorithms were originally developed for peripheral blood transcriptomes and may produce less reliable results when applied to mixed tissues such as decidua or endometrium, which contain diverse cell types with varying transcriptional profiles. Therefore, our immune infiltration findings should be considered exploratory, and validation using flow cytometry, immunohistochemistry, or spatial transcriptomics would be required to confirm these observations.

  Regulatory network analysis suggested that FGF2 and GIPR expression is influenced by miRNAs (e.g., miR-15a-5p for FGF2) and transcription factors (e.g., HIF1A for FGF2, SP1/SP3 for GIPR), indicating complex transcriptional and post-transcriptional control. Notably, miR-15a-5p is reported to be elevated in extracellular vesicles from RPL patients [42], and HIF1A is a known regulator of trophoblast ferroptosis under hypoxia in RPL [43]. These upstream regulators themselves represent potential therapeutic targets.

  Clinical implications and comparison with current diagnostic approaches: Currently, RPL diagnosis relies primarily on clinical history and exclusion of known etiologies through karyotyping, hysterosalpingography, and autoimmune screening. Non-invasive or minimally invasive molecular biomarkers remain largely unavailable. Our findings suggest that FGF2 and GIPR expression levels in endometrial or decidual tissues could potentially complement existing diagnostic workflows. However, several barriers to clinical translation remain: (1) tissue accessibility (endometrial biopsy is invasive), (2) need for standardized assay protocols, (3) requirement for large-scale validation across diverse populations, and (4) unclear predictive value for pregnancy outcomes. Future studies should explore whether these biomarkers can be detected in peripheral blood or cervical secretions to enable non-invasive testing.

  This study has limitations. First, the sample size for bioinformatics analysis (GSE165004: n = 48; GSE26787: n = 10) and clinical validation (n = 3 per group) was relatively small, which may affect the generalizability of findings across diverse RPL populations or clinical subtypes. The very small validation dataset (5 patients and 5 controls) is statistically insufficient to definitively confirm the results. Furthermore, the use of multiple machine learning algorithms on a limited sample size increases the risk of overfitting. Second, reliance on publicly available microarray data from GEO introduces potential biases from batch effects and varying preprocessing protocols. Third, patient heterogeneity (e.g., different gestational ages, varying numbers of previous losses, different underlying etiologies) may confound the results. Fourth, the control group characteristics—including whether they were fertile women with no history of pregnancy loss or women undergoing termination for non-medical reasons—need to be carefully considered when interpreting the findings. Fifth, the abortal tissue samples used for validation may not fully represent the decidual microenvironment during active pregnancy. Sixth, our experimental validation was limited to 3 RPL patients and 3 controls, which provides insufficient statistical power to draw definitive conclusions. Seventh, functional experiments (knockdown/overexpression studies, trophoblast-immune cell co-culture models, cytokine assays, and in vivo models) are necessary to establish the biological relevance of FGF2 and GIPR in RPL pathogenesis, but were not performed in this study. Eighth, the application of CIBERSORT to endometrial/decidual tissue data may produce unreliable results, as this algorithm was designed for peripheral blood. Future research should involve larger, multi-center prospective cohorts and integrated multi-omics data to validate these findings and explore the functional mechanisms of FGF2 and GIPR in RPL in vitro and in vivo. Additionally, spatial transcriptomics, single-cell multi-omics, proteomics, and organoid or trophoblast models would be valuable to further elucidate the roles of these molecules.


## Conclusion
  In summary, this study identifies FGF2 and GIPR as crucial immune-related biomarkers downregulated in RPL. Our findings suggest that altered expression of these genes may be associated with immune dysregulation at the maternal-fetal interface, particularly through interactions with monocytes and dendritic cells. Their dysregulation, potentially mediated by specific miRNAs and transcription factors, may contribute to the immune imbalance observed in RPL. However, these findings are preliminary and hypothesis-generating. Functional validation through in vitro and in vivo experiments, as well as large-scale clinical validation, is required before FGF2 and GIPR can be considered for diagnostic or therapeutic applications. Nonetheless, this study offers novel insights into RPL pathogenesis and highlights FGF2 and GIPR as promising candidates for future investigation.
