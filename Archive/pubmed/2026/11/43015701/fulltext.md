# Shenfu Injection attenuates isoproterenol-induced heart failure partly through modulation of the TNF-α/TNFR1/NF-κB signaling pathway

## Introduction
  The transition from a healthy heart to heart failure (HF) is a multifaceted process influenced by various factors that are primarily driven by the loss of cardiomyocytes. This can be caused by the functional or structural dysfunction of the heart attributed to conditions such as ischemic heart disease, high blood pressure, valvular disease, or even infections. These conditions lead to inflammation, necrosis, and intrinsic and extrinsic apoptosis pathways [1]. Exploring these molecular mechanisms can assist in identifying potential targets for pharmaceutical interventions aimed at enhancing cell survival, suppressing the inflammatory response, and blocking apoptosis. A hallmark feature of HF is fluid retention, which causes increased blood pressure and fluid build-up in the lungs, heart, and the rest of the body. Fluid retention in the lungs can lead to shortness of breath and the inability to engage in physical activities like exercise [2]. The various symptoms associated with HF lead to a poor quality of life, increased hospitalization rates, decreased physical function, and high rates of morbidity and mortality [3]. Different drugs that target various therapeutic pathways have been explored and used to treat HF. However, HF continues to be the main disease causing death globally, especially with the aging population. This places a major economic burden on the health and social systems. The continuous exploration and implementation of various therapeutic avenues are required, and alternative options like traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), which have been used to treat HF for many years, may open doors to new and effective methods of treatment. Therefore, the extensive exploration of the mechanisms of action of TCM and its targets holds immense potential for developing novel and effective HF therapies. Rigorous research is essential to further validate the efficacy and safety of TCM and facilitate its integration into conventional HF management, ultimately improving patient outcome.

  Nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) plays a central role in multiple biological processes, including inflammation, cell proliferation, and cell survival [4]. Emerging evidence suggests that NF-κB signaling exerts a dual role in the heart, with both adaptive and maladaptive effects depending on the context and duration of activation [5]. In particular, prolonged or persistent activation of NF-κB is considered detrimental, as it may contribute to heart failure (HF) progression by promoting inflammation and cardiomyocyte death [6]. Therefore, the timing and duration of NF-κB activation are critical determinants of its overall impact on cardiac function.The TNF-α/TNFR/NF-κB axis is particularly relevant to HF pathophysiology. Sustained elevation of TNF-α can drive cardiomyocyte apoptosis, amplify inflammatory responses, promote extracellular matrix remodeling, and ultimately contribute to ventricular dilation and contractile dysfunction [7]. Mechanistically, TNF-α activates NF-κB signaling through binding to its cell-surface receptors, TNFR1 and TNFR2. TNFR1 is more commonly associated with pro-inflammatory and pro-apoptotic signaling, including NF-κB activation and death-domain-related pathways, whereas TNFR2 may mediate adaptive or reparative effects in certain cardiovascular settings [8]. Accordingly, selective modulation of TNFR1-associated inflammatory signaling, rather than complete TNF-α blockade, may represent a more biologically plausible cardioprotective strategy [9, 10]. In the present study, network pharmacology and GEO dataset analyses consistently indicated a close association between TNF-α signaling and HF. Moreover, the key active compounds of SFI were predicted to interact with core genes involved in TNF-α/NF-κB pathway activation, suggesting thatShenfu Injection (SFI) may exert anti-HF effects, at least in part, through modulation of TNF-α-related inflammatory signaling.

  Shenfu Injection, an injectable formulation derived from Panax ginseng C.A. Mey. and Aconitum carmichaelii Debeaux, has been widely used in China for the management of cardiovascular disorders. The rationale for evaluating SFI in heart failure (HF) is supported not only by its clinical application but also by accumulating preclinical evidence. Previous in vitro studies showed that SFI protected neonatal rat cardiomyocytes against hypoxia/reoxygenation-induced injury by reducing apoptosis, increasing Bcl-2 expression, and suppressing caspase-3 activation [11]. In animal models of septic or inflammatory myocardial injury, SFI was further reported to attenuate myocardial histopathological damage, reduce cardiomyocyte apoptosis, improve mitochondrial structural injury, upregulate Bcl-2, and downregulate Bid, truncated Bid, and caspase-9 [12]. Additional studies have suggested that SFI may alleviate myocardial hypertrophy, inflammatory signaling, and adverse cardiac remodeling through pathways involving NF-κB and other cell-survival mechanisms [13]. Collectively, these findings provide a biological basis for the potential cardioprotective effects of SFI in HF, particularly through limiting inflammation, preserving cardiomyocyte viability, and suppressing apoptosis-related signaling [14, 15].

  Consistent with these observations, previous studies have also reported anti-inflammatory effects of SFI, including reduced expression of TNF-α, IL-6, myocardial TLR4, and NF-κB. These results are in line with our findings that key active compounds of SFI may interact with core genes involved in TNF-α-related signaling. Clinically, SFI has been widely applied as an adjunctive therapy for HF in China. Systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and clinical studies have suggested that SFI may improve cardiac function, quality of life, and exercise capacity, with an acceptable safety profile [16–18]. However, although these studies support the potential clinical benefit of SFI, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying its cardioprotective effects in HF remain insufficiently defined.

  Network pharmacology offers a systems-level approach for investigating the multicomponent, multitarget, and multipathway characteristics of traditional Chinese medicine formulations. In addition, molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations can provide complementary in silico evidence for the plausibility and stability of predicted compound–target interactions. In the present study, we combined network pharmacology with molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations to explore the potential mechanisms by which SFI may act against HF. We hypothesized that SFI may attenuate inflammation and apoptosis, thereby protecting against myocardial injury, through modulation of the TNF-α/TNFR1/NF-κB signaling pathway. To test this hypothesis, we established two HF-related models: an isoprenaline (ISO)-induced HF model in Sprague–Dawley rats and an ISO-induced H9c2 cell injury model. Using these models, we aimed to evaluate the cardioprotective effects of SFI and to further clarify its regulatory role in the TNF-α/TNFR1/NF-κB signaling pathway.


## Materials and methods

### Network pharmacology analysis
  The active compounds of Shenfu Injection (SFI), including those derived from Hongshen (HS) and Fuzi (FZ), were screened using the BATMAN-TCM platform according to predefined criteria. Compounds meeting the thresholds of oral bioavailability (OB) ≥ 30% and drug-likeness (DL) ≥ 0.18 were retained as candidate active ingredients [19]. These thresholds are widely used empirical criteria in network pharmacology studies to enrich for compounds with relatively favorable pharmacokinetic and drug-like properties while reducing low-confidence candidates. Because SFI is an injectable preparation, the OB/DL-based screening was used only as an initial database-filtering strategy and does not fully represent the pharmacokinetic characteristics of SFI after intravenous administration.

  The predicted targets of candidate compounds were standardized by converting protein names into official gene symbols using the UniProt database. Only targets annotated as Homo sapiens were retained, and duplicate or non-specific entries were removed. Heart failure-related targets were retrieved from the OMIM and GeneCards databases using the keyword “chronic heart failure.” After merging and de-duplicating the disease targets, the intersecting targets between SFI and heart failure were identified and visualized using Venny 2.1.

  A drug–component–target–disease interaction network was subsequently constructed, and topological analysis was performed using Network Analyzer to identify core compounds. Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses of the shared targets were then conducted using the Metascape platform [20]. The enrichment results were visualized as bar charts and bubble plots using the WeLifeInfo platform.


### GEO analysis
  The dataset GSE26887 was obtained from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) in MINiML format. This dataset was based on the GPL6244 platform and included 24 human myocardial tissue samples: 5 control samples, 7 diabetic heart failure samples, and 12 non-diabetic heart failure samples. To reduce disease heterogeneity, only non-diabetic heart failure samples (n = 12) and control samples (n = 5) were included in the present analysis, whereas diabetic heart failure samples were excluded. The Limma package (version 3.40.2) in R was utilized to assess different genes [21], with adjusted p-values computed to account for false discovery rates. The thresholds for screening differentially expressed mRNAs were set as “Adjusted P < 0.05 and log2(fold change) > 1 or log2(fold change) < -1.” Volcano plots and heatmaps were generated to visualize differentially expressed genes based on fold change and adjusted p-values. Furthermore, the ClusterProfiler package in R was utilized to conduct KEGG enrichment analysis to identify pathways potentially affected by changes in mRNA levels. The expression heatmap was presented using R’s circular heatmap functionality.


### Molecular docking
  The active ingredients with the highest centrality values were chosen for molecular docking with the core targets. The TCMSP database provided the molecular structures of the small-molecule components of SFI. The Protein Data Bank (PDB) (http://www.rcsb.org/) was used to obtain the target structures. The structures used in the docking analysis were caspase-3 (PDB ID: 5JFT; X-ray diffraction; 2.28 Å; zebrafish), caspase-9 (PDB ID: 2AR9; X-ray diffraction; 2.80 Å), TAK1/Tab1 (PDB ID: 5JGA; X-ray diffraction; 2.00 Å; human), IκBα from the IκBα/NF-κB complex (PDB ID: 1IKN; X-ray diffraction; 2.30 Å; human IκBα chain), and the TNFR1-associated TRADD N-terminal domain (PDB ID: 1F2H; solution NMR). These structures were selected according to the availability of pathway-relevant domains and structural quality. Before docking, co-crystallized ligands and water molecules were removed, and polar hydrogen atoms were added using PyMOL software (V2.5, DeLano Scientific LLC). Molecular docking was subsequently performed utilizing AutoDock Vina version 1.1.2. The results of the docking process were then displayed using PyMOL software [22, 23].Because 1F2H represents a TNFR1-associated TRADD domain rather than full-length TNFR1, docking results involving this structure were interpreted cautiously as supportive computational evidence.


### Molecular dynamics simulations
  Molecular dynamics simulations of the protein-ligand complex derived from molecular docking were conducted utilizing Amber18 [24]. The protein used the ff14SB force field, while the ligand utilized GAFF parameters, with AM1-BCC atomic charges calculated via ANTECHAMBER. The system was prepared in the leap module, adding hydrogen atoms and counterions, and using the TIP3P water model. The simulation workflow included energy minimization, heating to 300 K, equilibration, and a 200 ns production run at 2 fs time steps [25]. Equilibration was assessed mainly by RMSD convergence, RMSF, radius of gyration, and hydrogen-bond analyses during the production trajectory. Trajectories were saved every 40 ps and analyzed using CPPTRAJ, with binding free energy calculated via MMPBSA.py. GROMACS utilities were employed for RMSD, RMSF, and radius of gyration analysis, and results were visualized using Xmgrace and PyMOL. Ramachandran plot analysis was done with PROCHECK.


### Experimental verification

#### In vivo studies

##### Shenfu injection
  Shenfu Injection (SFI) was obtained as a commercially manufactured finished injectable pharmaceutical product from China Resources Sanjiu (Ya’an) Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Ya’an, Sichuan, China (batch no. 230802AK05). SFI is derived from Panax ginseng C.A. Mey. and Aconitum carmichaelii Debeaux. According to the manufacturer’s records, reference voucher specimens of P. ginseng (voucher no. A17201208001) and A. carmichaelii (voucher no. A17220921003) are retained in the Herbarium Center of China Resources Sanjiu Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd [26].

  UPLC-Orbitrap-MS was used for Shenfu injection quality control. HRMS data were acquired utilizing a Q Exactive HFX Hybrid Quadrupole Orbitrap mass spectrometer equipped with a heated ESI source (Thermo Fisher Scientific) through Full-MS-ddMS2 methods. The key parameters comprised sheath gas pressure (40 arb), auxiliary gas pressure (10 arb), spray voltage (+ 3000 V/-2800 V), ESI source temperature maintained at 350 °C, and ion transport tube temperature regulated at 320 °C. The scanning range was 70–1050 m/z, with primary resolution of 70,000 and secondary resolution of 17,500.


##### Laboratory animals
  The procedures utilized in this experiment received approval from the Animal Ethical Committee at the Hunan University of Chinese Medicine (Approval Number: LL202309230002). Male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats, aged 6 weeks and weighing approximately 220 ± 10 g, were sourced from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co. Ltd. (Hunan, China) (No.430727231103030316). All animals were kept under standard husbandry conditions.


##### Establishment of animal model and drug administration
  Following a week of adaptive feeding, the experimental rats were randomly assigned to the following groups (n = 8): the control group (CON group), the model group (ISO), the positive drug group(Meto group,10 mg/kg)and various treatment groups based on different SFI doses (3 ml/kg, 6 ml/kg, 12 ml/kg). The model group received subcutaneous multipoint injections of isoproterenol at a dosage of 5 mg·kg− 1·d− 1 for a duration of seven days, to establish a heart failure model, as indicated by previous research studies [27]. The SFI group received Shenfu injection and distilled water via gavage, while the Meto group was administered metoprolol intragastrically and sterile water through intraperitoneal injections for a duration of 15 days. The control group and ISO groups were given distilled water by gavage and were injected intraperitoneally with normal saline. Rats were anesthetized by inhaling 3% isoflurane to measure cardiac function using ultrasound M-mode echocardiography.

#### Observation indicators

##### Echocardiography
  Echocardiography was performed as previously described to assess cardiac function [28]. Data from three consecutive cardiac cycles were used to calculate the average value. The calculation of left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and left ventricular fractional shortening (LVFS) was performed using the software (VINNO6, Suzhou VINNO Technology Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China).


##### Terminal Deoxynucleotidyl Transferase Dutp Nick-End Labeling (TUNEL) staining
  Myocardial tissues were preserved in 10% formalin at 4 °C overnight, subsequently sectioned to a thickness of 5 μm, and stained with a TUNEL kit (Beyotime, Jiangsu, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions to evaluate cardiomyocyte apoptosis. The sections were treated with 50 µL of the TUNEL mixture at a temperature of 37 °C for 60 min, after which they were sealed using neutral resin. The stained sections were analyzed using a fluorescence microscope (Eclipse TE2000-U, Nikon). During image acquisition, DAPI fluorescence was captured at an excitation wavelength of 358 nm and an emission wavelength of 461 nm, while TUNEL/FITC fluorescence was captured at an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and an emission wavelength of 520 nm. The apoptotic rate was determined by counting the TUNEL-positive cells in each field of view.


##### Detection of blood biochemical indexes
  The rats were euthanized with urethane anesthesia (1.0 g/kg, i.p.) at the end of the experiment. Blood samples were obtained from the abdominal aorta and allowed to clot at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C). Following centrifugation, serum was extracted.


##### Western blot analysis
  Western blot analysis was conducted on cardiac muscle tissue following the previously established protocol [29]. Protein samples (30 µg) were subjected to denaturation at 95 °C, subsequently separated using 10% SDS-PAGE, and transferred onto NC membranes, which were then sectioned based on the molecular size of the target protein. For protein band analysis, membranes were immunoblotted with primary and secondary antibodies and exposed. The following were the primary antibodies used: TNF-α (AF7014,1:1000, Affinity), TNFR1(AF0282,1:1000, Affinity), IκBα (AF5002,1:1000, Affinity), NF-κB p65 (AF5006, 1:1000, Affinity), Caspase-3 (A23903, 1:1000, Affinity), Caspase-9 (AF6348,1:1000, Affinity), and Actin (AF7018, 1:1000, Affinity).


##### Immunohistochemistry of cardiac tissues
  Immunohistochemistry was performed as previously described. Sections were immersed in 3% hydrogen peroxide for 8 min and underwent antigen retrieval using a microwave, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C. The samples were subsequently incubated with an avidin-biotinylated horseradish peroxidase complex (Zymed Laboratories, diluted 1:200) for a duration of 1 h at 37 °C, followed by staining with a DAB kit (Zsbio, China). Sections were stained, then rinsed for 20 min with tap water, dehydrated with alcohol, made transparent with xylene, and sealed with resin adhesive.


### In vitro studies

#### Cell culture
  H9c2 cells (Priscilla, CL-0089) were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (P/S) in an incubator set at 37 °C with a 5% CO2 atmosphere. When the cell cultures reached a density of 85% in the medium, a cell passage was performed. The medium was refreshed two to three times each week. The ISO model of H9c2 cells was built to mimic myocardial injury in vivo. Briefly, when H9c2 cells reached an 80% confluence, the culture medium was removed, and H9c2 cells were washed three times with 1% FBS DMEM. The cells were pretreated with SFI at different concentrations for 3 h and then exposed to isoproterenol (50 µM) for 48 h in 1% FBS DMEM.

## Result

### Network pharmacological target prediction
  The active components and targets of SFI were screened by the BATMAN-TCM database. Fifty-five active components and 996 active targets were identified to create the target protein database for the drugs. The screening criteria were set as Scorecut off ≥ 20 and p < 0.05. A total of 1527 CHF-related disease targets from GeneCards and OMIM disease databases and 99 overlapping targets were identified (Fig. 1A). The findings from the “drug-component-disease-target” network are illustrated in Fig. 1B, P-Aminophenol, Para-Aminophenol, M-Aminophenol, Ortho_Aminophenol, Mescaline, and Ignavine were predicted by the database-based network pharmacology analysis as candidate active components of SFI (Table S1). However, UHPLC-QE-MS quality-control analysis mainly detected ginsenoside Re, ginsenoside Rg1, benzoylmesaconine, and ginsenoside Rb1 (Fig. 4 and Table S3). Therefore, the database-predicted components should be interpreted cautiously and require further confirmation by experimental component analysis. The mechanism by which KEGG predicts the prevention and treatment of HF by SFI may be associated with the TNF signaling pathway, Adrenergic signaling in cardiomyocytes, Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, and Renin secretion (Fig. 1C). GO enrichment analysis showed that the overlapping targets were mainly enriched in biological processes related to regulation of blood circulation, heart contraction, and muscle system processes; cellular components related to cation channel and transmembrane transporter complexes; and molecular functions related to voltage-gated ion channel activity, ion channel binding, and receptor ligand activity (Fig. 1G-I).

### GEO analysis
  We selected the dataset GSE26887, which included 5 control samples, 7 diabetic heart failure samples, and 12 non-diabetic heart failure samples. Only 12 samples of non-diabetic heart failure and 5 control samples were included for analysis in this study. Diabetic heart failure samples were excluded because diabetes may introduce additional metabolic and inflammatory transcriptional changes independent of ISO-related or general HF pathology, and their inclusion could increase heterogeneity in this small dataset. Because GSE26887 does not include SFI-treated samples, it was used in the present study only as an independent heart failure-related transcriptomic reference for disease-pathway prioritization, rather than as direct evidence of SFI responsiveness.Volcano and Heat maps of differentially expressed genes were drawn as illustrated in Figs. 1D and E, and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the overlapping DEGs in the datasets was conducted. The TNF, HIF-1, and TGF-beta signaling pathways may be involved in the mechanism underlying KEGG’s prediction of HF (Fig. 1F).

  Based on the findings from network pharmacology and the analysis of GEO datasets, we hypothesize that SFI may regulate inflammation and apoptosis to prevent HF and heart injury by inflammatory correlation pathways. We chose the classical TNF signaling pathway for in vivo and in vitro experimental verification.


### Molecular docking result
  Five database-predicted candidate components, namely Ignavine, M_Aminophenol, Mescaline, Ortho_Aminophenol, and P_Aminophenol, were chosen as docking components in SFI to interact with IκBα, NF-κB p65, TAK1, TNF-α, TNFR1, and TRAF2 in TNF signaling pathways, and pro-apoptotic factor (caspase- 3/caspase-9). The heat map illustrating the active components and target proteins, along with several examples of molecular docking modes, is presented in Fig. 2. The analysis revealed the binding modes between five bioactive components of SFI and key therapeutic targets in HF. The docking results showed different binding affinities among ligand-target pairs, suggesting potential interactions between selected SFI-related compounds and key therapeutic targets. In Fig. 2A, the Ignavine-related complexes exhibited the most favorable docking scores, whereas the other four components (M_Aminophenol, Mescaline, Ortho_Aminophenol, and P_Aminophenol) showed weaker predicted affinities and did not consistently reach the − 5 kcal/mol threshold. Therefore, the docking results were not generalized to all ligand-target pairs. Because docking scores and calculated binding free energies are predictive rather than direct experimental evidence, these results were interpreted as supportive molecular evidence rather than definitive proof of binding stability. These findings suggest that SFI may prevent or relieve HF through a potential multi-component and multi-target regulatory pattern involving cytokine signaling, especially the TNF signaling pathway, but further experimental validation is required.

### Molecular dynamics simulation result
  The top five highest-ranked docked complexes were further selected for molecular dynamics simulations to confirm the findings of the molecular docking analysis. The obtained RMSD, RMSF, radius of gyration, and hydrogen bonding data were analyzed to assess their relative stability. The RMSD variations of the protein-ligand complexes were key indicators of whether the simulations reached stability. As shown in Fig. 3A, the RMSD fluctuations of the Caspase 3-Ignavine, Caspase 9-Ignavine, and TAK1-Ignavine complexes were relatively smooth, ultimately stabilizing around 2 Å, 2.5 Å, and 2.5 Å, respectively. The IκBα-Ignavine complex reached a higher plateau of approximately 7 Å, suggesting that binding may be accompanied by notable conformational rearrangement rather than negligible structural change. The TNFR1-Ignavine complex showed larger RMSD fluctuations than the other complexes, indicating greater conformational flexibility after ligand binding. This may reflect adaptive rearrangement of the receptor binding region or relatively lower conformational stability of this complex; therefore, the TNFR1-Ignavine simulation should be interpreted cautiously and requires further experimental validation.

  Fig. 3Molecular dynamic simulation results. Simulation trajectories of Molecular dynamics simulation. Root mean square deviation, Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF), and Radius of Gyration (RoG)

  The radius of gyration was used to characterize the overall compactness of the protein structure. The results showed that the radii of gyration for the Caspase 3-Ignavine, Caspase 9-Ignavine, IκBα-Ignavine, and TAK1-Ignavine complexes were comparatively stable, suggesting that their overall compactness was maintained during simulation. However, the radius of gyration of the TNFR1-Ignavine complex showed more evident fluctuations, indicating changes in protein compactness after ligand binding. Together with the RMSD result, this suggests that Ignavine may induce a more flexible or rearranged TNFR1 conformation, which may be related to the dynamic nature of receptor-ligand recognition but also indicates that the stability of this complex should not be overinterpreted. RMSF values were used to quantify the structural stability, atomic mobility, and residual persistence of proteins during collisions. The results showed that the Caspase 3-Ignavine (in the 200–280 region), Caspase 9-Ignavine (in the 480–720 region), IκBα-Ignavine (in the 450–550 region), TAK1-Ignavine (in the 170–220 region), and TNFR1-Ignavine (in the 100–140 region) complexes exhibited higher flexibility in different regions, with many loop structures contributing to the high flexibility. Additionally, the binding free energies of these complexes (over 100–200 ns) were calculated using the MMGBSA method (Table S2). The MMGBSA analysis estimated binding free energies of -27 kcal/mol for the Caspase 3-Ignavine complex, -13 kcal/mol for the Caspase 9-Ignavine complex, -16 kcal/mol for the IκBα-Ignavine complex, -37 kcal/mol for the TAK1-Ignavine complex, and − 56 kcal/mol for the TNFR1-Ignavine complex. These values were used for relative comparison among complexes rather than as experimentally measured absolute binding free energies.

  These results suggest that Ignavine may interact with key targets, particularly TNFR1, TAK1, and Caspase 3. However, because the TNFR1-Ignavine complex showed relatively large RMSD and RoG fluctuations, the favorable MMGBSA value should be interpreted together with the trajectory stability rather than in isolation. TNFR1 remains biologically relevant because it is one of the receptors for TNF-α involved in the activation of NF-κB, but further biochemical experiments are required to confirm direct binding and functional consequences.

#### Effect of SFI on the cardiac function in HF rats
  The impact of SFI on ISO-induced CHF rats was investigated by assessing cardiac indices such as LVEF, LVFS, LVIDd, and LVIDs using echocardiography and Nt-proBNP level. #### CCK-8 assay in the detection of the optimal concentration of SFI
  To examine the cytotoxic impact of SFI on H9c2 cells, the cells were treated with varying concentrations of SFI (1‰, 2‰, 4‰, 8‰, 12‰, and 16‰, v/v) for a duration of 48 h. Treatment with 1‰, 2‰, and 4‰ SFI resulted in enhanced H9c2 cell activity, with the effect intensifying as the concentration of SFI increased. Consequently, we opted for 4‰ SFI for the cell experiments in the subsequent phase of our research (Fig. 8B).

  Findings from the CCK-8 assay indicated that after 48 h of stimulation with 50 µM ISO, H9c2 cell viability significantly decreased. In contrast, SFI markedly improved the H9c2 cell viability in comparison to the ISO model validation group (Fig. 8E), whereas TNF-IN did not significantly restore cell viability under the present experimental conditions.


#### Effect of SFI on the H9c2 cell morphology damaged by ISO
  As illustrated in Fig. 8C, the H9c2 cells in the control group exhibited an intact, spindle-shaped morphology with favorable adhesion capabilities. Following a 48-hour stimulation with ISO, the H9c2 cells exhibited gradual wrinkling, experienced a reduction in intercellular junctions, and detached some cells. SFI has been observed to enhance the morphological characteristics of H9c2 cells, In contrast, TNF-IN did not produce an obvious improvement in cell morphology compared with the ISO group.


#### Effect of SFI on the Myocardial Apoptosis damaged by ISO
  To investigate the impact of SFI on ISO-induced apoptosis, we assessed the quantity of apoptotic cells using flow cytometry (Fig. 8D). The ISO group exhibited a significant increase in the number of apoptotic cells, whereas SFI notably reduced cell apoptosis (Fig. 8F). However, TNF-IN did not significantly reduce ISO-induced apoptosis. This result suggests that direct TNF-α blockade alone was insufficient to reproduce the protective effects of SFI in this ISO-induced cell injury model. Therefore, the effect of SFI may involve broader regulation of inflammatory and apoptotic networks rather than a single TNF-α-dependent mechanism.


#### CCK-8 assay in the detection of the optimal concentration of ISO
  To assess the cytotoxic effect of ISO on H9c2 cardiomyocytes, a CCK-8 assay was conducted. Different concentrations of ISO were evaluated to determine the effective dosage that induces 20%–30% cell mortality. As illustrated in Fig. 8A, exposure to 50 µM ISO for a duration of 48 h led to a survival rate of 70%–80% in H9c2 cells. Consequently, 50 µM ISO for 48 h was chosen to induce myocardial damage in H9c2 cells.


#### Effect of SFI on the Inflammation damaged by ISO
  In comparison to the CON group, the levels of IL-6 and IL-1β in H9c2 cells in the ISO group exhibited a marked increase. When contrasted with the ISO group, both the SFI and TNF-IN groups demonstrated a significant decrease in the levels of IL-1β and IL-6. However, the differences observed between the SFI and TNF-IN groups were not statistically significant (Fig. 8G, and H).


#### Effect of SFI on the TNF-α/TNFR1/NF-κB signaling pathway
  The Western Blot analysis revealed that, in comparison to the CON group, the expressions of TNF-α, TNFR1, and NF-κB p65 proteins were markedly elevated in the ISO group. Conversely, the protein expression in the ISO + SFI and TNF-IN groups showed a significant decrease. Additionally, when compared to the CON group, the expression of IκBα proteins in the ISO group was notably decreased, whereas the ISO + SFI and TNF-IN groups exhibited a significant increase in IκBα protein levels (Fig. 8I, and J). However, the improvement produced by TNF-IN was mainly reflected in inflammatory signaling markers, whereas TNF-IN did not significantly restore cell viability or reduce apoptosis. This discrepancy suggests that inhibition of TNF-α signaling alone may be insufficient to fully reproduce the cytoprotective effect of SFI.


## Discussion
  In the present study, we combined network pharmacology, GEO dataset analysis, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulation, and experimental validation to investigate the potential protective effects of Shenfu Injection (SFI) against heart failure (HF). Our results showed that SFI improved cardiac dysfunction in isoproterenol (ISO)-induced HF rats, alleviated myocardial injury in ISO-treated H9c2 cells, reduced inflammatory responses, and suppressed apoptosis-related signaling. Mechanistically, these effects were associated with modulation of the TNF-α/TNFR1/NF-κB signaling axis, suggesting that SFI may exert cardioprotective effects through coordinated anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic actions Fig 9.

  Fig. 9Shenfu injection ameliorates isoprenaline-induced heart failure in SD rats by inhibiting the TNF-α/TNFR1/NF-κB signaling pathway

  ISO is widely used to induce experimental cardiac injury because excessive β-adrenergic stimulation promotes myocardial hypertrophy, fibrosis, oxidative stress, and ventricular remodeling, all of which contribute to HF-like pathological changes. In the present study, repeated ISO administration led to impaired cardiac function, as evidenced by reduced LVEF and LVFS and increased LVIDd and LVIDs, indicating successful establishment of the HF model. In addition, serum inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β and IL-6, were elevated, further supporting the presence of myocardial inflammatory injury. At the cellular level, H9c2 cells were selected as a cardiomyocyte-like injury model because they retain several cardiac-associated phenotypic features and are responsive to adrenergic stimulation and oxidative stress. After ISO exposure, H9c2 cell viability was significantly reduced and apoptosis was markedly increased, indicating that the in vitro injury model was also successfully established.

  Inflammation and apoptosis are both central processes in the pathogenesis of HF. Persistent inflammatory activation contributes to myocardial remodeling, extracellular matrix disruption, and progressive functional decline, whereas excessive cardiomyocyte apoptosis accelerates loss of contractile units and deterioration of cardiac performance. Pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 are consistently elevated in HF and are widely recognized as important mediators of disease progression [30]. These cytokines can exert negative inotropic effects, promote cardiomyocyte death, and amplify maladaptive remodeling [31–34]. In parallel, caspase family proteins serve as key executors of apoptotic signaling and may also participate in inflammation-related cell injury. In our study, ISO exposure was associated with increased levels of inflammatory cytokines and apoptosis-related proteins, supporting the close interaction between inflammation and apoptosis during HF progression.

  SFI has been widely used in China as an adjunctive therapy for cardiovascular diseases, including HF, and accumulating studies suggest that it may exert beneficial effects through anti-inflammatory, anti-apoptotic, anti-remodeling, and microcirculatory regulatory mechanisms [35, 36]. In the present study, SFI significantly improved cardiac function in ISO-induced HF rats, as indicated by increased LVEF and LVFS, reduced LVIDd and LVIDs, and decreased NT-proBNP levels. In addition, SFI reduced inflammatory cytokine levels and attenuated apoptosis in both in vivo and in vitro models. These findings are generally consistent with previous reports showing that SFI can improve myocardial energy metabolism, reduce ischemic or inflammatory injury, inhibit cardiomyocyte apoptosis, attenuate myocardial fibrosis, and suppress adverse remodeling through pathways involving NF-κB, MEF2, miR-19a-3p, and PI3K/Akt/eNOS signaling [37–41]. Taken together, our data further support the notion that SFI exerts multi-target cardioprotective effects rather than acting through a single pathway.

  Based on network pharmacology and GEO analysis, we initially hypothesized that TNF-α-related inflammatory signaling might represent an important mechanism underlying the action of SFI in HF. This hypothesis was further supported by molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation, which suggested potential interactions between key active compounds of SFI and several pathway-related targets. Experimental validation then showed that SFI reduced the expression of TNF-α, TNFR1, and NF-κB p65 while increasing IκBα expression in myocardial tissue and in ISO-injured H9c2 cells. These findings suggest that SFI may suppress activation of the TNF-α/TNFR1/NF-κB pathway under pathological conditions. Since sustained TNF-α elevation is known to promote inflammatory amplification, cardiomyocyte apoptosis, extracellular matrix remodeling, ventricular dilation, and contractile dysfunction, inhibition of excessive TNF-α/TNFR1-associated signaling may be beneficial in HF. Importantly, our findings are more consistent with selective attenuation of maladaptive inflammatory signaling than with complete blockade of TNF-α, which may be overly simplistic given the context-dependent effects of TNF receptor signaling [42, 43].

  The TNF-α/TNFR1/NF-κB axis is highly relevant to HF pathophysiology. TNF-α exerts its biological functions mainly through TNFR1 and TNFR2, which can activate downstream NF-κB-related signaling cascades. In cardiovascular disease, TNFR1 is more commonly linked to pro-inflammatory and pro-apoptotic responses, whereas TNFR2 may mediate adaptive or reparative effects in certain contexts [44–46]. Therefore, suppression of TNFR1-dominant pathological signaling may be a more biologically plausible explanation for the protective effects observed in this study. Notably, SFI also regulated apoptosis-related proteins in parallel with inflammatory markers, indicating that its actions likely involve crosstalk between inflammatory and apoptotic signaling networks. This is in line with the multi-component and multi-target characteristics of traditional Chinese medicine formulations.It should also be noted that TNF-α signaling is receptor-dependent.

  Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged.First, although H9c2 cells provide a convenient and widely used cardiomyocyte-like injury model, they cannot fully recapitulate the complexity of adult cardiomyocytes or the multicellular microenvironment of the failing heart. Second, this study did not include pathway rescue experiments or TNFR1-specific loss-of-function validation. Future work should employ TNFR1 silencing, gene knockdown, or selective pathway inhibitors to determine whether TNFR1/NF-κB signaling is necessary for the protective effects of SFI at the cellular and tissue levels.Third, the GEO analysis in this study was based on a single HF-related dataset (GSE26887) with a relatively limited sample size, which may affect the robustness of transcriptomic target identification.Future studies should integrate multiple HF transcriptomic datasets with appropriate batch-effect correction, or use overlapping targets from independent datasets, to obtain more robust HF-related candidate targets.Another limitation is that SFI is administered intravenously in clinical practice, whereas oral gavage was used in the present animal study. This inconsistency may limit the translational relevance of the dosing design and should be considered when interpreting the in vivo results.The gavage-based administration in this study should therefore be regarded as a preclinical experimental intervention model rather than a strict simulation of the clinical route of SFI administration.In addition, some docking structures did not fully represent native full-length human proteins. For example, the caspase-3 structure was derived from zebrafish, and the TNFR1-related structure represented a TRADD N-terminal domain rather than full-length TNFR1. Therefore, the docking and molecular dynamics results should be interpreted as supportive computational evidence rather than direct proof of ligand binding to native full-length human proteins.

  Mechanistically, the present findings are consistent with previous reports showing that SFI can exert anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic effects in cardiovascular injury models. Sustained TNF-α/TNFR1 activation may promote NF-κB nuclear signaling, inflammatory cytokine production, and apoptosis-related caspase activation, thereby contributing to myocardial injury and cardiac dysfunction. In this context, the observed reductions in TNF-α, TNFR1, NF-κB p65, IL-6, IL-1β, caspase-3, and caspase-9 support the involvement of this inflammatory-apoptotic signaling axis in the protective effects of SFI. However, because similar anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic pathways have been reported previously for SFI, the present study should be interpreted as an integrative and confirmatory investigation rather than the identification of a completely novel mechanism [36–41].


## Conclusion
  This study suggests that SFI protects against ISO-induced injury partly by inhibiting inflammation and apoptosis. Network pharmacology and molecular simulation analyses indicated that several candidate components of SFI may be associated with key targets, although the specific contribution of each monomer requires further experimental validation. SFI improved cardiac function, increased LVEF and LVFS, reduced inflammatory biomarkers such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, and decreased cell apoptosis, possibly through regulation of the TNF-α/TNFR1/NF-κB signaling pathway.
