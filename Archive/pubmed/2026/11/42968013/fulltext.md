# miR-206 orchestrates breast cancer radiosensitivity through a SIRT1/p53 feedback loop: a network medicine modelling study


## Background
  Radiotherapy remains a cornerstone of breast cancer treatment, yet radioresistance limits its efficacy. miR-206 acts as a tumor suppressor by inhibiting SIRT1 and stabilizing p53. However, how these molecular components are organized within larger regulatory networks, and whether such network-level hubs can be therapeutically prioritized, remains unclear.


## Results
  Network modeling predicted a previously unrecognized double-negative feedback loop (miR-206 ⊣ SIRT1 ⊣ p53 → miR-206) that functions as a predicted bistable switch, driving mutually exclusive commitment to either a radiosensitive (apoptotic) or a radioresistant (proliferative) fate. Monte Carlo simulations revealed that opposite perturbations of this circuit consistently drove the system toward the corresponding stable attractor. Edge disruption analysis identified the miR-206/SIRT1 and p53/miR-206 interactions as essential for maintaining bistability, while ATM exerted p53-dependent context-specific effects. Model predictions were consistent with independent experimental observations that miR-206 overexpression suppresses SIRT1, enhances p53 acetylation, and increases radiosensitivity.


## Conclusions
  This network medicine framework identifies the miR-206/SIRT1/p53 bistable switch as a key regulatory module governing cell-fate decisions in breast cancer radioresistance. The model provides a systems-level platform for prioritizing experimentally testable therapeutic strategies to overcome radioresistance.

## Introduction
  DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs), triggered by ionizing radiation, oxidative stress, or chemotherapeutic agents, activate the DNA damage response (DDR), a signaling network that safeguards genomic integrity by coordinating repair, cell cycle arrest, or apoptosis [1]. Radiotherapy exploits this vulnerability by inducing DSBs and remains a cornerstone of breast cancer treatment [1, 2]. However, its efficacy is limited by tumor radiosensitivity, a trait governed by the integrity of the DDR network; dysregulation within this network enables cancer cells to repair damage and evade death, driving therapeutic resistance [2].

  At the heart of this network lies tumor protein p53 (p53), a master tumor suppressor that orchestrates transcriptional programs controlling apoptosis, senescence, and cell cycle arrest [3]. In breast cancer, p53 inactivation is strongly associated with aggressive disease and poor outcomes [4]. p53 activity is tightly modulated by sirtuin 1 (SIRT1), an NAD⁺-dependent deacetylase that suppresses p53 transcriptional function by deacetylating key lysine residues, thereby blunting apoptosis and promoting survival [5]. SIRT1 overexpression is frequently observed in cancer and linked to therapeutic resistance [6].

  Adding further complexity, microRNAs (miRNAs) post-transcriptionally regulate gene expression and are frequently dysregulated in cancer [7]. Among them, microRNA 206 (miR-206) is a critical tumor suppressor consistently downregulated in breast cancer, where it inhibits proliferation, migration, and invasion while enhancing therapeutic responses [8]. Recent experimental evidence confirms that miR-206 overexpression suppresses SIRT1 activity and enhances p53 acetylation in breast cancer cells, thereby increasing their sensitivity to radiation [9], yet the underlying regulatory logic connecting these events remains unexplored.

  Despite this wealth of molecular detail, the systems-level logic by which the p53–miR-206–SIRT1 axis coordinates cell fate decisions, whether a cell proliferates or undergoes apoptosis, remains poorly understood. To address this gap, we adopted a network medicine framework by constructing a Boolean model of the miR-206/SIRT1/p53 regulatory network. To our knowledge, this is the first study to predict that the miR-206–SIRT1–p53 axis may function as a bistable regulatory switch governing radiosensitivity in breast cancer. This approach enables us to identify how network-level properties, specifically feedback loops and bistability, determine disease-relevant phenotypes and suggest therapeutic interventions.


## Methods

### Mapping the gene regulatory networks landscape in cancer cells: a fusion of public databases and tools
  We constructed a mechanistic gene regulatory network capturing the central role of the miR-206/SIRT1 axis in the cellular response to DNA damage in breast cancer. Experimentally validated molecular interactions were systematically curated from the primary literature via PubMed and cross-validated against curated databases, including BioGRID v3.5 [10] and TargetScan [11]. Only interactions supported by direct experimental evidence were retained. The curated network was implemented as a Boolean model using GINsim 3.0.0b [12]. The model file is available in the Code Availability section.


### Developing dynamic boolean network models, rules, and simulations from pubmed-based insights
  The regulatory network was formalized as a Boolean dynamical system, with nodes representing molecular entities, complexes, or cellular processes, and directed edges denoting activation or inhibition. Each node assumes a binary state (0 = inactive, 1 = active), and its next state is determined by a logical rule (AND, OR, NOT) derived from experimental evidence. All Boolean update rules are provided in Table S1.

  Network dynamics were simulated using asynchronous updating, in which a single node is randomly selected for update at each time step, reflecting the stochastic and unsynchronized nature of biological processes [13]. All simulations were performed using GINsim 3.0.0b. For attractor analysis, stable states (biological attractors) were identified as terminal nodes in the state transition graph with no outgoing transitions to other states. For Monte Carlo simulations, we performed 10,000 independent runs from random initial conditions, and phenotypic probabilities were calculated as the fraction of trajectories converging to each attractor [13].

  Attractors (stable states or cycles) were interpreted as distinct cellular phenotypes, while feedback circuits (positive for multistability, negative for oscillations) determined network behavior [12, 13]. Gain-of-function and loss-of-function perturbations were modeled by fixing selected nodes to the active (1) or inactive (0) state, independent of their regulatory inputs [13]. For further methodological details, see our previous studies [14–18].


### Proposed molecular mechanism in breast cancer
  Double-strand breaks (DSBs) induced by ionizing radiation activate ATM serine/threonine kinase (ATM) [19], which stimulates p53 through direct phosphorylation or by inhibiting MDM2 proto-oncogene (MDM2) [4, 20, 21]. p53 then transcriptionally upregulates miR-206 [22], which in turn represses multiple targets: SIRT1 [23], the anti-apoptotic protein BCL2 apoptosis regulator (BCL2) [24], the NAD⁺ salvage enzyme nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase (NAMPT) [25, 26], the cell cycle regulator cyclin D (CCND) [27], and the MET proto-oncogene, receptor tyrosine kinase (c-Met) [28]. SIRT1 inhibition prevents p53 deacetylation and inactivation [29], creating a positive feedback loop that amplifies p53 activity. C-Met inhibition suppresses downstream AKT serine/threonine kinase (AKT) signaling [30] and MYC proto-oncogene, bHLH transcription factor (c-Myc) expression [31], further reducing proliferation and survival signals.

  Activated p53 induces cyclin dependent kinase inhibitor 1A (p21) [32] and represses BCL2 [33, 34], while activating MDM2 under sustained damage [35]. p21 blocks CCND activity [36] and is itself suppressed by AKT [37], caspase 3 (CASP3) [38], and c-Myc [39]. BCL2 inhibition release BCL2-associated X, apoptosis regulator (BAX) [40], leading to CASP3 activation [41] and apoptosis [41]. Meanwhile, BCL2 and MDM2 cooperate to drive proliferation [42–44] and resistance [42].

  Based on these experimentally validated interactions (summarized in Supplementary Table S1), we constructed a Boolean network model to elucidate how the miR-206/SIRT1/p53 feedback loop orchestrates cell fate decisions, specifically proliferation, radioresistance, and apoptosis, in breast cancer cells.Drawing from the foundational interactions outlined earlier, we constructed our Boolean model to elucidate the regulation of cell death pathways in cancer cells.

Approximately 50% of the simulations corresponded to the absence of DNA damage (Input = 0), converging to the baseline proliferative state. ### The miR-206/SIRT1/p53 double-negative feedback loop is predicted to govern bistable cell fate decisions
  Having established that our model reproduces key experimental observations, we next sought to identify the regulatory architecture underlying these dynamics. According to René Thomas' theoretical framework [46], functional positive feedback circuits constitute the structural basis for multistability in logical regulatory networks, whereas negative feedback circuits are primarily associated with oscillatory dynamics. Analysis of the network topology revealed a previously undescribed double-negative feedback loop among miR-206, SIRT1, and p53 (miR-206 ⊣ SIRT1 ⊣ p53 → miR-206). The three regulatory interactions that comprise this circuit are supported by experimental evidence in the literature (Supplementary Table S2). The network also contained a p53/MDM2 negatve feedback loop, representing a classical mechanism regulating p53 activity [47]. Importantly, the miR-206/SIRT1/p53 circuit was the only positive feedback circuit identified in the network, suggesting that it is the principal regulatory architecture supporting multistable behavior.

  As demonstrated by the wild-type network analysis (Fig. 2A), under identical DNA damage (DDR ON) conditions the model exhibits two alternative stable attractors corresponding to radioresistance and apoptosis, consistent with bistable behavior. We next investigated the functional role of the miR-206/SIRT1/p53 circuit in controlling transitions between these alternative stable states.

  These perturbations were designed to test whether opposite regulatory configurations bias the network toward one of the two pre-existing stable attractors identified in the wild-type model (Fig. 2A), rather than serving as evidence for bistability itself. Oncogenic perturbation (1) uniformly drove the system into a stable radioresistant state (100% of attractors; Fig. 4A). Conversely, tumor-suppressive perturbation (2) locked the system exclusively into apoptosis (100% of attractors; Fig. 4B). These findings indicate that modulation of the circuit strongly biases attractor selection between the two stable states.

Removing either the miR-206⊣SIRT1 or p53 → miR-206 interaction eliminated the apoptotic attractor, thereby abolishing the bistable behavior and shifting the system toward a dominant radioresistant state (Table 1). In contrast, disruption of the SIRT1⊣p53 interaction preserved bistability, indicating that this interaction is not individually required to maintain the bistable switch.   Collectively, these results identify the miR-206/SIRT1/p53 circuit as the principal positive feedback architecture governing bistable cell-fate decisions in the model. Perturbation and edge-deletion analyses further demonstrate that this circuit regulates attractor selection and that the miR-206⊣SIRT1 and p53 → miR-206 interactions are critical for maintaining bistability. Together, these findings support the prediction that the miR-206/SIRT1/p53 circuit may function as a bistable switch, while acknowledging that experimental validation is required to confirm this behavior in biological systems.


### ATM influences radioresistance and apoptosis through p53-dependent signaling
  The miR-206–SIRT1–p53 feedback loop positions p53 as a central cell fate determinant. p53 activation, however, depends on upstream DNA damage signaling, particularly ATM, which directly phosphorylates and stabilizes p53 [32]. ATM exhibits paradoxical, context-dependent functions in breast cancer, promoting DNA repair and survival in some settings while facilitating cell death in others [45]. Indeed, radioresistant breast cancer cells show elevated ATM expression, accelerated H2A.X variant histone (γ-H2AX) clearance, and enhanced DNA repair efficiency, all of which can be reversed by ATM inhibition or knockdown [45]. Gain-of-function (E1) and loss-of-function (KO) perturbations of ATM were evaluated under fixed p53 activation or knockout using Monte Carlo simulations (10,000 runs). Both ATM E1 and ATM KO resulted in apoptosis when p53 was constitutively active, whereas both perturbations resulted in radioresistance when p53 was knocked out. The DNA damage input was fixed ON

  Under constitutive p53 activation (E1), both ATM overexpression (E1) and ATM knockout (KO) drove 100% of simulations toward apoptosis. Conversely, under p53 knockout (KO), both ATM E1 or KO perturbations resulted exclusively in the radioresistant phenotype. These findings indicate that p53 status determines the final cell-fate outcome, whereas ATM perturbation alone is insufficient to alter the terminal phenotype once p53 activity is fixed.

  These findings are consistent with the established role of ATM as an upstream activator of p53 in the DNA damage response [32]. Moreover, they complement the experimental observations of Bian et al. [45], who reported elevated ATM expression and enhanced DNA repair capacity in radioresistant breast cancer cells. Together, these findings suggest that the cellular consequences of ATM signaling depend on the functional status of p53, providing a mechanistic explanation for the context-dependent roles of ATM in breast cancer radiosensitivity.


### Clinical translation and proposed interventions
  Our model is consistent with the radiosensitizing effects of miR-206 upregulation reported by Seiri et al. [9], in which miR-206 suppresses SIRT1, enhances p53 acetylation, and promotes p53-mediated apoptosis following irradiation. Combined with our finding that the effects of ATM depend on p53 status (Sect. "ATM influences radioresistance and apoptosis through p53-dependent signaling"), these results provide a rationale for p53-guided therapeutic strategies to overcome radioresistance in breast cancer.

  Based on our model predictions, we propose the following hypotheses for preclinical evaluation in breast cancer models (e.g., p53-proficient MCF-7 xenografts):miR-206 restoration + radiotherapy: Delivery of miR-206 mimics (or viral vectors) concurrent with irradiation in p53-proficient tumors is predicted to amplify p53-dependent apoptosis and suppress clonogenic survival, consistent with the findings of Seiri et al. [9].ATM inhibition in p53-proficient tumors: Selective ATM blockade (e.g., AZD0156 or KU-55933) in tumors retaining functional p53 may impair DNA repair, increase unrepaired DNA damage, and facilitate p53-mediated apoptosis, thereby enhancing radiosensitivity.Patient stratification based on TP53 status: Because the model predicts that the cellular response to ATM perturbation depends on functional p53, assessment of TP53 status may help identify patients most likely to benefit from ATM-targeted radiosensitization strategies.

  These model-derived, p53-guided strategies provide experimentally testable hypotheses for improving breast cancer radiosensitization. Because these strategies are based on computational predictions, they require experimental and clinical validation. Analysis of public clinical datasets, such as TCGA or METABRIC, to examine associations between miR-206/SIRT1 expression and radiotherapy outcomes represents a logical next step toward assessing their translational relevance.


## Discussion and conclusion
  In this work, we constructed a Boolean network model of the miR-206/SIRT1/p53 axis in breast cancer (Fig. 1) to uncover the regulatory logic governing cell fate decisions under genotoxic stress. Analysis of the wild-type network revealed that DNA damage input determines three distinct phenotypic outcomes: proliferation, radioresistance, and apoptosis (Fig. 2A). Monte Carlo simulations indicated that radioresistance is the predominant attractor under genotoxic stress (Fig. 2B), which is qualitatively consistent with the clinical challenge of radioresistance in breast cancer, where tumors often persist despite radiotherapy [9].

  The model was validated against an independent experimental study by Seiri et al. [9], which was not used during model construction. Their work showed that miR-206 overexpression suppresses SIRT1 activity, increases p53 acetylation, and enhances radiation-induced apoptosis. Our in-silico perturbations faithfully recapitulated these observations (Fig. 3): miR-206 overexpression (miR-206 E1) suppressed SIRT1, activated p53, and drove the system toward apoptotic states, while miR-206 knockdown (miR-206 KO) stabilized radioresistant phenotypes, consistent with the low miR-206 levels Seiri et al. [9] reported in breast tumors. SIRT1 activation promoted radioresistance by repressing p53, whereas p53 activation induced apoptosis, confirming that our model captures the core regulatory logic of this axis.

  Although experimental validation of the predicted bistable switch is beyond the scope of this computational study, the consistency of our predictions with established roles for miR-206 as a tumor suppressor [8, 9], SIRT1 as a promoter of therapeutic resistance [5, 6], and p53 as a master regulator of apoptosis [3, 48], supports the model’s credibility as a mechanistic framework for understanding radioresistance.

  Importantly, the linear interpretation proposed by Seiri et al. [9], miR-206 inhibits SIRT1, which in turn activates p53, captures the individual molecular events. Our model provides a rational explanation for their observations by revealing that these interactions are embedded within a double-negative feedback loop (miR-206 ⊣ SIRT1 ⊣ p53 → miR-206) that operates as a positive feedback motif (Supplementary Table S2). This architecture transforms what appears linear into a self-reinforcing circuit that forces mutually exclusive cell fates, a systems-level property that emerges from the interconnection of components they experimentally characterized.

  While the model is minimal, it captures the core regulatory logic of the miR-206–SIRT1–p53 circuit. Minimal models are particularly effective at revealing emergent behaviors, such as bistability, that may be obscured in larger networks, making them powerful tools for uncovering fundamental regulatory principles.

  To evaluate this circuit, we performed Monte Carlo simulations under opposing perturbations (Fig. 4). Under oncogenic dominance (miR-206 knockdown, p53 knockdown, SIRT1 overexpression), the system collapsed into a single radio-resistant state. Under tumor-suppressive dominance (miR-206 overexpression, p53 overexpression, SIRT1 knockdown), the system locked exclusively into apoptosis, confirming that forcing the circuit eliminates the alternative stable state.

  This predicted behavior suggests that cancer cells may exist in two discrete functional states: a miR-206-high/p53-active state associated with enhanced radiosensitivity, and a SIRT1-dominant state characterized by suppressed p53 activity and resistance to radiation-induced cell death.

  We next sought to determine which interactions are essential for maintaining this behavior. Disruption analysis revealed that the miR-206/SIRT1 and p53/miR-206 interactions are indispensable; removing either completely abrogated apoptosis, collapsing the system into a single radioresistant state (Table 1). In contrast, removal of the SIRT1/p53 link preserved the two-state behavior, indicating that while this interaction modulates the circuit, it is not essential for the switch.

  Beyond this core circuit, we also examined how upstream signals integrate into the switch. The miR-206/SIRT1/p53 circuit integrates signals from ATM, but ATM's effect is dependent on p53 status (Fig. 5). When p53 is absent, ATM activation promotes DNA repair and survival, driving radioresistance. When p53 is active, ATM loss impairs repair and redirects signaling toward p53-mediated apoptosis. This p53-dependent effect offers a potential resolution to the long-standing paradox regarding ATM's opposing roles and provides a rational framework for patient stratification: ATM-targeted therapies may need to be guided by p53 status.

  Clinically, this architecture suggests that therapeutic modulation of miR-206 or SIRT1 may not produce gradual, dose-dependent effects. Instead, it may favor discrete transitions between resistant and sensitive phenotypes, which could help explain the binary, often unpredictable responses seen in patients undergoing radiotherapy. This highlights the potential value of strategies aimed at restoring miR-206 or inhibiting SIRT1 to shift resistant cells toward a radiosensitive state.

  These findings suggest that modulation of miR-206 or SIRT1 could serve as a therapeutic strategy to enhance radiotherapy response in breast cancer patients. Specifically, restoring miR-206 expression or inhibiting SIRT1 activity may shift the system from a radioresistant to a radiosensitive state, offering a rational basis for combination therapies.

  Our network medicine approach suggests that the miR-206/SIRT1/p53 axis may function as a regulatory switch controlling radiosensitivity. These findings illustrate how network-level analysis can uncover emergent properties that linear pathway models miss, with potential implications for predicting and overcoming therapy resistance. This framework positions network-based biomarkers, such as miR-206 and p53 status, as candidate determinants of therapeutic outcome.

  From a network medicine perspective, the regulatory logic we uncovered for miR-206 may represent a broader paradigm. The same regulatory logic may extend to other miRNAs targeting the p53/SIRT1 axis. Micro RNA 34a (miR-34a) and micro RNA 449a (miR-449a) family members all converge on this hub (Yamakuchi et al., 2009 [49]; Kumar et al., 2016 [50]), suggesting that p53 and SIRT1 function as a central integration point for multiple miRNA-mediated feedback loops. This redundancy may explain why cancer cells often require simultaneous disruption of multiple miRNAs to fully abrogate p53-dependent apoptosis, a finding that emerges naturally from a systems biology perspective but would be missed by linear models.

  This places the miR-206–SIRT1–p53 circuit among a small set of core regulatory motifs that dictate cell fate through switch-like behavior (see Fig. 6), a design principle increasingly recognized as central to cancer biology and therapy. These mechanistic insights directly translate into testable therapeutic strategies: miR-206 restoration combined with radiotherapy and ATM inhibition in p53-proficient tumors.   Finally, several limitations should be acknowledged. For instance, our model does not capture dose-dependent regulation or radiation dose responses, which would require quantitative frameworks such as ordinary differential equations (ODEs) or multivalued logical models. The model focuses on the core miR-206/SIRT1/p53 regulatory axis but does not explicitly include other known regulators of radiosensitivity, such as checkpoint kinase 1/2 (CHK1/CHK2), poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP), breast cancer susceptibility protein (BRCA) pathways, hypoxia signaling, or additional p53-associated miRNAs. These components were excluded to maintain focus on the miR-206/SIRT1/p53 core circuit and to avoid obscuring its emergent bistable behavior. However, their omission means that our model may not capture all contextual factors that influence radiosensitivity in vivo. Future model extensions incorporating these regulators could provide a more comprehensive view of the radiosensitivity network and may reveal how they modulate or override the bistable switch identified here.

  The present model focuses on the regulatory mechanisms controlling cell-fate decisions following DNA damage and does not aim to reproduce all quantitative aspects of radiation response, such as clonogenic survival, DNA repair kinetics, or dose–response relationships.

  Furthermore, although the model was constructed from experimentally supported cancer-related interactions, we acknowledge that some regulatory relationships were derived from different tumor contexts and may vary across breast cancer subtypes and TP53 mutational backgrounds. Future incorporation of subtype-specific data will further improve model refinement and translational relevance.

  We also acknowledge that our proposed therapeutic strategies are computational predictions that require experimental and clinical validation. Analysis of public clinical datasets, such as TCGA or METABRIC, to examine associations between miR-206/SIRT1 expression and radiotherapy outcomes would be a valuable next step to assess the translational relevance of our findings. While the predicted bistable switch requires experimental validation, our model provides a mechanistic framework for understanding how the miR-206/SIRT1/p53 axis controls cell fate decisions. Future experimental studies at the single-cell level could test whether this bistability manifests as bimodal population distributions, a hallmark of switch-like behavior. Such experiments would be essential to confirm the predicted all-or-none transitions and to guide the development of miR-206- or SIRT1-based therapeutic strategies. While our edge deletion analysis identified the miR-206/SIRT1 and p53/miR-206 interactions as essential for bistability, additional combinatorial perturbations could provide further insight into the circuit's robustness and represent a natural extension of this work.

  In summary, we have predicted a bistable switch in the miR-206/SIRT1/p53 axis, a double-negative feedback loop that forces breast cancer cells into mutually exclusive fates: apoptosis (radiosensitive) or radioresistance. Our model is consistent with the experimental findings of Seiri et al. [9] and provides a potential systems-level explanation for their observations by revealing that these interactions are embedded within a double-negative feedback loop. This work identifies the miR-206/SIRT1/p53 axis as a predicted central decision-making hub and reveals that therapeutic modulation of this circuit can flip resistant cells into a radiosensitive state, a clinically actionable strategy to overcome treatment failure.
