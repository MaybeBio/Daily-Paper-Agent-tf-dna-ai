# IGF2BP3 promotes M2 macrophage polarization and ESCC progression by increasing USP36-mediated β-catenin stabilization


## Background
  Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma is a highly aggressive malignancy with poor prognosis that is characterized by an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment that critically contributes to tumor progression and therapeutic resistance. RNA-binding proteins are emerging regulators of cancer biology; however, their role in shaping the tumor immune microenvironment of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma remains largely unexplored. This study aimed to investigate the function and mechanism of the RNA-binding protein IGF2BP3 in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma progression and immune modulation.


## Methods
  Single-cell RNA sequencing datasets including esophageal squamous cell carcinoma and adjacent normal tissues were integrated to identify dysregulated RNA-binding proteins. The functional role of IGF2BP3 was evaluated using in vitro assays, organoid models, and immunocompetent and immunodeficient mouse models. RNA immunoprecipitation, polysome profiling, ubiquitination assays, and luciferase reporter assays were employed to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms. Immune cell infiltration was analyzed by flow cytometry, immunohistochemistry, and multiplex immunofluorescence. Virtual screening, biolayer interferometry, and molecular docking were used to identify small-molecule inhibitors of IGF2BP3. Statistical analyses included Student’s t tests, one-way analysis of variance, Kaplan‒Meier survival analysis, and correlation analyses.


## Results
  IGF2BP3 was specifically overexpressed in epithelial tumor cells, exhibited progressively increasing expression during esophageal squamous cell carcinoma progression, and correlated with poor clinical outcomes. IGF2BP3 promoted tumor growth by activating Wnt signaling through the stabilization of β-catenin. Mechanistically, IGF2BP3 increased the translation of the deubiquitinase USP36 without altering the stability of its messenger RNA, leading to the USP36-mediated deubiquitination and stabilization of β-catenin. Activation of β-catenin signaling increased the transcription and secretion of transforming growth factor beta, which promoted the polarization of macrophages toward the immunosuppressive M2 phenotype. Pharmacological inhibition of IGF2BP3 with digoxin disrupted the IGF2BP3–USP36–β-catenin axis, reduced transforming growth factor beta production, suppressed tumor growth, and increased the efficacy of paclitaxel and PD-1 blockade in preclinical models.


## Conclusions
  This study identifies IGF2BP3 as a critical posttranscriptional regulator that links oncogenic signaling to immune suppression in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma. Targeting IGF2BP3 represents a promising therapeutic strategy to remodel the tumor microenvironment and improve responses to chemotherapy and immunotherapy.

## Background
  Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) remains a major global health burden, especially in East Asian regions where its incidence and mortality rates are extremely high [1–3]. ESCC is characterized by aggressive progression, frequent late-stage diagnosis, and a 5-year survival rate of less than 20%, reflecting the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies [4, 5]. Increasing evidence also indicates that the tumor microenvironment (TME), which consists of cancer cells, stromal components, immune infiltrates, extracellular matrix (ECM), and chemotactic cytokines, critically affects ESCC development and that its ability to restrain or promote tumor growth, invasion, and therapeutic resistance depends on its cellular and molecular configuration [6–13]. An immunosuppressive TME enriched in activated stromal cells, a dense ECM, and alternatively polarized immune cells potently facilitates tumor progression and undermines responses to both chemotherapy and immunotherapy in various cancers, including ESCC [14–16]. Therefore, elucidating the molecular regulators within ESCC cells that orchestrate the immunosuppressive TME is of major mechanistic and therapeutic significance.

  RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) have emerged as indispensable posttranscriptional regulators that affect RNA stability, splicing, localization, and translation, thereby influencing key oncogenic programs [17–20]. Beyond intrinsic tumor regulation, RBPs have recently been implicated in modulating TME composition, particularly through their effects on tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) [21–24]. Several RBPs promote M2 macrophage polarization, which is a protumorigenic macrophage phenotype associated with immunosuppression and metastasis [25–27]. Notably, insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA-binding protein 3 (IGF2BP3), an oncofetal protein aberrantly reactivated in numerous malignancies, plays a pivotal role in increasing tumor aggressiveness and remodeling the TME [24, 28–31]. However, how IGF2BP3 contributes to ESCC progression, especially its ability to induce or sustain an immunosuppressive TME, remains largely unknown.

  The Wnt/β-catenin pathway is a well-established oncogenic driver in ESCC that promotes tumor stemness, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance [32–36]. Although β-catenin regulation, particularly at the levels of protein stability and pathway activation, has been extensively characterized [37], emerging evidence suggests that RBPs also contribute to β-catenin regulation through posttranscriptional mechanisms. IGF2BPs are a family of oncofetal RBPs that regulate the stability and translation efficiency of specific target mRNAs [38]. Notably, IGF2BP3 has been reported to stabilize CTNNB1 mRNA in other diseases [39]; however, whether IGF2BP3 regulates β-catenin expression through posttranscriptional mechanisms in ESCC and contributes to Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation remain unclear.

  In this study, we revealed a previously unrecognized regulatory axis in which IGF2BP3 promotes the USP36-mediated deubiquitination of β-catenin, stabilizing the protein and activating Wnt signaling. This activation increases TGF-β expression and secretion, promoting M2 macrophage polarization in the context of ESCC. Using multiomics approaches, in vitro experiments, and preclinical models, we elucidate the underlying mechanism of this axis and validate its value as a therapeutic target using digoxin, an FDA-approved cardiac glycoside that is a direct inhibitor of IGF2BP3. Collectively, these findings highlight IGF2BP3 as a crucial nexus linking posttranscriptional regulation, Wnt signaling, TME remodeling, and clinical outcomes in ESCC.


## Methods

### Cell lines
  The human ESCC cell line KYSE-150 (RRID: CVCL_1348), human embryonic kidney cell line HEK293T (RRID: CVCL_0063), and human monocytic leukemia cell line THP-1 (RRID: CVCL_0006) were obtained from Shanghai Fu-Heng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), and the mouse ESCC cell line mEC25 (RRID: CVCL_C6JH) was a kind gift from Prof. Li Fu [40]. mEC25 and HEK293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Corning, USA), whereas KYSE-150 and THP-1 cells were maintained in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)−1640 medium (Corning, USA). All the cell lines were cultured in medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; ExCell Bio, China) and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin (NAM Biotech, China) and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂. All the cell lines were authenticated by morphology and RRID reference and were confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination before use.


### RT‒PCR and RT‒qPCR
  Total RNA (1.0 µg) was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the lncRNA First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (TIANGEN, Beijing, China). Quantitative real-time PCR (RT‒qPCR) was conducted using a QuantStudioTM5 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Scientific, USA), and the target RNA levels were normalized to those of GAPDH. The primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 1.


### Sample preparation and generation of single-cell suspensions
  Fresh esophageal tissue samples, including two adjacent normal tissue samples and six ESCC samples, were dissected into anatomical layers (mucosa, muscularis, and adventitia), which were pathologically verified by certified pathologists. The tissues were rinsed three times with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove blood and necrotic debris. All the freshly collected samples were processed within 30 min after resection. The tissues were minced into small fragments and collected in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco), followed by gentle enzymatic dissociation using a human tumor dissociation kit (Miltenyi Biotec) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The resulting cell suspensions were sequentially filtered through 40-μm nylon mesh, treated with red blood cell lysis buffer (10 ×, Sigma‒Aldrich), and subjected to dead cell removal using a dead cell removal kit (Miltenyi Biotec). Single-cell suspensions from all the samples were used for Chromium Next GEM Single-Cell 3’ library construction (10 × Genomics) following the manufacturer’s protocol. All single-cell transcriptome libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Novogene Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) to generate 150-bp paired-end reads.


### scRNA-seq data processing and clustering
  Raw FASTQ files were processed using Cell Ranger (v7.1.0; 10 × Genomics) to remove low-quality reads (Q30 < 80%) and adapter sequences. Reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38-2020-A) to generate a gene–cell expression matrix. Only genes expressed in at least 0.1% of cells were retained for downstream analyses. To obtain a high-quality dataset, the percentages of reads derived from mitochondrial and heat shock-related RNAs were calculated for each cell. Cells were filtered, and only those with high-quality profiles, defined by mitochondrial read percentages < 20%, detected gene counts between 400 and 6,000, and unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) between 500 and 30,000 were retained. The Python package Scrublet was applied to estimate potential doublets in each sample, with an expected doublet rate of 5%. Normalization was performed using the NormalizeData function (log normalization method, scaling factor = 10,000), followed by the identification of 3,000 highly variable genes (HVGs) using the FindVariableFeatures function. Batch effects across samples were corrected using Harmony (v0.1.1), and the resulting data were scaled with Scale Data. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the HVGs, and the optimal number of principal components (PCs; 30) was determined on the basis of elbow plots and Jack Straw analysis. Cells were clustered using the Leiden algorithm (resolution = 1.0), which iteratively groups cells with similar transcriptional profiles. The resulting k-nearest neighbor (KNN) graph was visualized using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP). All clustering and visualization analyses were conducted using the Scanpy package (v1.8.1) in Python, following the procedures outlined in the Scanpy documentation.

  For subclustering and cell type annotation within each major cluster, we generated a standardized Normalize function from UMI count data using Seurat (v4.2.0, R package). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the FindAllMarkers function in Seurat with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. P values were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction, and genes with an adjusted p value of < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Only genes detected in at least 20% of cells within a given cluster and exhibiting an average log fold change ≥ 0.25 (after log transformation) between the cluster and all other cells were tested. Cluster identities were assigned on the basis of the top-ranked DEGs and previously reported biologically relevant marker genes. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed on the basis of the normalized expression profiles of the annotated subclusters, and immune infiltration analysis of the TCGA cohort was conducted using the CIBERSORT [41] algorithm.


### Trajectory analysis
  Trajectory analysis of epithelial cells was performed using Monocle3 (v1.0.0). Cells were preprocessed by PCA-based dimensionality reduction and clustering, followed by trajectory inference using the learn_graph function. Cells were ordered along the inferred pseudotime trajectory using the order_cells function, with normal epithelial cells selected as the root cells. This analysis enabled characterization of the inferred epithelial cell state transition associated with positive IGF2BP3 expression.


### Plasmid construction and cell transfection
  Short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting IGF2BP3 (sh-IGF2BP3#1 and sh-IGF2BP3#2) were designed, cloned, inserted into the pLKO.1-Hygro lentiviral vector (Tsingke Bio, Beijing, China), and introduced into cells via lentiviral transduction. An IGF2BP3 overexpression plasmid was constructed using the pLVX vector (Geneseed Bio, Guangzhou, China). In addition, shRNAs targeting CTNNB1 and USP36 (sh-CTNNB1#1, sh-CTNNB1#2, sh-USP36#1, and sh-USP36#2) were synthesized by Tsingke Bio. Constructs for USP36, CTNNB1, and TGFB1 overexpression were also synthesized by Tsingke Bio. A catalytically inactive USP36C131A mutant plasmid was generated, along with truncation mutants encoding USP36 protein fragments containing the USP domain, central domain, and CTD; β-catenin (CTNNB1) protein fragments containing the NTD, Armadillo repeats, and CTD; and IGF2BP3 protein fragments containing RRM1-2, KH1-4, KH1-2, and KH3-4 domains (all from Tsingke Bio, Beijing, China). The USP36-dependent luciferase reporter plasmid was also synthesized by Tsingke Bio (Beijing, China). TOP Flash and FOP Flash reporter plasmids were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). The sequences of all shRNAs are provided in Supplementary Table 2.


### Western blotting
  Protein samples (20 μg) were separated on 6–15% sodium dodecyl sulfate‒polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‒PAGE) gels and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Protein signals were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) system (Tanon 5200, Tanon, Shanghai, China). Detailed information about the antibodies used is provided in Supplementary Table 3.


### Mouse and xenograft models
  Male BALB/c nude and C57BL/6 J mice (4–5 weeks old) were purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center (Shanghai, China). All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care Committee of the Second Hospital of Shandong University (approval number: KYLL2024850) and conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (GB/T35892-2018; Standardization Administration of China). For tumor xenograft studies, male C57BL/6 J mice were acclimatized for one week after they were purchased before tumor inoculation. Then, 5 × 10⁶ mEC25 cells suspended in 100 μL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were subcutaneously injected into the right axillary region of the mice. The tumor volume was calculated using the following formula: volume (mm3) = (length × width2)/2. At the experimental endpoints, the tumors were collected, weighed, and embedded in paraffin for immunohistochemical staining. Furthermore, protein and RNA were extracted for Western blotting and qPCR.


### Construction of the ESCC mouse model
  Five-week-old male C57BL/6 J mice (purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Co., China) were maintained under controlled conditions (23 ± 1 °C, 50 ± 10% humidity, 12-h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to food and water. 4NQO (100 μg/mL) was administered in the drinking water for 20 weeks to induce esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC). The 4NQO solution was freshly prepared and replaced weekly. The mice were then given normal drinking water for 12 additional weeks to allow tumor growth. At the study endpoint, the mice were euthanized, and esophageal tissues were collected for histopathological and molecular analyses.


### Transgenic mice
  The genetically modified mouse strains Mrc1-2A-CreERT2 (C57BL/6Smoc-Mrc1em(2A−CreERT2) Smoc, cat: NM-KI-220031) and R26-LSL-DTR (C57BL/6-Gt(ROSA)26S or tm1(HBEGF)Awai/J, cat: NMX-KI-192015) were obtained from the Shanghai Model Organisms Center (Shanghai, China). To generate transgenic mice with the R26-LSL-DTR and Mrc1-2A-CreERT2 alleles, genotypes were confirmed using PCR according to the instructions of a genetic mouse tail kit (D7283S; Beyotime, China). The PCR primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 4. R26-LSL-DTR mice were mated with Mrc1-2A-CreERT2 mice to produce offspring carrying both alleles. Pregnant females were monitored, and after birth, the pups were genotyped to confirm the presence of CreERT2 and LSL-DTR. Tamoxifen was administered at 6–8 weeks of age to activate CreERT2 and induce DTR expression. Phenotypic changes related to DTR expression were monitored using flow cytometry or immunohistochemistry. The colony was expanded, and the transgenic line was preserved for future studies.


### Tissue collection and preparation
  A total of 101 formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor samples and adjacent noncancerous esophageal tissues (at least 5 cm from the tumor margin) were collected from patients who underwent esophagectomy at the Second Hospital of Shandong University between January 2015 and August 2017. Patients were followed up from January 2017 to August 2020. Histopathological assessments were conducted by two independent pathologists based on the 8th edition of the UICC TNM classification. The inclusion criteria included histological confirmation of ESCC, curative R0 resection, and no prior anticancer treatments before tissue collection.


### Statistical analysis
  Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA) unless otherwise specified. Numerical variables were first evaluated for normality and variance homogeneity. For data that were approximately normally distributed with comparable variances, comparisons between two groups were made using a two-tailed Student’s t test, and comparisons among three or more groups were performed by one-way ANOVA. When variances were unequal, Welch’s t test (two groups) or Welch’s one-way ANOVA (multiple groups) was applied. For data that deviated from normality, nonparametric methods were used (the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for two-group comparisons and the Kruskal–Wallis test for comparisons across three or more groups). Multigroup comparisons were followed by appropriate post hoc tests as needed. Linear associations between continuous variables were assessed by Pearson’s correlation. Survival probabilities were estimated using Kaplan‒Meier curves, and differences between groups were analyzed with the log-rank test; multivariable survival analyses were performed using Cox proportional hazards regression to estimate hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals. Dose‒response curves and IC₅₀ values were derived by fitting log-logistic models. Data are reported as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments unless otherwise stated. All the statistical tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.


## Results

### IGF2BP3 is specifically expressed in esophageal epithelial cells and progressively upregulated in advanced ESCC
  To investigate the cellular alterations associated with ESCC development, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on six ESCC tissues and two adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 1a), which were stratified by anatomical layers (submucosal, muscular, and adventitial). Clustering analyses revealed distinct populations, including epithelial cells, myeloid cells, NK cells, B cells, T cells, endothelial cells, and fibroblasts, on the basis of the expression of related marker genes (Fig. 1b; S1a) by uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) and t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) [42]. We also integrated five additional cohorts of published ESCC scRNA-seq datasets encompassing a total of 699,731 cells [14, 43–46]. These independent datasets exhibited highly similar cellular architectures (Fig. 1c; Fig. S1b, c) with consistent marker-based cell-type annotations [42] (Fig. S1d–g). Notably, Gene Ontology (GO) biological process analysis revealed that RNA editing-related pathways were significantly enriched in tumor cells compared with normal cells (Fig. S1h). Given that the dysregulation of RBPs has been widely implicated in RNA editing during cancer progression in our previous work and other studies [17, 24, 47, 48], we next screened for dysregulated RBPs by integrating our scRNA-seq profiles with curated RBP gene lists. Notably, IGF2BP3 emerged as the only RBP consistently and specifically upregulated in tumor cells compared with that in the normal esophageal epithelium (Fig. 1d). In all the datasets, IGF2BP3 was predominantly expressed in epithelial cells and rarely expressed in stromal cells (endothelial cells and fibroblasts) or immune cells (Fig. S2a–d). Multiplex immunofluorescence staining confirmed that the IGF2BP3 protein was localized mainly in tumor epithelial compartments (Fig. 1e). At the cohort level, both the TCGA ESCC dataset and a large tissue microarray from our institution confirmed that IGF2BP3 was overexpressed in ESCC tumor tissues and associated with poor overall survival (Fig. 1f–i; Fig. S2e). High IGF2BP3 expression was consistently observed in 4NQO-induced ESCC model mice (Fig. S2f–i), and cross-species analysis revealed strong evolutionary conservation of IGF2BP3 (Fig. S2j). Reclustering of cancer cells across different ESCC pathological stages revealed that IGF2BP3 expression gradually increased with tumor invasion into the muscular layer and adventitia (Fig. 1j, k; Fig. S3a–c). IGF2BP3-positive cells (log-normalized counts > 1) were significantly enriched in patients with adventitial invasion, accounting for more than half of all malignant cells expressing IGF2BP3 (Fig. 1l). Monocle analysis [49] revealed that IGF2BP3-positive cancer cells exhibited a prominent dedifferentiated phenotype (Fig. 1m), indicating a positive correlation with high IGF2BP3 expression. Furthermore, infer-CNV analysis [50] revealed stepwise increases in CNV scores with the stages of ESCC progression (Fig. S3d–g), which strongly correlated with IGF2BP3 expression. ### IGF2BP3 Knockdown Inhibits ESCC Progression by Suppressing M2 Macrophage Infiltration
  To assess the functional role of IGF2BP3 in ESCC, we established KYSE150 and mEC25 cell lines with stable knockdown of IGF2BP3 (Fig. S4a, b). The results of xenograft experiments revealed that IGF2BP3 depletion markedly suppressed tumor growth, and this antitumor effect was notably stronger in immunocompetent C57BL/6 J mice than in immunodeficient BALB/c nude mice (Figs. 2a–c; S4c, d), suggesting that the TME, especially T cells, plays a potential role. Furthermore, TME analysis with CIBERSORT revealed that IGF2BP3 expression was significantly positively correlated with M2 macrophage infiltration (R = 0.29; P = 0.012) and negatively correlated with CD8 + T-cell infiltration (Fig. S4e–g). However, unlike a previous report showing that IGF2BP3 modulates CD8 + T-cell infiltration via PD-L1 [51], we did not detect an effect of IGF2BP3 on PD-L1 expression in ESCC cells (Fig. S4h). These findings suggest that IGF2BP3 may affect the immune microenvironment through M2 macrophages rather than directly via the PD-L1/CD8 + T-cell axis. Multiplex immunofluorescence staining confirmed that high IGF2BP3 expression was accompanied by increased accumulation of CD206⁺ tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) in ESCC tissues, with obvious spatial overlap between IGF2BP3-high regions and CD68⁺CD206⁺ M2-type macrophages (Fig. 2d, e; Fig. S4i). Flow cytometric analysis confirmed that IGF2BP3 knockdown significantly reduced M2 macrophage infiltration in xenograft tumors from C57BL/6 J mice (Fig. 2f, g; Fig. S5a). In line with these findings, immunohistochemical staining revealed a reduced proportion of M2-type macrophages among F4/80 + TAMs upon IGF2BP3 silencing (Fig. S5b–d), and RT‒qPCR revealed decreased expression of Cd206 and Cd163 in tumor tissues (Fig. S5e, f). Using a Tet-on inducible knockdown system, we verified that IGF2BP3 knockdown suppressed M2 macrophage and CD8 + T-cell infiltration and tumor growth in vivo (Fig. 2h–j and Fig. S6a–e) and cell growth in vitro (Fig. S7a, b). To determine whether M2 TAMs functionally mediate the tumor acceleration effect of IGF2BP3, we used iDTR and Cd206-Cre mouse models to specifically deplete CD206 + M2 macrophages (Fig. S8a–e). While exogenous IGF2BP3 overexpression markedly accelerated tumor growth, ablation of CD206 + M2 TAMs significantly blunted this acceleration in Cd206-DTR xenograft models (Fig. 2k–m). In vitro coculture assays supported that IGF2BP3 promoted M2 macrophage polarization (Fig. S9a–e). Moreover, IGF2BP3 knockdown impaired the anchorage-independent growth, migration, and invasion of ESCC cells (Fig. S9f–i). ### IGF2BP3 deficiency in cancer cells suppresses Wnt signaling via the downregulation of β-catenin
  Given that IGF2BP3 depletion reduced M2 macrophage infiltration and impaired tumor cell malignancy, we next explored whether IGF2BP3 drives ESCC progression by regulating key intracellular signaling pathways in tumor cells. We first screened major signaling pathways known to be critical for tumor progression [52] and found that increased Wnt signaling is tightly linked to tumor progression on the basis of the scRNA-seq data (Fig. 3a); GSEA of the scRNA (Fig. 3b) and TCGA data (Fig. S10a) confirmed that high expression of IGF2BP3 activates Wnt pathway activity. IGF2BP3 expression was positively correlated with the expression of the Wnt pathway target genes CCND1 and MYC in ESCC cancer cells according to the scRNA data (Fig. S10b, c), and the results further revealed prominent colocalization of β-catenin and IGF2BP3 in individual tumor cells (Fig. S10d). Using a TCF/LEF luciferase reporter assay, we demonstrated that IGF2BP3 significantly increased Wnt pathway activity in ESCC cells (Fig. 3c, d) but did not regulate IGF2BP1/2 expression (Fig. S11a), and individual knockdown of IGF2BP1 or IGF2BP2 failed to substantially suppress Wnt pathway activity (Fig. S11b, c). This finding is consistent with the results of the KEGG analysis shown in Fig. 3b. Importantly, western blotting and RT‒qPCR revealed that IGF2BP3 increased the expression of the β-catenin protein and the downstream effectors CCND1 and MYC (Fig. 3e, f; Fig. S11d–f). Moreover, exogenous CTNNB1 expression partially rescued the decreases in MYC and CCND1 expression (Fig. 3g) and cancer cell proliferation, invasion and M2 macrophage polarization caused by IGF2BP3 knockdown (Fig. S11g–k). High expression of β-catenin was detected in 4NQO-induced ESCC in mouse models (Fig. 3h, i) and in human ESCC tissue (Fig. 3j). β-Catenin expression was positively correlated with IGF2BP3 expression in clinical ESCC tissue samples (Fig. 3k). ### IGF2BP3 regulates β-catenin protein stability via USP36-mediated deubiquitination
  Interestingly, IGF2BP3 did not affect CTNNB1 mRNA levels in ESCC cells (Fig. 4a; Fig. S12a). However, chase assays with cycloheximide (CHX), a new protein synthesis inhibitor, revealed that IGF2BP3 depletion markedly accelerated β-catenin protein degradation (Fig. 4b). Consistent with these findings, MG132 treatment blocked the downregulation of β-catenin protein expression upon IGF2BP3 silencing in ESCC cells (Fig. S12b), and co-IP assays demonstrated increased β-catenin ubiquitination upon IGF2BP3 silencing (Fig. 4c). These findings suggest a posttranscriptional regulatory mechanism distinct from the canonical role of RBPs in stabilizing target mRNAs to promote their translation [53]. To identify the specific mediator responsible for β‑catenin protein regulation, we integrated ESCC proteomic datasets [54] with IGF2BP3 CLIP‑seq profiles and screened for candidate deubiquitinases (DUBs) associated with IGF2BP3. Among these candidates, only USP36 strongly upregulated β‑catenin protein expression (Fig. 4d; Fig. S12c, d). Ectopic USP36 overexpression attenuated the IGF2BP3 knockdown-induced ubiquitination of β-catenin (Fig. 4e), clearly reversed the reduction in β-catenin expression (Fig. S12e) and suppressed the tumor cell malignancy and impaired M2 macrophage polarization caused by IGF2BP3 depletion (Fig. S13a–e). Moreover, USP36 protein levels were elevated in both human ESCC tissues and 4NQO‑induced ESCC lesions in mice (Fig. S13f, g), USP36 and IGF2BP3 were coexpressed in the same cell populations in the scRNA-seq datasets (Fig. S13h), and USP36 expression was positively correlated with IGF2BP3 and β-catenin expression in ESCC tissue (Fig. S13i, j, k). ### USP36 directly interacts with β-catenin through its USP and CTD domains to prevent its degradation
  Following the identification of the IGF2BP3-regulated USP36/β-catenin axis, we explored the underlying mechanism through which USP36 modulates β-catenin. First, USP36 knockdown decreased the protein level but not the mRNA level of β-catenin (Fig. S14a, b). Cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays confirmed that USP36 depletion accelerated β-catenin turnover (Fig. S14c), whereas MG132 treatment failed to fully restore β-catenin protein levels in USP36-deficient cells (Fig. S14d). Co-IP analyses demonstrated that USP36 could interact with β-catenin (Fig. S14e, f). Ubiquitination assays revealed that USP36 preferentially reduced K48-linked polyubiquitination of β-catenin (Fig. 4f). Notably, the catalytically inactive USP36C131A mutant [55] failed to increase β-catenin protein levels or inhibit its ubiquitination (Fig. S14g–h), indicating that the deubiquitinase activity of USP36 is essential for β-catenin stabilization. Domain mapping revealed that the USP and CTD domains of USP36 were indispensable for binding to β-catenin, whereas β-catenin required its Armadillo repeats to mediate this interaction (Fig. 4g–j). Additionally, the U1 domain of USP36 was critical for mediating β-catenin deubiquitination (Fig. S14i). Structural predictions using AlphaFold2 further supported the direct binding between the USP/CTD domains of USP36 and β-catenin (Fig. S14j). Furthermore, we confirmed that the USP domain of USP36 is involved in β-catenin deubiquitination, whereas the CTD domain is involved in regulating the nucleocytoplasmic distribution of β-catenin (Fig. S14k), a finding consistent with the dual function characteristics of the USP protein family reported in previous studies [56]. Collectively, these results establish that USP36 directly interacts with β-catenin and stabilizes it through deubiquitination, which is mediated by its specific functional domains.


### IGF2BP3 promotes USP36 translation through the KH3-4 domain and drives β-catenin activation and M2 macrophage polarization in vivo
  To further investigate the mechanism by which IGF2BP3 regulates USP36 in ESCC cells, CLIP-seq analyses (PAR-CLIP) [57] were performed, and the results suggested that IGF2BP3 has the potential to bind to USP36 mRNA. IGF2BP3 knockdown inhibited USP36 protein expression without altering its mRNA level (Fig. 5a, b; Fig. S15a, b), indicating that IGF2BP3 regulates USP36 posttranscriptionally. Notably, the results after MG132 treatment and from the CHX chase assays did not support a role for protein degradation upon USP36 downregulation following IGF2BP3 knockdown (Fig. S15c–e), suggesting that IGF2BP3 promotes USP36 translation. Knockdown of IGF2BP1 or IGF2BP2 did not affect USP36 protein expression levels (Fig. S15f, g). A USP36 mRNA-dependent luciferase reporter confirmed the IGF2BP3-mediated translational activation of USP36 (Fig. 5c; Fig. S15h, i) and that IGF2BP3 binds to the USP36 CDS region in an m6A-dependent manner (Fig. S15j). Furthermore, SPR (Fig. S15k, l) verified the m6A interaction. Polysome profiling demonstrated that IGF2BP3 knockdown reduced the incorporation of USP36 mRNA into translational complexes (Fig. 5d), supporting the role of IGF2BP3 in promoting USP36 translation. RIP experiments verified that IGF2BP3 binds to USP36 mRNA through its KH3-4 domain (Fig. 5e–g). Additionally, exogenous overexpression of USP36 reversed the downregulation of the Wnt pathway target genes MYC and CCND1 caused by the silencing of IGF2BP3 (Fig. S15m). Collectively, these results suggest that IGF2BP3 promotes USP36 translation by directly binding to the CDS region of USP36 mRNA. Moreover, in vivo xenograft experiments revealed that IGF2BP3 knockdown significantly inhibited tumor growth, whereas overexpression of USP36 or β-catenin restored tumor proliferation (Fig. 5h–j). Immunohistochemical staining of xenograft tumor tissues from C57BL/6 J mice confirmed decreased expression of IGF2BP3, USP36, and β-catenin and reduced proportions of CD206 + M2 macrophages in the IGF2BP3 knockdown group; notably, these changes were reversed by USP36 or β-catenin overexpression (Fig. S16a–e). ### IGF2BP3 induces M2 macrophage polarization by increasing TGF-β transcription and secretion
  M2 macrophage polarization is driven by a panel of distinct cytokines, including IL-4, IL-13, IL-10, and TGF-β [58], and contributes to immunosuppression and TME remodeling [59, 60]. Given that our previous findings demonstrated that IGF2BP3 promotes M2 macrophage polarization via the USP36/β-catenin axis, we then identified the key downstream cytokines that mediate this effect through single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analyses. These analyses revealed minimal expression of CSF1 or IL-10 in ESCC epithelial cells, whereas the expression of TGF-β was strongly positively correlated with the expression of IGF2BP3, β-catenin, and USP36 (Fig. S17a–c). We performed RT‒qPCR and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs), which revealed that the IGF2BP3/USP36/β-catenin axis mediated TGF-β mRNA expression and protein secretion in ESCC cells (Fig. S17d–g) and in vivo (Fig. S17h–j).

  Furthermore, we determined that IGF2BP3/β-catenin activated TGF-β transcription (Fig. S18a-b), and the application of a β-catenin inhibitor indicated that TGF-β transcription was regulated by IGF2BP3 (Fig. S18c-d); notably, these findings are consistent with the regulatory mechanism by which β-catenin promotes the transcription of TGF-β in the heart [61]. The results of functional rescue assays confirmed that exogenous overexpression or inhibition of TGF-β not only reversed the impaired malignancy of IGF2BP3-deficient ESCC cells (Fig. S18e–g) but also restored M2 macrophage polarization in in vitro coculture systems (Fig. S18h, i). In addition, individual knockdown of CTNNB1 or USP36 markedly reduced TGFβ1 expression (Fig. S19a-b), suppressed Wnt pathway activity (Fig. S19c-d), and impaired the M2 polarization of THP-1-derived macrophages (Fig. S19e-f). Collectively, these results identify TGF-β as a key downstream effector of the IGF2BP3/USP36/β-catenin axis, which mediates the ability of IGF2BP3 to drive M2 macrophage activation in the context of ESCC.


### Digoxin directly targets IGF2BP3 and increases sensitivity to PD-1 blockade
  Given the lack of available IGF2BP3 inhibitors, we conducted a virtual screening of 1610 FDA-approved compounds from the ZINC15 database and identified digoxin as a candidate IGF2BP3-binding molecule (Fig. 6a). Biolayer interferometry confirmed direct binding between digoxin and IGF2BP3 (Fig. 6b; Fig. S20a), and molecular docking analyses predicted stable interactions mediated by eight key residues (Fig. 6c). Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations demonstrated stable ligand‒protein binding and reduced structural fluctuations (Fig. S20b–g). An ESCC organoid model and an in vivo C57BL/6 mouse xenograft tumor-bearing model confirmed the potential therapeutic effect of digoxin on ESCC (Fig. S21a–e). Digoxin treatment reduced cell viability in a dose-dependent manner, as compared with control cells, cells with IGF2BP3 knockdown or knockout exhibited decreased sensitivity (Fig. 6d, Fig. S21f, g). Functionally, digoxin inhibited ESCC cell proliferation, suppressed the IGF2BP3/USP36/β-catenin axis in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 6e) and promoted IGF2BP3 protein degradation (Fig. S21h). Furthermore, a more pronounced inhibitory effect was observed in mEC25 xenograft models with IGF2BP3 overexpression (Fig. S22a–c), and no obvious cardiotoxicity detected (Fig. S22d, e). Since M2 TAMs have been associated with reduced immunotherapy efficacy [62, 63], we examined the effect of IGF2BP3 on the response to PD-1 blockade. Anti-PD-1 antibody treatment alone slowed tumor growth, and IGF2BP3 knockdown significantly increased the antitumor efficacy of PD-1 blockade (Fig. 6f–h). Importantly, the therapeutic efficacy of the combination of digoxin with paclitaxel or PD-1 blockade was superior to that of each monotherapy (Fig. 6i-n). ## Discussion
  The dysregulation of RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) has increasingly been recognized as a fundamental driver of tumor progression, immune evasion, and therapeutic resistance, largely through their ability to orchestrate posttranscriptional regulatory programs [21–24, 47, 64, 65]. Although the intracellular functions of RBPs, including binding to IGF2BPs, have been studied, their contributions to shaping the TME remain insufficiently characterized. Here, we identify IGF2BP3 as a central posttranscriptional regulator that integrates the intrinsic malignant behavior of cancer cells with extrinsic immune suppression in the context of ESCC (Fig. 7). We revealed an IGF2BP3/USP36/β-catenin axis that promotes tumor progression and the polarization of TAMs toward the M2 phenotype mediated by TGF-β. Notably, this mechanism may compensate for the scarcity of canonical genetic Wnt pathway activation in ESCC, underscoring IGF2BP3 as a previously unrecognized yet promising therapeutic target.Fig. 7Schematic illustration of the mechanism by which IGF2BP3 promotes ESCC progression and M2 macrophage infiltration. IGF2BP3 increases the translation of USP36, leading to the deubiquitination and stabilization of β-catenin. Activated β-catenin is translocated into the nucleus and binds to the LEF1-TCF1 complex, thereby upregulating downstream target genes such as Myc, Cyclin D1, and TGFB1. Increased secretion of TGF-β from ESCC cells promotes M2 macrophage polarization, facilitating tumor progression. Digoxin disrupts the IGF2BP3USP36-β-catenin axis and increases the efficacy of anti-PD-1 antibody therapy, resulting in tumor regression in vivo

  During tumor evolution, the TME undergoes a transition from an immunoinflammatory state to an immunosuppressive state, which is paralleled by shifts in cellular composition and transcriptional programs [66–68]. Through a comprehensive analysis of scRNA-seq data, we revealed that IGF2BP3, which is predominantly expressed in epithelial cancer cells, is significantly upregulated during the malignant progression of ESCC. Despite increasing evidence supporting the oncogenic roles of IGF2BP3 in regulating mRNA stability, localization, and translation [24, 28, 29, 31, 51, 69, 70], its function in ESCC pathogenesis and TME remodeling remains largely unexplored. Our study extends these findings by revealing the crosstalk between IGF2BP3-expressing cancer cells and macrophages. Furthermore, IGF2BP3 is the most prominently overexpressed member of the IGF2BP family in ESCC cells, as confirmed in clinical tissue microarrays and 4NQO-induced ESCC mouse models. These findings firmly establish that IGF2BP3 is a key regulator of ESCC progression.

  Mechanistically, we demonstrated that IGF2BP3 increases the efficiency of USP36 mRNA translation, thereby increasing USP36 protein abundance. USP36 in turn stabilizes β-catenin by catalyzing K48-linked polydeubiquitination, leading to sustained Wnt signaling and increased cancer cell proliferation and invasion. We also revealed that IGF2BP3 regulates USP36 translation in an m6A-dependent manner. Importantly, this regulatory mode differs from the canonical function of IGF2BP3 in modulating mRNA stability, thus expanding the functional landscape of IGF2BP3 and supporting its bifunctional role in controlling both RNA stability and translation [48, 71]. Moreover, the identification of USP36 as a critical β-catenin deubiquitinase in ESCC implicates the USP36/β-catenin axis as a potential therapeutic target in tumors with high IGF2BP3 expression.

  We further elucidated the immunologic consequences of this regulatory axis by demonstrating that IGF2BP3 promotes an immunosuppressive TME through TGF-β-dependent M2 TAM polarization. TGF-β is a well-established driver of immune tolerance and M2 macrophage differentiation [72]. Our data suggest that IGF2BP3 orchestrates the polarization of M2 macrophages by increasing TGF-β expression and secretion in tumor cells. Both in vitro coculture experiments and in vivo mouse models demonstrated that IGF2BP3 knockdown mitigates TGF-β expression and secretion, leading to reduced M2 macrophage infiltration and decelerating tumor growth. The restoration of these effects by the overexpression of USP36 or β-catenin validated the central role of the IGF2BP3/USP36/β-catenin axis in TME regulation. These findings align with recent data linking β-catenin signaling to immune evasion and suggest that the posttranscriptional regulation of β-catenin by IGF2BP3 may compensate for the scarcity of genetic Wnt pathway activation in ESCC.

  Clinically, the elevated IGF2BP3 expression observed in ESCC samples correlates with poor prognosis and reduced responses to neoadjuvant chemotherapy and PD-1 blockade. Given previous evidence that M2 TAMs mediate therapeutic tolerance by remodeling the TME [73, 74], targeting IGF2BP3 may resensitize tumors to conventional and immunotherapeutic regimens. Through virtual drug screening, we identified digoxin, which is a widely used FDA-approved cardiac glycoside, as a potent direct IGF2BP3 inhibitor. Biolayer interferometry confirmed that digoxin binds to IGF2BP3 and alters downstream USP36 and β-catenin expression. In ESCC xenografts, digoxin suppressed tumor growth, reduced M2 TAM infiltration, and increased the efficacy of paclitaxel and PD-1 blockade. Although cardiac glycosides have been historically associated with antitumor activity [75, 76], this study is the first to implicate IGF2BP3 as a direct target in solid tumors. Importantly, antitumor effects were achieved at sub-cardiotoxic concentrations [77]. Additional pharmacogenomic studies will be essential to address interindividual variations in cardiac glycoside sensitivity.

  Although this study focused on ESCC, the regulatory mechanisms identified here may extend to other malignancies characterized by high IGF2BP3 expression. The capacity of IGF2BP3 to integrate oncogenic signaling with immunosuppressive remodeling positions it as a master regulator of the TME. Future work leveraging proteomic, transcriptomic, and epitranscriptomic profiling will be instrumental in identifying additional IGF2BP3 targets and clarifying its broader impact on immunotherapy resistance across cancer types.


## Conclusion
  In conclusion, this study identifies IGF2BP3 as a key posttranscriptional regulator that links oncogenic signaling with immune suppression in ESCC. By increasing USP36 translation, IGF2BP3 stabilizes β-catenin and sustains Wnt signaling, which in turn promotes transforming growth factor beta–dependent M2 macrophage polarization and tumor progression. This mechanism provides a nongenetic explanation for aberrant Wnt activation and highlights the importance of RNA binding proteins in tumor–immune crosstalk. Importantly, pharmacological inhibition of IGF2BP3 with digoxin suppressed tumor growth and increased the response to chemotherapy and PD-1 blockade, indicating that IGF2BP3 is a promising therapeutic target for overcoming immune evasion and treatment resistance in ESCC.
