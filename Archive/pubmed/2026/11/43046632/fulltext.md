# Exploring the role of HNF1B in the pathogenesis of autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD): an integrative bioinformatics study


## Objective
  Hepatocyte nuclear factor 1 beta (HNF1β) is crucial for renal tubule development, and its mutation causes renal cysts. However, its role in autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) remains unclear. This study explored the mechanism of hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-beta gene (HNF1B) in ADPKD using integrative bioinformatics analysis.


## Methods
  HNF1B expression was evaluated in ADPKD transcriptome datasets and validated via immunofluorescence in a 3D Madin-Darby canine kidney cell cyst model cultured over a 12-day timeframe (analyzed on days 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12). Based on HNF1B median expression, ADPKD tissues were divided into high- and low-expression groups for differential and enrichment analyses. Upstream transcription factors and binding sites were predicted using UCSC and JASPAR databases. Finally, cMAP and L1000FWD were used to screen therapeutic small molecules targeting HNF1β, followed by molecular docking.


## Results
  HNF1B was significantly downregulated in ADPKD tissues (p = 0.017) and correlated with cyst progression (p < 0.001), which was consistent with the 3D model findings (p < 0.001). Low HNF1B expression was associated with transcriptomic signatures reflecting activation of the wingless-related integration site (Wnt) and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) pathways and inhibition of the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) pathway. POU3F3, HNF4A, and TFAP2B were identified as potential upstream transcription factors. Mocetinostat (binding energy = −8.2 kcal/mol) and wortmannin (binding energy = −7.7 kcal/mol) showed stable binding to HNF1β.


## Conclusion
  HNF1B expression is significantly negatively correlated with cyst progression, suggesting a potential protective role in ADPKD, and its downregulation associates with pathways driving cyst progression.


## Introduction
  Polycystic kidney disease is a group of monogenic disorders mainly comprising autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) and autosomal recessive polycystic kidney disease (ARPKD). ADPKD is predominantly diagnosed in adult patients, whereas ARPKD is relatively rare and typically detected in the perinatal or infantile period [1]. ADPKD is primarily caused by mutations in polycystic kidney disease 1 (PKD1) and polycystic kidney disease 2 (PKD2), which encode polycystin-1 and polycystin-2, respectively [2]. The disease is pathologically characterized by progressively enlarging epithelial-lined cysts in both kidneys. These cysts mostly originate from abnormally proliferating renal tubular epithelial cells and are often accompanied by inflammation and interstitial fibrosis, eventually leading to end-stage renal disease [3]. The sustained expansion of ADPKD cysts is associated with activation of the cAMP signaling pathway, which promotes proliferation of cyst-lining epithelial cells and activates the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) [4,5]. Currently, specific interventions for ADPKD are lacking in clinical practice. Although tolvaptan, the only drug approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration and the European Medicines Agency, shows certain efficacy in slowing renal function progression, it neither reverses the disease nor provides a specific cure [6]. Therefore, further exploration of the pathogenesis of ADPKD and identification of more specific and safer therapeutic targets are urgently needed.

  Hepatocyte nuclear factor 1 beta (HNF1β), encoded by the hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-beta gene (HNF1B), is a master transcription factor highly expressed in renal tubular epithelial cells and serves a pivotal role in nephron differentiation and tubulogenesis [7]. In experimental models, conditional inactivation of Hnf1b in mouse renal tubules leads to tubular dysmorphogenesis and severe cystic kidney disease, establishing its physiological role in maintaining tubular integrity [8].

  Clinically, germline HNF1B pathogenic variants cause HNF1B-associated kidney disease (a distinct entity often classified under autosomal dominant tubulointerstitial kidney disease, ADTKD-HNF1B), characterized by congenital anomalies, renal hypoplasia, and multicystic dysplasia [9]. Although HNF1B nephropathy is a distinct genetic etiology, its clinical features—such as multiple renal cysts, enlarged kidneys, and progressive renal dysfunction—frequently overlap with ADPKD, posing diagnostic challenges in clinical practice [10]. Direct evidence linking HNF1B to ADPKD stems primarily from human genetic modifier studies; specifically, Bergmann et al. 2018 demonstrated that patients harboring co-inherited HNF1B and PKD1 mutations present with significantly accelerated cyst growth and early-onset disease, indicating a genetic modifying effect [11]. However, whether HNF1B expression or downstream pathway alterations directly participate in typical ADPKD pathogenesis remains insufficiently defined. Therefore, this study systematically explored the functional role and mechanistic network of HNF1B in the pathogenesis of ADPKD. Specifically, bulk and single-nucleus RNA sequencing datasets were mined to quantify HNF1B dysregulation across cyst progression. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) and co-expression network analysis were leveraged to decipher HNF1β-associated signaling alterations. Finally, structure-based molecular docking was employed to identify high-affinity small-molecule compounds targeting HNF1β, providing novel potential therapeutic candidates for ADPKD.


## Materials and methods

### Cell culture
  The MDCK (NBL-2) cells used in this study were purchased from Wuhan Zishan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China; Cat. No. STCC80001). The complete culture medium for MDCK cells consisted of DMEM-F12 (Gibco, Cat. No. C11330500BT) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Hyclone, Cat. No. SH30406.05) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution (Biosharp, Cat. No. BL505A). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Cells were generally passaged at a 1:2 ratio into new dishes, and the medium was replaced every 2 days.


### Cyst model construction
  MDCK cells, when cultured in a 3D extracellular matrix, serve as a widely established in vitro model for studying ADPKD cystogenesis due to their intrinsic capability to form polarized tubular cysts [12].

  MDCK cells were ensured to be in the logarithmic growth phase rather than fully confluent (approximately 60%–80% confluence). Cells were digested and resuspended in complete medium as described above. A 20 μL aliquot of the homogeneous cell suspension was transferred to a 1.5 mL tube, and cells were counted using a cell counter. The single-cell suspension was further diluted to 2 × 105 cells/mL for subsequent use.

  Type I collagen solution (Advanced Biomatrix, Cat. No. 5005) was retrieved from a 4 °C refrigerator and kept on ice to prevent solidification. The collagen matrix solution for one well of a 24-well plate was prepared by mixing 348 μL of type I collagen solution, 40 μL of MEM medium (10×) (Gibco, Cat. No. 11430030), 4 μL of HEPES (1 mol/L) (Procell, Cat. No. PB180325), and 6 μL of NaOH (1 mol/L) (Aladdin, Cat. No. S431779) using pre-chilled pipette tips. After adjusting the pH to 7.4, 2 μL of the MDCK single-cell suspension was added, mixed by pipetting, and then added to the 24-well plate. The plate was gently shaken to ensure complete coverage of the wells and uniform distribution of cells. The plate was incubated at 37 °C for 90 min.

  After collagen solidification, 1.5 mL of DMEM-F12 medium containing 10 μmol/L forskolin (TargetMol, Cat. No. T2939) was added to each well. The plate was returned to the 37 °C incubator, and the medium was replaced every 12 h. To systematically track the temporal dynamics of cyst expansion and HNF1β expression, a standardized longitudinal experimental timeline was established. MDCK cysts were monitored, harvested, and imaged at five predefined developmental time points—Days 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12—covering the sequential stages from early cyst initiation to progressive luminal expansion. Cyst images were captured under a Nikon Ti2-U inverted fluorescence microscope equipped with a 10× objective lens (Nikon Instruments Inc., Tokyo, Japan). Cyst diameters were measured (15 cysts randomly selected per group), and a cyst growth curve was plotted.


### Immunofluorescence staining
  Cysts were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, Cat. No. G1101), quenched with 50 mmol/L NH4Cl (Sinopharm, Cat. No. 10001518), permeabilised with 0.5% Triton X‑100 (Servicebio, Cat. No. G3068), and blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (Servicebio, Cat. No. GC305010). Primary antibody against HNF1β (1:500) (Abmart, Cat. No. T57236) was applied overnight at 4 °C, followed by fluorescent secondary antibody (1:500) (Abbkine, Cat. No. A23420) for 1 h at 37 °C in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Servicebio, Cat. No. G1012). Confocal optical sections of the 3D MDCK cysts were captured using a Nikon AXR inverted laser scanning confocal microscope (Nikon Instruments Inc., Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a 10× objective lens. Serial Z-stack images across the volumetric cysts were captured along the Z-axis at a Z-step size of 1.0 µm. The integrated optical density (IOD) of HNF1β and DAPI was measured with ImageJ, and the HNF1β/DAPI IOD ratio was calculated as a semi‑quantitative measure of HNF1β expression. Fifteen randomly selected cysts per group were analyzed.


### Data collection and preprocessing
  Bulk transcriptome datasets (GSE7869, GSE30528) and a single-nucleus transcriptome dataset (GSE185948) of ADPKD were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus database. GSE7869 contains 18 ADPKD cystic tissue samples (classified into small, medium, and large cysts as well as minimal cystic tissue) and 3 normal kidney cortex samples. GSE30528 contains 13 normal glomerular samples. Bulk transcriptome data were normalized using the ‘Limma’ package in R, and after merging, batch effects were corrected using the ‘sva’ package. Differences in HNF1B expression among groups were visualized using the ‘grafify’ package.

  The single-nucleus transcriptome dataset contained 5 normal kidney cortex samples. Quality control and analysis were performed using the ‘Seurat’ package. Quality control criteria were set as follows (1): number of genes detected per cell between 200 and 7,500 (2); percentage of mitochondrial genes not exceeding 30% (3); percentage of erythrocyte genes not exceeding 5% (4); total UMI count not exceeding 100,000. Data were normalized using the ‘NormalizeData’ function and then scaled using the ‘ScaleData’ function. Two thousand highly variable genes were selected for principal component analysis, and the ‘Harmony’ algorithm was used to integrate samples. Finally, t-SNE was applied for dimensionality reduction visualization, and cells were clustered at a resolution of 0.5. Each cell subpopulation was annotated according to marker genes from the CellMarker database and published literature (marker genes listed in Table 1).


### Differential expression analysis
  Based on the median expression level of HNF1B across all cystic tissue samples, the samples were divided into high-expression and low-expression groups. Subsequently, differentially expressed genes between the two groups were identified using the ‘Limma’ package (screening criteria: p < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1), and the results were visualized using a volcano plot drawn with the ‘ggrepel’ package.


### Enrichment analysis
  To clarify the functional changes in ADPKD cystic tissues under low HNF1B expression, functional enrichment analysis was performed. First, Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was carried out on the significantly upregulated differential genes in the low HNF1B expression group using the ‘clusterProfiler’ package. Second, to evaluate the impact of differential HNF1B expression on global signaling pathways, GSEA was performed on the expression data of all genes using the ‘GSEABase’ package based on the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes canonical pathway gene set (c2. cp. kegg_legacy. v2024. 1. Hs. symbols. gmt).


### Transcription factor and binding site prediction
  To explore the upstream regulatory network of HNF1B in ADPKD, the UCSC Genome Browser database was used to predict transcription factors that may bind to the promoter region of HNF1B, and candidate transcription factors with prediction scores above 600 were selected. Differentially expressed genes between ADPKD tissues and normal kidney tissues were identified using the ‘Limma’ package (screening criteria: p < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1). Then, the list of predicted candidate transcription factors was intersected with the above differentially expressed genes to identify upstream transcription factors that may actually regulate HNF1B expression in ADPKD. To verify the regulatory correlation, Spearman rank correlation analysis was performed between the expression levels of the screened transcription factors and HNF1B, and the results were visualized using the ‘ggpubr’ package. Finally, based on the JASPAR database, potential binding sites of these candidate transcription factors to the HNF1B gene were further predicted.


### Candidate drug prediction
  To identify small-molecule compounds capable of reversing ADPKD progression caused by downregulation of HNF1B expression, the L1000FWD and cMAP databases were used to screen for small-molecule compounds that showed significant positive correlation with the differential gene expression profile between the high- and low-HNF1B expression groups. Subsequently, the candidate small molecules identified from the two independent databases were intersected to determine potential therapeutic agents for ADPKD that may act by targeting HNF1β.


### Molecular docking
  To evaluate the reliability of drug-target binding, molecular docking was performed between the screened small-molecule drugs and HNF1β. The mol2 files of the small-molecule drugs were obtained from the PubChem database. The 3D structural model of human HNF1β was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 2DA6, corresponding to UniProt ID: P35680). Molecular docking was targeted at the functional POU homeodomain (residues 229–316). Water molecules and hetero molecules were removed from the protein receptor using PyMol software. The small molecules were converted into 3D structures using Chem3D, and the structures were optimized with minimal free energy. The protein receptor was processed for hydrogen addition using AutoDock Tools. The docking grid box center coordinates were set at X = 1.018, Y = −4.037, and Z = −4.029, with uniform grid box dimensions of 40 Å × 40 Å × 40 Å and a grid spacing of 0.375 Å across all candidate compounds. Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina software to calculate binding energies [13]. A lower binding energy indicates a more stable conformation formed between the ligand and receptor. Binding energy < −5 kcal/mol suggests good binding activity, while binding energy < −7 kcal/mol suggests strong binding activity. Finally, the molecular docking results were visualized using PyMol software.


### Statistical analysis
  Statistical analyses in this study were performed using R 4.4.0 and Graphpad Prism 9.0 software. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare HNF1B expression levels between the ADPKD and normal groups. The Jonckheere–Terpstra test was used to verify trend changes.


## Results

### Changes in HNF1B expression in ADPKD cystic tissues
  The expression level of HNF1B in cystic tissues from ADPKD patients was significantly lower than that in normal kidney tissues (p = 0.017) (Figure 1A). Further analysis showed that HNF1B expression gradually decreased with increasing cyst size (Jonckheere–Terpstra test: p < 0.001) (Figure 1B).


### Expression of HNF1B in different kidney cell types
  After quality control, a total of 56,728 high-quality cells were retained for subsequent analysis. After dimensionality reduction and clustering, 20 cell subpopulations were identified. Through classical marker annotation, these subpopulations were assigned to connecting tubule–principal cells, distal convoluted tubule cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, intercalated cells, podocytes, proximal tubule cells, and thick ascending limb cells of the loop of Henle. Because some cells co-expressed multiple markers at low levels and did not belong to independent cell subtypes, they were classified as unannotated cells (Figure 1C).

  HNF1B was highly expressed in various renal tubular epithelial cells, including connecting tubule–principal cells, distal convoluted tubule cells, proximal tubule cells, intercalated cells, and thick ascending limb cells (Figure 1D).


### Establishment and morphological observation of the 3D renal cyst model
  Under white light of an inverted microscope, MDCK cells cultured in type I collagen exhibited good three-dimensional growth characteristics. In the early stage of culture, cells formed dense solid spheroids. As culture time progressed, the cyst center gradually polarized and formed a clear single-layer cellular luminal structure, with lumen diameter significantly increasing with developmental days (Figure 2A, B).


### Immunofluorescence localization and semi-quantitative analysis of HNF1β
  Immunofluorescence staining results showed that HNF1β signals were localized in the nucleus at different developmental stages of MDCK cysts, highly overlapping with the DAPI-labeled nuclear regions. In early cysts, HNF1β fluorescence signals were strong and densely distributed. As culture time increased, the cyst lumen gradually expanded, and the fluorescence intensity of HNF1β in the single-layer cells at the edge visually showed a decreasing trend (Figure 2C). Semi-quantitative fluorescence intensity analysis revealed that HNF1β expression levels significantly decreased with cyst progression (Jonckheere–Terpstra test: p < 0.001) (Figure 2D).


### Prediction and correlation analysis of candidate upstream transcription factors of HNF1B
  The UCSC database was used to predict and screen 37 transcription factors that may regulate HNF1B. Differential expression analysis between cystic tissues and normal tissues yielded 1,324 differentially expressed genes (Figure 3A). After intersecting the predicted transcription factors with the differential genes, three candidate upstream transcription factors that showed significant co-expression with HNF1B in ADPKD were identified, including POU3F3, HNF4A, and TFAP2B (Figure 3B). Based on the JASPAR database, potential binding sites of these transcription factors to the HNF1B gene were further predicted (Figure 3C, E, G). Correlation analysis showed that the expression levels of these three transcription factors were all significantly positively correlated with HNF1B (POU3F3: r = 0.71, p < 0.001; HNF4A: r = 0.63, p < 0.001; TFAP2B: r = 0.76, p < 0.001) (Figure 3D, F, H).


### Identification of differential genes between high and low HNF1B expression groups
  By comparing the low- and high-HNF1B expression groups (low vs high HNF1B expression), a total of 340 differentially expressed genes were identified, of which 144 were upregulated and 196 were downregulated in the low-HNF1B expression group. The differential analysis results are presented in a volcano plot (Figure 4A).


### Enrichment analysis of differential genes
  To clarify the potential role of HNF1B in the pathogenesis of ADPKD, functional enrichment analysis was performed on the 144 significantly upregulated genes in the low-HNF1B expression group. GO analysis showed that these genes were significantly enriched in biological processes such as external encapsulating structure organization, extracellular matrix organization, ossification, and collagen fibril organization (Figure 4B). GSEA revealed that cystic tissues with low HNF1B expression were enriched for gene sets corresponding to wingless-related integration site (Wnt) and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) signaling pathway activation, as well as peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) signaling pathway suppression (Figure 4C).


### Drug Prediction targeting HNF1β
  Using the cMAP and L1000FWD databases for small-molecule drug prediction, 357 and 120 small-molecule compounds potentially acting by targeting HNF1β were screened, respectively. After intersecting the results from the two databases, 13 candidate small-molecule drugs were finally obtained.


### Molecular docking
  Molecular docking results are shown in Figure 5. Only 7 small molecules were ultimately able to form hydrogen bonds with HNF1β and thus interact. The binding energies of each drug to HNF1β are listed in Table 2. Among them, mocetinostat showed the lowest binding energy (binding energy = −8.2 kcal/mol), suggesting that this small molecule binds most stably to HNF1β.


## Discussion
  HNF1β, as an epithelial organ-specific transcription factor, plays a central regulatory role in multiple key processes of kidney development, including branching morphogenesis, nephron formation, and tubulogenesis. Defects in the HNF1B gene, which encodes HNF1β, lead to renal structural abnormalities and dysfunction [14]. ADPKD is usually caused by mutations in PKD1 or PKD2 and is pathologically characterized by progressively enlarging renal cysts [15,16]. Renal tubular epithelial cell differentiation and dysfunction are closely associated with kidney cyst formation, and HNF1β plays an important role in this process [17]. However, the specific role and downstream pathomechanisms of HNF1B in ADPKD cyst formation remain incompletely characterized. Therefore, this study performed an integrative bioinformatics analysis to generate hypotheses regarding the potential role and regulatory associations of HNF1B in ADPKD.

  The results of this study showed that HNF1B expression in ADPKD cystic tissues was significantly lower than that in normal kidney tissues and exhibited a continuous declining trend with cyst progression. These findings suggest that HNF1B may have a renoprotective role, and its downregulation may be associated with the development and progression of ADPKD cysts.

  To elucidate the regulatory mechanism underlying reduced HNF1B expression, the UCSC database was used to predict its upstream transcription factors, and expression correlations were used for validation. Computational binding predictions combined with expression correlation identified POU3F3, HNF4A, and TFAP2B as potential candidate upstream regulators of HNF1B in ADPKD. POU3F3 belongs to the POU family of transcription factors; its homozygous deletion has been shown to impair distal renal tubule differentiation and development in mice [18]. HNF4A regulates proximal tubule-related genes, and its deficiency can lead to tubulopathy with calcification. Moreover, HNF4A cross-regulates with HNF1A in multiple tissues [19,20]. TFAP2B belongs to the AP-2 family, regulates genes such as PKHD1 and CYS1, and its deletion in mice causes a cystic phenotype similar to ARPKD [21]. While these transcription factors are critical for renal tubular development, prospective functional assays—such as promoter-reporter assays, chromatin immunoprecipitation, and knockdown/overexpression studies—are necessary to definitively confirm these transcriptional interactions.

  To further explore the role of HNF1B in ADPKD pathogenesis, cystic tissues were grouped according to HNF1B expression levels, and differential analysis was performed. GO analysis showed that genes upregulated in the low-HNF1B expression group were significantly enriched in fibrosis-related biological processes, including extracellular matrix organization and collagen fibril organization. GSEA suggested that in cystic tissues with low HNF1B expression, the Wnt and TGF-β signaling pathways were activated, whereas the PPAR signaling pathway was inhibited. Multiple studies have shown that the Wnt signaling pathway plays an important role in cyst expansion [22]. Prior researches show HNF1β can suppress canonical Wnt signaling by competitively binding to Wnt-responsive elements, which aligns with the enriched Wnt signature observed in low-HNF1B ADPKD tissues [23–25]. The TGF-β signaling pathway is closely associated with renal interstitial fibrosis, a major driver of ADPKD progression [26,27]. Previous studies in Hnf1b-deficient mice have revealed that HNF1β affects TGF-β signal transduction and fibroblast activation by regulating the expression levels of transcription factors such as Twist2, suggesting that reduced HNF1B level is correlated with enhanced profibrotic signals [28]. PPARγ activation inhibits CFTR expression and thus delays cyst growth [29]. HNF1β regulates the expression of its coactivator PPARGC1A [30]. Thus, low HNF1B expression is associated with transcriptomic changes that promote cyst expansion and interstitial remodeling. While these individual pathways have been linked to HNF1β in various developmental or knockout models, the novelty of our study lies in translating these molecular interactions into the specific context of clinical ADPKD cyst progression. Our findings demonstrate that progressive HNF1B downregulation mirrors a coordinated shift toward Wnt/TGF-β activation and PPAR suppression during ADPKD tissue remodeling. Although these associations are primarily observational and require direct functional validation, they provide an integrative framework linking HNF1β dynamics to multiple pathogenic pathways in ADPKD.

  In summary, decreased HNF1B expression may promote ADPKD cyst expansion and renal interstitial fibrosis through the modulation of multiple signaling pathways. Therefore, HNF1β is a potential therapeutic target for ADPKD. The cMAP and L1000FWD databases were used to predict small-molecule compounds that may target HNF1β, and molecular docking was performed to validate their binding potential. Among these compounds, seven were able to stably bind to HNF1β. Mocetinostat is a histone deacetylase inhibitor; histone deacetylase activity has been associated with PKD1 expression suppression, suggesting its potential therapeutic value in ADPKD [31]. Bromocriptine, a dopamine D2 receptor agonist, may directly target the core pathways of ADPKD by inhibiting cAMP and MAPK signaling pathways, and thus may also have therapeutic value [32,33]. Pioglitazone, a PPARγ agonist, can delay ADPKD cyst progression by inhibiting CFTR; however, small-scale clinical trials have not shown clear efficacy, and further studies are needed [34]. Wortmannin is a PI3K inhibitor, and dapsone is an anti-inflammatory and antibacterial agent; their roles in ADPKD have not been reported [35,36]. Whether these candidate drugs can delay ADPKD progression by targeting HNF1β remains to be further investigated [37].

  Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, our analytical findings are primarily observational and hypothesis-generating. Although we observed that HNF1B expression progressively decreased with cyst expansion, this dynamic pattern does not definitively establish that HNF1B downregulation directly causes cyst progression. Instead, the observed reduction in HNF1B might represent a secondary phenomenon secondary to extensive epithelial cyst remodeling, tissue architectural distortion, or cellular dedifferentiation during disease development. Second, our transcriptomic analyses relied on retrospective public datasets with relatively constrained sample sizes, which may introduce potential selection bias. The integration of bulk tissue samples derived from different renal anatomical regions (including cystic tissue, renal cortex, and isolated glomeruli) could also introduce baseline cellular heterogeneity. Third, while we integrated bulk and single-nucleus transcriptomic datasets alongside a canine MDCK 3D cyst model, we did not perform direct functional experiments (such as targeted genetic knockdown/overexpression, promoter-luciferase reporter assays, or ChIP assays) to establish direct causal links between reduced HNF1B expression and downstream pathway alterations or upstream transcription factor binding. Finally, the use of MDCK 3D cyst model—while widely accepted for studying tubulogenesis—cannot fully recapitulate the complex human physiological microenvironment or species-specific gene regulatory networks. Further robust in vitro and in vivo functional validations in humanized models are required to confirm these predicted regulatory relationships.


## Conclusion
  In conclusion, HNF1B expression is significantly downregulated in ADPKD and negatively correlated with cyst progression, suggesting a potential protective association. POU3F3, HNF4A, and TFAP2B represent promising candidate upstream regulators supported by computational binding and co-expression analyses. Furthermore, reduced HNF1B expression is correlated with transcriptomic features of Wnt/TGF-β pathway activation and PPAR pathway suppression. These hypothesis-generating findings warrant future experimental validation to establish direct mechanistic and regulatory connections. Small-molecule compounds targeting HNF1β, represented by mocetinostat, show potential as novel interventional agents for ADPKD.
