### Major Concerns

- **Concern ID** R1-M1
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** Yes
- **Axis** Evidence sufficiency
- **Claim pointer** The review "examines how CFS advances fragments into lead series, preclinical candidates and clinical molecules" and lists specific oncology and antiviral examples.
- **Evidence pointer** Abstract, "AREAS COVERED" section. Location not provided.
- **Concern** The abstract asserts that CFS has advanced fragments into lead series, preclinical candidates, and clinical molecules, and names specific targets, but provides no data, case-study summaries, or success metrics. It is unclear whether these examples are presented as fully validated translational outcomes or as ongoing research efforts. The distinction between a fragment that has reached a clinical molecule and one that has merely been elaborated in vitro is critical and is not addressed.
- **Why it matters** The central value proposition of the review appears to rest on the translational impact of CFS. Without evidence of the stage of advancement for each named example, the claim is unverifiable and potentially misleading. Readers cannot assess the strength of the translational case or compare CFS against alternative approaches.
- **Resolution test** In the full manuscript, each named example should be accompanied by a clear statement of the highest stage of advancement reached, the evidence supporting that stage, and any relevant caveats. A summary table of fragment-to-clinical outcomes with references would satisfy this concern.

- **Concern ID** R1-M2
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** Yes
- **Axis** Claim substantiation
- **Claim pointer** "Computational and machine-learning methods can extend this information by exploring chemical space, ranking fragment elaborations and supporting multiparameter prioritization, but they do not replace experimental determination of binding modes or functional validation."
- **Evidence pointer** Abstract, "EXPERT OPINION" section. Location not provided.
- **Concern** This claim is presented as a definitive conclusion, but no evidence or discussion is provided to support it. The abstract does not describe any comparative analysis, case study, or literature synthesis that would justify the assertion that computational methods cannot replace experimental determination. It is also unclear whether this is the authors' opinion or a conclusion drawn from the reviewed literature.
- **Why it matters** The role of computational methods in fragment-based drug discovery is a rapidly evolving and contested area. A review that makes a strong claim about the limits of these methods should provide a reasoned argument based on specific examples or systematic evaluation. Without this, the claim appears as an unsupported opinion rather than a synthesized conclusion.
- **Resolution test** The full manuscript should include a dedicated section or subsection that critically evaluates the strengths and limitations of computational and machine-learning methods in CFS, with specific examples where predictions succeeded or failed relative to experimental structures. The conclusion should be derived from that analysis.
