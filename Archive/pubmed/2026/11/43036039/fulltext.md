# GPCR‐Mediated Regulation of Cytochrome P450 Drives Sulfoxaflor Resistance in Aphis gossypii Glover


## ABSTRACT
  Rapid evolution of insecticide resistance threatens agricultural productivity. While the fundamental molecular and biochemical mechanisms underlying resistance are well documented, the regulatory processes governing resistance‐associated genes, particularly those involving signal‐regulated transcription factors, remain poorly understood. Here, we investigated trans‐regulation of cytochrome P450 genes associated with sulfoxaflor resistance in Aphis gossypii Glover. Our findings suggest that G protein‐coupled receptor (GPCR)‐associated signaling and cAMP response element binding protein contribute to resistance‐associated P450 gene regulation. FISH and RT‐qPCR demonstrate that DopR1 transcripts are enriched in the epidermis, and β‐1A transcripts are enriched in the head. Molecular docking, heterologous expression, and biolayer interferometry assays demonstrate that DopR1 and β‐1A participate in cellular recognition or response to sulfoxaflor and activate downstream cAMP signaling. Silencing DopR1 and β‐1A reduced cAMP content, PKA activity, P450 enzyme activity, and resistance‐related P450 expression, thereby enhancing aphid sensitivity to sulfoxaflor. Inhibitor‐based suppression, pathway gene silencing, and dual‐luciferase reporter assays implicated AC‐2, AC‐5, PKA‐4, PKA‐7, CRTC, and CREB in regulating critical resistance‐related genes, especially CYP6CY19. Furthermore, functional validation using transgenic Drosophila melanogaster expressing CYP6CY13 and CYP6CY19 demonstrated increased sulfoxaflor tolerance. Collectively, these findings support a working model linking GPCR‐associated signaling to the regulation of selected resistance‐associated P450 genes in A. gossypii.


## Introduction
  Since the 1940s, chemical insecticides have been extensively used for pest control [1]. However, the development of insect resistance to these chemicals has become a significant challenge to sustainable pest management. Studies on pest resistance have revealed that strong selective pressures imposed by insecticides drive alterations in the genome and transcriptome of pests [2]. This process is commonly characterized by the upregulation of cytochrome P450 (P450s) [3, 4], which play a critical role in detoxifying insecticides. This elevated expression of P450s has been primarily attributed to increased gene copy number, changes in cis‐acting element structure or function, and variation in the activity or expression of trans‐acting factors [2, 4, 5, 6, 7]. Although considerable research has established the role of P450s in mediating insecticide resistance, the molecular mechanisms underlying their altered regulation in resistant insects remain an area of active research.

  G protein‐coupled receptors (GPCRs) are a diverse family of transmembrane receptor proteins, characterized by seven α‐helical domains, which are widespread in the cell membranes of both animals and plants. GPCRs play a pivotal role in cellular communication by binding to various signaling molecules and initiating conformational changes that activate downstream effectors, such as reactive oxygen species (ROS), adenylate cyclase (AC), and mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK). These signaling cascades ultimately regulate gene expression, which is critical for maintaining physiological and behavioral homeostasis [8, 9, 10, 11]. Recent studies suggest that GPCRs may be involved in insecticide resistance by regulating the expression of P450 genes [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. This association has been observed in a variety of insect species, including Musca domestica [13], Culex quinquefasciatus [14, 15], Cydia pomonella [16], and Bemisia tabaci [17]. However, the complexity of GPCR signaling pathways warrants further investigation to fully elucidate their role in regulating P450 expression and their contribution to pest resistance.

  The cotton aphid, Aphis gossypii Glover (Hemiptera: Aphididae), is a globally distributed major pest of cotton, with outbreaks posing significant threats to cotton production. The overreliance on chemical insecticides has led to the rapid development of resistance to commonly used insecticides in this species [18]. Numerous studies have highlighted the pivotal role of P450s in mediating insecticide resistance in A. gossypii, primarily through the upregulation of specific P450 genes [19, 20, 21]. For example, CYP6CY13 and CYP6CY19 have been found to be associated with resistance to several insecticides, including sulfoxaflor and imidacloprid [19, 20, 21]. Based on our previous findings that the overexpression of GPCR genes contributes to sulfoxaflor resistance in A. gossypii [22], we hypothesize that a regulatory interaction exists between GPCRs and P450s in this pest species.

  In this study, we identified two GPCRs in A. gossypii, dopamine receptor (DopR1) and adrenergic receptor (β‐1A), both of which contain structural domains typical of the rhodopsin‐like family and are known to be involved in insect growth and development [23, 24, 25]. In insects, DopR1 and β‐1A share functional similarities with their mammalian counterparts. Their activation triggers signaling through adenylate cyclase, promoting the production of cyclic AMP (cAMP), which subsequently activates the expression of protein kinase A (PKA) regulated genes [25, 26, 27]. Research has shown that cAMP binds to the regulatory subunits of PKA, leading to the dissociation of two regulatory subunits (PKARs) and two catalytic subunits (PKACs), thereby activating PKACs [28, 29, 30]. The activated PKACs phosphorylate the cAMP response element‐binding protein (CREB) in the nucleus [31]. Phosphorylated CREB forms a dimer and binds to cAMP response elements (CREs) in the promoter region of the target gene, thereby regulating transcription by either promoting or inhibiting expression [32, 33]. Furthermore, increased cAMP levels dephosphorylate and activate the CREB‐regulated transcription coactivator (CRTC) [34, 35], which further binds to CREB at the transcriptional sites, enhancing CREB activity and promoting gene transcription. Based on this theoretical framework, we propose a regulatory network, DopR1 or β‐1A/AC/cAMP/PKA/CREB/P450s, through which GPCRs may regulate resistance‐associated P450 expression and contribute to insecticide resistance in A. gossypii. This study aims to elucidate the signaling pathways involved, advancing our understanding of GPCR‐mediated insecticide resistance.


## Results

### Expression and Localization of DopR1 and β‐1A in Different Tissues of A. gossypii
  To characterize the expression profiles of the GPCR genes DopR1 and β‐1A across various tissues of A. gossypii, cotton aphids were dissected into the head, epidermis, and internal contents. RT‐qPCR analysis revealed that both DopR1 and β‐1A were expressed in all three regions, with distinct tissue‐specific patterns. DopR1 exhibited elevated expression in the epidermis compared to the head and internal contents, with 2.38‐fold and 2.23‐fold increases, respectively. Conversely, β‐1A displayed significantly higher expression in the head, with 4.23‐fold and 19.28‐fold increases, relative to the epidermis and internal contents, respectively (Figure 1A).

  Section‐based FISH analysis was used to provide qualitative visualization of DopR1 and β‐1A transcript distribution in aphid sections (Figure 1 and Figures S1 and S2). Fluorescence co‐localization of nuclear staining (blue) and target‐gene mRNA signals (red) indicated that DopR1 and β‐1A transcripts were detected in the head, epidermis, and internal contents of cotton aphids. Relatively stronger DopR1 signals were observed in the epidermis, whereas β‐1A signals were more apparent in the head, consistent with the RT‐qPCR‐based tissue‐expression pattern (Figure 1B and Figure S2). Because both transcripts were detected in multiple tissues, these results indicate relative tissue‐associated enrichment.

  To assess the target dependence of these FISH signals, we performed FISH following treatment with dsGFP, dsDopR1, or dsβ‐1A. RT‐qPCR confirmed that each dsRNA reduced expression of its corresponding target gene, while expression of the other receptor gene was not significantly changed (Figure S3). The epidermal DopR1 probe signal was weaker after dsDopR1 treatment than in dsGFP controls. Likewise, the β‐1A probe signal in the head was weaker after dsβ‐1A treatment (Figure S4). The reduction in each signal after knockdown of its target supports the specificity of the FISH staining.


### DopR1 and β‐1A Are Associated with Sulfoxaflor Recognition and Receptor‐Linked cAMP Responses
  DopR1 and β‐1A were heterologously expressed in HEK293T cells as EGFP‐tagged fusion proteins. EGFP fluorescence signals confirmed the successful expression of both fusion proteins, which were mainly distributed around the cell membrane and in the cytoplasm (Figure 2A). Following sulfoxaflor treatment, β‐1A‐expressing cells showed significantly higher intracellular cAMP content than the corresponding control cells at 10, 20, and 50 µM. In DopR1‐expressing cells, a significant increase relative to the control was observed only at 50 µM sulfoxaflor among the concentrations tested (Figure 2B).

  To further determine whether DopR1 and β‐1A could bind to sulfoxaflor, DopR1 and β‐1A were expressed and purified for subsequent binding assays. SDS‐PAGE analysis showed protein bands corresponding to the expected molecular weights of DopR1 and β‐1A proteins (Figure S5). We then used biolayer interferometry (BLI) to analyse binding responses between sulfoxaflor and DopR1 or β‐1A. The responses increased with the sulfoxaflor concentration (Figure 2C,E). Steady‐state affinity analyses yielded K D values of 33.91 ± 13.45 µM for DopR1 and 89.06 ± 20.53 µM for β‐1A (Figure 2D,F). An empty‐vector expression preparation lacking the target receptor was included as a background control. Background responses were observed, but a K D could not be determined (Figure S6).

  Molecular docking analysis was performed to evaluate the potential interactions between sulfoxaflor and DopR1 or β‐1A. The results showed that sulfoxaflor could be accommodated within the predicted binding pockets of both receptors, with binding energies of −7.6 kcal mol−1 for β‐1A and −6.5 kcal mol−1 for DopR1. In DopR1, sulfoxaflor was surrounded by several residues, including Leu‐211, Ile‐322, and Glu‐324, with interaction distances of approximately 3.0–3.5 Å (Figure S7A). In β‐1A, sulfoxaflor interacted with key residues, including Ser‐157 and Trp‐146, with interaction distances of approximately 3.2–3.4 Å (Figure S7B). These predictions suggest possible binding configurations but do not establish the involvement of specific residues. In addition, LC‐MS/MS analysis showed that the sulfoxaflor peak areas in DopR1‐ and β‐1A‐expressing cells were significantly increased compared with the control group, reaching 4.09‐ and 3.96‐fold of the control, respectively (Figure S7C). Taken together, these findings support the interaction of sulfoxaflor with both DopR1 and β‐1A.


### Regulation of P450 Gene Expression by DopR1 and β‐1A Confers Resistance to Sulfoxaflor in Cotton Aphids
  The expression levels of DopR1 and β‐1A were significantly upregulated in the sulfoxaflor‐resistant strain (SulR) compared with the susceptible strain (SS), by 2.22‐fold and 1.80‐fold, respectively (Figure 3A), and both genes were induced to different degrees of expression by sulfoxaflor exposure. Upon exposure to the LC25 of sulfoxaflor, DopR1 expression was consistently upregulated across all time points (Figure S8A), whereas β‐1A expression showed significant induction at 12 h and 24 h (Figure S8B). Treatment with the LC50 of sulfoxaflor resulted in significant induction and upregulation of DopR1 at all time points except at 24 h, while β‐1A expression exhibited a marked increase at all time points (Figure S8).

  The involvement of DopR1 and β‐1A in A. gossypii resistance to sulfoxaflor was further explored utilizing RNA interference (RNAi). The results demonstrated that silencing of DopR1 and β‐1A (Figure 3B) significantly enhanced the susceptibility of A. gossypii to sulfoxaflor, with the mortality rates of the dsDopR1 and dsβ‐1A groups increasing by 36.66% and 33.50%, respectively, compared to the dsGFP control (Figure 3C). To further investigate the underlying mechanism, we assessed the P450 enzyme activity in cotton aphids after DopR1 and β‐1A genes silencing. A noticeable reduction in P450 enzyme activity was observed after DopR1 and β‐1A gene silencing. Specifically, aphids fed with dsDopR1 and dsβ‐1A exhibited significant decreases in enzyme activity, with reductions of 52.36% and 49.51%, respectively, compared to the dsGFP group (Figure 3D). We further analyzed the expression of resistance‐associated P450 genes following DopR1 and β‐1A gene silencing. The results indicated that silencing of DopR1 resulted in significant downregulation of CYP6CY18 (57.19%), CYP6CY19 (26.02%), and CYP6CY4 (85.39%), with CYP6CY4 showing the greatest reduction. Similarly, silencing of β‐1A led to marked reduction in the expression of CYP380C6 (36.72%), CYP4CJ1 (41.14%), CYP6CY18 (73.77%), CYP6CY19 (30.53%), and CYP6CY4 (62.43%) (Figure 3E). In summary, both the enzymatic activity assessments and RT‐qPCR results suggest that DopR1 and β‐1A play a role in shaping A. gossypii resistance to sulfoxaflor by modulating the expression of resistance‐associated P450s.


### DopR1 and β‐1A Participate in cAMP/PKA Signaling Associated with Sulfoxaflor Resistance in Cotton Aphids
  Previous studies have shown that GPCRs are involved in multiple physiological processes and can be associated with cAMP‐related signaling in different systems [25, 26, 27]. Given this, we further explored the role of the AC/cAMP/PKA pathway in sulfoxaflor resistance in A. gossypii. Analysis of relative cAMP content revealed a 1.41‐fold increase in the SulR strain compared to the SS strain (Figure 4A). Consistently, the PKA activity in the SulR strain was significantly higher, showing a 1.40‐fold increase compared with the SS strain (Figure 4B), suggesting that both cAMP content and downstream PKA activity were increased in sulfoxaflor‐resistant aphids. To determine whether DopR1 and β‐1A participate in the regulation of this signaling pathway, cAMP content and PKA activity were measured after DopR1 and β‐1A gene silencing. Compared with the dsGFP control, silencing of DopR1 or β‐1A significantly reduced the cAMP content by 31.13% and 38.81%, respectively (Figure 4C). A similar pattern was observed for PKA activity, with both dsDopR1 and dsβ‐1A treatments showing significantly reduced PKA activity, 19.19% and 23.49% lower than the dsGFP control (Figure 4D). These findings suggest that DopR1 and β‐1A play a role in maintaining cAMP content and PKA activity.

  To further determine whether sulfoxaflor exposure could induce this signaling pathway, cAMP content and PKA activity were measured after treatment with the LC25 and LC50 concentrations of sulfoxaflor at different time points. The cAMP content showed an increasing trend after sulfoxaflor exposure, with significant induction observed at 12 h or later, most notably at the 36 and 48 h timepoints (Figure 4E). Similarly, following treatment with the LC25 or LC50 concentrations of sulfoxaflor, relative PKA activity was significantly increased at 36 and 48 h compared with the controls. No significant differences were detected at 12 or 24 h (Figure 4F). Sulfoxaflor exposure increased cAMP content and relative PKA activity at selected sampling times, indicating a time‐dependent biochemical response (Figure 4E,F). Since sulfoxaflor treatment promoted cAMP content and PKA activity, we next investigated whether pharmacological inhibition of this pathway would alter the response of A. gossypii to sulfoxaflor. Treatment with the cAMP pathway inhibitor bupivacaine HCl increased the mortality of aphids exposed to sulfoxaflor and decreased the LC50 from 198.48 to 154.89 mg L− 1 (Figure 4G and Table S6). Likewise, the PKA inhibitor H‐89 2HCl significantly increased sulfoxaflor toxicity, reducing the LC50 from 198.48 to 140.85 mg L− 1 (Figure 4H and Table S6). The pharmacological results therefore differed between treatments.


### DopR1/β‐1A‐Associated AC/cAMP/PKA Signaling Regulates P450 Expression and Contributes to Sulfoxaflor Resistance in A. gossypii
  Based on the above results showing that sulfoxaflor exposure induces cAMP accumulation and activates PKA signaling, and that inhibition of this pathway increases aphid susceptibility to sulfoxaflor, we further investigated the molecular components of the AC/cAMP/PKA pathway and its downstream regulatory mechanisms in A. gossypii. The expression levels of AC and PKA genes were upregulated in the SulR strain compared to the SS strain, with a noticeable upregulation in all three AC genes in SulR, with AC‐5 showing the greatest increase (2.02‐fold). Similarly, all six PKA genes were significantly upregulated in the SulR strain, with PKA‐7 showing the highest 3.22‐fold increase (Figure 5A).

  To determine whether the upstream GPCRs DopR1 and β‐1A regulate this pathway, we examined the expression of AC and PKA genes after DopR1 and β‐1A silencing. Silencing DopR1 significantly downregulated the expression of AC‐5 and PKA‐4. Similarly, silencing β‐1A led to a significant reduction in the expression of AC‐2, AC‐5, and PKA‐4 (Figure 5B). These results indicate that the two receptors influence the expression of selected AC and PKA genes. Next, RNAi experiments were performed to evaluate the functional contribution of selected AC and PKA genes to sulfoxaflor susceptibility. Silencing efficiency assays confirmed that the targeted AC and PKA genes were effectively knocked down after dsRNA treatment (Figure 5C). Subsequent bioassays showed that silencing AC‐2, AC‐5, PKA‐4, and PKA‐7 significantly increased the mortality of cotton aphids after sulfoxaflor treatment, with mortality increases of 41.39%, 38.42%, 39.91%, and 22.27%, respectively (Figure 5D). These findings provide clear evidence that AC‐2, AC‐5, PKA‐4, and PKA‐7 contribute functionally to sulfoxaflor resistance.

  To examine whether AC‐2 and AC‐5 affect cAMP content, we measured cAMP levels following their silencing. Both dsAC‐2 and dsAC‐5 treatments significantly reduced cAMP content compared with the dsGFP control (Figure 5E). Because AC is located upstream of cAMP‐mediated PKA activation, we further examined whether AC genes regulate downstream PKA gene expression. Silencing AC‐2 resulted in reduced expression of PKA‐2, PKA‐3, PKA‐4, and PKA‐7, while silencing AC‐5 significantly down‐regulated PKA‐2, PKA‐3, and PKA‐4 (Figure 5F). No significant change in PKA‐7 expression was detected after AC‐5 silencing (Figure 5F). Together, these results link AC knockdown to reduced cAMP content and altered expression of selected PKA genes.

  Furthermore, analysis of P450 gene expression following the silencing of key genes in the AC/cAMP/PKA pathway revealed substantial reductions in several P450 genes. Specifically, following silencing of AC‐2, the expression levels of CYP6A2, CYP6CY13, and CYP6CY19 were significantly reduced. Similarly, silencing AC‐5 led to significant downregulation of CYP6CY13, and CYP6CY19, and silencing PKA‐4 led to significant downregulation of CYP380C6, CYP6CY13, and CYP6CY19 while silencing PKA‐7 significantly decreased the expression of CYP3323A1, CYP6CY13, CYP6CY18, and CYP6CY19 (Figure 5G). Together, these results suggest that DopR1/β‐1A‐mediated activation of the AC/cAMP/PKA pathway promotes the expression of detoxification‐related P450 genes, thereby contributing to sulfoxaflor resistance in A. gossypii.


### CREB and CRTC Contribute to Resistance to Sulfoxaflor in Cotton Aphids by Regulating P450 Expression
  Based on the above results showing that the GPCR/AC/cAMP/PKA pathway regulates the expression of multiple P450 genes, we next investigated whether the downstream transcriptional regulators CREB and CRTC are involved in this regulatory process. The expression levels of CREB and CRTC were first compared between the SulR and SS strains. The results showed that both CREB and CRTC were significantly upregulated in the SulR strain, with CREB expression increasing 2.38‐fold and CRTC expression increasing 2.93‐fold compared to the SS strain (Figure 6A). In addition, sulfoxaflor treatment further induced the expression of CREB and CRTC at different time points under LC25 and LC50 exposure conditions, indicating that these two transcriptional regulators can respond to sulfoxaflor stress (Figure S9). These results suggest that CREB‐ and CRTC‐mediated transcriptional regulation may be associated with sulfoxaflor resistance in A. gossypii.

  To investigate whether CREB and CRTC contribute to sulfoxaflor sensitivity in A. gossypii, RNAi was employed to silence these genes (Figure 6B). Sulfoxaflor sensitivity assays conducted 48 h post‐dsRNA feeding demonstrated that knockdown of CREB and CRTC significantly increased aphid sensitivity to sulfoxaflor. Specifically, mortality rates increased by 37.90% and 27.21% following treatment with dsCREB and dsCRTC, respectively (Figure 6C), thus demonstrating that CREB and CRTC contribute to the reduced susceptibility of A. gossypii to sulfoxaflor. Further gene expression analysis revealed that silencing CREB led to a significant downregulation of several P450 genes, including CYP3323A1, CYP380C6, CYP4CJ1, CYP6CY18, CYP6A2, CYP6CY13, and CYP6CY19. Similarly, silencing CRTC resulted in a marked reduction in the expression of CYP3323A1, CYP380C6, CYP4CJ1, CYP6CY18, CYP6A2, CYP6CY13, and CYP6CY19 (Figure 6D). These findings suggest that CREB and its coactivator CRTC positively regulate the transcription of multiple P450 genes involved in sulfoxaflor resistance. To further clarify the regulatory role of the GPCR pathway, we found that silencing each of the examined pathway genes reduced CREB transcript levels. In contrast, CRTC expression increased after AC‐5 silencing and decreased after silencing DopR1, β‐1A, AC‐2, PKA‐4, or PKA‐7 (Figure S10). These results suggest that the GPCR/AC/cAMP/PKA pathway may regulate CREB‐ and CRTC‐associated transcriptional responses.

  To further elucidate the regulatory relationship between CREB and P450 promoters, we predicted the promoter regions of resistance‐related P450 genes and identified cAMP response elements (CREs, palindromic sequence TGACGTCA or half‐site sequences TGACG or CGTCA). The results showed that all eight P450 gene promoters contained CRE‐like sites (Table S7). Dual‐luciferase reporter assays were then performed to determine whether CREB could activate these P450 promoters. Compared with the promoterless control vector pGL3‐Basic, the promoter constructs of CYP380C6, CYP4CJ1, CYP6CY13, CYP6CY19, and CYP6CY18 showed higher luciferase activity. Moreover, co‐transfection with CREB further enhanced the promoter activities of several P450 genes, including CYP3323A1, CYP380C6, CYP6CY18, and CYP6CY19 (Figure 6E). These results indicate that CREB can activate specific P450 promoters, thereby providing functional evidence that CREB‐mediated transcriptional regulation contributes to P450 overexpression. To further identify potential CREB‐responsive elements in the promoters of P450 genes, CREB‐promoter docking prediction was performed for promoter fragments containing predicted CRE‐like motifs. Candidate CREB‐interacting regions were predicted in the promoters of CYP6CY13, CYP4CJ1, CYP6CY19, CYP380C6, CYP6CY18, and CYP3323A1, whereas no reliable CREB‐binding site was predicted for CYP6A2 or CYP6CY4 under the same analysis. The predicted binding sequences were TCTGACGTT for CYP6CY13, ACGTCAT for CYP4CJ1, TATGACA for CYP6CY19, GTCACAAG for CYP380C6, TGACGTA for CYP6CY18, and TGACATT for CYP3323A1 (Table S7 and Figure S11).


### Overexpression of CYP6CY13 and CYP6CY19 Increased Tolerance to Sulfoxaflor in D. melanogaster
  To functionally validate candidate P450s associated with GPCR/AC/cAMP/PKA/CREB‐related regulation, we selected CYP6CY13 and CYP6CY19 for further functional analysis. CYP6CY19 showed relatively consistent downregulation after silencing receptor‐level components, downstream AC/PKA components, and CREB/CRTC‐related regulators, whereas CYP6CY13 was consistently reduced after silencing downstream AC/PKA and CREB/CRTC‐related components. Transgenic D. melanogaster expressing CYP6CY13 and CYP6CY19 were created using the GAL4‐UAS system (Figure 7A) with RT‐qPCR results showing that CYP6CY13 and CYP6CY19 were significantly overexpressed in the GAL4‐UAS‐P450 line compared to the UAS‐P450 line alone (Figure 7B). Insecticide bioassays demonstrated that the GAL4‐UAS‐CYP6CY13 line exhibited significantly enhanced resistance to sulfoxaflor, as evidenced by reduced mortality of 40.74% and 36.11% at 1 and 2 mg L− 1, respectively (Figure 7C). Consistently, the GAL4‐UAS‐CYP6CY19 line also showed significantly increased resistance, with mortality reductions of 36.84% and 33.33% under the same conditions (Figure 7D).


## Discussion
  In our previous study, we identified the complete repertoire of GPCR genes in A. gossypii and showed that 34 of these genes are significantly upregulated in a sulfoxaflor‐resistant strain [22], suggesting a potential involvement of GPCR‐mediated signaling in insecticide resistance. However, the role of these GPCR genes in insecticide resistance, and how they act in concert with other genes that influence resistance phenotypes remained unclear. Our results link DopR1 and β‐1A to sulfoxaflor susceptibility, cAMP content, PKA activity, and the expression of selected P450 genes to provide new insight into the role of GPCRs in insects, the trans‐acting factors that regulate P450 genes involved in detoxification and the signaling pathways that coordinate the xenobiotic response.

  Using RT‐qPCR and mRNA in situ hybridization techniques, we detected DopR1 and β‐1A transcripts in the head, epidermis, and internal contents of cotton aphids. Notably, DopR1 showed significantly higher expression in the epidermis compared to the head and contents. This epidermal enrichment raises the possibility of a role in tissue‐associated responses to chemical stress. This pattern differs from the neural‐enriched expression profiles reported for some dopamine receptors and other GPCRs in certain insect species, where they are frequently associated with nervous system‐related signaling functions [16]. Therefore, the relatively high epidermal expression of DopR1 suggests that certain GPCR genes may have tissue‐specific or species‐specific physiological functions beyond canonical neural signaling [36, 37]. However, whether DopR1 is directly involved in epidermis‐linked responses remains to be further investigated. In contrast, β‐1A exhibits higher transcript levels in the head compared to the epidermis and contents. This is consistent with previous research showing that some GPCRs are more highly expressed in the head of insects than in other body parts [13]. Because the insect head contains major neural and sensory structures, the relatively high expression of β‐1A in this region may reflect a potential role in neural or sensory‐related signaling responses to external stimuli. Similar tissue‐enriched expression patterns have also been associated with stress‐related physiological functions in insects [38]. However, the precise tissue‐associated function of β‐1A in A. gossypii remains to be further investigated.

  In response to external stressors, such as pesticide exposure, insects activate adaptive stress responses, including the selective upregulation of specific genes that may enhance survival [39]. In this study, we found that the expression of DopR1 and β‐1A is induced to varying degrees upon exposure to sulfoxaflor. Previous studies have also shown that GPCRs can be induced by insecticides, and the overexpression of GPCRs has been implicated in the resistance of C. pomonella to lambda‐cyhalothrin [16, 40]. These transcriptional responses suggest involvement in the physiological response to sulfoxaflor exposure, but do not by themselves establish direct insecticide sensing at the body surface or in the head. To further test the functional relevance of these two receptors, we silenced DopR1 and β‐1A using RNAi, and found that this significantly increased the sensitivity of cotton aphids to sulfoxaflor, consistent with our previous work [22]. Additionally, we observed a decrease in P450 enzyme activity, along with a downregulation of the expression of several P450 genes. Collectively, these RNAi results support contributions of DopR1 and β‐1A to sulfoxaflor susceptibility and selected P450 responses. A similar phenomenon has also been observed in other insects. For example, in M. domestica, GPCRs regulate the expression of the P450 genes CYP12D1, CYP6A2, and CYP6A8, contributing to resistance to permethrin [41]. In Rhopalosiphum padi, silencing of GPCR genes led to the downregulation of five P450 genes, which were associated with R. padi resistance to lambda‐cyhalothrin and bifenthrin [42]. In combination with our findings, these studies provide growing evidence of the important role of GPCRs in mediating the response to insecticides via their regulatory role in P450 gene expression.

  The cellular and BLI assays provide additional information on sulfoxaflor‐associated responses. Sulfoxaflor treatment increased cAMP content in receptor‐expressing HEK293T cells under selected treatment conditions, supporting receptor‐associated cellular responses. LC‐MS/MS also detected increased cell‐associated sulfoxaflor, although this measurement cannot distinguish receptor binding from changes in uptake, retention, or other cellular processes. In the additional BLI experiments, purified DopR1 and β‐1A preparations exhibited concentration‐dependent responses. These results describe interactions under the tested conditions. However, the predicted binding pockets have not been validated by site‐directed mutagenesis, and the contributions of individual residues to sulfoxaflor binding remain unresolved. Further evidence is also needed to establish binding specificity.

  Our subsequent experiments provided additional evidence linking the two receptors to cAMP/PKA‐associated responses. Silencing of DopR1 and β‐1A significantly down‐regulated the expression of AC/cAMP/PKA pathway‐related genes (Figure 5B), and also reduced cAMP content and PKA activity in A. gossypii, whereas sulfoxaflor exposure increased these biochemical readouts under the tested conditions (Figure 4). In addition, silencing either AC‐2 or AC‐5 reduced cAMP content (Figure 5E). Together with the increased sulfoxaflor susceptibility and reduced expression of selected resistance‐associated P450 genes following AC knockdown (Figure 5D,G), these findings provide genetic and biochemical evidence consistent with a contribution of AC/cAMP‐associated signaling to resistance.

  The pharmacological results provided more qualified support. H‐89 treatment increased sulfoxaflor susceptibility, consistent with PKA involvement. However, although bupivacaine HCl treatment produced a numerically lower LC50, it did not significantly increase mortality relative to sulfoxaflor alone in the single‐concentration assay (Figure 4G). Previous work in Culex mosquitoes linked pharmacological inhibition of cAMP/PKA signaling to increased permethrin susceptibility, although the sensitizing effect of bupivacaine HCl varied with treatment concentration and mosquito strain, with no apparent sensitization at some lower concentrations [43]. In our inhibitor‐only assays, aphid mortality increased at 200 and 400 mg L−1 bupivacaine HCl and at 20–80 mg L−1 H‐89 2HCl (Figure S12). This toxicity limited the concentration range available for evaluating sensitization and complicated the interpretation of mortality at higher inhibitor concentrations. Our interpretation of cAMP involvement therefore relies primarily on the genetic and biochemical evidence.

  Among the genes tested, silencing AC‐2, AC‐5, PKA‐4, and PKA‐7 significantly increased sulfoxaflor susceptibility, supporting their functional contributions to resistance. Their transcriptional relationships nevertheless differed. Both AC‐silencing treatments reduced PKA‐2, PKA‐3, and PKA‐4 expression, whereas PKA‐7 was significantly reduced only after AC‐2 silencing and was not significantly changed after AC‐5 silencing (Figure 5F). However, its elevated expression in SulR aphids and the susceptibility phenotype following its knockdown support its selection for functional analysis. Gene‐specific contributions of AC and PKA family members have also been reported in C. quinquefasciatus, including AC004739 and PKA000798 [14], and distinct physiological functions have been described for mammalian AC and PKA family members [44, 45].

  After silencing of AC and PKA genes, we investigated the expression of resistance‐related P450 genes.   Knockdown of each of the four genes (AC‐2, AC‐5, PKA‐4, and PKA‐7) significantly downregulated several P450 genes, although the affected gene sets overlapped only partially (Figure 5G). Similar results have also been found in C. quinquefasciatus where silencing of AC and PKA genes led to the downregulation of insecticide resistance‐related P450 genes [14, 15]. In the present study, CYP6CY13 and CYP6CY19 genes were significantly down‐regulated following all four knockdown treatments, suggesting that these four signaling components may contribute to the regulation of both genes. Other P450 genes, however, showed treatment‐specific responses. For example, CYP6A2 expression decreased following AC‐2 knockdown but was not significantly affected by AC‐5 silencing, whereas knockdown of either CREB or CRTC also reduced its expression. Thus, silencing an upstream AC component did not necessarily reproduce the effects of silencing a downstream transcriptional regulator. Together, these findings suggest that individual AC and PKA family members contribute to the regulation of overlapping but distinct sets of resistance‐related P450 genes, indicating that the regulatory effects of GPCR/AC/cAMP/PKA signaling may vary among downstream targets.

  The RNAi and promoter‐reporter results identify CREB as a candidate transcriptional regulator associated with selected P450 responses. CREB is a transcription factor belonging to the bZIP superfamily that has been shown to play a role in a variety of biological processes [46, 47]. In a recent study linking CREB to P450 regulation, it was found that CREB exerts a neuroprotective function in B. tabaci by upregulating the key detoxification enzyme gene, CYP6CM1 [2]. In A. gossypii CREB/ATF‐mediated regulation of CYP6CY14 has previously been linked to cycloxaprid resistance [48]. Importantly, we found that CREB was upregulated in resistant cotton aphids and was significantly induced by sulfoxaflor exposure. These findings suggest the potential involvement of CREB in the development of sulfoxaflor resistance in A. gossypii. Promoter‐reporter assays showed that CREB altered the activities of selected P450 promoters. Silencing of CREB not only increased the mortality of A. gossypii following sulfoxaflor treatment but also resulted in decreased expression of resistance‐related P450 genes. Additionally, we found that the CREB‐regulated transcription coactivator (CRTC) showed a similar expression pattern to CREB (Figure 6A and Figure S9). This may be related to the fact that CRTC acts as a CREB coactivator [34, 35]. Although CREB showed an induction pattern generally similar to DopR1 and β‐1A, the weaker and less consistent induction of CRTC suggests that CREB/CRTC‐associated regulation may not follow a strictly linear cascade with identical temporal dynamics. In addition, because CRTC activity is regulated post‐translationally through phosphorylation/dephosphorylation, whereas our data only reflect transcript‐level changes, the involvement of CRTC should be interpreted mainly as a transcriptional‐response association. Further experiments examining CRTC phosphorylation, nuclear localization, and its interaction with CREB are needed to confirm this regulatory relationship.

  Our results suggest that GPCR signaling is an important upstream regulator of sulfoxaflor resistance in A. gossypii. However, the regulation of individual P450 genes does not appear to follow a completely uniform or strictly linear pattern. Among the examined P450 genes, CYP6CY19 showed relatively consistent downregulation after silencing receptor‐level components, downstream AC/PKA components, and CREB/CRTC‐related transcriptional regulators, supporting its role as a more consistently linked downstream effector of the proposed GPCR/AC/cAMP/PKA/CREB regulatory cascade. By contrast, CYP6CY13 was significantly reduced after silencing several downstream AC/PKA and CREB/CRTC‐associated components, but did not show the same response after DopR1 or β‐1A knockdown. This discrepancy suggests that CYP6CY13 may be influenced by downstream cAMP/PKA/CREB‐related regulation, while also being affected by additional regulatory or compensatory responses. Despite these differences in upstream regulation, transgenic D. melanogaster overexpressing CYP6CY13 and CYP6CY19 exhibited increased tolerance to sulfoxaflor (Figure 7).

  In conclusion, our results link DopR1 and β‐1A to sulfoxaflor susceptibility, cAMP content, PKA activity, and the expression of selected P450 genes in A. gossypii. RNAi and promoter‐reporter assays support gene‐dependent contributions of AC/PKA components and CREB/CRTC‐related regulation. CYP6CY19 shows relatively consistent responses across the tested interventions, and expression of CYP6CY13 or CYP6CY19 increases sulfoxaflor tolerance in D. melanogaster. These findings support a working model of GPCR‐associated P450 regulation (Figure 8). Collectively, these findings provide new insight into the intricate regulatory networks involved in the evolution of insecticide resistance.


## Experimental Section

### Insects
  The current study employed two strains of A. gossypii, one susceptible strain (SS) and one sulfoxaflor‐resistant strain (SulR). These strains originated from a field population of A. gossypii collected in 2016 from the Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region of China [19]. Both strains were propagated under laboratory conditions on cotton seedlings, specifically the Gossypium hirsutum L. variety Xinluzhong No. 76, in a controlled environment with temperatures of 22 ± 1°C, 60% relative humidity, and a 16:8 h (light: dark) photoperiod.


### Bioassays
  The toxicity of sulfoxaflor to A. gossypii was evaluated using the leaf‐dipping method, with minor modifications noted in previous studies [49, 50]. A stock solution of sulfoxaflor at 10000 mg L−1 was prepared in acetone and then diluted to the desired concentrations with deionized water containing 0.05% (v/v) Triton X‐100 for the bioassays. Cotton leaves measuring 22 mm in diameter were submerged in the insecticide solutions for 15 s and then air‐dried in a well‐ventilated space, utilizing water with 0.05% (v/v) Triton X‐100 as a control. Post‐drying, the cotton leaves were placed face down on the agar surface of a 12‐well cell culture plate. Thirty wingless adult aphids were introduced onto the plate and covered with Chinese art paper. Bioassays were conducted at a controlled temperature of 22 ± 1°C and under a 16:8 h (light: dark) photoperiod. Each experimental condition was replicated three times. The mortality rate of A. gossypii was determined after 48 h, and the median lethal concentration (LC50) values were determined using probit analysis in POLO Plus 2.0 statistical software (LeOra Software Inc., Berkeley, USA).


### Tissue Expression Profiles and FISH Localization of DopR1 and β‐1A in A. gossypii
  Apterous adult A. gossypii specimens were dissected into the head, epidermis, and internal contents (the residual material after extraction of the head and epidermis). During this procedure, approximately 600 individuals were processed, with three biological replicates per tissue site. The RNAiso Plus kit (Takara Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) was used to isolate the total RNA, and first‐strand cDNA synthesis was conducted employing the Hifair III first Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Yeasen Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). RT‐qPCR was performed according to the Hieff UNICON SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) guidelines, with a 10 µL reaction volume and a two‐phase amplification cycle: initial denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 10 s, annealing at 60°C for 30 s. The primers utilized are cataloged in Table S1.

  For fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), Cy3‐labeled probes targeting DopR1 and β‐1A were synthesized by Shanghai Gefan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Approximately 500 apterous adult cotton aphids were collected in RNase‐free tubes and treated with RNase‐free formaldehyde fixative for 24 h, followed by paraffin embedding post‐fixation. Sectioning was performed using an RM‐2016 microtome from Leica Instruments GmbH Co., Ltd. (Wetzlar, Germany). The FISH protocol, including cross‐hybridization steps, was followed according to the manufacturer's instructions provided by Shanghai Gefan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Slides were stained, and the results were observed under a confocal laser scanning microscope (ZEISS LSM 800 from Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany). A blank control without probe was used as a negative control under the same hybridization and imaging conditions to evaluate nonspecific background fluorescence. Digital FISH images were viewed, and representative fields were exported using CaseViewer software (3DHISTECH, Budapest, Hungary). Comparisons were qualitative and focused on the epidermal region for DopR1 and the head region for β‐1A. Yellow arrows were used to identify the anatomical regions examined in the representative images.


### Molecular Docking Analysis
  The three‐dimensional structures of DopR1 and β‐1A were predicted using AlphaFold 3 (https://deepmind.google/technologies/alphafold/alphafold‐server/). The structure of sulfoxaflor (Compound CID: 16723172) was sourced from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). The structure of sulfoxaflor was then optimized via energy minimization using ChemDraw (PerkinElmer, MA, 2019). The binding interactions of sulfoxaflor with DopR1 and β‐1A were analyzed using AutoDock Vina 1.1.2, and the requisite input files were prepared using AutoDock Tools 1.5.7. To improve docking precision, the exhaustiveness parameter was set to 24, while the default parameters were applied for Vina docking. The docking results were visualized using PyMOL 2.5.0 (https://pymol.org/2/).


### Heterologous Expression and Subcellular Localization of DopR1 and β‐1A in HEK293T Cells
  The open reading frames of DopR1 and β‐1A were cloned into the pcDNA3.1‐EGFP vector to generate EGFP‐tagged expression constructs. HEK293T cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin‐streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Cells were seeded onto confocal dishes and transfected with DopR1‐EGFP (DopR1), β‐1A‐EGFP (β‐1A) plasmids using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. At 24 h after transfection, cells were washed with PBS and stained with DAPI to visualize nuclei. Fluorescence signals were observed using a Zeiss LSM 800 confocal laser scanning microscope (Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany). EGFP fluorescence was used to indicate the cellular distribution of DopR1 and β‐1A fusion proteins.


### Measurement of Intracellular cAMP Content in HEK293T Cells
  To determine whether DopR1 and β‐1A mediate the cellular response to sulfoxaflor, HEK293T cells expressing DopR1‐EGFP (DopR1), β‐1A‐EGFP (β‐1A), or the control vector (Control) were treated with sulfoxaflor at final concentrations of 0, 10, 20, and 50 µM for 30 min. After treatment, cells were collected. The intracellular cAMP content was measured using a Human cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) ELISA Kit (Wuhan Saipei Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China). Absorbance was measured using a Spark multimode microplate reader (Tecan Group Ltd., Männedorf, Switzerland), and cAMP concentrations were calculated according to the standard curve. Each treatment contained three biological replicates.


### LC‐MS/MS Detection of Intracellular Sulfoxaflor
  To further evaluate sulfoxaflor accumulation in cells expressing DopR1 or β‐1A, HEK293T cells transfected with DopR1‐EGFP, β‐1A‐EGFP, or the control vector were treated with sulfoxaflor at 50 µM for 30 min. After treatment, cells were washed with cold PBS to remove extracellular sulfoxaflor and then collected. Sulfoxaflor was extracted using acetonitrile, followed by centrifugation at 12 000 × g, 10 min. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22‐µm membrane before LC‐MS/MS analysis. Sulfoxaflor was detected using high‐performance liquid chromatography‐tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC‐MS/MS) on a TSQ Altis triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA). Chromatographic separation was performed under gradient elution using mobile phase A, consisting of 0.1% formic acid in water, and mobile phase B, consisting of acetonitrile. The gradient program was as follows (A, v/v): 0.01 min, 3:7; 1.80 min, 8:2; 3.00 min, 8:2; 4.00 min, 3:7; and 5.00 min, 3:7. The flow rate was 0.2 mL min−1, and the detection temperature was 40°C. Mass spectrometric detection was conducted in positive electrospray ionization mode (ESI+). Quantification was performed using the transitions m/z 278.1→153.9 and 278.1→174.0, with a collision energy of 15 eV. An equal number of cells was used for each sample. The chromatographic peak area was used to compare the relative sulfoxaflor content among different cell samples.


### Biolayer Interferometry Assay
  The coding sequences of DopR1 and β‐1A were synthesized and cloned into pJL‐1 expression vector. The DopR1 construct was designed to express DopR1 fused with an N‐terminal Twin‐Strep‐tag II and a C‐terminal 6×His tag, whereas the β‐1A construct was designed to express β‐1A fused with a C‐terminal Strep‐tag II and 6×His tag. The theoretical molecular weights of the DopR1 and β‐1A fusion proteins were 56.9 kDa and 36.4 kDa, respectively. Both constructs contained a T7 promoter, ribosome‐binding site, T7 terminator, kanamycin resistance gene, and bacterial origin of replication, and were used for subsequent cell‐free membrane protein expression. DopR1 and β‐1A proteins were expressed using the PLDK002 cell‐free protein expression system (Suzhou PLD Technology Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China). Each 105 µL reaction contained 43 µL of component A, 35 µL of component B, and plasmid template at a final concentration of 5 ng µL−1. Brij35, a mild non‐ionic detergent, was added at a final concentration of 0.2% to improve the soluble expression of membrane proteins. The reactions were incubated at 25°C with shaking at 600 rpm for 16 h. After expression, the reaction mixtures were centrifuged at 12 000 × g for 2 min at 4°C, and the supernatants were collected for affinity purification. Following cell‐free expression, DopR1 and β‐1A were purified by sequential HisTrap excel and Strep affinity chromatography. The HisTrap excel column was equilibrated with 3–5 column volumes (CV) of equilibration buffer containing 50 mM Tris‐HCl and 300 mM NaCl, pH 7.8. After sample loading, the column was washed with 10–15 CV of equilibration buffer, and bound proteins were eluted using an imidazole of 250 mM. The eluate was subsequently applied to a Strep affinity column pre‐equilibrated with 3–5 CV of the same equilibration buffer. Following washing with 5–10 CV of equilibration buffer, bound proteins were eluted with buffer containing 50 mM biotin. The protein eluates were dialyzed for 19 h into storage buffer containing 50 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, 0.06% DDM, and 0.012% CHS, pH 7.5. The purified proteins were maintained in this buffer for storage, and the preparations were evaluated by SDS‐PAGE.

  Biolayer interferometry (BLI) assays were performed using an Octet RED96 molecular interaction system (ForteBio, Menlo Park, CA, USA) to evaluate the binding responses between sulfoxaflor and DopR1 or β‐1A. Purified receptor preparations were used as the immobilized ligands, and sulfoxaflor was used as the analyte. The assay buffer contained 50 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, 0.06% DDM, and 0.012% CHS, pH 7.5. His‐tagged DopR1 was diluted to 200 nM and immobilized on NTA biosensors. β‐1A was biotinylated using a Biotinylation Kit (Genemore, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Excess unreacted biotinylation reagent was removed using the desalting column supplied with the kit. Biotinylated β‐1A was diluted to 50 µg mL−1 and immobilized on streptavidin (SA) biosensors. Sulfoxaflor was prepared in assay buffer at concentrations of 12.5, 25, 50, 100, and 200 µm for DopR1 and 25, 50, 100, 200, 400, and 800 µm for β‐1A. Following ligand loading and baseline equilibration, biosensors were transferred to sulfoxaflor solutions for association and subsequently to analyte‐free assay buffer for dissociation. Association and dissociation were monitored for 180 s each for DopR1 and for 60 and 120 s, respectively, for β‐1A. An empty‐vector expression preparation lacking the target receptor was included as a background control. Measurements were performed at 30°C with shaking at 1000 rpm. The response from the reference channel was subtracted from the receptor‐channel response, followed by baseline alignment. For steady‐state affinity analysis, mean corrected responses over the final 5 s of the association phase were plotted against sulfoxaflor concentration and fitted to the equilibrium form of a 1:1 Langmuir binding model. Apparent equilibrium dissociation constants (K D) were reported with the standard errors of the fitted estimates. Figures were prepared using GraphPad Prism.


### Expression of DopR1, β‐1A, CREB, and CRTC in Response to Sulfoxaflor Treatment
  The sulfoxaflor‐resistant strain (SulR) was used for the sulfoxaflor induction assay. Apterous adults were treated with sulfoxaflor at LC25 (67.33 mg L−1) and LC50 (198.48 mg L−1) concentrations for 12 h, 24 h, 36 h, and 48 h. Fifteen live aphids were collected per treatment, with three biological replicates for each condition. RT‐qPCR was employed to assess the expression levels of the DopR1, β‐1A, CREB, and CRTC genes. The accession numbers of these genes are listed in Table S2, and the primers for the aforementioned genes are presented in detail in Table S1.


### Synthesis of dsRNA In Vitro
  Target gene fragments were amplified using RT‐PCR. The amplified fragments were then ligated into the pESI‐T vector (Yeasen Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) and introduced into the competent Escherichia coli DH5α strain for transformation. Plasmid DNA was subsequently isolated using the Plasmid Mini Kit I (D6943) (Omega Bio‐Tek, Norcross, USA), and its sequence fidelity was verified by Sanger sequencing (Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The target fragment was re‐amplified using the plasmid DNA as a template and transcribed to double‐stranded RNA (dsRNA) using the T7 RiboMAX express RNAi system (Promega Biotech Co., Ltd, Beijing, China). In parallel, dsRNA encoding the control green fluorescent protein (GFP) was synthesized under identical experimental conditions. The primers are provided in Table S3.


### In Vivo RNAi and Bioassays
  In the context of in vitro dsRNA feeding studies, sterilized glass tubes with dual apertures (3 cm in length and 2 cm in diameter) were employed, as delineated in prior research [19]. For each replicate, forty‐five apterous adult aphids were placed in a single tube for dsRNA feeding. Both dsRNA and dsGFP were incorporated into the artificial diets (0.5 mol L−1 sterile sucrose solution) at a final concentration of 200 ng µL−1. A control diet was formulated with an equivalent volume of nuclease‐free water. The feeding assay was replicated three times, with a total of 135 aphids per treatment. After a 48‐h feeding period, the treated A. gossypii were collected, and RT‐qPCR was performed to evaluate the efficiency of RNA interference. To assess the target dependence of the FISH signals, SulR aphids were treated with dsGFP, dsDopR1, or dsβ‐1A using the RNAi procedure described above. Knockdown of the corresponding transcript was verified by RT‐qPCR before FISH analysis. Sections from each treatment were hybridized with the DopR1 or β‐1A probe using the same FISH procedure described above.

  To evaluate the sensitivity of A. gossypii to sulfoxaflor after RNAi treatment, thirty adult aphids that had been fed artificial diets containing dsRNA and dsGFP for 48 h were transferred to cotton leaf discs treated with 198.48 mg L−1 (approximating the LC50 value) sulfoxaflor solution. Aphids fed with nuclease‐free water and those on dsGFP‐incorporated diets served as controls. The mortality rate was calculated after 48 h of exposure to sulfoxaflor, with each experimental condition at least replicated three times. All RNAi‐based functional assays were performed using the SulR strain, with dsGFP‐treated SulR aphids as the control.


### Measurement of Signaling Pathway Gene Expression and cAMP Content and PKA Activity in A. gossypii
  The expression levels of signaling pathway genes in the SulR and SS strains were quantified using RT‐qPCR. Thirty apterous adult aphids from each strain were collected in 1.5 mL RNase‐free tubes, with three biological replicates per strain. Total RNA isolation, first‐strand cDNA synthesis, and RT‐qPCR were performed as described in section 4.3. The primer sequences utilized are cataloged in Table S1.

  For cAMP content measurement, three replicates of each treatment were collected (SulR and SS, after RNAi, and insecticide induced), with approximately 50 mg of apterous adult aphids per replicate. The cAMP levels were measured following the instructions provided in the Insect cAMP Enzyme‐Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) Kit (Wuhan Saipei Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). Relative PKA activity was measured using the BBcellProbe PKA Activity Assay Kit (BestBio, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's endpoint protocol. Three independent biological replicates were analyzed for each experimental condition. Endpoint fluorescence readings were normalized to the total protein amount in each reaction and expressed as relative fluorescence units per milligram protein (RFU mg−1 protein). All samples within each comparison were assayed under the same reaction and fluorescence acquisition conditions.


### Inhibitor Experiments
  The leaf‐dipping method was employed to determine the maximum non‐lethal concentration (MNLC) of the cAMP pathway inhibitor (Bupivacaine hydrochloride, Cnscn Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Tianjin, China) and the PKA inhibitor (H‐89 2HCl, Adooq Bioscience LLC, Irvine, USA) for the SulR strain. Briefly, aphids were exposed to a series of inhibitor concentrations in the absence of sulfoxaflor, and mortality was recorded. The MNLCs of the two inhibitors (Bupivacaine hydrochloride: 100 mg L−1, H‐89 2HCl: 10 mg L−1) (Figure S12) were used to treat SulR aphids to assess their effects on sulfoxaflor susceptibility in the resistant strain.


### Determination of P450 Enzyme Activity
  After 48 h dietary regimen of dsRNA, treated aphids were swiftly immobilized in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for subsequent analysis of cytochrome P450 monooxygenase (P450) activity. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA protein assay, and enzyme activity was normalized to protein content. The assay for P450 enzymatic activity was performed employing a modified protocol based on established literature [19]. Briefly, the prepared aphid samples were homogenized into powder and suspended in 600 µL of 0.1 mol L−1 phosphate buffer (pH 7.5) containing 10% glycerol, 1 mmol L−1 DTT, 1 mmol L−1 PMSF, and 1 mmol L−1 EDTA. The homogenate was centrifuged at 14 000 r min−1 for 10 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was collected and centrifuged again under the same conditions for 5 min. The resulting supernatant was used as the crude enzyme extract. For the enzymatic reaction, 365 µL of 0.1 mol L−1 Tris‐HCl buffer (pH 7.8), 5 µL of 40 mmol L−1 7‐ethoxycoumarin, 10 µL of 10 mmol L−1 NADPH, and 125 µL of crude enzyme extract were sequentially added to the reaction tube. The mixture was incubated at 30°C with shaking for 15 min, and the reaction was immediately terminated on ice by adding 150 µL of 15% TCA. After centrifugation at 10 000 r min−1 for 2 min, 100 µL of the supernatant was mixed with 45 µL of 1.6 mol L−1 glycine‐NaOH buffer (pH 10.5) to adjust the final pH to approximately 10. Fluorescence of the product 7‐hydroxycoumarin was measured using a fluorescence microplate reader at an excitation wavelength of 358 nm and an emission wavelength of 456 nm. Enzyme activity was calculated based on a standard curve of 7‐hydroxycoumarin and expressed as nmol 7‐hydroxycoumarin/(min·mg protein). Each treatment was repeated thrice, with each repetition consisting of 200 wingless adult cotton aphids.


### Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assays
  Promoter amplification primers (Table S4) for the P450 genes were designed based on previously reported P450 gene sequences [19]. The promoter sequences were amplified using ApexHF HS DNA Polymerase CL (Accurate Biotechnology, Co., Ltd, Changsha, China), with cotton aphid DNA as the template. The transcription factor CREB binding sites were predicted by JASPAR (http://jaspar.genereg.net/). To investigate the regulatory effect of CREB on P450 promoters, the intact P450 promoters were cloned into the pGL3‐Basic vector using T4 Ligase (Takara Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd. Beijing, China) with SmaI and BglII as the digestion sites, and the full‐length CREB coding sequence was cloned into the insect expression vector pIEX‐4 using T4 Ligase with BamHI and KpnI as the digestion sites. Sf9 cell transfection and dual‐luciferase assays were performed according to a previously published method with minor modifications [51]. Briefly, the cell spreading medium (CSM) was prepared using 1.5 mL of Sf‐900 II SFM medium containing 10% FBS and 8.5 mL of Sf‐900 II SFM medium. Sf9 cells were seeded into 6‐well plates and incubated with CSM for 20 min to promote cell attachment. Sf9 cells were transiently co‐transfected using 8 µL of Cellfectin II reagent with 1 µg of the pGL3‐P450 promoter luciferase reporter plasmid, 1 µg of the pIEX‐CREB plasmid, and 0.1 µg of the internal Renilla luciferase control plasmid (phRL‐TK). Five hours post‐transfection, the transfection medium was replaced with SF‐900 SFM medium containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin‐streptomycin. After 48 h, 50 µL of cell lysates were assayed for firefly and Renilla luciferase activities using the Dual Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Kit II (Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Shanghai, China). Luciferase activity was detected under luminescence using a TECAN Spark multimode microplate reader (Tecan Group Ltd., Männedorf, Switzerland). Four treatments were included in the dual‐luciferase reporter assay: pGL3‐Basic, pGL3‐Basic + CREB, pGL3‐Promoter, and pGL3‐Promoter + CREB. The pGL3‐Promoter group was used to determine the basal activity of each P450 promoter in Sf9 cells, whereas the comparison between pGL3‐Promoter and pGL3‐Promoter + CREB was used to evaluate the additional activation mediated by exogenous CREB. The pGL3‐Basic + CREB group was included to assess whether CREB induced non‐specific activation in the absence of a P450 promoter. The P450 promoter‐reporter assays performed in the same experimental batch shared the pGL3‐Basic and pGL3‐Basic + CREB background controls. Three replicates were set up for each transfection.

  For CREB‐promoter docking prediction, the CREB DNA‐binding domain structure and promoter fragments containing predicted CRE‐like motifs were used as input files. Docking was performed using the same procedure as described in Section 4.4, and candidate binding regions were selected based on predicted binding score, sequence similarity to CRE motifs, and visual inspection of the CREB‐DNA interaction model.


### Transgenic Expression of CYP6CY13 and CYP6CY19 in D. melanogaster
  The transgenic D. melanogaster lines were generated by Fungene Biotech Co., Ltd. Briefly, the open reading frames (ORFs) of CYP6CY13 and CYP6CY19 were cloned into the pJFRC28‐10XUAS‐IVS‐GFP‐p10 donor vector to generate the UAS‐CYP6CY13 and UAS‐CYP6CY19 constructs. The recombinant constructs were injected into the attP40 D. melanogaster strain using the PhiC31‐mediated site‐specific integration system. Positive transformants were screened and confirmed by molecular identification to establish the UAS‐CYP6CY13 and UAS‐CYP6CY19 transgenic lines. To overexpress P450 genes, the UAS‐CYP6CY13 and UAS‐CYP6CY19 lines were crossed with the Tub‐Gal80ts; Tub‐Gal4 driver line, respectively. The resulting transgenic lines, Tub‐Gal80ts; Tub‐Gal4> UAS‐CYP6CY13 (GAL4‐UAS‐CYP6CY13) and Tub‐Gal80ts; Tub‐Gal4> UAS‐CYP6CY19 (GAL4‐UAS‐CYP6CY19), were used as P450‐overexpressing lines. Before bioassays, both the overexpression and control flies were subjected to heat induction at 30°C for 4 h to ensure the same pretreatment conditions. Male and female adult flies were collected separately and then introduced into each replicate at a fixed 1:1 sex ratio to minimize sex‐ratio bias. The UAS‐CYP6CY13 and UAS‐CYP6CY19 lines without the GAL4 driver served as the control group. The expression levels of CYP6CY13 and CYP6CY19 in transgenic flies were determined by RT‐qPCR, with DmRPL11 and DmRPL29 used as reference genes (Table S5).

  To evaluate the susceptibility of the GAL4‐UAS‐P450 lines to sulfoxaflor, feeding bioassays were conducted with minor modifications based on a previously reported method [52]. Sulfoxaflor solutions at concentrations of 1 and 2 mg L−1 were prepared in 5% sucrose. A layer of defatted cotton (approximately 1 cm thick) was placed at the bottom of a single‐open‐ended plastic tube (2.4 cm in diameter and 9.5 cm in length). Five milliliters of the prepared solution were gently dispensed dropwise onto the cotton until it was fully absorbed. The saturated cotton served as both a feeding substrate and a stopper to seal the tube, ensuring that no free liquid remained inside. The control group received 5% sucrose solution without insecticide under identical conditions. Ten female and ten male flies (3‐5 days old) were introduced into each tube. Mortality was assessed after 48 h. Each treatment consisted of three biological replicates (Figure 7A).


### Statistical Analysis
  The LC25 and LC50 values, along with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs), were calculated using POLO Plus 2.0 statistical software (LeOra Software Inc., Berkeley, USA). The relative quantification of gene expression levels was determined by implementing the 2−ΔΔCT methodology [53]. The firefly luciferase activity was standardized by the Renilla luciferase activity (ratio of firefly luciferase to Renilla luciferase activity) and expressed as relative luciferase activity. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 9.0.0 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, USA), and statistical analyses were performed using DPS software. For comparisons between two independent groups, Student's t‐tests were used. Comparisons of multiple treatments with a control were analyzed using one‐way ANOVA followed by Dunnett's multiple comparisons test, whereas comparisons among all groups were analyzed using one‐way ANOVA followed by Duncan's multiple range test. Experiments involving treatment and sampling time or sulfoxaflor concentration were analyzed using two‐way ANOVA, followed by Dunnett's comparisons with the corresponding control. Each target gene was analyzed separately. Asterisks indicate significant differences in specified pairwise or treatment‐versus‐control comparisons, whereas lowercase letters summarize multiple comparisons, with groups sharing at least one letter showing no significant difference and groups with no shared letters differing significantly (p < 0.05).
