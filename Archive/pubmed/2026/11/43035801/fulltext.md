# Integrative computational evaluation of tazemetostat for ARID1A-deficient anaplastic thyroid cancer via predicted polypharmacology and epigenetic target engagement


## Background
  Anaplastic thyroid carcinoma (ATC) is a rare, highly aggressive malignancy with limited therapeutic options, due largely to rapid metastasis and treatment resistance. A potential vulnerability in a subset of ATC cases is the loss of ARID1A, a core subunit of the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex, which induces a synthetic lethal dependency on EZH2, the catalytic subunit of the opposing PRC2 complex. Although tazemetostat was previously approved for selected cancers, recent safety concerns and market withdrawal have limited its direct clinical applicability; nevertheless, it remains a clinically characterized EZH2 inhibitor useful for mechanistic interrogation of ARID1A-EZH2 dependency. This study used tazemetostat as a mechanistic computational probe to evaluate EZH2-centered vulnerability and predicted protein interactions in ARID1A-deficient thyroid cancer.


## Results
  Through network pharmacology, we identified five hub targets central to ARID1A-deficient thyroid cancer progression: EZH2, EGFR, MAPK1, GSK3B, and CYP3A4. Molecular docking predicted favorable binding of tazemetostat to all targets, with docking scores ranging from ΔG =  − 9.04 kcal/mol (EZH2) to − 13.27 kcal/mol (EGFR). Triplicate molecular dynamics simulations supported overall pose stability across the five complexes, with target-dependent inter-replica variability. Statistical analysis indicated the strongest inter-replica consistency for EZH2 and MAPK1, whereas CYP3A4, EGFR, and GSK3B showed significant replica-level differences despite maintaining stable overall trajectory behavior.


## Conclusions
  Our findings support tazemetostat as a mechanistic computational probe for investigating EZH2-centered vulnerability in ARID1A-deficient anaplastic thyroid cancer, with predicted binding to EZH2 and additional cancer-relevant proteins. These results suggest potential polypharmacology; however, docking and MD simulations demonstrate binding stability rather than confirmed functional inhibition or pathway modulation. Experimental validation will be required to establish the biological significance of these predicted interactions.


## Introduction
  Anaplastic thyroid carcinoma (ATC) and its poorly differentiated counterparts represent the most lethal and treatment-refractory forms of thyroid cancer, accounting for a disproportionate share of thyroid cancer-related mortality despite their rarity [1]. Although they constitute only 1–2% of all thyroid cancers, these aggressive variants are responsible for the majority of thyroid cancer deaths globally and present a significant clinical challenge [2, 3]. ATC is characterized by rapid progression, aggressive metastasis, and a profound lack of effective therapies [4]. The dismal prognosis for patients stems largely from frequent dedifferentiation and resistance to conventional modalities, including radioiodine therapy and targeted agents against canonical drivers such as BRAF and RET [5–8]. This therapeutic void underscores an urgent need to identify biologically rational targets and therapeutic strategies for these aggressive tumors [9]. In this context, the growing understanding of cancer epigenetics, particularly dysregulation of chromatin remodeling complexes, offers a promising avenue for precision investigation [5, 10].

  A compelling therapeutic vulnerability arises from the synthetic lethal interaction between the SWI/SNF and Polycomb Repressive Complex 2 (PRC2) chromatin remodeling systems [11]. Inactivating mutations or loss of ARID1A, a core SWI/SNF subunit, results in impaired chromatin accessibility and transcriptional dysregulation, creating a compensatory dependency on the opposing PRC2 complex for transcriptional repression [12, 13]. This dependency is mediated by the catalytic subunit Enhancer of Zeste Homolog 2 (EZH2), which deposits the repressive histone mark H3K27me3 [14, 15]. Experimental literature has established that EZH2 inhibition can reduce this repressive mark and reactivate silenced genes in ARID1A-deficient cancers. Consequently, ARID1A-deficient cancer cells may display heightened sensitivity to pharmacologic EZH2 inhibition, a principle validated across multiple tumor types and supported by preclinical studies of EZH2 blockade in SWI/SNF-altered cancers [13, 16, 17]. The prior clinical development of tazemetostat underscores the druggability of EZH2; however, recent withdrawal due to hematologic second primary malignancy risk limits direct repurposing and shifts the relevance of the present work toward mechanistic target prioritization and validation [18–22].

  While the biological rationale for targeting EZH2 in ARID1A-deficient thyroid cancer is robust, the specific molecular mechanisms, signaling networks, and potential off-target effects of tazemetostat in the context of thyroid carcinoma remain poorly characterized [23, 24]. Current research lacks a comprehensive, system-level analysis that integrates network pharmacology, protein–protein interactions, and pathway enrichment to elucidate the pleiotropic effects of EZH2 inhibition beyond direct epigenetic modulation [25]. A detailed in silico investigation is essential to map the complex interplay between ARID1A loss, EZH2 activity, and downstream oncogenic signaling, thereby prioritizing the target and identifying potential combination strategies or safety considerations.

  No previous computational study has specifically investigated tazemetostat in ARID1A-deficient thyroid cancer using an integrative network pharmacology, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics simulation approach. Therefore, to address this gap, the present study applied an integrative computational workflow combining network pharmacology, protein–protein interaction analysis, pathway enrichment, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, and ADMET profiling, as summarized in Scheme 1. Using this approach, we identified five core targets EZH2, EGFR, MAPK1, GSK3B, and CYP3A4 as biologically relevant nodes associated with ARID1A-deficient thyroid cancer. Docking analysis predicted favorable binding of tazemetostat to these targets, with docking scores ranging from -9.04 kcal/mol for EZH2 to − 13.27 kcal/mol for EGFR, while triplicate MD simulations supported overall pose stability and generally low inter-replica variability across the complexes, although GSK3B showed comparatively greater backbone variation than the other targets. Collectively, these findings suggest a possible multi-target binding profile and predicted polypharmacology of tazemetostat in this setting.   To clarify the intended scope of this work, the present study should be interpreted primarily as a mechanistic, hypothesis-generating computational investigation rather than as a direct clinical repositioning proposal for tazemetostat. Tazemetostat was selected because it is a clinically characterized, selective EZH2 inhibitor and therefore provides a useful experimental molecular probe for interrogating the ARID1A-loss/EZH2-dependency axis in aggressive thyroid cancer. Within this framework, the scientific significance of studying tazemetostat lies in evaluating whether an EZH2-centered vulnerability in ARID1A-deficient ATC is supported by convergent network, docking, and molecular dynamics evidence, while also identifying additional predicted protein interactions that may inform future studies of pathway crosstalk, resistance, pharmacologic handling, and safer next-generation EZH2-directed strategies.


## Methodology
  This study investigates the mechanistic relevance of EZH2-centered target engagement in ARID1A-deficient thyroid cancer using tazemetostat (EPZ-6438) as a computational probe within an integrated workflow that includes network pharmacology, ADMET/toxicology analysis, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations.


## Network pharmacology and toxicology analysis
  PubChem was used to access the 2D and 3D molecular structures of tazemetostat. After that, the data was imported into ProTox-III and ADMETlab 2.0 to analyze the drug's pharmacokinetic and toxicological properties, like those related to absorption, metabolism and organ toxicity, by applying network algorithms [26].


## Target identification
  To identify disease-relevant genes associated with ARID1A-deficient thyroid cancer [26], information was collected from publicly available resources, including GeneCards [27], OMIM [28], and literature-supported thyroid cancer datasets. Only Homo sapiens entries were retained. Gene identifiers obtained from different sources were standardized to UniProt gene symbols, and duplicate entries were removed to ensure consistency across downstream analyses [29, 30]. The disease-gene collection step was intended for hypothesis generation using broad thyroid cancer resources and literature-supported aggressive thyroid cancer biology; it was not designed as a dedicated ATC-specific external validation analysis. Accordingly, the resulting gene set should be interpreted as a computationally prioritized disease-relevant set requiring further validation in ATC-focused public datasets and experimental systems.

  Tazemetostat-associated targets were compiled from DrugBank [31], SwissTargetPrediction [32, 33], ChEMBL [34], and published literature describing its EZH2 selectivity and reported off-target profiles. Because EZH2 is the established primary target of tazemetostat, it was included as a reference therapeutic node. The intersection between disease-associated genes and tazemetostat-related targets was then identified using Venn analysis to generate a candidate target set. For disease-gene prioritization, the GeneCards relevance score was used as an initial filtering parameter, and genes with a relevance score of  ≥ 15 were retained for downstream analysis (Table S11). Manual curation was subsequently applied to remove redundant or poorly annotated entries and to prioritize genes with literature-supported relevance to chromatin regulation, oncogenic signaling, or drug metabolism in aggressive thyroid cancer. The final prioritized genes were then carried forward for PPI network construction and subsequent topological analysis.


## Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis
  The overlap between tazemetostat-associated targets and thyroid cancer-related genes was identified using Venn analysis. After removal of duplicate entries, the overlapping genes were imported into the STRING database [29] to construct a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network. To retain only high-confidence interactions, a STRING confidence score threshold of > 0.7 was applied. The resulting network was then visualized and analyzed in Cytoscape [30, 35]. Topological parameters, including degree, betweenness centrality, closeness centrality, and Maximum Clique Centrality (MCC), were calculated to identify hub genes within the network. The MCC method was used to rank genes based on their interaction strength within the STRING-derived network and to identify the most central nodes according to their connectivity and contribution to the overall network structure. The top 10 genes were selected and ranked based on their interaction scores, which were normalized to account for network size and density. Final target prioritization was not based on network topology alone. Instead, the five core targets (EZH2, GSK3B, EGFR, MAPK1, and CYP3A4) were selected by integrating: (i) their presence in the disease–drug overlap set, (ii) high-confidence STRING connectivity, (iii) prominent network topology, and (iv) literature-supported biological relevance to chromatin regulation, oncogenic signaling, or pharmacologic handling of tazemetostat in aggressive thyroid cancer [25, 36, 37]


## Gene ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway enrichment
  The hub genes were then analyzed by GO (biological process, molecular function, cellular component) [38] and KEGG pathway enrichment by using tools like DAVID or Metascape [39]. Enrichment results showed the terms of chromatin remodeling (e.g., histone methylation, assembling of a nucleus) and kinase activity, apoptotic control, and metabolism of drugs.

  Significant KEGG enrichment pathways include: MAPK signaling, EGFR signaling, PI3K-AKT signaling, xenobiotic metabolism through CYP3A4, and epigenetic silencing through EZH2. It is known that EZH2 plays an essential role in controlling chromatin and is also linked to cancer [5, 40]. On the basis of such analyses, the five genes nominated were rationalized as therapeutic and mechanistic targets to be interrogated for further in silico studies.


## Molecular modeling and simulations
  Molecular docking was performed using MOE software 2022, with the 3D structures of EZH2, GSK3B, EGFR, MAPK1, and CYP3A4 obtained from the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/) and the ligand tazemetostat obtained from PubChem. Protein and ligand structures were prepared using MOE QuickPrep, followed by hydrogen addition and energy minimization. Docking was carried out at the reported active sites using the AMBER99 force field, and the top three poses were retained based on docking score. To assess docking protocol reliability, redocking of the native co-crystallized ligand was performed for each receptor using the same preparation steps and docking parameters as the main study. The agreement between native and redocked poses was evaluated by RMSD, with values below 2.0 Å considered acceptable for pose recovery (Table S12). These validation steps were intended to support structural plausibility of the docking protocol, but do not establish biological activity of the docked complexes.

  Molecular dynamics simulations were then performed for each selected protein–ligand complex using three independent replicas. The purpose of the MD analysis was to evaluate trajectory convergence and stability of the selected docked conformations over time, rather than to independently validate target inhibition or pathway modulation. For each replica, ligand RMSD, protein Cα RMSD, binding-pocket RMSD, and radius of gyration (Rg) were extracted from the 100 ns trajectories. Descriptive statistics were calculated over the full trajectory and over the equilibrated phase, defined consistently here as the last 25% of the trajectory. One-way ANOVA was used to assess inter-replica differences in time-averaged metrics, and Tukey’s HSD test was applied when ANOVA indicated significance. Thus, non-significant ANOVA results were interpreted as supportive of acceptable inter-replica consistency, whereas significant ANOVA/Tukey results were interpreted as evidence of replica-level differences rather than reproducibility. Target prioritization was performed using relevance scoring, filtering criteria, manual curation, and network topology, with the workflow summarized in Table S11. Pearson correlation coefficients between replicas were calculated for each metric using the equilibrated phase to assess temporal similarity. Replica-averaged time series were down-sampled to 1 ns intervals for cross-protein comparison. ANOVA/Tukey analysis was used to assess differences in replica-averaged metrics, whereas Pearson correlation was used to evaluate temporal similarity of trajectory patterns between replicas during the equilibrated phase. All analyses were conducted in Python using NumPy, Pandas, SciPy, and Statsmodels. For each one-way ANOVA, F-statistics, degrees of freedom, exact p-values, and effect sizes (η2 and ω2) were calculated for the equilibrated phase (last 25% of each trajectory), and Tukey’s HSD outputs were further summarized using adjusted p-values and pairwise effect estimates.


## Results

### Results of network pharmacology
  Network pharmacology is a useful approach for studying complex disease-related and drug-response mechanisms by considering interactions among genes, proteins, and pathways [41]. In this study, network pharmacology was used to investigate the mechanistic relevance of tazemetostat in ARID1A-deficient thyroid cancer. This analysis highlighted major molecular nodes and signaling pathways computationally associated with the ARID1A-deficient thyroid cancer context through several stages of analysis [42].


## Preliminary network analysis of the mechanistic relevance of tazemetostat
  Toxicity prediction using ProTox-III was performed to evaluate the organ-specific safety profile of tazemetostat. The compound was predicted to have a moderate hepatotoxicity risk (probability: 0.68), while possible neurotoxic, nephrotoxic, and respiratory toxic liabilities were predicted with probabilities of 0.75, 0.52, and 0.75, respectively (Table S14). These results should be interpreted as predictive rather than definitive, indicating that although hepatotoxicity may require attention, neurological, renal, respiratory, and metabolism-related safety considerations warrant attention. In silico ADMET profiling using ProTox-III and ADMETlab 2.0 further suggested acceptable drug-like and pharmacokinetic properties, including predicted intestinal absorption and blood–brain barrier permeability, which supported the inclusion of tazemetostat for further computational investigation (Fig. 1) [43].Fig. 1Radar chart of predicted toxicity of tazemetostat

  Importantly, this preliminary network pharmacology and ADMET analyses were used to contextualize, rather than determine, candidate prioritization. The identification of CYP3A4 should not be interpreted as a favorable therapeutic interaction; instead, it suggests pharmacokinetic complexity, since CYP3A4-mediated metabolism may influence drug clearance, bioavailability, and the risk of drug–drug interactions when tazemetostat is co-administered with other CYP3A4 substrates or inhibitors. Therefore, this finding should be regarded as a pharmacologic consideration requiring cautious translational interpretation and possible clinical monitoring, rather than as a direct therapeutic advantage. However, the primary rationale for selecting tazemetostat was not its toxicity or ADMET profile, but its established role as a selective EZH2 inhibitor and the strong biological rationale linking ARID1A loss to EZH2 dependency in SWI/SNF-deficient cancers. ARID1A deficiency creates a compensatory reliance on PRC2/EZH2-mediated repression, providing the mechanistic basis for synthetic lethality. This rationale is further supported by preclinical evidence of EZH2 inhibitor efficacy in thyroid cancer models, our docking-based demonstration of stable predicted binding to ATC-relevant drivers including EGFR and MAPK1, the potential to overcome resistance to conventional redifferentiation therapies, and emerging links to immune modulation within the tumor microenvironment. Together, these findings provide the mechanistic foundation for selecting tazemetostat for in silico analysis in ARID1A-deficient thyroid cancer.


## Target gene identification via venn overlap
  The first step involved retrieving disease-associated genes from publicly available resources, including GeneCards, OMIM, and literature-supported thyroid cancer resources, to generate a broad disease-relevant gene set for hypothesis generation. This collection step was intended to identify genes broadly associated with aggressive thyroid cancer biology and was not designed as a dedicated ATC-specific external validation analysis. ARID1A mutations are well-established drivers of chromatin remodeling defects, leading to transcriptional dysregulation and contributing to tumor progression.

  Simultaneously, 13 molecular targets of tazemetostat (primarily EZH2) were obtained from sources such as DrugBank and SwissTargetPrediction. EZH2 is a component of Polycomb Repressive Complex 2 (PRC2) that is involved in silencing tumor suppressor genes through histone H3 lysine 27 methylation. To identify potential mechanistic nodes, we applied Venn analysis to determine the intersection between ARID1A-deficient thyroid cancer-related genes and tazemetostat-related targets (Fig. 2a). The intersection analysis identified 188 overlapping targets between tazemetostat and thyroid cancer-associated genes (Fig. 2). Because this broad overlap could reduce specificity, additional filtering criteria, including functional annotation and pathway enrichment analysis, were applied to reduce redundancy and focus on biologically relevant genes. This analysis prioritized five target genes: EZH2, GSK3B, EGFR, MAPK1, and CYP3A4, which were strongly connected to disease-relevant network modules and were prioritized as computationally associated targets for further investigation.   To improve transparency and reproducibility, the manual curation workflow was systematically summarized. Drug-associated targets were collected from the selected drug-related database sources, while disease-associated targets were retrieved from the relevant disease databases (Fig. S3). ## Protein–protein interaction network analysis (PPI)
  In further examining the association between these target genes, and their capabilities to interact with one another in relation to ARID1A-deficient thyroid cancer, we developed a Protein–Protein Interaction (PPI) network with the help of STRING database [45]. This is a network-based method which enables visualization and quantification of gene/protein interactions using computational data. In the PPI analysis, entered five identified genes (EZH2, GSK3B, EGFR, MAPK1, and CYP3A4) and calculated the interaction between them with a minimum confidence threshold of 0.7 (which is usually used to find the interaction between proteins) (Fig. 2b, c). The network topology was then visualized and analyzed further with the help of Cytoscape [35] and this provided an insight into relationship between these targets [46].

  The useful outcome of the PPI network analysis was that it showed the identification of EZH2 and EGFR as the significant hubs of the network. The degree (number of direct interactions) and betweenness centrality of both genes are high, which can be interpreted as evidence that they function as major regulators of thyroid cancer-related cellular processes. Topological analysis of the STRING-derived PPI network further highlighted EZH2, EGFR, MAPK1, GSK3B, and CYP3A4 as prominent nodes within the disease–drug overlap network. EZH2 and EGFR showed particularly strong network prominence, supporting their relevance to chromatin regulation and growth-factor signaling in aggressive thyroid cancer. MAPK1 and GSK3B were retained because of their connectivity within kinase-associated signaling modules, whereas CYP3A4 was interpreted more cautiously as a pharmacology-related node with potential relevance to drug disposition rather than as an equally established driver of ATC biology. EZH2, an epigenetic-regulating protein, and EGFR, one of the key proteins in cell signaling and survival, may act in concert and contribute to the aggressive phenotype of ARID1A-deficient thyroid tumors. Further, GSK3B and MAPK1 were identified as biologically relevant connected nodes that may contribute to downstream signaling effects through their interactions with the major hubs. This discussion points to the complexity of the effects of tazemetostat, which may extend beyond its primary target EZH2 to a broader regulatory network of cancer-related genes. However, final target prioritization integrated network topology with literature-supported biological relevance rather than relying on centrality metrics alone [47]. In addition to network topology, Maximum Clique Centrality (MCC) was used. These additional methods further support the prioritization of the topologically selected targets, offering a more comprehensive and biologically grounded prioritization.


## Enrichment analysis of gene ontology (GO)
  Subsequently, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was done to gain a more insight into the biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components of the identified target genes. The GO analysis is extensively applied to classify genes and their functions into hierarchical terms that can give insights into mechanism involved in the disease. In this analysis, relied on the tools like DAVID that is designed to interpret the gene lists effectively and map them to existing biological processes as well as Metascape.

  The most enriched biological process terms in the GO analysis were related to cancer biology, including chromatin organization, histone methylation, kinase activity, apoptosis regulation, response to external stimuli, tumor growth modulation, and cellular stress response (Fig. 3). In particular, enrichment of chromatin organization and histone methylation is consistent with EZH2-mediated PRC2 biology, which is commonly dysregulated in cancer [48]. Kinase activity involving genes such as GSK3B and MAPK1 is relevant to cellular responses, proliferation, and survival signaling. ## KEGG pathway enrichment analysis
  In order to further explore the biological context of the prioritized targets in ARID1A-deficient thyroid cancer, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed [49]. KEGG analysis provides a systems-level view of the molecular pathways and networks in which the identified targets may participate. The enriched pathways included MAPK signaling, EGFR signaling, drug metabolism through cytochrome P450 (CYP3A4), and epigenetic regulation related to PRC2/EZH2 [50]. Dominant pathways also included neuroactive ligand–receptor interaction, calcium signaling, Rap1 signaling, and cAMP signaling. MAPK signaling is a well-established pathway involved in cell growth, differentiation, and survival, and its dysregulation is common in thyroid cancer. MAPK1, a central effector in this pathway, is associated with oncogenic processes such as tumor growth and metastasis. Similarly, EGFR signaling is important in growth factor response and has been implicated in thyroid cancer progression and treatment resistance [51]. The enrichment of CYP3A4-related pathways suggests potential pharmacokinetic relevance, including possible effects on drug metabolism and drug–drug interaction risk, rather than a directly favorable therapeutic interaction. Likewise, enrichment of PRC2/EZH2-related terms is consistent with the known biological role of EZH2-mediated H3K27me3 deposition and transcriptional repression in ARID1A-deficient cancers [52]. However, these pathway associations should not be interpreted as direct evidence that tazemetostat functionally regulates these pathways or reactivates silenced tumor suppressor genes in the present study. Rather, the enrichment results provide biologically plausible context for the prioritized targets and support the hypothesis that EZH2-centered epigenetic regulation and kinase-associated signaling pathways may both be relevant to ARID1A-deficient thyroid cancer biology.   Pairwise Pearson correlation analysis of the equilibrated phase of the triplicate MD simulations demonstrated target- and metric-dependent agreement among replicas (Table S13; Figure S1). The strongest temporal reproducibility was observed for the EGFR–tazemetostat complex, particularly for ligand RMSD (r = 0.847–0.927), pocket RMSD (r = 0.609–0.798), backbone RMSD (r = 0.668–0.833), and radius of gyration (r = 0.553–0.764). CYP3A4 also showed strong ligand RMSD agreement (r = 0.801–0.895), with moderate agreement for pocket RMSD (r = 0.445–0.661) and backbone RMSD (r = 0.525–0.725). EZH2 displayed moderate ligand RMSD reproducibility (r = 0.588–0.770), with weaker agreement for pocket RMSD (r = 0.229–0.469) and backbone RMSD (r = 0.282–0.544). GSK3B showed moderate agreement for ligand RMSD (r = 0.573–0.765) and pocket RMSD (r = 0.410–0.641), but weaker agreement for backbone RMSD (r = 0.157–0.410). MAPK1 likewise displayed moderate ligand RMSD (r = 0.346–0.603) and pocket RMSD (r = 0.310–0.568) agreement, with weaker backbone RMSD reproducibility (r = 0.195–0.430). In contrast, Rg correlations were weak or inconsistent for CYP3A4, EZH2, and MAPK1, indicating that global compactness fluctuations were less reproducible than local binding-stability metrics.


## Residue-level flexibility analysis by RMSF
  RMSF analysis provided residue-level insight into the dynamic behavior of all five protein–tazemetostat complexes. In the CYP3A4 complex (Fig. S2a), most residues fluctuated within approximately 0.7–1.0 Å, with several localized peaks and a maximum fluctuation approaching ~ 1.8 Å, indicating the highest regional flexibility among the five systems while still supporting preservation of the overall protein framework. The EGFR complex (Fig. S2b) showed a comparatively restrained fluctuation pattern, with most residues remaining near 0.65–0.85 Å and only a few sharp peaks reaching approximately 1.4–1.6 Å, consistent with a stable global fold and limited local mobility. The EZH2 complex (Fig. S2c) displayed repeated localized peaks up to about 1.5–1.6 Å superimposed on a stable low-to-moderate baseline, suggesting the presence of several flexible segments without evidence of widespread structural destabilization. The GSK3B complex (Fig. S2d) exhibited the lowest overall residue-level mobility, with most residues remaining within approximately 0.6–0.8 Å and only a few local peaks near 1.2–1.35 Å, indicating a comparatively rigid complex throughout the simulation. The MAPK1 complex (Fig. S2e) showed a stable baseline with several pronounced peaks reaching approximately 1.4–1.55 Å, again suggesting localized flexibility superimposed on an otherwise preserved protein architecture. Collectively, these RMSF profiles indicate that the five complexes maintained predominantly low-to-moderate residue-level fluctuations, with enhanced motion confined to discrete segments rather than distributed broadly across the structures, supporting the conclusion that the observed MD dynamics were mainly local rather than global and were fully consistent with the RMSD and radius of gyration analyses showing overall structural stability.


## Discussion
  The presented integrated network pharmacology, molecular docking, and simulation analyses provide mechanistic insight into why EZH2-centered investigation may be relevant in ARID1A-deficient thyroid cancer. ARID1A, a component of the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex, is increasingly linked to aggressive thyroid cancer phenotypes, chromatin compaction, lineage dedifferentiation, and treatment resistance. For example, ARID1A loss in BRAF V600E-mutant thyroid models has been associated with reduced thyroid differentiation gene accessibility and worse survival [54]. In this context, the reliance of ARID1A-deficient cells on opposing PRC2/EZH2 activity represents a plausible vulnerability. Previous literature has shown synthetic lethality between ARID1A mutation and EZH2 inhibition in other cancers [55].

  Our network pharmacology analysis identified five central genes EZH2, GSK3B, EGFR, MAPK1, and CYP3A4 linking ARID1A loss to the predicted actions of tazemetostat. Among these, EZH2 emerged as the most biologically and mechanistically relevant target because of its direct role in PRC2-mediated H3K27 trimethylation, gene silencing, and the established synthetic lethal relationship with ARID1A deficiency. Its central importance was further supported by its prominent hub status in the PPI network, while docking and MD simulations suggested stable predicted interaction with tazemetostat. The mechanistic importance of EZH2 in this study is closely linked to its role in H3K27me3-mediated transcriptional repression. In the setting of ARID1A deficiency, loss of SWI/SNF chromatin-remodeling activity increases dependence on PRC2-mediated repression, allowing EZH2 to maintain silencing of genes involved in differentiation and tumor suppression. Thus, the identification of EZH2 as a central node in the present network is not only topological but also biologically coherent with the known ARID1A–PRC2 synthetic lethal framework. Within this context, the predicted interaction of tazemetostat with EZH2 is relevant because it is consistent with the hypothesis that EZH2 inhibition could reduce repressive chromatin signaling and potentially alleviate lineage repression in aggressive thyroid cancer, although direct effects on H3K27me3 and downstream transcription were not measured in the present computational study. EGFR and MAPK1 also appear biologically plausible in the ATC context, given the well-established importance of MAPK-pathway signaling in aggressive thyroid tumor progression. By contrast, GSK3B and especially CYP3A4 should be interpreted more cautiously as broader network-associated or pharmacology-related nodes rather than equally established primary drivers of ATC biology. Although the docking results suggested stable predicted interactions of tazemetostat with several kinase-associated targets, these observations should be interpreted cautiously. Tazemetostat is primarily recognized as a relatively selective EZH2 inhibitor rather than as an established multi-kinase scaffold. Therefore, the predicted binding to EGFR, MAPK1, and GSK3B is best interpreted as a hypothesis-generating observation of potential polypharmacology within the present computational framework, rather than as evidence of known scaffold-driven kinase inhibition. In addition, such kinase-associated docking signals may include false positives inherent to structure-based screening approaches and therefore require biochemical and cellular validation to establish functional relevance. Overall, these findings support the disease relevance of the identified network, while additional validation using ATC-focused public datasets would further strengthen the biological interpretation [56].

  Mechanistic plausibility is also supported with the help of gene ontology and KEGG enrichment. The observed enrichment of the chromatin organization and histone methylation is exactly in line with the biology of ARID1A/PRC2 interaction known in the case of SWI/SNF loss, repressive chromatin marks become abundant, differentiation programs are silenced, and tumors become more aggressive. The activation of the kinase activity and the MAPK signaling pathways (MAPK1, EGFR) confirms the interdependence of even epigenetically regulated thyroid cancers on conventional survival/proliferation signaling pathways, and creates the potential that EZH2 inhibition has off-target effects beyond simple chromatin re-setting [54]. The presence of CYP3A4 in the network is of interest in terms of translatability: the significant drug metabolism gene poses the notion that the pharmacokinetics, off target metabolism, and possible drug interactions of tazemetostat should be closely considered in thyroid cancer patients who also may be treated with several therapies.

  Together, these mechanistic layers suggest a testable hypothesis: in ARID1A-deficient thyroid cancer, loss of SWI/SNF activity may increase dependence on EZH2-mediated chromatin repression and silencing of differentiation-associated tumor suppressor programs. EZH2 inhibition by tazemetostat may therefore provide a model for investigating whether reduced repressive chromatin signaling can restore thyroid lineage genes such as PAX8 and NKX2-1 or promote apoptosis. However, these effects were not measured in the present computational study. Similarly, the predicted links to EGFR/MAPK1 signaling and CYP3A4-mediated drug metabolism should be interpreted as hypotheses regarding broader pathway interactions that require experimental validation, not as evidence of comprehensive anti-tumor activity.

  The molecular docking and MD simulations of tazemetostat binding to key proteins involved in ARID1A-deficient thyroid cancer provide valuable insights into the stability and dynamics of these complexes. The docking analysis reveals favorable binding poses for tazemetostat with all five target proteins: CYP3A4, EGFR, EZH2, GSK3B, and MAPK1. For CYP3A4, the best-ranked pose showed a docking score of ΔG = − 12.28 kcal/mol, with predicted hydrogen-bond interactions involving Gln-92 and hydrophobic interactions involving Phe-70, Arg-118, and Pro-120. Similarly, for EGFR, (ΔG = − 13.27 kcal/mol) shows key interactions with hydrophobic residues like Leu-694, Val-702, and Leu-820, as well as polar interactions with Thr-766 and Asn-818. In EZH2, (ΔG = − 9.04 kcal/mol) is stabilized by hydrophobic contacts with Val-139, Ala-141, and Tyr-138, and polar interactions with Asp-140 and Cys-182. For GSK3B, (ΔG = − 13.07 kcal/mol) is stabilized by hydrogen bonds with Tyr-221 and Asp-264, and hydrophobic contacts with Val-263, Ser-66, and Gly-202. Finally, for MAPK1, (ΔG = − 12.62 kcal/mol) is stabilized by hydrophobic interactions with Leu-170, Leu-156, and Met-107, alongside polar interactions with Asn-154 and Ser-153.

  The MD simulations provide further support by showing that, during the equilibration phase (typically 0–3000 configurations), the ligand and protein undergo significant adjustments, especially in CYP3A4 (0–3000 configurations), EGFR (0–5000 configurations), and EZH2 (0–5000 configurations). After these fluctuations, the RMSD of the ligand, protein, and pocket stabilizes, indicating that the systems have reached an optimal binding conformation. Statistical analysis of MD simulations for five protein–drug complexes (CYP3A4, EGFR, EZH2, GSK3B, and MAPK1) revealed strong reproducibility across replicates, particularly for EZH2 and MAPK1, with low variability in RMSD values and no significant differences between replicates (ANOVA p > 0.05). CYP3A4 exhibited the lowest RMSD values across all metrics, while GSK3B showed the highest variability, suggesting greater sensitivity to initial conditions or conformational dynamics. Ligand RMSD, pocket RMSD, and backbone RMSD were more stable for EZH2, while Rg remained consistently low across all complexes. These findings highlight the overall stability of the systems, with GSK3B showing higher replicate variability, warranting further exploration. Pearson correlation analysis showed target- and metric-dependent inter-replica reproducibility, with EGFR displaying the strongest overall temporal agreement, while EZH2, GSK3B, and MAPK1 showed comparatively more variable behavior for some descriptors. In general, ligand RMSD was more reproducible across replicas than radius of gyration, suggesting that local binding stability was more consistent than global compactness fluctuations. Consistent with this, RMSF analysis revealed predominantly low-to-moderate residue-level fluctuations in all five complexes, with higher mobility restricted to localized segments rather than the overall protein structures. Together, these findings support stable protein–ligand complex formation with target-specific local flexibility rather than global structural destabilization.

  Although the docking and MD results support stable and energetically favorable binding of tazemetostat to EZH2, EGFR, MAPK1, GSK3B, and CYP3A4, these computational observations should not be interpreted as direct evidence of functional inhibition or pathway modulation. Rather, they indicate a predicted multi-target binding profile and potential polypharmacology that may be relevant to ARID1A-deficient thyroid cancer. The biological consequences of these predicted interactions, including possible effects on kinase activity, chromatin regulation, cell survival, and differentiation, remain to be tested experimentally.

  This positioning is important for interpreting the role of tazemetostat in the present article. The compound is not proposed here as an immediately translatable ATC therapy; rather, it is used as a well-defined EZH2-inhibitor scaffold to test the plausibility of EZH2-centered target engagement in an ARID1A-deficient disease context. The novel insight generated by this approach is the integration of the canonical ARID1A-EZH2 synthetic-lethal relationship with predicted links to EGFR/MAPK signaling, GSK3B-associated stress and survival pathways, and CYP3A4-mediated pharmacologic handling. These predicted non-EZH2 interactions should be regarded as hypothesis-generating polypharmacology signals, not as evidence that tazemetostat functions as a proven multi-kinase inhibitor.

  Anaplastic thyroid carcinoma (ATC) is a near-uniformly lethal malignancy with a median survival of only 3–6 months. Current treatment options are limited to multimodal approaches, including surgery, radiation, and systemic therapies, and resistance to conventional agents is nearly universal. Although tazemetostat is known to be rapidly absorbed following oral administration, with peak plasma concentrations achieved within 1–2 h, and is primarily metabolized by CYP3A4 with subsequent hepatobiliary excretion, these pharmacokinetic properties should be interpreted as contextual background rather than as justification for direct clinical use in ATC. Instead, the present findings support mechanistic investigation of EZH2 dependency in ARID1A-deficient ATC and motivate future preclinical evaluation of alternative EZH2 inhibitors or safer rational combination strategies [25]. Importantly, since the completion of our computational analyses, tazemetostat has been subject to voluntary market withdrawal in major approved markets, including withdrawal from the U.S. market and all Ipsen markets, due to an increased risk of hematologic second primary malignancies observed in clinical trials [21, 22]. While this safety concern precludes immediate clinical application of tazemetostat in ATC, our findings retain mechanistic value. The synthetic lethal dependency of ARID1A-deficient thyroid cancers on EZH2 remains a biologically relevant vulnerability, and the predicted multi-target interactions identified here may inform the evaluation of next-generation EZH2 inhibitors with improved safety profiles or alternative EZH2-directed strategies.

  The implication of the recent tazemetostat safety signal is therefore that the translational emphasis of this manuscript should shift away from direct repurposing of tazemetostat and toward validation of the ARID1A-EZH2 therapeutic axis itself. The observed risk of secondary hematologic malignancies substantially weakens the feasibility of immediate clinical application, even in a disease with the poor prognosis of ATC. Nevertheless, these safety concerns do not negate the biological relevance of EZH2 dependency in ARID1A-deficient tumors. Instead, they indicate that future work should prioritize safer EZH2 inhibitors, optimized dosing or combination approaches, and rigorous preclinical risk–benefit assessment before any ATC-directed clinical translation is considered [21, 22].

  It is important to recognize that ARID1A mutations occur in only a subset of ATC cases [8, 56]. Therefore, the EZH2-directed mechanistic strategy discussed here would be relevant only to a molecularly defined subgroup rather than all ATC patients. Furthermore, ARID1A alterations frequently co-occur with other driver mutations commonly found in ATC, including BRAF V600E, TP53, TERT promoter, and PIK3CA [8, 56]. The synthetic lethal dependency on EZH2 has been validated in the context of BRAF V600E co-mutation in mouse models [56]. Patient stratification based on ARID1A status, ideally through immunohistochemistry or next-generation sequencing, would be essential for any future clinical trial of EZH2 inhibitors in ATC. While validation using publicly available datasets such as TCGA or cBioPortal could further support our findings, such analyses are beyond the scope of the current computational study and represent an important direction for future investigation.

  Resistance mechanisms to EZH2 inhibition may include compensatory EZH1 upregulation, sustained EGFR/MAPK signaling, CYP3A4-mediated metabolic adaptation, and acquired EZH2 mutations or ARID1A reversion. To overcome resistance, rational combinations are proposed: EZH2 inhibitor plus MEK/EGFR inhibitor to block MAPK escape; plus, redifferentiation therapy (e.g., retinoic acid) to reverse lineage dedifferentiation; plus, immune checkpoint blockade to enhance immunogenicity; or plus PARP inhibitor to exploit replication stress. Given tazemetostat’s withdrawal due to secondary malignancy risk, alternative EZH2 inhibitors (e.g., valemetostat) may be preferred for testing these combinations in ARID1A-deficient ATC models. Our computational framework supports EZH2 as a mechanistically relevant target with predicted stable binding to EZH2, EGFR, MAPK1, GSK3B, and CYP3A4; however, docking and MD simulations indicate binding stability rather than confirmed functional inhibition. Future experimental work must test these combinations while monitoring resistance mechanisms to determine durable therapeutic benefit.


## Limitations
  This study is entirely computational and lacks biochemical, cellular, and in vivo validation. Molecular docking predicts plausible binding poses, and molecular dynamics simulations assess the stability of those poses over time; however, neither approach establishes target inhibition, pathway modulation, or therapeutic efficacy. Although docking validation and MD stability analyses support the structural plausibility of the selected complexes, they do not prove that the starting poses correspond to biologically active inhibitory conformations. Likewise, docking scores and predicted binding energies are useful for comparative ranking within the computational workflow but should not be interpreted as direct surrogates of biological potency or efficacy. Our study also does not directly assess H3K27me3 levels, kinase phosphorylation, or other functional epigenetic and signaling outcomes, which require experimental testing. In addition, the recent withdrawal of tazemetostat from clinical use due to secondary malignancy risks limits its direct translational potential. Therefore, our findings are best viewed as a hypothesis-generating mechanistic framework supporting EZH2 as a therapeutic target in ARID1A-deficient ATC, rather than as an endorsement of tazemetostat itself. Future work should validate the predicted interactions and functional consequences using biochemical binding assays, cellular target-engagement studies, and ARID1A-stratified in vitro and in vivo models. All ADMET results presented here are based solely on in silico predictions and should be interpreted as preliminary assessments rather than definitive clinical safety data.

  A further limitation is the absence of external validation using ATC-specific datasets stratified by ARID1A status. The current disease-gene and network analyses draw on broad thyroid cancer resources and literature-supported aggressive thyroid cancer biology; therefore, the prioritized targets should not be interpreted as independently validated ATC-specific markers. Future studies should test the expression, dependency, and clinical relevance of EZH2, EGFR, MAPK1, GSK3B, and CYP3A4 in ATC-focused transcriptomic, genomic, and proteomic datasets, ideally incorporating ARID1A alteration status and common co-mutations such as BRAF, TP53, TERT promoter, and PIK3CA. Experimental validation should further assess H3K27me3 modulation, thyroid-lineage marker restoration, apoptosis, and pathway changes after EZH2 inhibition in ARID1A-deficient ATC cell lines, organoids, or xenograft models.


## Conclusion
  This study provides a computational, hypothesis-generating framework supporting the biological relevance of EZH2-centered targeting in ARID1A-deficient thyroid cancer. Network pharmacology, PPI analysis, and functional enrichment identified EZH2, EGFR, MAPK1, GSK3B, and CYP3A4 as proteins connected to chromatin regulation, oncogenic signaling, and pharmacologic handling. Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations predicted stable interactions of tazemetostat with these targets, suggesting a possible EZH2-centered polypharmacology profile. However, these results demonstrate predicted binding stability rather than confirmed functional inhibition, pathway modulation, or therapeutic efficacy. In light of recent safety concerns regarding tazemetostat, the findings should not be interpreted as support for direct clinical use in ATC, but rather as a mechanistic rationale for further validation of the ARID1A-EZH2 axis, safer EZH2 inhibitors, and rational combination strategies in ATC-specific experimental models and datasets.
