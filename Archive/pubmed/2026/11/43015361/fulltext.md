# P53-assisted METTL14-mediated m⁶A modification of SLC7A11 drives ferroptosis and inflammation in polycystic ovary syndrome


## Abstract
  Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is characterized by chronic inflammation and follicular arrest, yet the molecular drivers linking these pathologies to granulosa cell (GC) death remain elusive. Here, we identify a ferroptosis-inflammation cascade as a critical determinant of ovarian dysfunction in PCOS. We demonstrate that the N⁶-methyladenosine (m⁶A) methyltransferase METTL14 is aberrantly upregulated in human PCOS GCs, correlating with oxidative damage. Mechanistically, we uncover a non-canonical paradigm where upregulated P53 functions as an essential RNA-binding cofactor, recruiting METTL14 to the SLC7A11 transcript. Validated by AlphaFold3 modeling, this METTL14-p53 complex catalyzes SLC7A11 m⁶A hypermethylation and mRNA destabilization. The resulting SLC7A11 deficiency dismantles antioxidant defenses, driving ferroptosis and amplifying local inflammation. Crucially, in a DHEA-induced PCOS rat model, genetic ablation of Tp53 severed this pathogenic axis, restoring redox balance, suppressing inflammation, and reversing the polycystic phenotype. Collectively, our findings reveal a pivotal epigenetic mechanism linking m⁶A reprogramming, iron-dependent cell death, and inflammation, highlighting the METTL14-p53-SLC7A11 axis as a therapeutic target in PCOS.

  


## Introduction
  Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a common and heterogeneous endocrine disorder affecting approximately 10–13% of reproductive-aged women worldwide1,2. Clinically defined by hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, and polycystic ovarian morphology3, PCOS is now understood not only as a reproductive disorder but as a systemic condition associated with chronic low-grade inflammation and metabolic dysregulation4–6. Although current treatments range from lifestyle modification to pharmacotherapy, the etiology of PCOS remains unclear, and therapeutic outcomes are often unsatisfactory5,7. A major obstacle to effective treatment is the incomplete understanding of the molecular mechanisms that initiate the self-perpetuating cycle of ovarian oxidative stress and local inflammation, both of which are central to follicular dysplasia.

  Ovarian granulosa cells (GCs) are essential for oocyte maturation and steroidogenesis, processes that depend on robust mitochondrial function and redox homeostasis8,9. Recent studies indicate that GCs from PCOS patients exhibit heightened susceptibility to ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death caused by excessive lipid peroxide accumulation10,11. Unlike apoptosis, ferroptosis is immunogenic; it promotes the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that initiate local inflammation12. This suggests that GC ferroptosis may act as a pathogenic trigger, establishing a “ferroptosis-inflammation” feedback loop that disrupts the ovarian microenvironment, impairs endometrial receptivity, and ultimately contributes to the anovulation and infertility observed in PCOS13. However, the upstream molecular events that prime PCOS GCs for this ferroptosis-inflammation cascade remain poorly characterized.

  A key defense mechanism against ferroptosis involves SLC7A11 (also known as xCT), the catalytic subunit of the cystine/glutamate antiporter system Xc⁻14,15. SLC7A11 mediates cystine uptake for glutathione (GSH) biosynthesis, thereby maintaining GPX4 activity and facilitating the detoxification of lipid peroxides. In PCOS, SLC7A11 expression is markedly reduced in GCs, resulting in redox imbalance and increased ferroptotic vulnerability16,17. While transcriptional regulation of SLC7A11—such as its suppression by the tumor suppressor p53 under specific stress conditions—has been reported18,19, the post-transcriptional mechanisms controlling its mRNA stability and decay in PCOS are still largely unknown. This knowledge gap highlights the potential role of epitranscriptomic regulation, particularly N⁶-methyladenosine (m⁶A) modification.

  As the most abundant internal modification in eukaryotic mRNA, m⁶A dynamically influences RNA fate—including stability, degradation, and translation—through the coordinated actions of “writers” (e.g., METTL3, METTL14), “erasers” (e.g., FTO), and “readers.”20,21 Growing evidence links aberrant m⁶A methylation to metabolic and reproductive disorders, including defects in insulin signaling and folliculogenesis22,23. Although global m⁶A alterations have been reported in PCOS24, the specific function of the methyltransferase METTL14 remains unclear. Moreover, whether METTL14 collaborates with stress-responsive factors, such as p53, to mediate epigenetic silencing of anti-ferroptotic genes represents a novel and unexplored area of investigation.

  In this study, we identify a previously unrecognized epigenetic pathway that contributes to PCOS pathogenesis. We show that METTL14 is significantly upregulated in PCOS GCs and forms a pathogenic complex with p53. This collaboration enhances m⁶A methylation and subsequent degradation of SLC7A11 mRNA, thereby depleting GCs of their antioxidant defenses. The resulting ferroptosis not only impairs GC survival but also induces a secondary inflammatory response. By elucidating this METTL14–p53–SLC7A11 ferroptosis-inflammation axis, our findings offer new molecular insights into PCOS and suggest potential therapeutic targets for restoring ovarian function.


## Results

### METTL14 upregulation correlates with elevated m⁶A levels and ferroptotic features in ovarian granulosa cells from patients with PCOS
  To identify epigenetic drivers of PCOS, we first interrogated the transcriptomic landscape of human ovarian GCs using the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) dataset GSE8043225. This analysis revealed a specific upregulation of the m⁶A methyltransferase METTL14 mRNA in GCs from patients with PCOS compared with healthy controls (Fig. 1A), hinting at an aberrant epitranscriptomic regulatory mechanism. To validate this clinical observation and explore its functional consequences, we utilized a dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA)-induced PCOS rat model.   We next performed a comprehensive screening of key m⁶A regulators, including “writers” (METTL3, METTL14, METTL16, WTAP) and “erasers” (FTO, ALKBH5). Notably, among these factors, only METTL14 exhibited a consistent and significant upregulation at both the mRNA and protein levels in PCOS ovarian tissues (Fig. 1F, G), identifying it as the primary candidate driving the m⁶A surge. To pinpoint the cellular source of this METTL14 overexpression, we performed dual-immunofluorescence staining for METTL14 and the granulosa cell marker follicle-stimulating hormone receptor (FSHR). The results confirmed that METTL14 accumulation was predominantly localized within FSHR-positive GCs (Fig. 1H–K), suggesting a specific role in follicular dysfunction.

  To delineate the downstream pathways modulated by this METTL14-high signature, we conducted m⁶A-methylated RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (MeRIP-seq). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of differentially methylated transcripts revealed a striking enrichment in the p53 signaling pathway and ferroptosis (Supplemental Fig. S1D). Concurrently, differential expression analysis within the sequencing data reaffirmed that METTL14 was the most significantly upregulated m⁶A writer (fold change = 3.98, P < 0.001). This unbiased omics profiling strongly implicated ferroptosis as a key downstream event. Indeed, ultrastructural examination of PCOS GCs by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed hallmark features of ferroptosis, including shrunken mitochondria with increased membrane density and vanishing cristae (Fig. 1L), while classical apoptotic features like chromatin condensation were notably absent. Collectively, these multi-dimensional data establish a strong correlation between METTL14 overexpression, aberrant m⁶A reprogramming, and the activation of ferroptosis in PCOS GCs.


### Ferroptosis is the primary mechanism driving cell death and inflammation in KGN cells in vitro
  To determine the specific form of cell death responsible for granulosa cell (GC) loss in PCOS, we employed an in vitro model using KGN cells26,27. Since ferroptosis is an immunogenic cell death that releases damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), we evaluated its role in the chronic inflammation of PCOS.   Notably, ferroptosis blockade by Fer-1 suppressed the inflammatory response, whereas inhibitors of pyroptosis, apoptosis, or necroptosis produced no significant effect (Fig. 3J–M), indicating that ferroptosis pathway activation is closely tied to inflammation in this context. To determine whether lipid peroxidation or reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation drives the inflammatory cascade, we treated testosterone-exposed KGN cells with selective pharmacological agents. Testosterone exposure elevated malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, a marker of lipid peroxidation, and concurrently upregulated the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 (Fig. 3N, O). However, co-treatment with either the broad-spectrum ROS scavenger N-acetylcysteine (NAC) or the specific lipid peroxidation inhibitor Liproxstatin-1 produced inhibitory effects similar to those of Fer-1, reducing both MDA accumulation and cytokine release (Fig. 3N, O). To further assess the link between lipid peroxidation and the inflammatory response, Pearson correlation analyses across treatment groups revealed significant positive correlations between MDA levels and the concentrations of IL-1β (Fig. 3P), TNF-α (Fig. 3Q), and IL-6 (Fig. 3R). Collectively, these pharmacological and correlational findings support that lipid ROS accumulation acts as a key mediator in initiating the inflammatory cascade during granulosa cell ferroptosis.

  Given that both ferroptotic stress and the resulting inflammatory microenvironment can influence epitranscriptomic modifications34,35, we next asked whether these pathological processes reciprocally regulate RNA N6-methyladenosine (m⁶A) methylation. To test this independently of androgenic stimulation, we induced ferroptotic and inflammatory stress using SLC7A11-IN-1, a specific inhibitor of the system X- transporter SLC7A11. Pharmacological inhibition of SLC7A11 significantly increased METTL14 protein abundance, SLC7A11 transcript-specific m⁶A methylation, and global RNA m⁶A levels (Supplementary Fig. S3E–G). These findings point to a potential feedback mechanism through which ferroptosis-associated stress and inflammation dynamically modulate the m⁶A machinery in GCs.


### SLC7A11 is identified as a direct downstream target of METTL14-mediated m⁶A modification
  Although basal SLC7A11 expression is generally sufficient to suppress ferroptosis in resting GCs36,37, transcriptomic profiling of ovarian tissue from rats with Mettl14 knockdown suggested that METTL14 regulates SLC7A11 mRNA levels (Supplementary Fig. S4A), pointing to SLC7A11 as a potential downstream target of METTL14. To dissect this regulatory mechanism, we first established both in vivo and ex vivo genetic manipulation models. In vivo, a lentivirus expressing short hairpin RNA targeting METTL14 (sh-METTL14) was locally injected into the ovaries of PCOS rats to achieve gene knockdown (Supplementary Fig. 4B–D). Ex vivo, four genetic manipulation systems were generated by transducing KGN cells with lentiviral vectors: the CONTROL group, the CONTROL+sh-METTL14 group, the sh-SLC7A11+sh-NC group, and the sh-SLC7A11+sh-METTL14 group. Knockdown efficiency was subsequently confirmed (Supplementary Fig. S4B–G).

  To elucidate the direct interaction between METTL14 and SLC7A11 mRNA, we first performed validations in vitro. Methylated RNA immunoprecipitation (meRIP) assays revealed a significant enrichment of SLC7A11 mRNA by the m6A antibody, confirming that SLC7A11 transcripts undergo endogenous m6A modification (Fig. 4A). Consistently, RIP assays in genetically modified KGN cells showed that knockdown of either METTL14 or SLC7A11 markedly reduced the enrichment of SLC7A11 mRNA in the complex (Fig. 4B), further supporting that METTL14 directly targets SLC7A11 transcripts under basal conditions. We next examined the pathological relevance of this mechanism in vivo using PCOS models. In vivo immunoprecipitation assays demonstrated that SLC7A11 mRNA was significantly enriched in anti-METTL14 immunoprecipitates from rat ovarian tissues under PCOS conditions compared with controls (Fig. 4C). Moreover, in vivo meRIP and RIP assays confirmed the presence of m6A modifications on SLC7A11 transcripts. Importantly, in vivo knockdown of METTL14 (PCOS+sh-METTL14) effectively reversed both the pathologically elevated m6A modification and the enhanced transcript enrichment observed in PCOS (Fig. 4D, E). Together, these in vitro and in vivo findings indicate that METTL14-mediated m6A modification of SLC7A11 is specifically activated and amplified within the PCOS microenvironment.   To identify specific m⁶A modification sites, sequence analysis using the SRAMP algorithm predicted 84 high-confidence RRACH motifs within the SLC7A11 mRNA (Fig. 4F and Supplementary Table 1). To test whether these m⁶A motifs mediate post-transcriptional silencing of SLC7A11, dual-luciferase reporter assays were performed. METTL14 overexpression significantly reduced luciferase activity from the reporter containing the wild-type SLC7A11 3′ UTR (Slc7a11-3′ UTR), but this effect was completely abolished in cells transfected with a reporter carrying a mutant m⁶A consensus sequence (Slc7a11-mut-3′ UTR). In contrast, METTL14 did not significantly affect reporter activity from constructs containing the wild-type or mutant coding sequence (Slc7a11-CDS or Slc7a11-mut-CDS) (Fig. 4G, H). These results indicate that METTL14-mediated repression of SLC7A11 depends specifically on m⁶A sites within its 3′ UTR. Finally, the effect of METTL14 on SLC7A11 mRNA stability was assessed using actinomycin D (ActD) transcription blockade assays. In the CONTROL+sh-METTL14 group, METTL14 knockdown significantly extended the half-life of SLC7A11 mRNA, confirming that METTL14 depletion enhances transcript stability (Fig. 4I). In contrast, in the two groups containing sh-SLC7A11 (sh-SLC7A11+sh-NC and sh-SLC7A11+sh-METTL14), SLC7A11 mRNA was rapidly degraded following ActD treatment with a steep slope, driven by exogenous shRNA-mediated cleavage, and no significant difference was observed between these groups (Fig. 4I). Together, these findings demonstrate that METTL14-mediated m⁶A methylation acts as a destabilizing mark, specifically targeting the 3′UTR of SLC7A11 and thereby promoting its mRNA degradation.

  To define the functional causality of the METTL14-SLC7A11 axis in vivo, we performed a genetic rescue experiment. We first validated the transduction and overexpression efficiencies of AAV-METTL14 and AAV-SLC7A11 in rat ovarian tissues, confirming successful upregulation of both targets at mRNA and protein levels (Supplementary Fig. S4H–N). We reasoned that if METTL14 overactivation contributes to ovarian dysfunction primarily through SLC7A11 suppression, restoring SLC7A11 expression should attenuate these pathological changes.

  As expected, co-transduction of AAV-SLC7A11 in AAV-METTL14-treated rats reversed the hormonal dysregulation induced by METTL14 overexpression, with significant recovery of reduced follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and estradiol (E2) levels and marked reduction in luteinizing hormone (LH) and testosterone (T) (Fig. 4J–M). Histological analysis showed that SLC7A11 restoration alleviated cystic ovarian morphology, reducing cystic follicles and restoring granulosa cell layer thickness (Fig. 4N). Biochemical assays revealed that SLC7A11 overexpression suppressed systemic oxidative stress and ferroptosis in AAV-METTL14 rats, reflected by decreased serum malondialdehyde (MDA) and lipid peroxides (LPO) alongside restored glutathione (GSH) content and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity (Fig. 4O–R). Together, these genetic rescue results indicate that SLC7A11 functions as a direct and essential downstream effector of METTL14-mediated m⁶A modification, driving ferroptosis-dependent ovarian dysfunction in PCOS.


### METTL14 dictates granulosa cell ferroptosis and ovarian function by regulating SLC7A11 stability
  To explore the genetic regulatory relationship between METTL14 and its downstream target SLC7A11 in GCs, we performed functional assays using four established in vitro KGN cell groups38,39. Quality control confirmed that lentiviral sh-METTL14 reduced METTL14 expression along with global m⁶A abundance, whereas targeted SLC7A11 knockdown in the sh-SLC7A11+sh-NC group did not reverse upstream METTL14 expression (Supplementary Fig. S4D–G and Fig. 5A). Phenotypic analysis showed that under the CONTROL background, silencing METTL14 alone (CONTROL+sh-METTL14 group) upregulated SLC7A11 expression and attenuated basal ferroptosis and inflammation in GCs. This was reflected by reduced intracellular Fe²⁺ accumulation, decreased lipid peroxidation (MDA and LPO), improved antioxidant indices (GSH and SOD) (Fig. 5B–G), and diminished ROS fluorescence along with downregulated transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) (Fig. 5H, I). Conversely, the sh-SLC7A11+sh-NC group, used as an in vitro ferroptosis model, exhibited severe ferroptotic damage, ROS accumulation, and inflammatory responses. In the double-knockdown group (sh-SLC7A11+sh-METTL14), concurrent METTL14 depletion failed to mitigate the pathological damage caused by SLC7A11 deficiency, with ferroptotic and inflammatory indices remaining elevated and showing no significant difference from the sh-SLC7A11+sh-NC group (Fig. 5B–I). Western blot analysis further indicated that although METTL14 knockdown alone improved the expression profile of ferroptosis-related proteins (TFR1, ACSL4, FPN, and GPX4), this effect was blocked in the double-knockdown group (Fig. 5J, K, A–D).   We subsequently validated these protective effects in vivo using a DHEA-induced PCOS rat model. Compared with the PCOS+sh-NC group, local ovarian injection of lentiviral sh-METTL14 (PCOS+sh-METTL14 group) reduced global m⁶A levels in ovarian tissues (Fig. 5K) and restored SLC7A11 mRNA expression (Fig. 5J–E, F). Physiological analysis revealed that METTL14 knockdown improved the characteristic endocrine dysregulation in PCOS rats, restoring the balance of serum FSH, LH, estradiol (E₂), and testosterone (Fig. 5L–O). However, in the PCOS+sh-METTL14+sh-SLC7A11 group, concomitant SLC7A11 knockdown negated the therapeutic effects of METTL14 depletion, leading to re-elevated LH and testosterone levels and decreased FSH and E₂ levels (Fig. 5L–O). Histological analysis showed that, compared with the PCOS+sh-NC group, the PCOS+sh-METTL14 group exhibited improved ovarian morphology, with fewer cystic follicles, more healthy corpora lutea, and increased granulosa cell layer thickness; these improvements were reversed in the PCOS+sh-METTL14+sh-SLC7A11 group (Fig. 5P). ## Discussion
  PCOS is a systemic metabolic and reproductive disorder characterized by chronic low-grade inflammation and oxidative stress45,46. Although classical features, such as hyperandrogenism are well-defined, the molecular drivers that initiate and sustain the ovarian inflammatory microenvironment remain poorly understood47,48. Here, we identify a novel epitranscriptomic mechanism connecting iron-dependent cell death to ovarian dysfunction. We show that the m⁶A methyltransferase METTL14 is pathologically upregulated in PCOS GCs, where it establishes a pathogenic partnership with p53 to destabilize the mRNA encoding the key cystine/glutamate antiporter SLC7A11. This axis initiates a ferroptosis-inflammation cascade: SLC7A11 loss dismantles antioxidant defenses, promoting lipid peroxidation and ferroptotic cell death, which in turn triggers the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6). Our identification of the METTL14-p53-SLC7A11 axis offers a molecular basis for the self-perpetuating “oxidative stress-inflammation” cycle in PCOS.

  Epigenetic reprogramming, particularly RNA m⁶A modification, has emerged as a critical regulator of metabolic homeostasis and reproductive health22,23. While the “writer” METTL3 has been implicated in PCOS49, the specific role of METTL14 remained elusive. Our findings establish METTL14 as a key pathogenic driver in GCs. In contrast to its protective function in other contexts—such as mitigating oxidative stress via FOXO1 in the gastrointestinal tract50—we found METTL14 to be deleterious within the hyperandrogenic milieu of PCOS. It promotes ferroptosis by selectively targeting the SLC7A11-GPX4 antioxidant axis. Crucially, our data reveal a reciprocal positive feedback loop: not only does METTL14 drive ferroptosis, but ferroptotic stress itself further stabilizes METTL14 and increases global m⁶A levels (as shown in Fig. 5). This feed-forward mechanism likely explains the chronic and progressive nature of ovarian dysfunction in PCOS.

  A seminal finding of our study is the elucidation of a “writer-cofactor” mechanism involving the tumor suppressor p53. While p53 is classically known as a transcriptional repressor of SLC7A1118,19, our data uncover a novel, non-canonical post-transcriptional role. Supported by AlphaFold3 structural modeling and robust interaction assays (Co-IP/RIP), we demonstrate that p53 physically recruits or stabilizes the METTL14 complex on the SLC7A11 3’UTR. This interaction facilitates precise m⁶A deposition and accelerates mRNA decay. The functional dependency of this complex was definitively proven by our epistatic rescue experiments: METTL14 overexpression failed to suppress SLC7A11 or induce ferroptosis when p53 was silenced. This suggests that p53 acts as an obligate “molecular guide” for METTL14 specificity in this context. This discovery expands the paradigm of p53 biology, illustrating how cellular stress signals integrate with the epitranscriptomic machinery to determine cell fate.

  Our study redefines GC death in PCOS by establishing its identity as ferroptosis—an immunogenic form of cell death—rather than silent apoptosis. We confirmed that the suppression of SLC7A11 leads to lipid ROS accumulation, which directly correlates with the secretion of DAMPs12 and inflammatory cytokines. This aligns with the “sterile inflammation” theory of PCOS45,51, suggesting that ferroptotic GCs act as distinct inflammatory hubs that perpetuate local tissue damage and androgen production. Therefore, genetic ablation of Tp53 acts as a “molecular brake,” effectively severing this pathogenic axis. By restoring SLC7A11, Tp53 silencing not only prevents GC loss but also extinguishes the upstream spark of ovarian inflammation, providing a dual therapeutic benefit.

  Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, although we used DHEA-induced rat models and human cell lines, access to human clinical samples was restricted, and the clinical sample size could not be expanded in this study. Further validation in large clinical cohorts is therefore required to confirm the diagnostic value of the METTL14-p53 signature in PCOS patients. Second, although SLC7A11 was identified as a primary target, m⁶A modifications are transcriptome-wide, suggesting that METTL14 likely regulates a broader network of metabolic transcripts contributing to the PCOS phenotype. Future multi-omics studies integrating m⁶A-seq with metabolomics will be essential for mapping this complete regulatory landscape. Finally, the specific upstream signals driving the initial upregulation of METTL14—whether hyperinsulinemia, androgen excess, or inflammatory cytokines—remain to be fully dissected.

  In summary, our study unveils a pathogenic METTL14-p53-SLC7A11 axis that orchestrates granulosa cell ferroptosis and subsequent inflammation in PCOS. By mechanistically characterizing p53 as a critical RNA-binding cofactor for METTL14-mediated SLC7A11 destabilization, we provide a structural and functional rationale for targeting this complex. Therapeutic strategies that disrupt the METTL14-p53 interaction or inhibit ferroptosis may offer a promising avenue for preserving ovarian function and breaking the inflammatory cycle in individuals with PCOS.


## Materials and methods

### Animals
  Six-week-old female Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats were purchased from Hunan Slaughter Laboratory Animal Co. Ltd (Hunan, China; License No. SYXK (Gui) 2024-0004). Rats were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility under a 12-h light/dark cycle at 20 ± 0.5 °C with 30–50% humidity. Food and water were provided ad libitum. Bedding was changed daily. Animals were acclimatized for at least 1 week prior to experiments. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangxi University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Approval No. DW20240603-145).


### Animal models and treatments
  The PCOS rat model was established by dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) administration as previously described43,52. Briefly, rats received daily subcutaneous injections of DHEA (60 mg/kg; dissolved in 0.3 mL castor oil; MedChemExpress) for 21 consecutive days. Control rats were injected with an equivalent volume of castor oil. PCOS model validity was confirmed on day 10 of the estrous cycle via vaginal cytology, serum hormone profiling, and ovarian histopathology in a pilot cohort (n = 3). The in vivo study comprised six experimental series.


### Surgical procedure for local ovarian microinjection
  For all in vivo local ovarian gene intervention studies, including virus-mediated gene knockdown and overexpression, rats were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane delivered in fresh gas at a flow rate of 4 L/min (delivery rate: 0.41 mL/min). Animals were placed in a lateral recumbent position, and a 1–2 cm abdominal incision was made bilaterally. The skin, fascia, and muscle layers were carefully dissected bluntly to expose the moist white adipose tissue surrounding the ovaries. Gentle traction was applied to adequately reveal the pink, lobulated ovaries. Using a 50 μL microsyringe, the viral vector suspension was slowly injected into the ovarian parenchyma. To ensure even distribution, each ovary was injected at two distinct sites, with a total injection volume of 30 μL per ovary (60 μL per animal). The needle was retained in situ for 5 min following each injection to prevent viral fluid backflow. After the procedure, the reproductive tract was repositioned, and the incisions were sutured. Penicillin (80,000 U/mL; Sichuan, China) was administered intramuscularly to prevent infection.


### Animal grouping and in vivo gene interventions
  To comprehensively investigate the pathophysiology of PCOS and the role of the METTL14-P53-SLC7A11 axis, rats were randomly assigned to the following experimental groups:


#### Model validation group
  Rats were divided into a standard control group (CONTROL) and a dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA)-induced PCOS model group (PCOS).


#### Targeted ovarian local knockdown
  Using the aforementioned surgical protocol for microinjection, lentiviral vectors (titer: 5 × 10⁸ TU/mL; GeneChem, Shanghai, China) encoding short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting Mettl14 (sh-METTL14), Slc7a11 (sh-SLC7A11), Tp53 (sh-Tp53), or a negative control (sh-NC) were transduced into the ovaries of CONTROL and PCOS rats.


#### AAV-mediated ovarian local overexpression
  Four weeks prior to DHEA induction, AAV9-METTL14 or AAV9-SLC7A11 (titer: 5 × 10¹² v.g./mL; Hanbio Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was administered via local ovarian injection, employing an identical surgical paradigm. AAV9-EGFP and AAV9-mCherry served as respective matched controls.


#### In vivo rescue and exacerbation intervention groups
  To verify the epistatic interactions within the METTL14-P53-SLC7A11 axis, additional dual-intervention cohorts were established. In the rescue experiments, a subset of PCOS rats transduced with AAV9-METTL14 also received a local ovarian co-injection of sh-Tp53 or AAV9-SLC7A11 to assess the reversal of disease phenotypes. Conversely, in the exacerbation model, rats received a co-injection of AAV9-METTL14 and lentiviral sh-SLC7A11 to evaluate the synergistic worsening of ferroptosis and ovarian dysfunction.


#### Transduction efficiency validation
  A separate cohort of rats underwent local ovarian injection of Lenti-EGFP. Transduction efficiency was subsequently verified via polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and Western blot analysis.


### Anesthesia, euthanasia, and sample collection
  Prior to sacrifice, all rats were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane (5% for induction, 2% for maintenance). Under adequate anesthesia, blood samples were collected via cardiac puncture for subsequent serum biochemical and hormonal analyses. Following blood collection, rats were immediately euthanized by cervical dislocation. Ovarian tissues were rapidly dissected, rinsed, and collected for histological examination and molecular.


### Cell culture and ex vivo model construction
  The human granulosa-like tumor cell line KGN (Procell, Wuhan, China) was cultured in DMEM/F-12 medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 15% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. The cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Upon reaching 90% confluence, the cells were passaged at a ratio of 1:4 using 0.25% trypsin solution (without EDTA, containing phenol red; ServiceBio, G4005-100ml). All experiments were conducted using cells between passages 4 and 30. Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; ServiceBio, G4103-5ml) strictly according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

  To recapitulate the hyperandrogenic microenvironment of PCOS ex vivo, KGN cells were stimulated with 1 µM testosterone. Additionally, to establish a classical ex vivo ferroptosis model, cells were treated with the specific ferroptosis inducer Erastin (10 µM)26. To recapitulate the hyperandrogenic microenvironment of PCOS ex vivo, KGN cells were stimulated with 1 µM testosterone. Additionally, to establish a classical ex vivo ferroptosis model, cells were treated with the specific ferroptosis inducer Erastin (10–20 µM)32.


### Pharmacological inhibition of cell death pathways
  To verify the specificity of ferroptosis and exclude other forms of programmed cell death, KGN cells were pretreated with various pharmacological inhibitors before stimulation with Testosterone (1 μM) or Erastin (10 μM). Cells were co-incubated with the pan-caspase inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK (20 μM; Sigma-Aldrich) to block apoptosis, the RIPK1 inhibitor Necrostatin-1s (Nec-1s, 10 μM; Selleck Chemicals) to block necroptosis, or the caspase-1 inhibitor VX-765 (10 μM; Selleck Chemicals) to block pyroptosis. All inhibitors were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and added to the culture medium 2 h prior to cell death induction. Cells treated with an equal volume of DMSO served as the vehicle control. Cell viability, reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, and intracellular Fe²⁺ levels were then assessed as described below.


### Ex vivo viral transduction
  To maintain consistency with the in vivo gene intervention approach, gene silencing was performed using the aforementioned lentiviral vectors (sh-METTL14, sh-SLC7A11, sh-Tp53, or sh-NC), while gene overexpression was achieved using adeno-associated virus serotype 9 (AAV9) vectors (AAV9-METTL14, AAV9-SLC7A11, or their respective controls). Cells were seeded into 6-well or 24-well plates and transduced at 50–60% confluence. For 6-well plates, 50 µL of viral suspension was added per well; for 24-well plates, 10 µL was added per well. To promote viral entry and improve transduction efficiency, the infection enhancer HitransG (Genechem, Shanghai, China) was added to the culture medium following the manufacturer’s instructions. After a 24-h co-incubation, the virus-containing medium was removed and replaced with fresh complete medium. Cells were then cultured for an additional 48–72 h prior to being harvested for downstream functional assays. Sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 2.


### Histopathological analysis and immunohistochemistry (IHC)
  Ovaries were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned (5 μm). H&E staining was performed for morphological analysis. Cystic follicles were scored using a semi-quantitative double-blind method (Scale 0–3)50. For IHC, sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) via microwave heating. After blocking with 5% BSA, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with either anti-METTL14 antibody (1:200; #ab308576; Abcam) or anti-SLC7A11 antibody (1:200; #ab175186; Abcam). Sections were then incubated with an HRP-conjugated Rabbit-on-Rodent Polymer detection reagent (Biocare Medical). Immunoreactivity was visualized using DAB substrate.


### Immunofluorescence (IF) staining
  To assess protein co-localization (METTL14, FSHR, and SLC7A11) or expression, cells or tissue sections were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 and blocked with goat serum. Samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against METTL14 (anti-METTL14 antibody; 1:200; #ab308576; Abcam), FSHR (anti-FSHR antibody; 1:100; #22665-1-AP; Proteintech), and SLC7A11 (anti-SLC7A11 antibody; 1:200; #ab175186; Abcam), followed by Alexa Fluor 488- or 594-conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen). Nuclei were stained with DAPI. Images were captured using a confocal microscope (Leica).


### Western blot analysis
  Proteins were extracted using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Lysates were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-METTL14 antibody (1:2000; #ab308576; Abcam), anti-SLC7A11 antibody (1:2000; #ab175186; Abcam), anti-GPX4 antibody (1:3000; #67763-1-Ig; Proteintech), anti-ACSL4 antibody (1:1000; #22401-1-AP; Proteintech), anti-FPN antibody (1:1000; #26601-1-AP; Proteintech), anti-TFR1 antibody (1:1000; #10084-2-AP; Proteintech), anti-p53 antibody (1:2000; #10442-1-AP; Proteintech), anti-cleaved caspase-3 antibody (1:2000; #9664; Cell Signaling Technology), anti-PARP antibody (1:3000; #9532; Cell Signaling Technology), anti-GSDMD-N antibody (1:5000; #ab219800; Abcam), anti-MLKL antibody (1:5000; #ab243142; Abcam), anti-IL-1β antibody (1:5000; #26048-1-AP; Proteintech), anti-TNF-α antibody (1:5000; #60291-1-Ig; Proteintech), anti-IL-6 antibody (1:2000; #30953-1-AP; Proteintech), and anti-GAPDH antibody (1:10,000; #60004-1-Ig; Proteintech). HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied for 1 h. Bands were visualized using ECL reagents (Millipore).


### RNA extraction and RT-qPCR
  Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Takara). cDNA was synthesized using HiScript III SuperMix (Vazyme). qPCR was performed using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) on a Bio-Rad CFX96 system. Relative expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔct method, normalized to β-actin. Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 2.


### RNA stability assay (actinomycin D assay)
  To evaluate the post-transcriptional stability of SLC7A11 mRNA, KGN cells were treated with culture medium containing the global transcription inhibitor actinomycin D (ActD; 5 μg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich) after undergoing various viral transductions, including matched negative controls (sh-NC or AAV-EGFP), targeted knockdown groups (sh-METTL14, sh-SLC7A11, or sh-Tp53), a METTL14 overexpression group (AAV-METTL14), and a dual-intervention rescue group (AAV-METTL14 + sh-Tp53). Cells were harvested at specified time points post-treatment (0, 3, and 6 h), and total RNA was extracted. Residual SLC7A11 transcript levels were then quantitatively assessed using RT-qPCR. Relative mRNA abundance was calculated using the 2−ΔΔct method, and degradation kinetics curves were fitted via nonlinear regression analysis to determine the mRNA half-life.


### Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)
  To investigate endogenous METTL14/p53/SLC7A11 interactions, KGN cells and rat ovarian tissues were lysed in ice-cold IP Lysis Buffer (Pierce) supplemented with protease inhibitors. After BCA protein quantification, equal amounts of lysates (500–1000 μg) were pre-cleared with Protein A/G magnetic beads for 1 h at 4 °C. The pre-cleared lysates were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with antibodies against METTL14, p53, SLC7A11, or normal IgG, and subsequently with fresh Protein A/G beads for 2 h. Immune complexes bound to the beads were washed five times with lysis buffer, eluted by boiling (95 °C, 10 min) in 1× SDS-PAGE loading buffer, and analyzed by Western blotting alongside 10% input lysates.


### RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)
  The physical interaction between RNA-binding proteins and their target transcripts was validated in KGN cell lysates and rat ovarian tissue homogenates using the Magna RIP™ Kit (Millipore, USA). Clarified ovarian lysates were separated into Input, immunoprecipitation (IP), and negative control IgG fractions. Each fraction was incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific primary antibodies against p53 or METTL14, or with normal IgG. The complexes were captured with protein A/G magnetic beads, washed stringently six times, and digested with proteinase K at 55 °C for 30 min. Purified co-precipitated RNA was analyzed by RT-qPCR to assess enrichment of SLC7A11 or Tp53, normalized to the corresponding Input or IgG fractions.


### MeRIP-qPCR, global m6A measurements and m6A dot blot

#### MeRIP-qPCR
  The methylated RNA immunoprecipitation (MeRIP) assay was performed using the Magna MeRIP™ m6A Transcriptome-wide N6-methyladenosine Detection Kit. The detailed experimental procedures were as follows: total RNA was first extracted from ovarian tissues and the human ovarian granulosa cell line KGN using TRIzol reagent. Approximately 20 μg of purified RNA was fragmented by incubation with RNA fragmentation buffer. Subsequently, 1 μg of fragmented mRNA was reserved as the input control, while the remaining RNA was incubated overnight at 4 °C with either m6A-specific antibody (Proteintech, Wuhan, China) or IgG isotype control antibody in 500 μL of immunoprecipitation buffer. Antibody-RNA complexes were captured using protein A/G magnetic beads, followed by stringent washing steps to remove nonspecifically bound components. After elution, RNA was purified using the RNeasy Mini RNA Purification Kit (Qiagen). Both input control RNA and immunoprecipitated m6A-modified RNA were reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara Bio Inc.). Finally, the expression levels of target transcripts were analyzed by quantitative real-time PCR, and the relative enrichment of m6A modification was calculated using the input group as an internal references53. The primer sequences for MeRIP-qPCR are listed in Supplementary Table 3.


#### Global m6A measurements
  Colorimetric quantification of m6A content in total RNA was performed using the m6A RNA Methylation Quantification Kit (Epigentek, Cat# P-9005-96). Each assay well was coated with 200 ng of RNA, and then the m6A content was captured.


#### Dot blot
  Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent. Purified mRNA was denatured at 95 °C for 3 min, then spotted onto Amersham Hybond-N+ membranes and crosslinked under UV light for 60 min before the membranes were washed with TBST and blocked with 5% skim milk powder for 1 h. The blocking solution was discarded, and the membranes were subjected to an overnight incubation containing primary antibody (1:1000). The next day, the membranes were washed with TBST and the secondary antibody was incubated for 1 h at room temperature. m6A levels were detected using an ECL chemiluminescence kit (Tanon, Cat# 180-5001). The NC membrane was stained with methylene blue and then washed with ddH2O until clear spots appeared.


### Measurement of oxidative stress and ferroptosis markers

#### Biochemical assays
  Malondialdehyde (MDA) levels were measured in ovarian tissue and ovarian GCs under different conditions using the thiobarbituric acid method (Beyotime, S0131S). In addition, reduced glutathione (GSH) levels in tissue and cell samples were measured using the GSH/GSSG assay kit (Beyotime, S0053). In addition, superoxide dismutase (SOD) levels in tissue and cell samples were measured using the total SOD assay kit with WST-8 (Beyotime, S0101S). In addition, lipid peroxidation (LPO) levels in tissue and cell samples were measured using the LPO assay kit (Beyotime, S0101S). MDA, SOD, LPO and GSH levels were calculated from ovarian granulosa cell protein concentrations. In addition, Fe2+ levels were measured using the Ferrous Content Assay Kit (Solarbio, Cat# BC5415) according to the instructions.


#### Lipid ROS assessment
  To comprehensively evaluate the level of ex vivo lipid peroxidation, cells were first gently washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and then incubated with the lipid reactive oxygen species (ROS)-sensitive fluorescent probe C11-BODIPY 581/591 (10 μM; Thermo Fisher Scientific) in serum-free medium at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. After incubation, cells were washed three times with PBS to remove unbound probe. Intracellular lipid ROS accumulation was assessed by monitoring the shift in the probe’s fluorescence emission from red (non-oxidized) to green (oxidized). Images were immediately acquired using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany) equipped with appropriate filter sets54.


### ELISA for hormones and cytokines
  Serum sex hormones (testosterone, E₂, LH, and FSH) and pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6) in ovarian tissue homogenates and cell culture supernatants were quantified using specific sandwich ELISA kits (Elabscience, Wuhan, China). Serum was separated by allowing whole blood to clot naturally, followed by centrifugation at 1000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. Ovarian tissues were homogenized in ice-cold PBS, and the clarified supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. Cell culture supernatants were centrifuged at 1000 × g for 20 min to remove cellular debris. All subsequent detection procedures were performed strictly according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader, and analyte concentrations were calculated using standard curves fitted with a four-parameter logistic (4PL) model.


### Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)
  Ovarian tissues or cell pellets were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde and 1% osmium tetroxide. They were then stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate and photographed with a Hitachi H7650 microscope (Tokyo, Japan). The focus of the ovarian tissues was on the changes in the number and morphology of mitochondria in ovarian GCs in the TEM images. The TEM results of animal experiments were verified by three independent experiments. The in vitro experiments were verified by three independent experiments. In each independent sample, we randomly selected three fields of view to quantify mitochondria with typical features of iron mutations. Representative images with typical features were selected for the results.


### AlphaFold3 structural prediction
  The ternary complex structure of METTL14, p53, and the SLC7A11 RNA motif was predicted using the AlphaFold 3 server (Google DeepMind). Protein sequences (UniProt) and the specific SLC7A11 RNA sequence were input for modeling. High-confidence models (pLDDT >70) were visualized using PyMOL55. Use software such as PyMOL to open the downloaded cif file for subsequent structural analysis and verification. Predicted structures were retrieved from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk).


### Statistical analysis
  Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 or SPSS 24.0. Two-group comparisons were analyzed using a two-tailed Student’s t test. Comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparisons test. For factorial experiments, two-way ANOVA was used to assess the main and interaction effects of the two factors tested in each experiment, including METTL14 manipulation (knockdown or overexpression), SLC7A11 manipulation (knockdown or overexpression), and p53 knockdown, as applicable; Tukey’s multiple-comparisons test was used for post hoc comparisons. Correlations were assessed using two-tailed Pearson correlation analysis. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
