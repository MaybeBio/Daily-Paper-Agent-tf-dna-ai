# Live-cell imaging reveals two modes of TERRA association with telomeres and the dynamics of TERRA R-loops


## Abstract
  Telomeres are transcribed into TERRA long non-coding RNAs. When associating with telomeres, TERRA can regulate telomere maintenance by semiconservative DNA replication, telomerase and homology-directed repair (HDR). In addition, TERRA alters telomeric structure and protein composition. Yet, the molecular mechanisms and dynamics by which TERRA associates with telomeres remain elusive. To investigate TERRA-telomere interactions, we combine PP7 stem-loop TERRA tagging, genome editing and live-cell imaging. Using a deep-learning method for spot detection and single-particle tracking analysis, we demonstrate that TERRA associates with telomeres through two distinct modes. On one hand, TERRA interacts frequently and transiently with the telomeric shelterin protein TRF2. On the other hand, TERRA engages in long-lasting interactions which persist from seconds to several minutes forming R-loops with telomeric DNA. TERRA R-loops preferentially occur at short telomeres, and their formation requires factors involved in HDR, but not TRF2. Altogether, we reveal two parallel mechanisms of TERRA recruitment to telomeres, which differ substantially in their dynamics and structure, and we elucidate the dynamics of telomeric R-loops. Our findings provide a framework for understanding TERRA-triggered telomere functions during aging and carcinogenesis.


## Introduction
  Telomeres are specialized nucleoprotein structures at chromosome ends that maintain genomic integrity and regulate cellular lifespan [1]. In differentiated human cells, telomeres shorten because the end replication problem and the repression of telomerase [2]. Telomere shortening limits the replicative potential of pre-cancerous cells in humans, providing a powerful tumor suppressive mechanism [3]. On the other hand, telomere shortening in healthy somatic cells may contribute to tissue exhaustion during aging [4].

  Telomerase counteracts telomere shortening in germline and stem cells. In addition, telomerase is upregulated in most tumors of epithelial origin enabling immortality of cancer cells [5–7]. Besides telomerase, homology directed repair at telomeres can also counteract telomere shortening [8]. In many sarcomas, tumors of the nervous system and others, telomeres are maintained by a recombination-based mechanism called Alternative Lengthening of Telomeres (ALT) instead of telomerase. In ALT cancers, telomeric 3′ overhangs invade and anneal to homologous telomeric sequences which serve as a template for telomere elongation.

  Recent work from several laboratories indicates that TERRA lncRNA is a major regulator of telomere-mediated cell survival. TERRA is transcribed at most if not all telomeres from subtelomeric promoters into the telomeric tract [9–14]. Thus, TERRA molecules contain multiple 5′-UUAGGG-3′ repeats at their 3′ ends, which mediate formation of RNA:DNA hybrids called R-loops [15]. By forming R-loop structures at telomeres, TERRA interferes with semiconservative DNA replication promoting a switch in telomere maintenance from semiconservative DNA replication to a homology-directed repair (HDR) mechanism known as break-induced replication (BIR) [16, 17]. Thereby, TERRA ensures telomere maintenance in ALT cancer cells [18]. Of note, TERRA abundance drops in telomerase positive cancer cells during S phase [13] but this repression is lost in ALT cancer cells [19]. The ability of TERRA to induce BIR at telomeres has also been recapitulated in telomerase positive HeLa cancer cells by experimental induction of TERRA transcription [17].

  In Saccharomyces cerevisiae cells, which were experimentally deprived of telomerase, TERRA R-loops formed specifically at short telomeres slowing down telomere erosion by promoting telomere elongation by DNA recombination [20]. Also in telomerase-negative human cells, TERRA R-loops form at short telomeres, contributing to the cohesion of sister telomeres, which delays senescence onset by mechanisms that remain to be explored [21]. Intriguingly, TERRA R-loops form at telomeres post-transcription in dependency of both, the RAD51 DNA recombinase and its enhancer RAD51AP1 [15, 17, 22, 23], which act in the same genetic pathway providing non-redundant functions for TERRA R-loop formation [17]. In addition, the shelterin component TRF2 can physically interact with TERRA and promote TERRA R-loops [24]. This activity is unleashed upon TRF1 dissociation from telomeres that occurs during oxidative stress [24, 25].

  Besides its roles in BIR, TERRA may regulate telomerase under conditions that are not entirely clear. In S. cerevisiae and Schizosaccharomyces pombe, TERRA has been proposed to promote telomere maintenance sustaining telomerase recruitment to telomeres [26, 27]. On the other hand, TERRA is a potent inhibitor of human telomerase in vitro [28] and it partially colocalizes with telomerase in human cells [29]. Finally, while TERRA is found mostly in the nucleus, it is exported to the cytoplasm during tumorigenesis when telomeres become critically short at a stage called cell crisis [30]. Very importantly, TERRA acts during cell crisis as a damage signal that activates an innate immune pathway to trigger autophagic cell death [30]. In addition, cytoplasmic TERRA may be translated into dipeptide proteins and trigger an inflammatory response [31].

  Despite many characterized functions, the mechanisms of TERRA recruitment to telomeres remain ill-defined and its dynamics are unknown. Here, we use genome editing and live single-molecule microscopy to elucidate the mechanisms of TERRA recruitment to telomeres and TERRA R-loop dynamics. We demonstrate that TERRA diffuses through the nucleus engaging in numerous transient interactions with telomeres. When telomeres become critically short, TERRA engages preferentially with telomeres forming R-loops, which are mediated by HDR factors that last from seconds to several minutes. In addition, TERRA contacts telomeres transiently via interactions with TRF2. Our results uncover the existence of two parallel TERRA recruitment pathways to short telomeres, with the long-lasting R-loop mediated interactions underlying the engagement of HDR-mediated telomere repair.


## Materials and methods

### Cell lines and cell culture
  All cell lines were derived from HeLa cells and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM), high glucose, GlutaMAXTM Supplement, pyruvate (Gibco), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 100 U/ml of penicillin/streptomycin. Cells were maintained in a controlled humidified atmosphere with 5% (v/v) CO2, at 37°C. Live cell imaging experiments were performed in an equilibrated incubation chamber at 37 °C and 5% CO2, in FluoroBrite DMEM (Gibco), supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/ml of penicillin/streptomycin, and 1x GlutaMAX.


### Plasmids, genome editing, and transfections
  All plasmid transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For the generation of the Halo-TRF1 clone (Fig. 1), cells were transfected with a plasmid expressing Cas9 and a sgRNA targeting the genomic region proximal to the start codon of TRF1 [32] together with a non-linearized repair template plasmid. For the generation of the repair template, the homology regions from genomic DNA from HeLa cells and the Halo tag sequence from the JF920304.1 HaloTag CMV-neo vector pHTN (Promega) were amplified and combined by overlapping PCR. They were then subcloned into pCR®4-TOPO vector (Invitrogen). A day prior to transfections, 5.5 × 105 cells were seeded in six-well plates with 2 ml complete DMEM and were left to attach. Two wells were used per transfection. Cells were transfected using 1 μg of the Cas9 construct and 1.5 μg of the repair template, per well. Six hours post-transfection, cells were detached using trypsin and the two wells were combined into a 10 cm culture dish. Twenty-four hours post-transfection puromycin (1 μg/ ml) was added to the cells for 5 days. Cells were then seeded into 96-well plates as single cells by limiting dilution, in the absence of puromycin and were left to form colonies. Two weeks later, single clones were transferred to 12-well plates, were expanded and frozen. Correct insertion of Halo-tag in all alleles was confirmed by PCR from genomic DNA and by detection of a higher molecular weight band by western blot (Fig. 1B and C).

  These cells were then used for the generation of rtTA + PCP-GFP + HeLa cells for PP7-15qTERRA expression as previously described [15]. Cells were sorted into single cells based on GFP expression and expanded for the next step. For the generation of the piggybac (pgb) cell lines, the whole TRE-PP7-alone or TRE-PP7-15qTERRA templates were subcloned from the plasmids described previously [15] into a vector backbone containing the pgb inverted repeats [kind gift from I.M. Cheeseman (Whitehead Institute)]. Transfections were performed as described above, using 400 ng of the Super pgb transposase PB210PA-1-SBI plasmid (System Biosciences) and 2.1 μg of the pgb construct (pgb-PP7alone/pgb-PP7-15qTERRA) per well. The cells were selected with puromycin (1 μg/ ml) and the antibiotic was kept onward. The cells were sorted to single cells into 96-well plates in medium containing puromycin. Upon formation of single colonies, they were expanded and screened by microscopy. Positive clones were then screened by Telomere Restriction Fragment analysis for assessment of telomere length. Clones with telomere length ~10kb were cultured for multiple weeks in the presence of the telomerase inhibitor BIBR1532 at 20 μM, until they reached an average telomere length of about 5kb. To accelerate telomere shortening, the clones were rendered telomerase-negative through bulk CRISPR/Cas9 knockout of hTERT as previously described [33], by co-transfecting a pCDNA3.3 vector expressing Cas9 and conferring blasticidin resistance and two pU6 vectors with different sgRNAs against hTERT as described [33]. Following transfection, cells were selected with blasticidin (10 μg/ ml) for one week and then propagated in the absence of blasticidin, but in the presence of 20 μM BIBR1532 (except HeLa-10kb where telomere length was maintained). qTRAP assays to assess telomerase activity were performed 1 week post-transfection as previously described [34].

  For transient expression of RNaseH1, cells were transfected as described above with 1.5 μg of either an empty vector (EV, pcDNA6-myc-His) or a plasmid for overexpression of RNaseH1 (pcDNA6-RNAseH1-myc-His). Cells were harvested or imaged 48 h post-transfection. Further information on all the plasmids used can be found in Supplementary Table S1.

  For depletion of TRF1, RAD51AP1, or TRF2, cells were transfected using calcium phosphate as previously described [35], using the siRNAs listed in Supplementary Table S2. Cells were harvested or imaged 72 h post-transfection.


### Telomeric FISH on metaphase spreads
  For preparation of metaphase spreads, cells were treated with 0.05 μg/ml demecolcine for 2 h before harvesting by trypsinization, resuspended in hypotonic solution (0.056 M KCl), and incubated at 37°C for 7 min. Cells were fixed in cold methanol:glacial acetic acid (3:1) solution overnight at 4°C. Fixed cells were dropped onto glass slides, incubated at 70°C for 1 min in a humidified oven, and air-dried overnight at room temperature. Slides were incubated in 4% formaldehyde in PBS for 5 min, washed three times in 1× PBS and dehydrated consecutively with 70%, 95%, and 100% ethanol. Hybridization was done with 100 nM Cy3-[CCCTAA]3 PNA probe (PNA Bio) in 70 μl hybridization mix [10 mM Tris-Cl pH 7.4, 70% formamide, 0.5% blocking reagent (Roche)], at 80°C for 3 min, followed by incubation for 3 h at room temperature, in a humidified chamber. Slides were washed twice with wash buffer 1 (10 mM Tris-Cl pH 7.4, 70% formamide) for 15 min/wash and then washed three times with wash buffer 2 (0.1 M Tris-Cl pH 7.4, 0.15 M NaCl, 0.08% Tween-20) for 5 min/wash. DAPI was added to the second last wash at 0.1 μg/ml. Slides were dehydrated with 70%, 95%, and 100% ethanol, air-dried, and mounted with Vectashield. Images were acquired with an Upright Zeiss Axioplan equipped with a 100×/1.40 oil objective.


### Western blot
  For preparation of total cell extracts, cells were collected by trypsinization, washed with 1× PBS, counted and resuspended in 2× Laemmli buffer, to a final concentration of 10 000 cells/μl. Samples were incubated at 95°C for 5 min. Proteins were separated on a 4%–15% SDS–PAGE precast gel (Mini-PROTEAN TGX Gels, Bio-Rad) and transferred onto a 0.2 μm nitrocellulose membrane (Amersham). Membranes were blocked with blocking solution [3% BSA (w/v) in 1× PBS with 0.1% Tween-20 or 5% milk (w/v) in 1× PBS with 0.1% Tween-20] for 1 h at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies (Supplementary Table S3) at 4°C overnight. Membranes were washed three times for 5 min/wash with 1× PBS with 0.1% Tween-20 and then incubated with Horseradish Peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (Supplementary Table S3) in blocking solution, for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were again washed three times for 5 min/wash with 1× PBS with 0.1% Tween-20. ChemiGlow Chemiluminescence Substrate (Bio Techne) was used to develop the signal, which was detected by a Fusion FX imaging system (Vilber).


### RNA isolation, RNA dot blot, and RT-qPCR
  RNA was isolated using the NucleoSpin RNA Isolation kit (Macherey-Nagel) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Three rDNase treatments were performed, two on-column and one in solution.

  For RNA dot blot, purified RNA was digested with RNase (DNase-free, Roche), as a control for 1 h at 37°C. All samples were denatured at 65°C for 3 min and blotted onto a Hybond-XL membrane (Amersham) using a dot-blot apparatus (Bio-Rad). RNA was UV-crosslinked to the membrane. The membrane was then blocked in Church buffer [0.5 M NaHPO4, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0, 1% (w/v) BSA, 7% SDS] for at least 1 h at 50°C and hybridized with a 32P-radiolabeled telomeric probe in Church buffer at 50°C overnight. The membrane was washed twice in warm 2× SSC, 0.5% SDS, and twice in warm 1× SSC, 0.5% SDS, for 15 min/wash. The membrane was exposed to a phosphoimager screen. Radioactive signal was detected with a Typhoon Biomolecular Imager (GE). After signal detection, the membrane was stripped by incubation with boiled 0.1× SCC, 0.1% SDS at 50°C, three times for 15 min each and was checked for abolishment of signal. It was then blocked again in Church buffer for at least 1 h at 55°C. A 32P-radiolabeled 18S rRNA oligonucleotide probe was used for hybridization at 55°C overnight. The membrane was then treated as described for the telomeric probe.

  For RT-qPCR analysis of 15qTERRA levels, 3 μg of RNA were reverse-transcribed using 200 U of SuperScript III reverse transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific), GAPDH and TERRA reverse primers (Supplementary Table S2). Reverse transcription was performed at 55°C for 1 h, followed by heat inactivation at 70°C for 15 min. Reactions without reverse transcriptase were included as a control. 5% of the reaction were mixed with 2× Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 0.5 μM forward and reverse qPCR primers (Supplementary Table S2). qPCR was carried out at 95°C for 10 min, followed by 95°C for 15 s, and annealing and extension at 60°C for 1 min for 40 cycles in a QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Relative expression levels were analyzed by normalization first to GAPDH housekeeping mRNA and then to the PP7-alone 10kb sample.


### Telomere restriction fragment length analysis
  Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted with phenol-chloroform-isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1) and digested with HinfI and RsaI (NEB) in CutSmart buffer (NEB) overnight at 37°C. Two μg of digested gDNA/lane were resolved by constant-field gel electrophoresis in 0.8% agarose gel in 1× TBE for 17 h at 70 V. The gel was dried for 2 h at 50°C, denatured in 0.5 M NaOH, 1.5 M NaCl for 15 min on a shaker at room temperature, neutralized in 0.5 M Tris-Cl pH 7.0, 1.5 M NaCl for 10 min on a shaker at room temperature, and then blocked in Church buffer (as described above) for at least 1 h at 50°C. The gel was hybridized with a 32P-radiolabeled TeloC probe in Church buffer at 50°C overnight. Washes were done for 1 h/wash at 50°C, in 4× SSC, followed by 4× SSC 0.1% SDS and 2× SSC 0.1% SDS. The gel was then exposed to a phosphoimager screen. Radioactive signal was detected with a Typhoon GE.


### Halo labeling and immunofluorescence
  To assess whether Halo-TRF1 is correctly targeted to telomeres, cells were grown on round coverslips in six-well plates. For labeling of the Halo-tag, Janelia Fluor® 646 HaloTag® Ligand (Promega) was resuspended in DMSO to a 200 μM stock and diluted in complete warm cell culture medium to a final concentration of 200 nM (labeling medium). Cells were labeled by exchanging the medium with 2 ml labeling medium and incubated for 15 min in a controlled humidified atmosphere with 5% (v/v) CO2 at 37°C. Coverslips were washed three times with fresh warm cell culture medium before being washed twice with warm 1× PBS. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 10 min at room temperature, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, in 1× PBS (PBST) for 5 min at RT, and washed with 1× PBS. Coverslips were then incubated for 1 h at RT in blocking buffer (2%BSA, 10%goat serum in 1× PBS). Incubation with primary antibody against Rap1 (Supplementary Table S3) in blocking buffer was performed overnight at 4°C, in a humidified chamber. The following day, coverslips were washed three times with PBST for 5 min at RT each, before incubation with secondary antibody (Supplementary Table S3) in blocking buffer for 45 min at RT in a humidified chamber. Coverslips were then washed three times for 5 min at RT with PBST, nuclei were counterstained with 0.1 μg/ml DAPI in PBST for 5 min at RT, washed once with PBST and coverslips were mounted with Vectashield. Images were acquired with a Leica SP8 confocal microscope equipped with a 63×/1.40 oil objective and a DFC 7000 GT camera.


### Live-cell imaging
  For live cell imaging experiments, cells were seeded in absence of BIBR1532 onto glass bottom 35 mm dishes and were induced for pgb-construct expression with 1 μg/ml doxycycline, 24 h prior to imaging. Halo labeling was carried out as described above. After one wash with complete cell culture medium, cells were washed with FluoroBrite DMEM (Gibco), supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/ml of penicillin/streptomycin and 1× GlutaMAX twice, before being placed back in the incubator for at least 15 min.

  Live cells were imaged using a Nikon Confocal Spinning Disk microscope equipped with two Photometrics Prime 95B cameras and sCMOS Grayscale Chips. Imaging was performed with a 100× oil objective in an equilibrated incubation chamber at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Before each experiment, a set of fluorophore-coated beads was imaged for alignment of the movies.

  For 10 s movies, images were acquired as multi-channel single planes at a rate of 20 frames per second (50 ms exposure, 200 frames per movie). For the longer 5 min movies, images were acquired as multi-channel single planes at a rate of 1 frame per second (50 ms exposure, 300 frames per movie).


### Image processing and single particle tracking
  Before any image analysis was performed, images were first affinely aligned on Fiji [36], using the descriptor-based registration (2D/3D) plugin [37] and an image of fluorophore-coated beads as a reference. Aligned movies were then spatially cropped, following single cell detection on Fiji (Fig. 2B), so that each crop contains only one cell. In each crop, spots were detected independently per frame and channel, where one channel contains the telomeres (Halo-TRF1 signal) and the other the TERRA (or PP7-alone), using Spotiflow models trained on the Telomeres and Terra datasets, respectively, which consist of annotated frames of the movies obtained by the procedure described above, as previously described [38]. The optimized probability threshold on each dataset was used for Spotiflow. Single-particle tracking was performed using TrackMate [39] and the TrackMate CSV importer, with a spot radius of 0.15 μm and simple linear assignment problem tracking with the following parameters for telomeres/TERRA: linking maximum distance 0.22/0.60 μm, gap-closing maximum distance 0.44/1.0 μm and gap-closing maximum frame gap to 10 frames. For analysis of 10 s movies, tracks were processed using KNIME (see below). Longer 5 min movies, were manually analyzed, after name randomization for blind analysis of movies cropped to single-cells, focusing on interactions which lasted at least 10 s.


### Tracks processing and analysis
  Single-particle diffusion and telomere co-localization analysis was performed using the KNIME Analytics Platform [40] with a data processing workflow that is available from the Lingner lab upon request. The workflow determines IDCs for each particle as a measure of mobility as previously described [41]. It evaluates pair-wise co-localization of individual spots from both live imaging channels via a nearest neighbor search and defines two spots as co-localizing if their distance is below a user-defined threshold (0.33 µm = 3 pixels). Complete tracks are then considered co-localizing if they contain at least 5 co-localizing spot pairs. Only tracks that were at least 0.5 s (10 frames long), and only cells that contained at least one particle of each kind meeting this requirement were included in the analysis. Halo-TRF1 intensity was calculated as the mean intensity of the first five frames of each corresponding track. PP7-alone/15qTERRA intensity was calculated by subtracting the mean of the mean intensity of the first five frames of five randomly drawn circles in the nucleoplasm (diameter 3 pixels), from the mean intensity of the first five frames of each corresponding track. Residence time was calculated using the colocalization streak measure (subtraction of the number of last to first frame of colocalization) multiplied by the time interval between frames (0.05 s), for tracks that were at least 70 frames long and their continuity of colocalization ratio (ratio of number of colocalizations over colocalization streak) was at least 0.33. For the calculation of the mean intensities of telomeres per cell (Supplementary Fig. S4), only telomere tracks that were at least 0.5 s long were considered, and the co-localizing telomeres were selected for long co-localizations of TERRA as described above (at least 70 frames long; the continuity of colocalization ratio was at least 0.33 and the colocalization streak was at least 70 frames in order to focus on longer-lasting associations).


### Software and statistical analysis
  All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism with details indicated in figure legends. SuperPlots were generated on GraphPad Prism as previously described [42]. All image analysis and processing were performed using Fiji [36] and single particle tracks processing was performed using KNIME [40]. Illustrations were created using Adobe Illustrator and BioRender.com.


## Results

### A system for TERRA and telomere live cell imaging
  We previously established an experimental system to study TERRA localization and association with telomeres [15]. Transgenic TERRA was expressed and tagged with PP7 stem-loop structures and bound by co-expressed PCP-GFP fusion proteins that associated with the PP7 stem-loops. Using anti-GFP antibodies, TERRA was visualized in fixed cells. By expressing the transgenes from plasmids or a chromosome-internal locus we demonstrated that transgenic TERRA associates with telomeres post-transcription [15]. Moreover, transgenic TERRA was found to be recruited preferentially to short telomeres, forming R-loops in a RAD51-dependent manner [15]. We also demonstrated that endogenous TERRA requires RAD51 for generating R-loops [15]. Furthermore, R-loops by endogenous TERRA form also post-transcription in trans during oxidative stress [25] or upon CRISPR activation [17]. Overall, these findings indicate that endogenous TERRA molecules dissociate largely from telomeres during transcription and that they can re-associate with telomeres subsequently. Therefore, we presume that transgenic TERRA ectopically expressed from telomere external sites provides a valuable proxy for studying the localization of TERRA.

  To investigate TERRA dynamics and the mechanisms that underlie TERRA movement and localization we undertook a live-cell imaging approach. To visualize both telomeres and TERRA in live cells, we genomically engineered HeLa cells having an average telomere length of ~10 kb. To visualize telomeres, we fused a Halo-tag to the N-terminus of the telomeric shelterin component TRF1 through modification of the first exon using CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing (Fig. 1A–C). We confirmed genome editing by genomic PCR and western blot analysis (Fig. 1B and C) and selected a clone which was homozygous for Halo-TRF1. Halo-tagged TRF1 localizes to telomeres (Supplementary Fig. S1A) and is functional, as we observed no significant accumulation of abnormal telomeres when we analyzed telomere morphology by FISH on metaphase chromosome spreads (Fig. 1D). Depletion of TRF1 served as a control for induction of chromosomal abnormalities as previously reported [43]. N-terminally tagged TRF1 was also not impairing telomere function in other studies [44, 45] and was still able to physically associate with TERRA in vitro [46].

  To track TERRA, we fused PP7-stem loops to the 5′ end of 15qTERRA downstream of a dox-inducible promoter [15]. The PP7-15qTERRA construct comprises 24 copies of PP7 stem loops, followed by the 15q subtelomere sequence and ~90 copies of TTAGGG repeats (Fig. 1A). The transgene was inserted at a few loci of the HeLa genome with a piggyBac-transposase. As a control, we inserted a transgene for expression of 24 copies of the PP7 stem-loops alone (referred to as PP7-alone hereafter). To visualize tagged TERRA, a nuclear localization sequence carrying GFP-PCP protein which binds to the PP7 stem-loops was stably co-expressed upon lentiviral transduction (Fig. 1A). Up to 48 GFP-PCP molecules can bind the 24 PP7 stem-loops. We confirmed the expression of PP7-15qTERRA by RNA dot-blot hybridization (for detection of UUAGGG repeats) (Fig. 1E), by RT-qPCR (for the 15q subtelomere sequence) (Supplementary Fig. S1B) and by counting GFP–PCP spots under the microscope (Supplementary Fig. S1C).

  To be able to compare cells with long versus short telomeres, we knocked out the hTERT gene in the engineered HeLa cells as previously described [33] and propagated them in the presence of the telomerase inhibitor BIBR1532 (Supplementary Fig. S1D). Starting from the engineered HeLa clone with ~10 kb telomeres corresponding to a juvenile state [47] (referred to as HeLa-10kb cells henceforth, for the engineered cells expressing PP7-15qTERRA), we generated cells carrying critically short telomeres with an average telomere length of ~3 kb (referred to as HeLa-3kb cells, for the engineered cells expressing PP7-15qTERRA), as determined by telomere restriction fragment analysis (Fig. 1F). The difference in telomere length was also apparent when we compared the intensity of Halo-TRF1 foci labeled with JF646 Halo-ligand in live cells, as it was significantly lower in cells with short telomeres (Fig. 1G and Supplementary Fig. S1E), as previously reported [45]. As expected, the TRF1 signal does not scale linearly with telomere length since the density of TRF1 is lower at long telomeres [45, 48]. Of note, the expression of transgenic PP7-15qTERRA was reduced during the longer subculturing that was required to obtain short telomeres (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Fig. S1B and D). To determine whether the PP7-15qTERRA spots correspond to single RNA molecules, we plotted and compared the distribution of intensities of PP7-alone and PP7-15qTERRA (Supplementary Fig. S2). We observed one major peak of similar mean intensity for both RNAs [between 10 and 12 (A.U.)]. This indicates that the detected PP7-15qTERRA spots correspond mostly to single RNA molecules (Supplementary Fig. S2). Notably, we also detect larger TERRA clusters that represent multiple molecules (see below). Hence, we constructed a system that allows for live-cell visualization of both telomeres and single TERRA molecules.


### TERRA diffuses rapidly when compared to telomeres
  To study TERRA and telomere dynamics in parallel, we co-expressed PP7-15qTERRA and GFP-PCP. Endogenously Halo-tagged TRF1 was labeled with JF646-HaloTag ligand. We imaged HeLa-10k and HeLa-3kb live cells after hTERT gene disruption in absence of BIBR1532. We used a confocal microscope equipped with two cameras for simultaneous acquisition of PP7-15qTERRA and Halo-TRF1 (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Movie 1). Both TRF1-labeled telomeres and TERRA appeared as dots in the nucleus. For image processing and single particle tracking (Fig. 2B), we first cropped each cell to a single channel image, and used Spotiflow [38] for threshold-independent spot detection. The spots were then analyzed with the TrackMate software [39] for single particle tracking. Following processing of the tracks using KNIME [40], we were able to extract information about the movements and dynamics of TERRA and telomeres.

  During the 10 s movies, telomeres moved very slowly within a confined space. The calculated Instantaneous Diffusion Coefficient (IDC) [41] for telomeres was on average 0.047 μm2s−1 (Fig. 2C and D and Supplementary Fig. S3A and B) which is consistent with previously reported values [45, 49, 50]. Notably, the IDC of short telomeres is increased (Supplementary Fig. S3B), in agreement with previous reports [51]. In contrast, TERRA molecules showed more heterogeneous modes of movement, with some molecules showing low mobility diffusing within a confined space while others traversed within 10 s larger distances inside the nucleus of up to 2 µm (Fig. 2C). The average IDC of TERRA was 0.14 μm2s−1 (Fig. 2D and Supplementary Fig. S3A and B) and much higher than that of telomeres. The distribution of PP7-15qTERRA IDCs indicated the existence of two populations. The less mobile TERRA population overlapped with that of Halo-TRF1 (0.06 μm2s−1) whereas the more mobile population (0.16 μm2s−1) represented freely diffusing TERRA molecules (Fig. 2E and Supplementary Fig. S3C and D). The here measured mobility for non-telomere-bound PP7-15qTERRA is similar to previously published values for tagged TERRA molecules [0.09 µm2s−1 (ref [52]) and 0.15 µm2s−1 (ref [53])]. For comparison, rapidly diffusing telomerase populations showed faster diffusion coefficients of 0.3 and 1.3 µm2s−1 (ref [49]). We acknowledge that the PP7 tags as well as its associated PCP-GFP molecules are expected to diminish the diffusion coefficient of transgenic TERRA. Altogether our analysis reveals that while telomeres show a rather homogenous and low mobility, TERRA molecules move as two populations which can be distinguished by their low and high mobility, potentially representing freely diffusing, and chromatin-bound populations.


### TERRA associates with telomeres via two modes
  We quantified the number of TERRA-tracts that colocalized with a telomere during the 10 s movies (Fig. 3A). While the average percentage of tracks colocalizing with telomeres did not change, a considerable number of individual cells in which many (>25%) TERRA-tracts were colocalizing with telomeres was prominent in the HeLa-3kb population (Fig. 3A). Notably, when considering single cell averages, we did not observe a preferential association of TERRA at telomeres that show stronger TRF1 signals and therefore may be longer (Supplementary Fig. S4A and B). If at all, we observe in HeLa-3kb cells a slight trend for TERRA preferentially co-localizing with telomeres that have a lower TRF1-intensity than the total average in the cell.

  Assessment of the dynamics of telomere-TERRA colocalizations on kymographs identified two substantially different patterns of TERRA association with telomeres (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Movies S2 and S3). One TERRA fraction engaged in long-lasting associations with telomeres whereas another fraction exhibited frequent transient associations. This differential association prompted us to test whether there is any difference in the duration of TERRA association with telomeres, depending on their length. To identify high confidence interactions rather than transient or random binding events of TERRA to telomeres, we applied in the 10s movies (200 frames) the following two filtering criteria. First, we only considered TERRA tracks that could be followed for at least 3.5 s (≥70 frames). The 3.5 s threshold ensured that particles remained sufficiently long in the focal plane to reliably track their interaction with telomeres. Second, we only considered TERRA tracks colocalizing with telomeres when in at least 33% of the frames both TERRA and telomere signals could be detected, with both residing within a 3-pixel area. While these criteria are stringent, they prioritize high-confidence measurements of stable residence time and exclude highly fragmented interactions, thereby ensuring the robustness of our analysis. Associations lasted in both HeLa-10kb and HeLa-3kb cells from fractions of a second to interactions that persisted throughout the 10 s imaging timeframe (Fig. 3C). However, on average TERRA was seen to associate significantly longer with the short telomeres of HeLa-3kb cells (Fig. 3C). The difference between HeLa-10kb and HeLa-3kb was also apparent when plotting the probability of survival of TERRA-telomere associations (Fig. 3D), where it was evident that the interactions are more persistent in HeLa-3kb cells. To gain further insight into the associations that persisted beyond the 10 s time course, we imaged the cells at a rate of 1 frame per second (fps) for 5 min and measured the duration of TERRA-telomere associations that lasted longer than 10 s (Fig. 3E and Supplementary Movie S4). The half-life of these interactions was 2 min, which was two-fold higher at short telomeres (HeLa-3kb). 20% of the interactions in HeLa-3kb persisted even throughout the 5 min duration of the movies (Fig. 3E). Altogether, by analyzing TERRA-telomere association in high temporal resolution, we observe that TERRA associates very frequently in a transient manner with telomeres. In addition, TERRA makes rarer long-lived interactions that last for several minutes.


### Long telomeres can accommodate several TERRA molecules
  As seen in Supplementary Fig. S2, most analyzed nucleoplasmic TERRA molecules correspond to single molecules. When analyzing telomere-colocalizing TERRA and plotting the spot intensity of PP7-15qTERRA at telomeres as a function of residence time in HeLa-3kb cells, we again saw that most spots correspond to single molecules. Notably, there is no significant correlation between residence time and spot intensity (Supplementary Fig. S4C). However, in addition to the single TERRA molecules (Supplementary Movies S3 and S2, lower arrow), we also see multiple TERRA molecules at single telomeres (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Movie S2, upper arrow), in particular for longer-lasting associations (Supplementary Fig. S4C). Importantly, however, we did not see their intensity to diminish during the time course indicating that all TERRA molecules stayed together, behaving as one unit in single spots. In HeLa-10kb cells, TERRA intensity indicates that mostly multiple TERRA molecules may associate with a single telomere (Supplementary Fig. S4D). However, also in these cells, there was no correlation between residence time and TERRA intensity. Previous studies also reported TERRA clustering at telomeres [29, 52].


### Longer-lasting TERRA-telomere associations represent R-loops
  TERRA R-loops form at telomeres post-transcription in a RAD51 and RAD51AP1-dependent manner [15, 17, 22, 54]. We hypothesized that the longer-lasting TERRA-telomere interactions we observed may correspond to R-loops. To address this, we overexpressed RNaseH1—the enzyme that degrades the RNA moiety in DNA:RNA hybrids—in HeLa-10kb and Hela-3kb cells (Fig. 4A) and measured the duration of TERRA associations with telomeres. While RNaseH1 overexpression had no marked effects on the duration of TERRA-telomere associations in HeLa-10kb cells (Fig. 4B), the longest interactions that remained in HeLa-3kb cells lasted only 8 s (Fig. 4C). Therefore, we infer that the longer-lasting TERRA-telomere interactions at short telomeres in HeLa-3kb cells, which are RNaseH1 sensitive represent R-loop structures.

  To test whether the R-loop mediated interactions depend on HDR proteins as expected from previous reports on fixed cells, we depleted RAD51AP1 (Fig. 4D). We chose depletion of RAD51AP1 over RAD51, as depletion of the latter quickly impairs cell viability. Indeed, RAD51 and RAD51AP1 are both required for generating TERRA R-loops providing non-redundant functions [17]. Like RNaseH1 overexpression, depletion of RAD51AP1 in HeLa-10kb cells had no effect on the duration of TERRA-telomere associations (Fig. 4E). In contrast, partial depletion of RAD51AP1 decreased the duration of TERRA-telomere associations in HeLa-3kb cells, as expected from a protein that promotes TERRA R-loop formation (Fig. 4F). The above results indicate that the longer lasting associations between TERRA and telomeres in cells with short telomeres represent, at least in part, R-loops. They are sensitive to overexpression of RNaseH1 and the depletion of RAD51AP1. On the other hand, TERRA associations with long telomeres appear not to depend on R-loops.


### TRF2 mediates transient associations of TERRA with telomeres
  While we reasoned that the R-loop mediated TERRA-telomere interactions are longer lasting because of the stability of DNA:RNA hybrids, we hypothesized that transient associations of TERRA with telomeres may be mediated by a protein. It has recently been reported that unlike other shelterin components, TRF2 exhibits higher diffusion rates and more dynamic interactions with telomeres [45]. Since TRF2 was also shown to bind TERRA [55, 56] and since it promotes TERRA binding to telomeric DNA in vitro [57] it deemed as a potential candidate for mediating the transient dynamic associations of TERRA with telomeres. We depleted TRF2 in HeLa-10kb and HeLa-3kb cells (Fig. 5A) and analyzed the duration of TERRA-telomere associations. In HeLa-10kb cells, no effect was observed upon TRF2 depletion (Fig. 5B). Strikingly, in HeLa-3kb cells TRF2 depletion reduced the association time of transient interactions below 8 s, while the longer lasting interactions remained unaffected (Fig. 5C). Altogether, this indicates that at cells with critically short telomeres TRF2 is mediating dynamic associations of TERRA with telomeres whereas the long-lasting interactions are R-loop dependent. In cells with long and healthy telomeres TERRA also visits telomeres but these interactions do not require TRF2 and do not involve R-loops.


## Discussion
  Telomere shortening due to the end replication problem or accidental telomeric DNA damage poses a threat to all proliferating cells which do not express telomerase. As an alternative to telomerase, HDR provides a telomere healing and elongation pathway to counteract cellular senescence or apoptosis. HDR is repressed at long and intact telomeres. However, it counteracts telomere shortening and delays cellular senescence at critically short telomeres [20]. Furthermore, HDR, and not telomerase, is the mechanism for telomere length maintenance in ALT cancers [8]. Importantly, in ALT cancer and in cells approaching cellular senescence TERRA R-loops are crucial to trigger telomere maintenance by HDR [18, 20]. More specifically, replication stress induced by TERRA R-loops [18] can mediate a switch towards break-induced replication [17]. In addition, it has been proposed that TERRA R-loops may stimulate directly the HDR reaction [22], further highlighting their role as regulators in HDR-mediated telomere maintenance/repair. Other less-well characterized roles of TERRA at telomeres include the stimulation of chromosome end joining [58], the regulation of telomerase [26–28], and chromatin structure [25, 59]. However, how TERRA finds and associates with telomeres in live cells to elicit its functions has so far been undetermined.

  Our live-cell imaging analysis reveals the existence of two parallel pathways which are active in cells with very short telomeres corresponding to an aged state [47]. The data suggest the following model (Fig. 6). TERRA diffuses rapidly through the nucleus and makes frequent transient interactions with telomeres. In cells with short telomeres the transient interactions occur through binding of TERRA to TRF2. TRF2 itself binds to telomeres in a dynamic fashion [45], which may decrease the residence time of TERRA at telomeres but also facilitate its association, if TRF2 captures TERRA in the nucleoplasm prior to telomere binding. In addition, we acknowledge that TRF2 depletion may impair TERRA association with telomeres through effects on telomere structure. In parallel, TERRA binds to short telomeres forming R-loops post-transcription. These binding events are much longer lived and last from seconds to several minutes. To our knowledge, our data in which we detect RNaseH1-sensitive TERRA molecules at telomeres and measure their residence time represent the most direct measurement of TERRA R-loop persistence in live cells. In previous work, TERRA R-loop dynamics were inferred only indirectly from the turnover of an engineered DNA-RNA binding protein (RHINO) [60]. These long-lived R-loop structures may facilitate the onset of telomere repair and elongation by HDR. Complementary to this, the TRF2-mediated telomere interactions may serve to increase the local concentration of TERRA near telomeres facilitating its stable association via HDR-factor mediated R-loops when short telomeres need to be repaired. This function may be particularly pertinent in cells with low TERRA concentration. In addition, TRF2 may even catalyze telomeric R-loops on its own under specialized damage conditions. Normally, TRF1 represses the ability of TRF2 to catalyze TERRA R-loops at telomeres [24]. In presence of high oxidative stress, however, TRF1 dissociates from telomeres and TERRA R-loops are formed by the action of TRF2 [25]. In cells with long and healthy telomeres, we also see that TERRA still visits telomeres frequently, but these interactions do not rely on neither TRF2 nor R-loops.

  The mechanisms of TERRA recruitment to long telomeres remains to be uncovered. It is possible that a telomeric protein that binds TERRA and is more abundant at long telomeres is involved. TRF1 exhibits both of these characteristics, as it binds to TERRA [55] and scales with telomere length [45, 48]; however, its involvement remains speculative at this time. The function of TERRA at long telomeres remains also unclear. It has been proposed that TERRA limits telomerase action at long telomeres to mediate telomere length homeostasis [29]. On the other hand, TERRA levels are extremely low in telomerase-positive cancer cells during late S phase when telomerase is supposed to act while TERRA is much more abundant in G1 and G2 [13, 19]. Thus, an exciting possibility may be that TERRA represses human telomerase outside of S phase, to prevent the inappropriate action of telomerase at telomeres and broken DNA ends.

  In previous work, we used fixed cells to assess TERRA association with telomeres [15] and we demonstrated that TERRA R-loops form post-transcription most frequently at telomeres when they are short. However, the several-minutes long fixation protocol may have preserved mostly the long-lived TERRA-R- loop dependent telomere interactions while losing information on more the transiently associating or diffusing molecules that may have been washed out during the procedure. Thus, through live-cell imaging we overcome these limitations and obtain an accurate picture not only on R-loop dependent associations but also the protein-mediated associations which require TRF2. Our live-cell imaging data also demonstrate that even within single cells, TERRA has a slight preference to associate with shorter telomeres which carry less TRF1 molecules (Supplementary Fig. S4). This makes biological sense when considering that the repair of the shortest telomeres is most urgent. Our data are in apparent disagreement with a recent paper [29], which proposed that TERRA associates within single cells with the telomeres that were inferred to be the longest, based on the intensity of RAP1 foci. However, multiple methodological differences may explain the divergent conclusions.

  Intriguingly, our findings on TERRA dynamics and recruitment to telomeres show interesting parallels with the ribonucleoprotein telomerase [49, 61]. Both TERRA and telomerase interact with telomeres via proteins and through base-pairing. TERRA binds TRF2 whereas telomerase the shelterin component TPP1. In addition, TERRA and telomerase both engage in long-lasting interactions with telomeres mediated by base-pairing with telomeric DNA, on one hand in the form of an R-loop (TERRA) or on the other hand at the 3′overhang of telomeres (telomerase). An important difference is that for telomerase, TPP1-mediated recruitment is coupled to the binding by base-pairing to the 3′ overhang whereas for TERRA the TRF2 and R-loop mediated pathways can occur in parallel.

  In the future, it will be important to unravel the mechanisms that incite the TERRA recruitment pathways at short telomeres. It will also be fascinating to combine live cell imaging of TERRA and HDR factors, in order to understand their coordination during recombination-based repair of telomeres in pre-senescent cells as well as in ALT cancer.


### Limitations of the study
  PP7-15qTERRA was expressed from chromosome internal sites which allowed to study post-transcriptional association of TERRA with chromosome ends. We note that a putative co-transcriptional association of endogenous TERRA with telomeres cannot be detected with this system. Our study does also not consider different stages of the cell cycle, which may influence the modes of interaction of TERRA and telomeres. Lastly, the presence of PP7 stem loops as well as the binding of PCP-GFP may alter the diffusion velocity of TERRA as compared to endogenous TERRA, and different methods should be developed in the future for direct visualization of endogenous TERRA molecules.
