# Single-cell and spatial transcriptome analyses unveil that MYH11 links stemness to immunotherapy resistance in non-small cell lung cancer


## Background
  Immunotherapy is an essential treatment strategy for lung cancer, yet resistance to it is often unavoidable, resulting in treatment failure and poor prognosis. The cancer stem cell (CSC) theory offers a valuable perspective for understanding tumor metastasis and resistance to immunotherapy. However, there is a scarcity of multi-omics studies that identify tumor stemness subpopulations and their associated signaling pathways. In this study, we combined single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) with spatial transcriptomics sequencing (ST-seq) to identify a myosin heavy chain 11 (MYH11)-high stemness subpopulation linked to immunotherapy resistance.


## Methods
  We conducted quality control and removed batch effects from two scRNA-seq datasets, then annotated cell types using established marker genes. Malignant cells were distinguished from epithelial cells with fastCNV. Stemness subpopulations were identified using a machine learning-derived messenger RNA (mRNA) stemness index (mRNAsi) and CytoTRACE analysis. To functionally characterize the stemness subpopulation, we employed Monocle3 pseudotime trajectory analysis, CellChat cell-cell communication analysis, and predicted transcription factors (TFs). We also performed consensus clustering on bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data, followed by immune-related scoring, immune cell deconvolution, and drug sensitivity prediction. Clinical validation was achieved through multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) and immunohistochemistry (IHC). Experimental validation included short hairpin RNA (shRNA)-mediated MYH11 knockdown, real-time quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) and western blot analyses, and tumor sphere formation assays to assess cancer stemness.


## Results
  We identified nine major cell types and found a significant enrichment of epithelial cells in tumors resistant to immunotherapy. Using fastCNV, we identified malignant cells and defined seven malignant cell subtypes based on stemness scoring. Notably, the Stem-subtype was significantly enriched in resistant tumors. Functional analysis showed that the Stem-subtype could differentiate into other malignant cell subtypes, with a strong enrichment in the NOTCH signaling pathway. Analysis of fibroblast subpopulations revealed that MYH11+ fibroblasts were closely linked to the Stem-subtype, collectively contributing to immunotherapy resistance. Consensus clustering identified two stemness-related subtypes, with Stemness I associated with poor prognosis. Immune scoring and immune cell analysis further indicated an unfavorable response to immunotherapy in Stemness I. Drug sensitivity prediction identified potential therapeutic agents, and in silico knockout experiments revealed downstream target genes. Validation with clinical samples confirmed that MYH11-high cancer cells were closely correlated with exhausted CD8+ T cell infiltration and poor immunotherapy outcomes. Experimental validation demonstrated that MYH11 knockdown significantly reduced the expression levels of CXCL12 and TUBA1A, as well as the expression of core NOTCH pathway genes. Furthermore, the tumor sphere-forming capacity was markedly suppressed.


## Conclusions
  In summary, our multi-omics analysis revealed a MYH11-high malignant cell stemness subpopulation and a MYH11+ fibroblast subpopulation, both of which are closely linked to immunotherapy resistance and poor prognosis in lung cancer.


## Introduction
  Lung cancer continues to have the highest incidence and mortality rates globally (1). While immunotherapy has notably enhanced the prognosis for non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients, resistance to such treatments persists as a formidable challenge (2). Addressing immunotherapy resistance and creating personalized treatment strategies remain vital research priorities.

  Conventional bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) (3) and whole-exome sequencing (4) have significantly advanced the identification of molecular markers and potential therapeutic targets related to immunotherapy resistance in NSCLC. Despite these advancements, these methods are limited in resolving specific cell subtypes and their spatial localization within the tumor microenvironment. In contrast, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) is a powerful technology that offers transcriptomic profiling at single-cell resolution (5). This capability provides unparalleled advantages in identifying cell subtypes within malignant tissues and elucidating the mechanisms underlying immunotherapy resistance in lung cancer (6). Spatial transcriptomics sequencing (ST-seq) further enhances this by offering a robust platform to map the spatial distribution of immune cells and characterize the immune microenvironment, facilitating the discovery of predictive biomarkers and mechanisms of immunotherapy resistance (7,8).

  Cancer stem cells (CSCs) are a subset of cells with the ability to self-renew, driving tumor initiation, metastasis, and resistance to therapy (9). Recent studies have shown that CSCs also facilitate immune evasion and resistance to immunotherapy (10). In the tumor immune microenvironment, interactions between tumor cells and immune cells, such as T cells, B cells, macrophages, and NK cells, as well as stromal components like cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and adipocytes, provide growth factors and paracrine signals that promote CSC formation. Consequently, CSCs increase intratumoral heterogeneity and contribute to tumor recurrence, metastasis, and treatment resistance (11,12). Targeted therapies aimed at CSCs offer potential solutions for overcoming immune evasion and reversing immunotherapy resistance, which is a significant focus in addressing lung cancer treatment resistance (13). Despite this, the identification of key molecular markers for CSCs in NSCLC and their spatial relationship with immune cells remains inadequately understood and requires further research.

  Myosin heavy chain 11 (MYH11) is crucial for smooth muscle contraction and significantly influences cell motility and tissue homeostasis (14). Increasing evidence links MYH11 dysregulation to tumor progression. For example, MYH11 expression is notably reduced in gastric and colorectal cancers, with mutations such as missense mutations or deletions correlating with increased tumor aggressiveness (15). MYH11 is also involved in the development and progression of bladder cancer (16) and prostate cancers (17). Recent studies emphasize the role of MYH11+ CAFs in colorectal cancer, especially in left-sided tumors, where their presence is linked to greater tumor invasiveness and poorer prognosis. These MYH11+ CAFs facilitate tumor cell migration and invasion by interacting with immune cells like macrophages (18,19). In lung cancer, MYH11 expression is lower than in normal lung tissue, and reduced MYH11 levels are associated with poor outcomes (20). Recent research on MYH11 and the immune microenvironment shows that MYH11+ αSMA+ CAFs form a monolayer around tumor nests, hindering T cell infiltration into the tumor parenchyma, which suggests a potential mechanism for immune resistance (21). Moreover, MYH11+ fibroblasts have distinct roles in stem cell maintenance, metabolic activity, and immune regulation (22). In gastric cancer, MYH11+ fibroblasts are closely linked to decreased infiltration of CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and NK cells (23).

  In this study, we combined scRNA-seq, ST-seq, and our own immunotherapy cohort to pinpoint molecular markers linked to immunotherapy resistance in NSCLC. The scRNA-seq analysis utilized two independent lung cancer immunotherapy datasets to identify stemness subpopulations strongly associated with resistance. A corresponding ST-seq dataset was used to map the spatial distribution of these subpopulations and their interactions with immune cells. Clinical samples were then analyzed to assess the prognostic significance of these findings. Our results identify a stemness subpopulation closely tied to immunotherapy resistance in NSCLC, clarify its spatial distribution and potential signaling pathways, and suggest a possible therapeutic target for overcoming resistance. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR reporting checklist (available at https://tlcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tlcr-2026-0927/rc).


## Methods

### Patient samples
  This study involved the collection of formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples from 129 patients with NSCLC who underwent immunotherapy at Gaozhou People’s Hospital. These samples underwent immunohistochemical (IHC) staining, and 14 representative cases were chosen for multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) analysis. This study analyzed data from NSCLC patients who underwent immunotherapy at the Gaozhou People’s Hospital Affiliated to Guangdong Medical University between January 2019 and December 2025. The primary inclusion criteria were: (I) a pathological confirmation of lung squamous cell carcinoma or lung adenocarcinoma before treatment; (II) no other primary malignancies; and (III) receipt of standard immunotherapy, either alone or combined with chemotherapy, without restrictions on the specific immunotherapeutic agents used. Exclusion criteria were: (I) missing follow-up or pathological data; (II) absence of corresponding clinical samples for testing; and (III) uncertain pathological type or presence of small cell lung cancer components. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. All procedures involving human participants were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Gaozhou People’s Hospital Affiliated to Guangdong Medical University (approval No. GYLLYJ-2024183). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. Eligible patients were followed up according to standard protocols until the last visit.


### Data collection
  This study utilized multiple publicly available datasets to characterize NSCLC at the scRNA-seq, ST-seq, and bulk RNA-seq levels. The primary datasets included scRNA-seq data from GSE205335 and GSE207422 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). ST-seq data were sourced from matched samples corresponding to the GSE207422 dataset (https://zenodo.org, accession number: 8227624). Bulk RNA-seq and clinical data for clustering subtypes were acquired from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), encompassing lung adenocarcinoma and lung squamous cell carcinoma histologies. Furthermore, messenger RNA (mRNA) expression profiles from 30 patients undergoing immunotherapy were obtained from GSE207422.


### scRNA-seq data processing and analysis
  scRNA-seq data from 43 NSCLC tissues (GSE205335 and GSE207422) were analyzed using the “Seurat” and “SingleR” R packages. The analysis adhered to standard quality control protocols, employing functions such as PercentageFeatureSet, NormalizeData, RunPCA, FindNeighbors, FindClusters, RunUMAP, and FindAllMarkers. Cell types were identified with the “SingleR” function and manually annotated based on well-established cell-type markers from the literature (8). These markers included epithelial cells (EPCAM, KRT19), fibroblasts (COL1A1, DCN), endothelial cells (EGFL7, PECAM1), T cells (CD3D, CD3E), B cells (CD79A, MS4A1), neutrophils (S100A8, FCGR3B), macrophages (C1QB, CD14), and mast cells (KIT, MS4A2). The sources of all scRNA-seq datasets and their corresponding clinical data are presented in Table S1.


### Calculation of mRNA stemness index (mRNAsi)
  The mRNAsi was derived from pluripotent stem cell RNA-seq data obtained from the Progenitor Cell Biology Consortium database (syn2701943) and was normalized to the mean. Stemness features were identified through the one-class logistic regression (OCLR) machine learning algorithm, followed by leave-one-out cross-validation (24). Subsequently, a Spearman correlation analysis was conducted between the normalized expression matrix of NSCLC samples and the stemness signature. The Spearman correlation coefficients were rescaled to a 0 to 1 range to calculate the mRNAsi, with higher values indicating greater tumor dedifferentiation and a more pronounced stem-like phenotype.


### fastCNV analysis and malignant cell definition
  Chromosomal copy number variations (CNVs) on a large scale were inferred using the “fastCNV” R package, with B cells and T cells as reference populations. A threshold of 0.2 was established based on the distribution of CNV scores: cells scoring above this threshold were classified as malignant, while those scoring below were identified as normal epithelial cells. Malignant cell subpopulations underwent further analysis, including dimensionality reduction, clustering, and manual annotation.


### CytoTRACE and monocle3 trajectory analysis
  Cell differentiation states were assessed using the CytoTRACE algorithm (25), which provides a score indicating transcriptional diversity and stemness. For malignant cells, CytoTRACE scores, calculated via the CytoTRACE R package, range from 0 to 1, with higher values signifying less differentiation and greater stemness. The subpopulation exhibiting the lowest CytoTRACE score served as the root node for pseudotime analysis, conducted with the “Monocle3” R package, to reconstruct developmental trajectories across various cell types (26). Pseudotime scores were then utilized to map cell progression along the differentiation axis.


### Cell-cell communication analysis
  Intercellular communication was assessed using the “CellChat” R package (27). The analysis employed the CellChatDB.human database to explore signaling interactions and the functional roles of cell populations, emphasizing differential interactions and signaling pathways among malignant cell subpopulations and between the Stem-subtype and four fibroblast subpopulations. Cell types with fewer than 10 cells or with gene expression detected in less than 10% of cells were excluded. Communication probabilities were determined using the truncated mean method. Differential interaction networks between cell types were identified by comparing interaction weights and counts. Cell-cell communication was visualized through heatmaps and chord diagrams.


### Transcription factor (TF) activity inference
  TF activity was inferred using the “decoupleR” framework based on curated TF-target interactions (28). We selected human DoRothEA regulons with confidence levels A, B, and C. The run_ulm function was employed to estimate TF activity by applying a univariate linear model that integrates TF expression levels with their target genes.


### Functional enrichment analysis
  We conducted a functional enrichment analysis of marker genes from the stemness subpopulation using Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Gene Ontology (GO) analyses, with a focus on stemness features and immunotherapy-related pathways (29). In summary, we utilized the “clusterProfiler”, “GOplot”, and “GseaVis” R packages for functional enrichment and visualization.


### SPOTlight deconvolution analysis
  Spatial transcriptomic deconvolution was conducted with the “SPOTlight” R package, as outlined by Elosua-Bayes et al. (30). This analysis was extended beyond the nine major cell types to include malignant cell subpopulations and fibroblast subpopulations. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed to statistically analyze the spatial cell proportions. Additionally, spatial cell-cell communication between the stemness subpopulation and four fibroblast subtypes was analyzed using “CellChat” R package.


### Stemness subtype identification and stemness gene correlation analysis
  Stemness subtypes were delineated using the “ConsensusClusterPlus” R package (31), with marker genes of the Stem-subtype serving as core genes. K-means clustering, employing Euclidean distance, was conducted with 80% resampling of samples across 1,000 iterations. The optimal number of clusters (k) was determined by analyzing the consensus matrix and cumulative distribution function (CDF) plots. To assess differences in overall survival among the identified stemness subtypes, Kaplan-Meier survival curves were constructed. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted between MYH11 and published common stemness-related genes, followed by the generation of correlation scatter plots.


### In silico knockout analysis
  To predict the functional role of MYH11 at the single-cell level, we utilized “scTenifoldKnk” R package to conduct a virtual knockout in the stemness subtype or MYH11+ fibroblasts (32). This algorithm identifies significantly dysregulated genes with a Z-score greater than 2. Notably, known downstream targets like TUBA1A and CXCL12 were among the top-ranked perturbed genes.


### Functional analysis of post-clustering subtypes
  Functional analyses of the two post-clustering subtypes included immune cell prediction, immune cell proportion estimation, and drug sensitivity prediction. Briefly, the cell-type identification by the estimating relative subsets of RNA transcripts (CIBERSORT), “CIBERSORT” R package was used to estimate immune cell proportions and analyze correlations between genes and immune cells in the Stemness I and Stemness II subtypes (33). The estimate R package was used to calculate and compare StromalScore, ImmuneScore, and ESTIMATEScore between the two subtypes (34). Drug sensitivity prediction and statistical analysis for common drugs were performed using the “oncoPredict” R package (35).


### mIF
  Samples were embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 5 μm, and prepared as FFPE slides. mIF was performed using the Triple Label Four-Color Multiplex Fluorescence Staining Kit (Beyotime, P1351S) to assess the following markers: Pan-cytokeratin (PanCK) (Proteintech, 26411-1-AP), MYH11 (Proteintech, 21404-1-AP), Fibroblast Activation Protein (FAP) (Proteintech, 11779-1-AP), PD1 (Proteintech, 18106-1-AP), CD3 (Proteintech, 17617-1-AP), and CD8 (Proteintech, 66868-1-Ig). The procedure included deparaffinization, antigen retrieval, blocking of endogenous peroxidase, and incubation with immunostaining blocking buffer (Beyotime, P0102) for 60 minutes at room temperature, followed by incubation with primary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature. Sections were then treated with secondary antibodies for 30 minutes at room temperature, followed by tyramide signal amplification for 10 minutes at room temperature. After the first round of staining, antibody stripping was performed before proceeding to the second and third rounds of antibody labeling. 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Proteintech, 28718-90-3) was applied for 5 minutes at room temperature for nuclear staining. Slides were mounted in anti-fade medium and stored at 4 ℃ until imaging.


### IHC staining
  Tissue sections underwent a series of processes: fixation, deparaffinization, rehydration, and antigen retrieval. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with an anti-MYH11 primary antibody (Proteintech, catalog number 21404-1-AP) at a 1:1000 dilution. The percentage of positive cells was categorized as follows: 0–5%=0; 6–25%=1; 26–50%=2; 51–75%=3; 76–100%=4. Staining intensity, encompassing both cytoplasmic and nuclear staining, was scored as: no staining =0; weak staining =1; moderate staining =2; strong staining =3. The final IHC score, ranging from 0 to 12, was determined by multiplying the percentage score by the intensity score. Based on the median MYH11 IHC score, a score of 6 was defined as the cutoff threshold. Cases with an IHC score <6 were classified into the low-expression group, and those with an IHC score ≥6 were classified into the high-expression group. All clinical cases used for IHC analysis and their corresponding clinicopathologic characteristics are provided in Table S2.


### Cell lines and culture
  The A549 cell lines were sourced from HyCyte (Suzhou, China) and cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Procell, RPMI-1640), which was supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, C0232) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (HyClone, SV30010). The cells were maintained at 37 ℃ in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.


### RNA extraction and real-time quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR)
  Total RNA was extracted using the Cell/Tissue Total RNA Kit (Yeasen, 19221ES50). cDNA synthesis was performed with ®Hifair III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix for qPCR (gDNA digester plus) (Yeasen, 11141ES60). qRT-PCR was then carried out using ®Hieff UNICON Universal Blue qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen, 11184ES08), with GAPDH serving as the internal control. The primer details are provided in Table S3. All experiments were carried out with three biological replicates.


### Short hairpin RNA (shRNA) and transient transfection
  shRNAs were sourced from HyCyte (Suzhou, China). Two independent shRNAs constructs, shMYH11#1 and shMYH11#2, were designed to target different coding regions of human MYH11, along with a non-targeting scrambled control shRNA (shNC) for transient transfection. A549 cells were seeded into six-well plates with antibiotic-free complete medium and cultured overnight to achieve approximately 70–80% confluence by the time of transfection. Plasmids were introduced into the cells using Lipofectamine 300 (Invitrogen, L3000008). Cells were collected 48–72 hours post-transfection for RNA and protein extraction. The knockdown efficiency was verified through qRT-PCR and Western blotting. The shRNA sequences are detailed in Table S4.


### Western blotting
  Cellular proteins were extracted using RIPA buffer (Beyotime, P0013C), separated on SDS-PAGE gels, and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. Primary antibodies against specific proteins were diluted in primary antibody dilution buffer (Beyotime, P0023A) and applied to the membranes overnight at 4 ℃. The membranes were then incubated with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 hour. Protein expression levels were quantified through chemiluminescence. Details of the antibodies used in the western blot analysis are provided in Table S5.


### Sphere formation assay
  Two thousand A549 cells, transfected transiently with shMYH11#1, shMYH11#2, or shNC plasmids for 48 hours, were seeded into each well of ultralow-attachment 6-well plates (Corning, 3471). The wells contained serum-free RPMI 1640 medium, supplemented with 1X B27 supplement (Gibco, 17504044), 20 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (NovoProtein, C029-B), and 20 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor (NovoProtein, C046-A). After 7 days, the spheres formed were quantified under a microscope, counting those with a diameter of ≥50 μm.


### Statistical analysis
  We conducted all statistical analyses using R (version 4.5.3) and GraphPad Prism (version 11.0). To evaluate survival, we employed Kaplan-Meier curves and log-rank tests. We utilized univariate Cox regression analysis via the survival R package to identify factors affecting survival in the immunotherapy cohort, multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed to identify independent prognostic factors. For continuous variables, we applied a t-test when data followed a normal distribution; otherwise, we used the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. A P-value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.


## Results

### Multi-omics identification of stemness-related genes associated with immunotherapy resistance in NSCLC
  The study workflow is illustrated in Figure 1A. To identify key genes associated with immunotherapy resistance in NSCLC, we integrated published scRNA-seq, ST-seq, and bulk RNA-seq datasets, along with our own IHC and mIF cohorts. The scRNA-seq datasets included GSE205335 and GSE207422, featuring tissue types from non-responsive primary lung tumors (P_NR), responsive primary tumors (P_R), and non-responsive metastatic lymph nodes (LN_NR). After rigorous quality control (Figure S1A) and batch effect correction (Figure S1B), we retained 205,283 cells for further analysis. Using established cell markers for lung cancer scRNA-seq analysis, we categorized these cells into nine major cell types: Epithelial cells (n=52,868), Fibroblasts (n=5,639), Mast cells (n=1,858), Plasma cells (n=18,193), Endothelial cells (n=6,132), T cells (n=57,632), B cells (n=18,536), Macrophages (n=41,950), and Neutrophils (n=2,475) (Figure 1B). Each cell type was accurately matched with its annotated marker genes (Figure 1C and Figure S1C,S1D). Notably, there was a significant increase in the proportion of epithelial cells in P_NR, especially in LN_NR (Figure 1D), indicating that epithelial cells might be crucial in immunotherapy resistance. We then conducted fastCNV analysis on epithelial cells to identify malignant cells, distinguishing 16,357 malignant cells from 36,511 normal epithelial cells (Figure 1E and Figure S1E). Malignant cells and fibroblasts are widely recognized as pivotal in driving immunotherapy resistance in lung cancer. Consequently, we performed further subpopulation analysis on these two cell types, resulting in 29 clusters for cancer cells (Figure 1F) and 22 clusters for fibroblasts (Figure 1G).


### The MYH11-high stemness subpopulation is associated with immunotherapy resistance in NSCLC
  To identify the stemness subpopulation among malignant cells, we initially conducted stemness scoring (mRNAsi) on these subpopulations. This analysis revealed that clusters 3, 5, 6, 10, and 16 had the highest mRNAsi scores (Figure 2A). Further CytoTRACE analysis, which evaluates differentiation potential, showed that clusters with elevated mRNAsi scores also had greater differentiation potential, while those with lower scores were well-differentiated (Figure 2B). From these findings, we categorized the malignant cells into seven subpopulations: the Stem-Subtype, characterized by the highest stemness score and differentiation potential, and Differentiated-Subtype 1 and Differentiated-Subtype 2, which had the lowest stemness scores, indicating well-differentiated subpopulations (Figure 2C). Notably, marker genes in the Stem-Subtype included MYH11 and the stemness gene WNT11, suggesting a strong association of MYH11 with stemness characteristics in NSCLC immunotherapy (Figure 2D). We found that the Stem-Subtype was significantly enriched in P_NR, while Differentiated-Subtype 1 was notably reduced (Figure 2E). A volcano plot of differentially expressed genes in the Stem-Subtype identified MYH11 as the most highly expressed gene (Figure 2F), and UMAP visualization confirmed prominent expression of MYH11 and WNT11 in the Stem-Subtype (Figure 2G). Volcano plots for differentially expressed genes in other malignant cell subpopulations are presented in Figure S2A.

  We conducted a functional characterization of the Stem-Subtype. Using Monocle3 pseudotime trajectory analysis alongside CytoTRACE, we identified the Stem-Subtype as the differentiation origin, indicating its potential to evolve into other malignant cell types (Figure 3A). The differentiation trajectory of these malignant cells showed a gradient from high to low MYH11 expression (Figure 3B). Analysis of cell-cell communication among the seven malignant cell subpopulations revealed that the Stem-Subtype primarily interacted with Differentiated-Subtype 1 and Differentiated-Subtype 2 (Figure 3C). The main ligand-receptor pairs in NOTCH signaling were JAG1-NOTCH2 and JAG1-NOTCH3, suggesting a functionally relevant signaling pathway (Figure 3D). To identify potential TFs, we performed TF prediction across the seven malignant subpopulations. The findings indicated that the Stem-Subtype was associated with TFs such as GLI2, HOXA5, and HOXC8 (Figure 3E and Figure S2B). Pathway enrichment analysis using Stem-Subtype marker genes showed that GO functional enrichment was closely related to stemness pathways (Figure 3F). GSEA identified HALLMARK NOTCH SIGNALING and HALLMARK WNT BETA CATENIN SIGNALING as the two most significantly enriched pathways (Figure 3G). Further KEGG pathway analysis indicated enrichment in the TNF signaling pathway and VEGF signaling pathway (Figure S2C). Overall, these results demonstrate that the Stem-Subtype is a core cell subpopulation driving stemness-associated immunotherapy resistance in lung cancer.


### MYH11+ fibroblast subpopulations are closely associated with immunotherapy-resistant stemness
  To pinpoint fibroblast subpopulations closely associated with stemness traits linked to immunotherapy resistance in NSCLC, we conducted stemness-related mRNAsi scoring on these subpopulations. The analysis revealed that clusters 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 8, 11, and 16 had notably elevated mRNAsi scores (Figure 4A). Using stemness scores and cell marker genes, we categorized the fibroblasts into four primary subpopulations: PLAAT4+ Fibroblasts, CYP1B1+ Fibroblasts, MYH11+ Fibroblasts, and IGF2+ Fibroblasts (Figure 4B), with their respective marker genes detailed in Figure 4C. Similar to the Stem-Subtype, MYH11+ Fibroblasts were significantly enriched in P_NR, while CYP1B1+ and IGF2+ Fibroblasts were notably diminished (Figure 4D). Differential expression analysis across fibroblast subtypes indicated that MYH11 was the most significantly upregulated gene in MYH11+ Fibroblasts (Figure 4E), whereas its expression was considerably lower in other subtypes (Figure 4F). Volcano plots for differentially expressed genes in other fibroblast subtypes are presented in Figure S3A.

  We conducted a cell-cell communication analysis between the Stem-Subtype and four fibroblast subpopulations. The findings revealed that the Stem-Subtype had the most robust interaction with MYH11+ Fibroblasts (Figure 4G), particularly through the NOTCH signaling network, as illustrated in the communication heatmap (Figure 4H). Ligand-receptor analysis further identified JAG1-NOTCH3 as the primary interacting pair (Figure S3B). Monocle3 pseudotime trajectory analysis of fibroblast subtypes suggested that MYH11+ Fibroblasts are the origin of differentiation, indicating their potential to transform into other fibroblast types, with the differentiation trajectory following a gradient from high to low MYH11 expression (Figure 4I). To identify potential TFs, we performed TF prediction across the four fibroblast subpopulations. The results indicated that MYH11+ Fibroblasts were associated with TFs such as SIRT1, FOXP3, and SMAD7 (Figure S3C,S3D).


### Spatial localization of the stemness subpopulation
  To better understand the spatial localization of the cell subpopulations mentioned earlier, we conducted a cell type deconvolution analysis using “SPOTlight”. The correlation heatmap of cell types indicated clear discriminability among the identified populations (Figure 5A). We initially mapped the spatial distribution of all nine cell types in P_NR and P_R (Figure 5B,5C). The findings revealed a significant enrichment of epithelial cells and fibroblasts in the P_NR group. Further spatial mapping of subpopulations showed that the Stem-Subtype was primarily located within P_NR tissues, with MYH11+ fibroblasts nearby, suggesting a crucial spatial arrangement that may contribute to immunotherapy resistance. Interestingly, Differentiated-Subtype 2 was infrequently observed in P_NR tissues. Conversely, P_R tissues showed a notable decrease in MYH11+ fibroblasts and the Stem-Subtype, while Differentiated-Subtype 2 and CYP1B1+ fibroblasts were the dominant cell types (Figure 5D-5F). CD8+ T cells, essential in NSCLC immunotherapy, were predominantly found in P_R tissues and significantly reduced in P_NR tissues (Figure 5G). Spatial expression distribution analysis of MYH11, activation and exhaustion markers of CD8+ T cells was also performed. The results showed that in P_NR tissues, the expression of MYH11 was significantly increased, accompanied by a significant decrease in the CD8+ T cell activation marker IFNG, while the CD8+ T cell exhaustion markers PDCD1/CXCL13 were significantly elevated (Figure 5H). We also conducted an analysis of the NOTCH signaling pathway at the spatial transcriptomic level, revealing the strongest cell-cell communication between the Stem-Subtype and MYH11+ fibroblasts, with JAG1-NOTCH3 identified as the primary ligand-receptor pair, aligning with our previous single-cell sequencing results (Figure 5I).


### Identification of immunotherapy resistance-associated subtypes in NSCLC by bulk RNA-seq clustering
  We initially conducted differential expression analysis on bulk RNA-seq data from P_NR and P_R, revealing significantly different expression profiles between the two groups, as shown in the heatmap (Figure S4A). Using TCGA lung cancer transcriptome data and Stem-Subtype marker genes, we then performed “ConsensusClusterPlus” clustering analysis. The optimal cluster number was determined to be K=2, categorizing NSCLC patients into two primary subtypes: Stemness I and Stemness II (Figure 6A,6B; Figure S4B). Survival analysis demonstrated that Stemness I was linked to a significantly poorer prognosis (Figure 6C). Subsequent immune cell deconvolution analysis of the two subtypes uncovered distinct immune cell proportions between Stemness I and Stemness II (Figure 6D). Notably, Stemness II showed enrichment of resting NK cells and monocytes, suggesting a potentially better response to immunotherapy (Figure S4C). Additionally, correlation analysis between stemness-related genes and immune cells indicated that MYH11 was significantly associated with various immune cell types, including immunotherapy resistance-associated M2 macrophages (Figure 6E).

  We conducted immune-related scoring on the two clustered stemness subtypes. The findings indicated that Stemness II had significantly higher ESTIMATEScore, ImmuneScore, and StromalScore than Stemness I, suggesting a potentially more favorable response to immunotherapy (Figure 6F). Drug sensitivity prediction using oncoPredict showed that Stemness II was significantly more sensitive to SB505124, Axitinib, BI-2536, OSI-027, and PAK_5339 compared to Stemness I (Figure 6G-6H). To explore potential downstream genes regulated by MYH11, we performed an in silico knockout of MYH11 in the Stem-Subtype and MYH11+ fibroblasts. The results indicated that TUBA1A exhibited the most pronounced expression change following MYH11 knockout in the Stem-Subtype, while CXCL12 showed the most significant change in MYH11+ fibroblasts (Figure 6I; Figure S4D). Additionally, we analyzed the correlations between MYH11 and stemness-related genes, revealing strong associations with SOX2, ABCG2, NOTCH3, and JAG1 (Figure 6J; Figure S4E).


### Validation of the clinical significance of MYH11 in immunotherapy by mIF and IHC, in vitro functional validation
  To further validate the link between MYH11 and resistance to immunotherapy, we conducted mIF staining on P_NR and P_R samples. We used PanCK as a marker for cancer cells, FAP for CAFs, and CD3/CD8/PD1 for exhausted CD8+ T cells (Figure 7A). The results indicated a significant enrichment of FAP+ MYH11+ cells and PanCK+ MYH11+ cells in P_NR tissues (Figure 7B). Notably, the number of PanCK+ MYH11+ cells showed a strong correlation with the number of PD1+ CD8+ T cells. Similarly, the count of FAP+ MYH11+ cells was significantly correlated with PD1+ CD8+ T cell numbers (Figure 7C). We also performed IHC analysis of MYH11 in a cohort of 129 NSCLC patients undergoing immunotherapy (Figure 7D). We found that the MYH11 staining score was significantly higher in the P_NR group than that in the P_R group, high MYH11 expression was linked to a significantly poorer prognosis in these patients (Figure 7E). Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis further identified high MYH11 expression, lymph node positivity, and poor tumor differentiation as unfavorable prognostic factors for NSCLC patients receiving immunotherapy. Multivariate COX proportional hazards regression analysis ultimately identified the MYH11 score as an independent prognostic factor (Figure 7F).

  To further investigate the relationship between MYH11, cancer stemness, and the NOTCH signaling pathway, we constructed two independent shRNAs targeting MYH11. Western blotting confirmed a marked reduction in MYH11 protein levels (Figure 8A), while qRT-PCR verified a significant decrease in MYH11 mRNA levels, indicating successful knockdown (Figure 8B). We then use experiments to validate the results of in silico knockout analysis. The GEPIA database revealed a strong positive correlation between MYH11 expression and the expression of CXCL12 and TUBA1A (Figure 8C). Consistently, qRT-PCR demonstrated that CXCL12 and TUBA1A mRNA levels were significantly downregulated following MYH11 knockdown (Figure 8D). We also examined the NOTCH signaling pathway. Based on the predicted JAG1-NOTCH3 ligand-receptor pair, MYH11 knockdown significantly decreased the protein levels of JAG1 and NOTCH3. Additionally, levels of NICD and HES1, key components of NOTCH signaling, were significantly reduced (Figure 8E). These results suggest that MYH11 may regulate NOTCH signaling through the JAG1-NOTCH3 ligand-receptor axis. Finally, tumor sphere formation assays were conducted to assess cancer stemness. MYH11 knockdown significantly suppressed both the number and size of spheres formed by the lung cancer cell line A549 (Figure 8F). Collectively, these findings indicate that MYH11 may modulate the stemness of lung cancer cells through the NOTCH signaling pathway.


## Discussion
  Immunotherapy resistance remains a significant cause of treatment failure and poor prognosis in NSCLC (36). Although there have been advances in understanding the mechanisms behind this resistance, effective strategies to overcome it are still scarce. Exploring the cellular composition and tumor microenvironment of lung cancer tissues post-immunotherapy resistance may be crucial for reversing immune resistance. The combination of scRNA-seq and ST-seq (8,37) has become a pivotal method for elucidating the mechanisms of immunotherapy resistance in lung cancer. This approach is particularly useful for characterizing immune cell composition, spatial relationships, and identifying potential molecular markers and therapeutic targets. In this study, we integrated two independent single-cell datasets and identified nine major cell types commonly found in lung cancer tissues, aligning with previous reports on lung cancer cellular composition (38). Subsequent spatial transcriptomic deconvolution analysis revealed the spatial distribution of these cell types and their subtypes, offering insights into the potential mechanisms underlying immunotherapy resistance in NSCLC.

  We initially compared the cellular composition of immunotherapy-resistant and immunotherapy-sensitive lung cancer tissues at a single-cell level. Epithelial cells were notably enriched in resistant tissues. Fibroblasts have been widely implicated in immunotherapy resistance in lung cancer (21), so both cell types were included for further analysis. Scoring the stemness of cancer cells is crucial for identifying stemness subpopulations, aligning with previous studies (39). CytoTRACE is a well-established method for assessing differentiation potential (40), and when combined with Monocle3 analysis, it accurately reveals the evolutionary trajectory of cell subpopulations. Our findings showed that the MYH11-high stemness subpopulation could differentiate into other cell subtypes, consistent with the differentiation potential characteristic of CSCs. Analysis of malignant cell subpopulations revealed a significant increase in the Stem-Subtype following immunotherapy resistance, supporting previous findings (41). Ligand-receptor communication analysis between the stemness subpopulation and other malignant cell subtypes identified JAG1-NOTCH3 as a potential interaction pair. JAG1-NOTCH3 signaling has been extensively reported to be closely associated with tumor stemness (42) and immune suppression (43). Pathway enrichment analysis of stemness subpopulation marker genes also showed enrichment in stemness-related signaling pathways, including NOTCH SIGNALING and WNT BETA-CATENIN SIGNALING. These findings collectively suggest that MYH11-high malignant subpopulations may possess enhanced stemness features, thereby driving immunotherapy resistance in NSCLC. TF prediction for the stemness subpopulation identified HOXA5 as a potential regulator, which has been implicated in tumor progression and stemness features in prostate cancer (44). Another predicted TF, GLI2, has also been reported to be closely associated with tumor stemness in gastric cancer (45).

  CAFs are crucial elements of the tumor microenvironment, contributing significantly to immunotherapy resistance. CAFs alter the immune landscape to foster tumor growth by inducing T cell differentiation into regulatory T cells (Tregs) through antigen presentation without co-stimulatory molecules or by secreting cytokines and chemokines that attract M2 macrophages. These processes lead to treatment resistance, especially against cancer immunotherapy, and result in poor prognosis (46). Our study identified that MYH11+ fibroblasts exhibited the highest stemness scores and the potential to differentiate into other fibroblast subtypes. This finding aligns with research in cervical cancer, where scRNA-seq and ST-seq analyses highlighted MYH11+ fibroblasts’ unique roles in maintaining CSCs, metabolic activity, and immune regulation, particularly in promoting tumor cell proliferation, migration, and inhibiting apoptosis (22). Similar studies in colorectal cancer have linked MYH11+ fibroblasts with poor prognosis (18). Notably, MYH11+ fibroblasts were significantly elevated in immunotherapy-resistant lung cancer tissues. Research on lung cancer immunotherapy has shown that MYH11+ fibroblasts can exclude T cells from tumors, facilitating immune evasion (21). We discovered that the stemness subpopulation had the strongest interaction with MYH11+ fibroblasts, especially through the NOTCH signaling pathway, suggesting these cell types may be key players in stemness-related pathways. TFs prediction for MYH11+ fibroblasts identified FOXP3 as a potential regulator, closely associated with lung cancer immunotherapy (47), and SMAD7, which is also implicated in tumor immunotherapy response (48).

  Previous studies have established a link between stemness-related subpopulations in colorectal cancer and tumor metastasis (49). In our research, we identified a MYH11-high stemness subpopulation that is spatially enriched in immunotherapy-resistant primary tumors. Notably, MYH11+ fibroblasts were observed surrounding this stemness subpopulation, suggesting a possible mechanism of immune resistance. Earlier research indicated that COL11A1+ CAFs form a barrier at the tumor boundary, potentially obstructing T cell infiltration and inducing immune resistance (8). Our clinical sample validation further implied that the tumor stemness subpopulation and MYH11+ fibroblasts might be spatially linked to PD1+ CD8+ T cells enrichment, which is closely associated with T cell exhaustion and confirmed to be related to immunotherapy resistance in lung cancer (50). Conversely, CD8+ T cell infiltration is often linked to immunotherapy sensitivity. Our findings revealed significant CD8+ T cell infiltration in sensitive tumors, aligning with previous studies on lung cancer immunotherapy (51). In summary, our spatial analysis suggests that MYH11+ fibroblasts surrounding the stemness subpopulation may decrease CD8+ T cell infiltration and increase exhausted T cells, potentially contributing to immunotherapy resistance in lung cancer.

  We conducted consensus clustering analysis using bulk RNA-seq data and identified two primary stemness-related subtypes. Our analysis showed that the Stemness I subtype was linked to poor prognosis, lower immune scores, and altered immune cell proportions, aligning with previous lung cancer clustering studies (52). Additionally, we discovered that the stemness-related genes WNT11 and SOX2 were correlated with immune cell proportions. Further correlation analysis indicated that MYH11 expression was strongly associated with WNT11 and SOX2, suggesting MYH11 as an immunotherapy-related stemness gene. Drug sensitivity prediction using “oncoPredict”, a method applied in lung cancer research (53), revealed that the two stemness subtypes exhibited different sensitivities to agents like SB505124, Axitinib, and BI-2536. In silico knockout is a crucial method for exploring downstream targets, especially for core genes in key cell subpopulations, and is extensively used to identify key genes and pathway alterations (54). Our in silico knockout analysis pinpointed TUBA1A and CXCL12 as the most significantly altered genes. Notably, CXCL12 has been reported to be closely linked with CAF-mediated immunotherapy resistance (55).

  Although the study successfully identified MYH11-high stemness and fibroblast subpopulations linked to immunotherapy resistance in NSCLC using multi-omics analysis, several limitations necessitate further research. First, the specific therapeutic agents that target these stemness subpopulations and their effectiveness need additional assessment. Second, the precise molecular mechanisms through which MYH11-high stemness subpopulations contribute to immunotherapy resistance in NSCLC have yet to be clarified.


## Conclusions
  In this study, we utilized a multi-omics approach to identify and characterize a Stem-subtype and a MYH11+ fibroblast subpopulation closely linked to immunotherapy resistance in NSCLC. These subpopulations demonstrated high stemness scores and differentiation potential, with pathway enrichment analysis indicating significant activation of the NOTCH signaling pathway. Consensus clustering identified the Stemness I subtype, which was associated with poor immunotherapy response and unfavorable survival outcomes. Clinical validation using our cohort through IHC and mIF confirmed that MYH11 expression was closely related to exhausted CD8+ T cells and poor prognosis. Our findings collectively suggest that MYH11-high stemness and fibroblast subpopulations may be key mechanisms driving immunotherapy resistance in NSCLC, providing a promising foundation for future precise targeting of these cell subpopulations to counteract immunotherapy resistance.
