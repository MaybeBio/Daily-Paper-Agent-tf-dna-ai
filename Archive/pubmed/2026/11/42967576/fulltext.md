# Bioinformatics Analysis to Reveal Potential Targets of Huntington's Disease


## ABSTRACT
  Huntington's disease (HD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disease resulted from the expansion of CAG repeats in huntingtin (HTT) gene and characterised by motor impairment, psychiatric disorders and cognitive decline. Currently, there are no available disease‐modifying treatments. In this study, we performed an integrative analysis of HD datasets downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database using machine learning approaches, Mendelian randomisation (MR) and single cell RNA‐sequencing (scRNA‐seq) analysis to elucidate underlying disease mechanisms. The machine learning algorithms of LASSO, random forest and SVM‐RFE identified the decreased ADRA2A in HD. In addition, MR analysis revealed HD‐associated proteins such as LILRA4 and UBE2D1, associated with immune response and ubiquitination metabolism, respectively. scRNA‐seq analysis further identified the transcription factors of SOX4 and ETV1 through SCENIC analysis. Pseudotime trajectory analysis highlighted heparan sulphate sulfotransferase activity, involving genes CHST11, HS3ST3A1 and HS6ST3 that drive cell differentiation. Moreover, cell‐cell communication analysis demonstrated enhanced NRG1/3‐ERBB4 signalling pathway in HD. This study presents multiple potential targets for HD treatment but further experimental validation is required to confirm their functional relevance.


## Introduction
  Huntington's disease (HD) is an autosomal dominant neurogenerative disease caused by the expansion of CAG repeats in huntingtin gene (HTT) [1]. The prevalence of HD worldwide is 4.3–10.6 per 100,000 individuals and the onset of HD usually occurs at a mean age of 45. Individuals carrying more than 36 CAG repeats are at high risk of developing the disease and the number of repeats is inversely correlated with the onset age. The most common symptom of HD is chorea, which can progress into akinesia as the disease advances. Other symptoms involve the psychiatric disorders, like bipolar disorder and depression. Cognitive decline is another key feature, manifesting as the deficits in speech, memory, vision and attention [2]. Disease progression can be tracked by measuring the neurofilament light chain and mutant HTT (mHTT) levels in cerebrospinal fluid or serum. Currently, there are no disease‐modifying treatments for HD and the clinical management focuses on symptom control. Chorea is often treated with dopamine‐deleting agents like tetrabenazine. Psychiatric symptoms may be managed with the selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) such as fluoxetine, while cholinesterase inhibitors are taken to mitigate the cognitive impairment [3]. Given the central role of mHTT in HD pathogenesis, various HTT‐lowering therapies are under development, such as RNA interference, antisense oligonucleotides and gene‐editing approaches like CRISPR‐Cas9. However, the efficacy of these approaches remains uncertain and none of them have yet advanced into clinical use [4].

  The pathogenesis of HD is multifaceted. HTT functions as a scaffold protein involved in vesicle trafficking of numerous cargo proteins. mHTT causes the failure in the delivery of neurotransmitter receptors including GABA (γ‐aminobutyric acid) and AMPA (α‐amino‐3‐hydroxy‐5‐methyl‐4‐isoxazolepropionic acid) receptors, leading to impaired synaptic transmission. In addition, mHTT compromises axonal transport of brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), which is crucial for the survival of neurons [5]. mHTT also dysregulates gene transcription and disrupts mitochondrial function [6]. Conversely, the neuroinflammation and autophagy dysfunction results in the accumulation of mHTT, creating a pathogenic feedback loop [7]. Recent advances in bioinformatics have enabled systematic analysis of HD mechanisms through computational approaches. Several studies have conducted bioinformatic analysis of HD datasets from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database [8, 9, 10, 11]. However, these studies focused on protein‐protein interaction networks and the application of machine learning and Mendelian randomisation (MR) methods in HD research remains limited.

  Machine learning algorithms offer effective approaches for gene selection, narrowing down a wide variety of variables to those that convey significant information. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) employs L1 regularisation within a linear regression framework to perform feature selection. Random forest (RF), as an ensemble learning algorithm, ranks feature importance based on Gini impurity reduction. Support vector machine recursive feature elimination (SVM‐RFE) iteratively removes features with low weights and preserves those most relevant to the response variable [12, 13]. In addition, MR is a genetic instrumental variable method that utilises single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) from genome‐wide association study (GWAS) to infer causal relationships between exposures and outcomes [14, 15]. By leveraging genetic variants, MR minimises confounding bias and is widely applied to identify disease risk factors and treatment response variables [16]. As shown in Figure 1, in this study, we first retrieved HD microarray datasets from the GEO database and identified two cohorts, GSE24250 and GSE1751. We then applied machine learning algorithms and MR analysis to identify HD‐associated genes based on the differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Furthermore, we performed single cell RNA‐sequencing (scRNA‐seq) analysis on the dataset GSE271852 to investigate transcription factors (TFs), cell trajectory and cell‐cell communication. By integrating multiple bioinformatic approaches, this study aims to uncover potential molecular targets in HD, thereby providing deeper insights into its pathological mechanisms and highlighting possible intervention points for future therapies.


## Methods

### Data Collection
  The microarray and scRNA‐seq datasets were obtained from GEO database. GSE24250 microarray dataset included peripheral blood samples from 8 HD patients (mean age ± SD: 51.2 ± 6.1 years; 1 male, 7 females) and 6 control patients (mean age ± SD: 41.5 ± 10.6 years; 3 males, 3 females) [17]. GSE1751 microarray dataset contained blood samples from 12 symptomatic HD patients (mean age: 49 years; 7 males, 5 females) and 14 age‐ and gender‐matched controls [18]. GSE271852 scRNA‐seq dataset comprised of 45‐day‐old telencephalic organoids derived from isogenic human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) RUES2 cell lines, including 3 WT (20 CAG repeats) and 3 HD (56 CAG repeats) organoids [19, 20].


### Differential Expression and Functional Enrichment Analysis
  Differential expression analysis was performed using the R package limma, with DEGs defined using thresholds of |log2 fold change (FC)| > 1 and p value < 0.05. Functional enrichment analysis of the DEGs was conducted using the R package clusterProfiler for Gene Ontology (GO) and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), with a significance threshold of p value < 0.05 [21]. Additionally, gene set variation analysis (GSVA) was employed as an unsupervised and non‐parametric algorithm to evaluate the enrichment of gene sets in the transcriptome data. In this study, GO gene sets were downloaded from Molecular Signature Database (MsigDB) and GSVA scores were calculated for each gene set to assess variations in biological functions across different samples [22].


### Identification of Key Genes by Machine Learning Algorithms
  Genes derived from the intersection of DEGs identified in both the GSE24250 and GSE1751 datasets were subjected to machine learning analysis. The gene expression matrix was transposed and scaled, with each sample labelled according to its respective group as the input data. The LASSO was executed using glmnet R package with ten‐fold cross‐validation applied to determine the optimal penalty parameter. The RF was run using random forest R package, constructing 500 classification trees and assessing gene importance based on %IncNodePurity. The SVM‐RFE was conducted via e1071 R package, with the point of minimum cross‐validation error serving as the threshold for identifying valuable genes. The intersection of the results from these three machine learning algorithms was defined as key genes. To evaluate the prognostic ability of the identified key genes, the area under the curve (AUC) of receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was analysed with the pROC R package.


### Immune Cells Infiltration
  CIBERSORT R package was employed to assess the relative proportions of 22 immune cell types across control and HD group in both the GSE24250 and GSE1751 datasets. In addition, Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to examine the associations between the identified key genes and these immune cell populations.


### Mendelian Randomisation Analysis
  Protein quantitative trait loci (pQTLs) for the investigated genes were obtained from deCODE database, which conducted a large‐scale pQTL study involving 35,559 Icelanders [23]. GWAS data for HD were sourced from the FinnGen biobank (finngen_R12_G6_NEUATR, cases n = 1457, controls n = 498,891). SNPs significantly associated with exposure (p < 5 × 10−8) were selected and clumped to ensure independence (r 2 < 0.01, distance window, 10,000 kb). For MR analysis, the Wald ratio method was applied for single SNPs, while the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method was used when two or more SNPs were available. Heterogeneity and pleiotropy were assessed using Cochran's Q test and MR‐Egger intercept test, respectively [24]. All MR analysis were carried out using the TwoSampleMR R package.


### Evaluation of Protein Importance by Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)
  Based on the HD‐associated proteins by MR analysis, we constructed the eXtreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) machine learning model and employed the SHAP values to assess the importance of proteins [25]. These analyses were implemented via the xgboost and shapviz R packages.


### scRNA‐seq Analysis
  We utilised the Seurat R package to process scRNA‐seq dataset GSE271852. Read10× function was used to load sample data and create a Seurat object, followed by quality control to exclude cells expressing less than 200 genes or exhibiting more than 10% of mitochondrial genes. Then the data was normalised and the top 2000 detected highly variable genes were employed for principal component analysis (PCA). The Harmony function was used to integrate the individual samples. Subsequently, the uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) embeddings were generated based on Harmony reduction. Similarly, a graph‐based clustering was created also using Harmony reduction. Finally, the clusters were annotated by comparing the cluster‐specific genes identified by FindAllMarkers function with the cell markers derived from CellMarker2.0 database. The differential expression analysis between HD and WT cells was conducted using FindMarkers function (logfc.threshold = 0.25, min.pct = 0.25) [26].


### The Regulons of TFs
  The regulons of TFs were identified by the single‐cell regulatory network inference and inference (SCENIC) algorithm. A log‐normalised expression matrix constructed via Seurat and a motif dataset (hg19‐tss‐centred‐10kb‐7species.mc9nr.feather) were incorporated to SCENIC to create regulons of TFs. The interactions between the downstream targets and TFs were predicted with GENIE3 function. Then the gene regulatory networks also referred as the regulons were generated using runSCENIC function. Finally, the AUCell function was utilised to score the activity of each regulon in each cell. In addition, to assess the regulons of TFs specifically active in particular cell types or groups, the regulon specificity scores (RSS) were computed via calcRSS function [27].


### Pseudotime Trajectory of the Cells
  Pseudotime trajectory of the cells was constructed using Monocle 2 algorithm. The gene expression matrix in the scale of unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts derived from the Seurat was used to create an object by newCellDataSet function. The DEGs identified by differentialGeneTest function were employed to order the cells in the trajectory. The plot_cell_trajectory function was used to visualise the minimum spanning tree on cells. In addition, the branch expression analysis modelling (BEAM) function was performed on the branch point to identify the genes that exhibited significant branch‐dependent expression patterns [28, 29].


### Cell–Cell Communication
  CellChat R package was utilised for intercellular communication analysis. After creating the CellChat object, we set up the reference database using the secretory signalling pathway. The interactions of the ligand–receptor pairs and the communication network between different cell types were inferred by computeCommunProb and computeCommunProbPathway functions [30, 31].


### Statistical Analysis
  All statistical analysis were performed using R software 4.4.1. Student's t‐test or Wilcoxon signed‐rank test were used to compare the difference between two groups, and all statistics were considered statistically different at p < 0.05.


## Results

### Identification of DEGs and Enriched Pathways
  Differential gene expression analysis was performed between control and HD group. In the GSE24250 dataset, 245 DEGs were identified (Supporting Information S1: Table S1) and GSEA revealed significant enrichment in pathways related to negative regulation of nucleobase‐containing compound metabolic process, cell morphogenesis involved in neuron differentiation and negative regulation of transcription RNA polymerase II (Figure 2A,B). Additionally, GSVA highlighted altered pathways including cardiac muscle cell membrane repolarisation, calcium ion import, and negative regulation of cell cycle (Figure S1A). Notable dysregulated genes included TAF13, involved in transcription initiation; neuroligin (NLGN), a neuronal surface protein that promotes synapse formation and synaptic transmission; and CACNA2D1, a subunit of voltage‐dependent calcium channel that modulates calcium current density. In the GSE1751 dataset, 777 DEGs were identified (Supporting Information S1: Table S2). GSEA indicated enrichment in pathways associated with production of molecular mediator of immune response, positive regulation of cellular biosynthetic process and positive regulation of RNA metabolic process (Figure 2C,D). GSVA further identified altered pathways involving positive regulation of oxidative stress induced cell death, ERBB signalling pathway and endocardial cushion formation (Figure S1B). Key altered genes included ATF1, encoding an activating transcription factor; SEMA3E, which mediates reorganization of actin cytoskeleton leading to retraction of cell projections; and DKK2, involved in embryonic development via modulation of Wnt signalling pathway. The identified pathways align with the established HD pathophysiology, including transcription dysregulation, membrane neurotransmission and cytoskeleton [32], confirming that the analysed HD datasets accurately reflect the characteristics of HD.


### Machine Learning Analysis
  To further screen the key genes associated with HD, 13 overlapping genes derived from DEGs of GSE24250 and GSE1751 dataset were assessed via three machine learning algorithms (Figure 3A). The LASSO regression algorithm selected 4 genes of ADRA2A (coefficient: −10.273), DDX1 (coefficient: −0.24846), PTOV1 (coefficient: 2.5871) and CNTN6 (coefficient: 2.4313) (Figure 3B,C). The RF algorithm evaluated the gene importance by the indicator of increase in node purity and selected 5 genes (Figure 3D,E). The SVM‐RFE algorithm selected the top 2 ranked genes of ADRA2A and PTOV1 (Figure 3F). After intersection of the three results, ADRA2A was identified (Figure 3G) and it demonstrated the consistent change in both GSE datasets: decrease in HD versus control group (Figure 3H). Moreover, ROC analysis revealed the AUC value of ADRA2A as 1 and 0.89 in GSE24250 and GSE1751 dataset, respectively, demonstrating its good prognostic ability (Figure 3I).


### Evaluation of Immune Cells Infiltration
  Given the observed association between DEGs and immune response pathways, we further investigated the distribution of immune cell populations between control and HD group in two GSE datasets. In the GSE24250 dataset, the T cells gamma delta and NK cells resting were reduced in HD group, whereas macrophage M1 was elevated (Figure S2A). The correlation study showed that ADRA2A expression was positively associated with B cells native and T cells gamma delta, but revealing negative correlations with macrophage M1 and mast cells activated (Figure S2B). In the GSE1751 dataset, the B cells memory, T cells regulatory (Tregs) and mast cells activated were significantly decreased in HD group, while T cells CD4 memory activated, NK cell activated and neutrophils showed a notable increase (Figure S2C). ADRA2A demonstrated positive correlations with B cells memory and T cells regulatory, and negative correlations with T cells CD4 memory activated and neutrophils (Figure S2D).


### Causal Effect Estimation of Proteins on HD
  To identify the proteins that exhibited causal effect on HD, we performed MR analysis using pQLTs of proteins from deCODE database. We screened 54 proteins from DEGs of GSE24250 dataset and 76 proteins from DEGs of GSE1751 dataset, ultimately identifying eight proteins significantly associated with HD (Supporting Information S1: Table S3). Both Cochran's Q and MR‐Egger intercept tests confirmed the absence of significant heterogeneity and pleiotropy for these eight HD‐associated proteins (Supporting Information S1: Tables 4 and 5). Among the identified proteins, LILRA4, MBL2 and SPINK2 demonstrated positive correlations with HD risk, whereas BHMT2, PCDH8, DNAJB2, PTGS2 and UBE2D1 showed negative correlations (Figure 4A). The five proteins derived from GSE24250 dataset (LILRA4, MBL2, SPINK2, BHMT2 and PCDH8) were incorporated into the XGBoost algorithm, which achieved perfect predictive accuracy (AUC = 1.0). The remaining three proteins derived from GSE1751 dataset (DNAJB2, PTGS2 and UBE2D1) also formed a highly predictive model (AUC = 0.997) (Figure 4B). Moreover, the protein importance was assessed based on SHAP value. Particularly, LILRA4 and UBE2D1 demonstrated the highest SHAP value on GSE24250 and GSE1751 dataset, respectively (Figure 4C,D).


### scRNA‐seq Profiling
  To further reveal the mechanisms underlying the HD, we analysed 59,756 cells derived from GSE271852 scRNA‐seq dataset. The data underwent a series of processing steps, including quality control, normalisation, PCA, integration and clustering. Finally, UMAP plotted 11 clusters (Figure S3A,B). Based on canonical cell markers, four cell types were annotated (Figure 5A). The microglial cells exhibited high levels of ribosome‐related genes, such as RPS28 and RPL39. The oligodendrocytes and astrocytes expressed elevated levels of PCDH9, PTPRD, PARD3B and DLGAP1, which were associated with the junction and function of synapses. The neurons demonstrated increased levels of MAP1B and SOX11, which were linked to microtubule assembly and transcription (Figure 5B). In addition, the relative proportion of microglial cell, oligodendrocyte, astrocyte and neuron in WT group were 86.85%, 8.02%, 3.92% and 1.21%, respectively, while those in HD group were 89.43%, 6.73%, 1.5% and 2.34%, respectively (Figure S3C). Moreover, 109 DEGs between the two groups were identified (Figure 5C). The upregulated DEGs involved microtubule‐associated genes such as STMN2, NEFL and NEFM. The downregulated DEGs comprised transcription regulators such as PANTR1A, ID3 and HES6. GO analysis showed the enriched terms linked to cytoplasmic translation and microtubule‐mediated mitochondrion localization (Figure 5D).


### Identification of Regulons of TFs
  We identified 40 regulons comprising 508 downstream genes through SCENIC analysis. Based on RSS value, SOX4 displayed the highest specific score in microglial cell, while POU2F1 and CREBL2 were highly associated with oligodendrocyte and astrocyte, respectively. ETV4 showed the highest specific score for neuron (Figure S4A). In addition, we identified that SOX4 and ETV1 displayed high specific score in HD group (Figure S4B). Consistently, the activities of these 2 TFs were also upregulated in HD group (Figure S4C).


### Transcriptomic Dynamics by Pseudotime Trajectory
  We employed the pseudotime trajectory method to study the differentiation dynamics of cells of HD group. The results showed that microglial cell and neuron were regarded as the initial point for trajectory (state 1), followed by gradual differentiation into two directions: astrocyte (state 2) and oligodendrocyte (state 3) (Figure 6A–C). Moreover, based on BEAM analysis, the genes regulating cell differentiation were partitioned into two major clusters. The expression levels of cluster 1 genes were gradually upregulated along with the process of cell differentiation from pre‐state to state 2. Conversely, the expression levels of cluster 2 genes were progressively elevated along with the cell differentiation from pre‐state to state 3 (Figure 6D). GO analysis demonstrated that the cluster 1 genes were enriched for heparan sulphate sulfotransferase activity, whereas cluster 2 genes were primarily associated with glutamate receptor activity and transmembrane receptor protein tyrosine phosphatase activity (Figure 6E). Notably, the heparan sulphate sulfotransferase activity in cluster 1 represented a distinct functional pathway in HD group compared with WT (Figure S5). Additionally, we identified the hub genes of CHST11, HS3ST3A1 and HS6ST3, which were sulfotransferases, in the cluster 1 via maximum clique centrality (MCC) index of cytoHubba function in Cytoscape software (Figure 6F), suggesting their potential significance in HD‐specific pathways.


### Cell–Cell Communication
  Cell–cell communication was carried out to infer the ligand–receptor interactions among different cell types in WT and HD groups. The total number of interactions was significantly increased in HD group (Figure 7A). Moreover, astrocyte and oligodendrocyte exhibited a great level of overall signalling patterns, representing their main roles in the intercellular communication (Figure 7B). In particular, the information flow results showed that NRG signalling pathway was significantly elevated in HD group (Figure 7C). Then using astrocyte and oligodendrocyte as the incoming signal source, we compared the signalling patterns between HD and WT group. Notably, the ligand‐receptor pairs NRG1‐ERBB4 and NRG3‐ERBB4 were significantly upregulated between oligodendrocyte and astrocyte in HD group (Figure 7D). The signalling pathway network also exhibited the strengthened NRG signalling in HD group (Figure 7E). Moreover, the scRNA‐seq data revealed that the NRG1, NRG3 and ERBB4 were mainly expressed in oligodendrocyte and their levels were significantly upregulated in HD group (Figure S6). These results suggested that dysregulated NRG signalling may represent a crucial altered pathway in HD pathology.


## Discussion
  In this study, through the integration of three machine learning algorithms of LASSO, RF and SVM‐RFE, we identified ADRA2A as a key gene downregulated in HD. ADRA2A encodes the α2A‐adrenergic neurotransmitter receptor which mediates the presynaptic feedback inhibition of the release of noradrenaline from adrenergic neurons [33, 34]. Moreover, ADRA2A is involved in many other biological functions, such as sedation, analgesia, platelet aggregation and the regulation of blood pressure and heart rate [35]. The observed reduction of ADRA2A in HD may attenuate the inhibitory effect on noradrenaline release. In turn, the elevated noradrenaline could exacerbate glutamate toxicity on synapses. In addition, the impaired ADRA2A signalling may affect cardiac functions, as supported by GSVA results showing the altered functions involving voltage gated calcium channel activity, heart rate by cardiac conduction, cardiac muscle cell membrane repolarisation and cardiac muscle contraction. Notably, the approved vesicular monoamine transporter 2 (VMAT2) inhibitors, such as tetrabenazine and valbenazine modulate HD chorea by reducing monoamine storage and release [36]. Thus, our findings may provide a new monoaminergic target to modulate synaptic dysfunction for HD treatment.

  Neuroinflammation is a prominent feature of neurodegenerative diseases, such as HD and it is marked by the microglia activation in response to the stimulus, leading to a cascade of the release of cytokines, chemokines, complement proteins and reactive oxygen species [37, 38]. Furthermore, the infiltrated blood‐borne monocytes differentiate into dendritic cells and macrophages. Dendritic cells function as antigen‐processing and presenting cells which regulate T cell response. Macrophages polarise into proinflammatory M1 subtype or anti‐inflammatory M2 subtype. The anti‐inflammatory macrophages act by engulfing the invaded pathogens and cell debris [39]. Our immune cell infiltration analysis revealed elevated levels of the myeloid cells, including macrophage M1 and neutrophils in HD, indicating enhanced inflammation. In addition, in GSE24250 dataset, the lymphocytes of T cells gamma delta and NK cells were downregulated, whereas in GSE1751 dataset, the lymphocytes of CD4 memory activated and NK cells were upregulated. This discrepancy may be attributed to confounding factors, since in GSE1751 dataset the blood samples were derived from patients undergoing the histone deacetylase inhibitor treatment [18]. However, the role of lymphocytes in HD remains inadequately characterised and further investigations are essential to elucidate their specific contributions to disease progression.

  Through MR analysis, we identified several proteins potentially associated with HD. These include immune‐related proteins LILRA4 and MBL2, metabolic enzymes and inhibitors (BHMT2, DNAJB2, PTGS2, UBE2D1 and SPINK2), and the cell adhesion molecule PCDH8. Using these proteins, we constructed an XGBoost machine learning model and identified two top‐ranked genes according to SHAP values: LILRA4, which displayed a positive correlation with HD suggesting a detrimental effect, and UBE2D1, a ubiquitin conjugating enzyme demonstrating a negative correlation indicative of a protective effect. The ubiquitin‐proteosome system represents a major pathway for protein degradation. The proteins are tagged with ubiquitin chains under the assistance of ubiquitin activating enzymes, ubiquitin conjugating enzymes and ubiquitin ligase. Then the polyubiquitinated proteins are delivered to proteasome for degradation. In HD, the mHTT impair the ubiquitin‐proteasome system, which in turn, promotes the aggregation of mHTT [40]. Consequently, targeting the ubiquitin‐proteasome system to facilitate the clearance of mHTT represents a promising approach for HD therapy.

  We further analysed a scRNA‐seq dataset of organoids in order to reveal additional potential targets for HD. Four major cell types of microglial cell, astrocyte, oligodendrocyte and neuron were annotated. Moreover, the DEGs between HD and WT group were mainly enriched for functions related to cytoplasmic translation and cytoskeleton‐mediated intracellular transport, consistent with previously reported pathways involving brain development and synapse maturation [19]. Through SCENIC analysis, we identified two TFs exhibiting high specific scores in HD: SOX4, a member of SOX family characterised by a common high‐mobility‐group (HMG) domain [41, 42], and ETV1, which belongs to E26 transformation‐specific (ETS) family [43]. Both SOX4 and ETV1 play crucial roles in tissue development, suggesting their potential significance in HD pathogenesis.

  Furthermore, pseudotime trajectory analysis enabled the identification of genes driving cell differentiation in HD. Among these, we detected elevated heparan sulphate sulfotransferases (HSSTs) including HS3ST3A1 and HS6ST3. HSSTs catalyse the biosynthesis of heparan sulphate (HS), a sulphated polysaccharide belonging to the sulphated glycosaminoglycans family [44]. Particularly, HS could interact with pathogenic proteins such as β‐amyloid peptide and α‐synuclein, accelerating their aggregation. The aggregation of misfolded proteins represents a hallmark of multiple neurodegenerative diseases including Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, prion and Huntington's diseases [45]. Therefore, further investigation of the participation of HSSTs in mHTT aggregation may reveal novel therapeutic targets for modulating disease progression.

  Finally, cell‐cell communication analysis revealed upregulated NRG1/3‐ERBB4 signalling in HD. NRG1 is a transmembrane protein containing the epidermal growth factor (EGF)‐like domain that undergoes proteolytic cleavage by enzymes including β‐secretase BACE1 to produce the soluble active form. The soluble NRG1 binds to and stimulates tyrosine kinase receptor ERBB4, predominantly expressed on GABAergic interneurons. The NRG1‐ERBB4 signalling plays critical roles in circuit assembly, peripheral myelination, and homoeostasis of synaptic functions. Emerging evidence from mouse models demonstrated that both potentiation and impairment of this pathway lead to behavioural deficits, suggesting that restoring NRG1 signalling to the normal levels may offer therapeutic benefits for neurological disorders including HD [46].

  However, this study has several limitations. First, the GSE24250 dataset exhibited a significant gender imbalance, with most HD samples being female, introducing a potential gender bias in our analysis. Second, the scRNA‐seq dataset was derived from the organoids. While organoids provide a valuable tool for studying cell‐cell interactions, they do not develop mHTT aggregates and may not completely recapitulate the complexity of the human HD brain. Furthermore, as our findings were based on in silico analyses, it is essential to validate the expression change of the key genes in biological samples and investigate their functions through cellular and animal models. Finally, future studies could benefit from employing advanced single‐cell computational approaches such as scCross and MomicPred [47, 48, 49, 50, 51], which integrate multiple feature datasets to infer cellular alterations in HD.


## Conclusions
  Based on the GEO datasets, we systematically employed multiple bioinformatic approaches including machine learning algorithms, MR and scRNA‐seq analysis to identify an array of potential targets for HD. These encompass ADRA2A, LILRA4, UBE2D1, transcription factors SOX4 and ETV1, heparan sulphate sulfotransferase, and NRG1/3‐ERBB4 signalling pathway. Notably, the ADRA2A identified by machine learning approaches may represent a promising monoamine‐associated target with significant therapeutic potential for HD.
