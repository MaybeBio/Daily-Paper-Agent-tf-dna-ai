# Pathomics-based prediction of natural regulatory T cell infiltration in breast cancer


## Background
  Natural regulatory T cells (nTregs) are a key immunosuppressive component of the tumor microenvironment (TME), but their assessment typically requires specialized molecular assays. This study aimed to develop and validate a deep learning-based pathomics model to predict nTregs infiltration directly from routine hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained whole slide images (WSI) of breast cancer and evaluate its prognostic significance.


## Methods
  Data from 1097 breast cancer patients in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) were analyzed. A cohort of 928 patients with complete RNA-seq data was used to establish the prognostic value of nTregs (estimated by ImmuneCellAI). A subset of 791 patients with matched high-quality H&E-stained WSI was randomly split into training (n = 633) and validation (n = 158) cohorts. A total of 1488 quantitative pathomic features were extracted. After feature selection via minimum Redundancy Maximum Relevance (mRMR) and Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE), a Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM) classifier was trained to predict high versus low nTregs status, generating a continuous Pathomics Score (PS). The PS was validated against FOXP3 immunohistochemistry (IHC) and evaluated for its association with overall survival (OS). Multi-omics analyses explored the underlying biology of PS-defined groups.


## Results
  High nTregs infiltration was an independent predictor of poor OS (HR = 1.58, 95% CI 1.10–2.28, p = 0.013). The GBM model achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) of 0.81 (95% CI 0.78–0.85) in the training cohort and 0.72 (95% CI 0.63–0.80) in the validation cohort. The PS showed a strong correlation with FOXP3+ cell density (p < 0.001) and was independently associated with worse OS (HR = 1.68, 95% CI 1.15–2.46, p = 0.008). Patients with high PS exhibited a distinct transcriptomic signature enriched for immune activation pathways (e.g., estrogen responses) and upregulated immune checkpoint genes (e.g., CD276, TNFSF4, TNFSF9), alongside an immunosuppressive microenvironment characterized by increased nTregs and M2-like macrophage estimates.


## Conclusion
  We developed and validated a pathomics model that non-invasively predicts nTregs infiltration and patient prognosis from standard H&E images. The PS serves as a novel, accessible digital biomarker that captures the complexity of an inflamed yet immunosuppressive TME and has the potential to augment clinical decision-making, particularly in resource-limited settings.


## Introduction
  Breast cancer remains one of the most prevalent malignancies affecting women worldwide, with an estimated 2.3 million new cases diagnosed in 2022, accounting for 12.0% of all cancer cases globally [1]. Despite advances in early detection and treatment, breast cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women, with approximately 660,000 deaths reported in 2022. Current therapeutic strategies, including surgery, chemotherapy, radiation, and targeted therapies, have significantly improved survival rates. However, substantial challenges remain in predicting patient outcomes and understanding the mechanisms driving disease progression [2].

  Regulatory T cells (Tregs) are a heterogeneous population that plays a pivotal role in maintaining immune homeostasis. However, their strong immunosuppressive capabilities are frequently exploited by tumors to evade immune surveillance, thereby promoting tumor progression [3, 4]. The association between high density of tumor-infiltrating Tregs and unfavorable clinical outcomes has been extensively documented across a wide spectrum of human carcinomas, including breast, ovarian, lung, and hepatocellular carcinomas, supporting the hypothesis that Treg infiltration constitutes a key immune escape mechanism and a potential prognostic biomarker [5–7]. Specifically, long-term follow-up studies have confirmed that intratumoral FOXP3+ Treg enrichment is an independent predictor of poor prognosis in breast cancer [8]. Despite this prevailing association, the prognostic significance of Tregs is not universal. Emerging evidence, particularly from colorectal cancer, indicates that high FOXP3+ Treg infiltration can paradoxically correlate with improved prognosis in specific contexts [9, 10]. This discrepancy may partly stem from the functional heterogeneity among Treg subsets. Notably, induced regulatory T cells (iTregs), which expand during cancer progression, demonstrate considerable plasticity, while natural regulatory T cells (nTregs)-a stable, thymus-derived immunosuppressive population-may provide a more consistent biological signal associated with patient outcomes [11, 12]. Consequently, accurately quantifying specific Treg subsets, such as nTregs, is essential to delineate their distinct prognostic and therapeutic roles.

  However, accurately assessing the infiltration of nTregs in tumor microenvironment (TME) remains a considerable clinical and technical challenge. The current gold standard, flow cytometry, is restricted by its requirement for fresh tissue, high operational costs, and logistical demands for real-time processing [13, 14]. In contrast, in silico deconvolution methods (e.g., ImmuCellAI, CIBERSORTx) infer immune cell composition from bulk RNA sequencing data, yet their clinical translation is hindered by dependencies on specialized genomic assays, batch effect variability, and the lack of spatial resolution [15, 16]. Although emerging multimodal approaches integrating radiomics and deep learning have shown promise in improving prognostic stratification in breast cancer [17, 18], the potential to directly and specifically predict the spatial abundance of a key immunosuppressive subset like nTregs from ubiquitous, cost-effective hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained slides has not been fully realized, representing a significant unmet need in digital pathology.

  H&E-stained sections are the most conventional and easily accessible histological data in the pathological diagnosis of breast cancer, including tumor cell morphology, interstitial structure, and histological features related to immune cells. With the development of digital pathology and machine learning methods, H&E images have been used to predict immune-related molecular states and prognostic features in breast cancer, such as PD-L1 and IDO1 expression [19, 20]. Therefore, constructing a pathomics model based on H&E images is a feasible approach for non-invasive or low-cost assessment of tumor-immune microenvironment characteristics [21, 22]. ## Methods

### Public data acquisition and cohort curation
  This study utilized data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Initially, 1097 patients with breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA) were retrieved. Clinical data processing: From the 1082 treatment-naïve primary breast cancer cases, patients were excluded based on the following criteria: unrecorded survival data (53 cases), survival time < 1 month (49 cases), male patients (12 cases), and missing RNA-seq data (40 cases). This resulted in a final survival dataset of 928 patients. Pathological data processing: A total of 1062 H&E pathological images were initially obtained. After excluding 120 samples with substandard image quality, 942 pathological data samples remained. Data integration: By integrating the clinical and pathological datasets, 791 patients with overlapping clinical, RNA-seq, and high-quality pathological information were identified for subsequent pathomics investigations. Cohort construction: The 791 patients were randomly divided into a training cohort and a validation cohort at an 8:2 ratio, comprising 633 patients (Training cohort) and 158 patients (Validation cohort), respectively (Figure S1, Supporting Information).


### Prognostic association of nTregs in breast cancer
  The relative infiltration abundance of nTregs in breast cancer samples was estimated by ImmuneCellAI (http://bioinfo.life.hust.edu.cn /web/ImmuCellAI/) based on the RNA-seq expression matrix [16]. To apply the continuous infiltration abundance of nTregs for survival analysis and subsequent pathomics modeling, in this study, the “surv_cutpoint()” function in the “survminer” package of R software was used to determine the optimal survival cutoff value. This analysis took overall survival (OS) data as outcome variables, nTregs infiltration abundance as continuous input variables, and screened the optimal cutoff value related to the OS outcome based on the maximally selected rank statistics method [23]. To prevent the sample size of a certain group from being too small after grouping, the minimum sample ratio for each group was set at 30% (minprop = 0.3) during the analysis. The optimal cutoff value of nTregs was finally obtained as 0.008, and based on this, 928 patients were divided into the low nTregs infiltration group and the high nTregs infiltration group (Supplemental Table S1, and R1).


### Histopathological image processing and feature extraction
  The H&E-stained WSI were obtained from the TCGA database. The images were in svs format, with magnifications of either 20 × or 40 × . During preprocessing, the OTSU algorithm was employed to identify and segment tissue areas, effectively removing background regions while retaining relevant tissue [24]. For images at 40 × magnification, they were divided into sub-images of 1024 × 1024 pixels; for those at 20 × magnification, they were cut into 512 × 512-pixel sub-images and subsequently up-sampled to 1024 × 1024 pixels [25, 26]. All sub-images were then subject to a joint visual quality review by two pathologists to exclude those with obvious pollution, defocusing or blurring, or more than 50% blank areas. The automated quality control in this study primarily involved OTSU tissue area segmentation and screening based on the proportion of blank areas. Contamination and image blurring were assessed visually by the pathologists, without the application of additional automated clarity scoring or contamination area quantification algorithms. After quality control, 10 qualified sub-images were randomly selected from each pathological image for subsequent feature extraction and pathomics analysis.

  Using the Python-based package “PyRadiomics” (https://pyradiomics.readthedocs.io/en/latest/), the sub-images were subjected to image standardization and feature extraction. A total of 1488 features were obtained, including 93 primary features (first-order features + second-order features) and 8 higher-order features (Wavelet (LL, LH, HL, HH), LoG (kernel size: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5), Square, SquareRoot, Logarithm, Exponential, Gradient, and LBP2D). For each patient's pathology image, the corresponding 10 sub-images were individually processed to extract features, and the average value of these features was taken as the pathomics characteristic for each sample, which was used for subsequent data analysis [27, 28].

  Due to the high dimension of pathomics features and the possible information overlap among different texture features and high-order transformation features, this study adopted the maximum relevance minimum redundancy (mRMR) algorithm for preliminary feature screening. mRMR assessed the correlation between features and nTregs infiltration groups based on mutual information, while reducing the redundancy among candidate features, thereby retaining features related to the predicted label and with less repetitive information before modeling [29]. After obtaining the top 30 candidate features via mRMR screening, the recursive feature elimination (RFE) method was further applied to optimize the feature subset, and the final subset of features was used to construct a Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM) model.


### Development and validation of the pathomics prediction model
  Based on the pathomics features obtained by mRMR-RFE screening, this study used the GBM classification model to predict the high and low infiltration status of nTregs. GBM builds a prediction model by gradually integrating multiple weak classifiers, which is suitable for handling the potential nonlinear relationship and feature interaction between high-dimensional pathomics features and classification labels [30]. The model was constructed using the “train ()” function in the “caret” package of R language, and the model method was set to method = “gbm”. During the training process, fivefold cross-validation was adopted, with classProbs = TRUE and summaryFunction = two-ClassSummary set, and ROC was used as the model optimization metric. The classification label was nTregs infiltration status, where low nTregs infiltration was defined as the negative category and high nTregs infiltration was defined as the positive category. The model output was the probability that the sample was predicted to be high nTregs infiltration. The main hyperparameters of the final GBM model were set as follows: “n.rees = 50, interaction.depth = 3, shrinkage = 0.1, n.minobsinnode = 10.” (Supplemental R2).

  Model performance was evaluated using a comprehensive set of metrics for binary classification, with “high nTregs infiltration” defined as the positive class. The following metrics were calculated for both the training and validation cohorts: Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC-ROC), Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUPRC), Accuracy (ACC), Sensitivity (SEN), Specificity (SPE), Precision (PPV), and the F1-Score. Beyond discriminative ability, the model’s calibration (agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes) was assessed using calibration curves and the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. The overall prediction error was quantified using the Brier score, with lower values indicating better calibration. The clinical net benefit of using the model for decision-making across various risk thresholds was evaluated and visualized using Decision Curve Analysis (DCA).


### Derivation of the pathomics score and multi-omics mechanistic exploration
  The Pathomics Score (PS) was defined as the predicted probability output by the GBM classification model, which was used to represent the possibility that a sample was predicted to be in a high nTregs infiltration state. The GBM model took the H&E pathomics characteristics screened by mRMR-RFE as input variables, and used the high and low infiltration status of nTregs as classification labels. Among them, the high nTregs infiltration group was defined as the positive category (Y = 1), and the low nTregs infiltration group was defined as the negative category (Y = 0). Therefore, PS can be expressed as: PS = P (Y = 1 | X), where X represented the final pathomics features included in the model. The higher the PS value, the higher the probability that the model predicts the sample to be in a high nTregs infiltration state. To further evaluate the prognostic stratification value of PS, this study took PS as a continuous variable and combined OS.time and OS status to calculate the optimal survival cutoff value using the R package “survminer”. Finally, the PS cutoff value was obtained as 0.3513, and based on this, the patients were divided into the PS-high group and the PS-low group (Supplemental Table S2, and R3). This grouping was used for subsequent survival analysis, differential expression analysis, immune-related analysis and mutation spectrum analysis.

  To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the PS, we performed multi-omics analyses on the cohort stratified by high and low PS. Differential Gene Expression (DGE) analysis was performed using the “DESeq2” package (version 1.40.2) in R. We used the Wilcoxon rank-sum test to compare gene expression levels between high and low PS groups. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was subsequently performed on the resulting ranked gene list using the R package “clusterProfiler”, with the Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) (c2.cp.kegg.v7.5.1.symbols.gmt) and Hallmark (h.all.v7.5.1.symbols.gmt) gene sets to uncover enriched biological pathways and processes. To characterize the tumor immune microenvironment, the gene expression matrix of breast cancer samples was uploaded to the CIBERSORTx analytical portal (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/) to estimate the relative abundance of various immune cell types. Differences in immune cell infiltration between PS groups were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Furthermore, to explore the somatic mutation landscape, mutation data for TCGA-BRCA patients were downloaded from the GDC Data Portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). For the 791 overlapping samples with available pathomics data, somatic variants stored in Mutation Annotation Format (MAF) were analyzed and visualized using the R package “maftools”, focusing on the top 15 most frequently mutated genes.


### Wet-lab validation (FOXP3-IHC)
  To further verify the correlation between the PS and the expression of the nTregs infiltration marker FOXP3, samples of breast cancer patients diagnosed from January 2025 to January 2026 in Guangxi Medical University Cancer Hospital were collected. A total of 30 patients in the PS-high group and 30 patients in the PS-low group were included. All samples were stained with H&E and underwent full-field digital section scanning. Based on the machine learning model established in the early stage, feature extraction and PS value calculation were carried out for WSI images. Meanwhile, FOXP3 IHC staining was performed on the corresponding tissue sections, and double-blind independent scoring was conducted by two experienced pathologists according to the standardized evaluation method for FOXP3+ Tregs in breast cancer [31]. Finally, the correlation between PS values and FOXP3 scores was statistically analyzed to verify the recognition ability of the PS model for nTregs infiltration.


### Statistical analysis
  Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as number (percentage). The chi-square test was used for comparisons of categorical variables. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For variables that significantly deviated from a normal distribution, group comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. OS was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier curves with the log-rank test for univariate comparison. Univariate and multivariate cox proportional hazards regression models were constructed to identify independent prognostic factors. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was calculated to assess monotonic relationships between continuous variables. Model performance was evaluated using AUC-ROC, AUPRC, ACC, SEN, SPE, PPV, and F1-Score. Model calibration was assessed with calibration curves and the Brier score, and clinical utility was evaluated using DCA. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.0).

### Biological mechanisms analysis of PS
  To explore the biological mechanisms underlying PS, we performed GSEA and differential analysis on transcriptomic and genomic data from the PS-high and PS-low groups.

  GSEA was conducted using the Hallmark and Kyoto KEGG gene sets to identify enriched pathways in PS-high vs. PS-low groups. In the Hallmark gene set, DEGs in the PS-high group were significantly enriched in pathways related to epithelial-mesenchymal transition, interferon alpha/gamma responses, estrogen responses (early/late), apoptosis, and E2F targets (Fig. 6A).   Consistent with the pathway findings, the PS-high group exhibited a significantly upregulated expression of key immune checkpoint and co-stimulatory molecules, including CD276, TNFSF4, TNFSF18, TNFSF9, and ICOSLG (Fig. 6C). This pro-inflammatory transcriptional profile corresponded to a more complex and active tumor immune microenvironment, characterized by significantly increased infiltration of naive B cells, dendritic cells, T follicular helper cells, and CD8⁺ T cells in the PS-high group (Fig. 6D).

  In addition, Genomic analysis further distinguished the two groups. Missense mutations were the most prevalent alteration type. While TP53 mutations were frequent in both groups (> 30%), the mutation rate of PIK3CA was notably higher in the PS-high group, suggesting a distinct genomic context associated with the PS-defined phenotype (Fig. 6E, F).


## Discussion
  Our data and experiments showed that breast cancer tumors harbor distinct histomorphological features that are indicative of nTregs infiltration abundance. These features could be captured and quantified by a deep learning system trained on H&E-stained WSI. Leveraging the widespread use of H&E slides in clinical settings and the presence of corresponding histopathological images, RNA-seq data, and clinical results in the TCGA-BRCA dataset, the development of a machine learning model using H&E images is crucial for delving into the connections between pathological morphological traits and immune microenvironment characteristics.

  This study does not claim to be the only investigation focused on forecasting immune characteristics of breast cancer from H&E images. Recent advancements in AI-driven histopathology have indeed shown the potential to predict various immune features directly from routine H&E slides. Specifically, Shamai et al. [19] employed H&E images to classify PD-L1 expression in breast cancer, while Kobayashi et al. [32] quantified tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs)-including CD3, CD8, FOXP3, and PD-L1-in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) using a combination of H&E and IHC. Additionally, Nam et al. [33] classified TIL immune phenotypes (desert, excluded, inflamed) in papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) based solely on H&E. Beyond these data, Westenend et al. [34] predicted TME characteristics for prognosis in BC, and Hu et al. [35] extracted features associated with epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) from H&E in BC. Unlike prior studies (Table 5) that primarily target TIL density or broad immune phenotypes, our model captures a more specific, functionally relevant immune component, and links it directly to patient survival outcomes.   The predictive model was based on a set of pathomics features quantifying image intensity, texture, and local structure. Although the direct, one-to-one mapping of each feature to a discrete biological process remains to be fully elucidated, the strong association of the aggregate PS with FOXP3 expression and patient survival confirms that these features encode meaningful biological signals related to the immunosuppressive TME. Furthermore, this PS was evaluated for its clinical relevance. In survival assessments, a higher PS was independently associated with poorer OS even after adjusting for standard clinicopathological variables. This finding connects the image-based prediction of nTregs to a key patient outcome. To explore the biological underpinnings of this association, multi-omics analyses were conducted on patient groups stratified by PS. The results indicated that the PS-high phenotype was characterized by distinct transcriptional pathways and an immune-activated TME, providing a potential molecular context for the observed prognostic difference.

  Our multi-omics analysis revealed a unique and paradoxical immune landscape in PS-high tumors, marked by the simultaneous upregulation of both activating and inhibitory immune markers such as CD276 (B7-H3), TNFSF4 (OX40L), TNFSF9 (4-1BBL), and TNFSF18 (GITRL). This observation implied a sustained immune activation state that could potentially hinder effective anti-tumor immune responses [36–38]. Furthermore, the PS-high microenvironment, despite hosting increased infiltrates of CD8⁺ T cells, B cells, and macrophages, was associated with a worse prognosis. This suggested that the functional polarization, rather than just the quantity, of these cells influences clinical outcomes. The scenario likely represented immune "bystander" infiltration [39]. CD8⁺ T cells might be fatigued, B cells inclined toward regulatory phenotypes, and macrophages oriented towards a pro-tumor, M2-like state. Recent evidence suggested that dysregulated R-loop metabolism in TNBC, mediated by BPHL, can directly upregulate immune checkpoint molecules (e.g., PDCD1, CTLA4) and foster a T-cell suppressive environment, which aligns with our transcriptomic findings [40]. Therefore, the PS-high phenotype may encompass an "inflamed-suppressive" niche, where heightened immune activity is effectively counteracted by simultaneous immunosuppressive mechanisms, a concept in line with the predominant presence of nTregs anticipated by the PS itself.

  Several limitations and corresponding future directions are acknowledged. The study employed a training-validation split for internal validation with monte-carlo cross-validation, assessed with ROC curves, calibration, Brier score, and DCA. However, the performance evaluation and hyperparameter tuning were not conducted using a nested cross-validation framework. Therefore, while our GBM-based model serves as a valid exploratory proof-of-concept, further validation in larger, multi-center cohorts is necessary to determine its relative merits and the optimal algorithmic choice for this specific task. Additionally, the proposed biological mechanisms, such as the presence of dysfunctional immune cell states in the PS-high microenvironment, are inferred from computational deconvolution and correlation analyses. Direct experimental validation using techniques like single-cell RNA sequencing and multiplex immunohistochemistry is crucial to accurately characterize the functional phenotypes and spatial contexts of the immune infiltrates. This validation will strengthen the biological plausibility of the "inflamed-suppressive" niche hypothesis and enhance the clinical utility of PS as a biomarker.

  In summary, this study developed and validated a pathomics model to predict the infiltration of immunosuppressive nTregs directly from routine H&E-stained WSI of breast cancer. The derived PS strongly correlated with gold-standard FOXP3 expression, functioned as an independent prognostic biomarker for OS, and was linked to a distinct molecular and immune-activated TME. This work establishes a novel, accessible approach to translate complex immune biology into a practical, image-based diagnostic tool with the potential to enhance prognostic stratification and inform therapeutic decisions, especially in resource-limited settings.
