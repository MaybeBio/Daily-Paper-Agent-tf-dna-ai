# Structural basis of TACO1 function in mitochondrial translation elongation


## Abstract
  Protein synthesis depends on specialized regulatory factors to overcome difficult-to-translate sequences during elongation that cause ribosomal stalling. In human mitochondria, this function is carried out by TACO1 (Translation activator of COX1). Here, we report the cryo-electron microscopy structure of human TACO1 bound to the mitoribosome, revealing its mechanistic role as a dedicated elongation regulator. Unlike the prokaryotic elongation factor EF-P and the eukaryotic eIF5A, which bind the ribosomal E site and stabilize P-site tRNA to facilitate peptide bond formation, TACO1 adopts a distinct strategy. It binds near the A site, closely interacting with the Sarcin Ricin loop and the L7/L12 stalk, similar to general translation elongation factors, such as EF-Tumt and mtEF-G1mt, and contacts the A-site tRNA. Using AlphaFold analysis and molecular dynamics simulations, we further show how a series of conformational changes allow its N-terminal domain to reorient and transiently engage with the A-site tRNA via a conserved and functionally critical basic amino acid patch. The bacterial homolog of TACO1, namely YebC2, shares the structural features required for its function. Overall, we present an evolutionarily conserved mechanism employed by TACO1 to enhance mitochondrial translation efficiency.


## Introduction
  Mammalian mitochondrial ribosomes (mitoribosomes) are responsible for translating a compact genome that encodes essential subunits of the oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) system. Owing to their unique structural and functional features, mitoribosomes rely on specialized translational regulators to ensure fidelity and efficiency during protein synthesis1.

  Across all domains of life, polyproline stretches are notoriously difficult to translate due to the intrinsic inefficiency in forming consecutive proline–proline peptide bonds, which often results in ribosomal stalling. These translation obstacles are known to be resolved by specialized elongation factors: bacterial elongation factor P (EF-P) and members of the ATP-binding cassette family-F (ABCF) 2–4; the orthologs of EF-P, eukaryotic initiation factor 5A (eIF5A) in the eukaryotic cytosol, and aIF5A in archaea. These factors share a highly conserved mechanism of action; they bind at the interface of the small and large ribosomal subunits, positioned between the peptidyl (P) and exit (E) tRNA binding sites. The N-terminal domain of EF-P undergoes essential post-translational modifications and interacts with the acceptor stem of the P-site tRNA near the peptidyl transferase center (PTC), thereby stabilizing the stalled ribosome and promoting peptide bond formation5–8.

  Although mitochondria lack a homolog of these factors, recent evidence suggests that the mitochondrial protein TACO1 may have evolved to fulfill a similar function. TACO1 is a member of the DUF28/YebC protein family and was initially identified as a translation activator of COX1 (cytochrome c oxidase subunit I). Mutations in TACO1 have been linked to cytochrome c oxidase deficiency and late-onset Leigh syndrome9. Recent mitoribosome profiling and biochemical studies have demonstrated that TACO1 promotes translation elongation of COX1 and other mitochondrial transcripts by alleviating ribosomal pausing at polyproline motifs10. Supporting this functional role, bacterial homologs of TACO1 have been implicated in resolving analogous translational challenges: in Bacillus subtilis, YebC2 binds to the 70S ribosome and mitigates polyproline-induced stalling independently of EF-P11; similarly, a YebC2 homolog in Streptococcus pyogenes enhances the translation of polyproline-rich proteins both in vitro and in vivo 12. Together, these findings suggest an evolutionarily conserved role for DUF28/YebC family proteins in promoting ribosome progression through difficult-to-translate sequences. However, the mechanistic details of this process remain to be elucidated.

  In this study, we present a cryo-EM structure of human TACO1 bound to an actively translating mitoribosome, revealing its mechanism for stabilizing aminoacyl-tRNA and preventing premature binding of translation factors EF-Tu mt and mtEF-G1mt. We carry out AlphaFold analysis and molecular dynamics simulations to reveal how inter-domain reorganization of TACO1 can enable its function. Our findings establish TACO1 as part of the mitochondrial translational control machinery and highlight its importance for efficient protein synthesis


## Results

### Determination of the structure of the translating human mitoribosome bound to TACO1
  To capture the monosome-TACO1 complex, we overexpressed TACO1-FLAG in HEK293 cells and performed FLAG-IP (Supplementary Fig. 1). Additionally, we captured TACO1-containing complexes in a dataset obtained from IP of FLAG-tagged RCC1Lv1, a putative guanine nucleotide exchange factor involved in mitochondrial translation 13. The recovery of TACO1-containing complexes in this dataset was unexpected and provided independent support for the TACO1-ribosome interaction, while reducing concern that it arose from TACO1 overexpression. The yield from TACO1-IP was low (A260 ~ 0.7), indicating very limited or transient binding of TACO1 to the monosome. Thus, we employed a triple blotting strategy to increase the concentration of the sample on the cryo-EM grid (see methods). For RCC1L-IP, the yield was sufficient (A260 ~ 2.0–2.5) for us to employ the standard grid preparation approach optimized for mitoribosome samples14. The quality of the micrographs was suitable for further processing and particle picking. The particle population in the TACO1-IP dataset mostly constituted monosome and mtLSU, whereas that in the RCC1L-IP dataset was more heterogeneous, including monosome, mtLSU and mtSSU, which were sorted with 2D classification (Supplementary Fig. 2). For the purpose of this study, we focused on the monosome class. Overall, 101 601 and 416 898 monosome particles were pooled in TACO1-IP and RCC1L-IP datasets, respectively. In both maps, a weak unassigned density was observed close to the A site (Supplementary Fig. 2). Monosome particles from the two datasets were separately subjected to a series of focused classifications with signal subtraction (FCwSS) on the weak unassigned density and tRNA-binding region, respectively. Notably, the density exhibited a co-enrichment with A/A-tRNA in both datasets. Finally, we obtained 24 792 and 36 890 monosome particles bound to the unknown density from RCC1L-IP and TACO1-IP datasets. The two particle sets were pooled to produce a ~ 2.64 Å resolution reconstruction (consensus; state 1). The local resolution of the unassigned density was ~4.5 Å, which was sufficient for unambiguous assignment of the secondary structure (Supplementary Fig. 3). The unknown density was similar in both maps and located in close proximity to the A-site tRNA. This allowed us to identify the bound factor to be TACO1 using its AlphaFold model (AF-Q9BSH4) as the initial template 15,16. Complete domain 2 (residues 134-297) could be modeled; however, there was little visible density for the N-terminal domain 1 (residues 58-133). We carried out another step of FCwSS using a spherical mask covering domain 1 guided by the AF-model (Supplementary Fig. 2). This led to the identification of 7024 particles with an ordered domain 1 (D1-ordered; state 2) with an overall resolution of 4.05 Å. This allowed us to model almost the entire domain 1 (residues 73-133) except the N-terminal loop (up to residue 72). In the other class, domain 1 remained disordered (Fig. 1a-c; Supplementary Fig. 2). An additional density adjacent to TACO1 could be assigned to the C-terminal domain of bL12m. ### Mechanism of TACO1 binding to mitoribosome and A-tRNA
  Our cryo-EM data indicate that TACO1 primarily binds to the mitoribosome with tRNAs at the A site and P site in the classical A/A- and P/P- states, respectively (Fig. 1a,b; Supplementary Fig. 2). It interacts with the L7/L12 stalk (rRNA helices H43/44; uL11m and bL12m), the Sarcin-Ricin loop (SRL; rRNA H95 loop), rRNA H89 loop and the A-site tRNA (Fig. 1a-f; Supplementary Fig. 4). We identified two distinct states of TACO1 bound to the mitoribosome: consensus (domain 1 disordered) and D1-ordered (domain 1 ordered) (Fig. 1a–c). In the latter state, domain 1 (residues 72-133) is docked between uS12m of mtSSU and SRL of mtLSU (Figs. 1b and 1g). The majority of the TACO1 interactions with the mitoribosome involve domain 2 (Fig. 1d, f). TACO1 is likely recruited to the ribosome by bL12m-CTD that binds to a negatively charged patch on domain 2 of TACO1 (Fig. 1e and Fig. 2a). Domain 2 is packed against rRNA helices H43/H44 (of the L7/L12 stalk) and H89. The SRL sits against a cleft in domain 2 (Fig. 1h). Protein uL11m from the L7/L12 stalk does not directly participate in any interactions with TACO1, but makes contact with bL12m, which sterically orients the TACO1-bL12m complex. This is largely consistent with previously published work, which identified bL12m and uL11m as binding partners of TACO110. Docking of bL12m is stabilized by electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions (Fig. 1e, Fig. 2a, and Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4). The interface with rRNA is largely stabilized by polar interactions, except R141 (domain 2), which stacks with A2178 base from H43, and Y138 (domain 2), which stacks with U2960 of H89 (Fig. 1h). Importantly, the corresponding residue Y84 in bacterial YebC2 (S. pyogenes) was identified to be crucial for its activity12. Basic residues K159 and K170 are oriented towards A-tRNA, forming potential electrostatic interactions (Fig. 1f). Overall, TACO1 is appropriately positioned so that it closely approaches the A-site tRNA and appears to interact with the shoulder region of A-tRNA in a non-specific manner via an electrostatic interface (Fig. 1f; Fig. 2a).Fig. 2TACO1-ribosome interface, evolutionary conservation and comparison with other translation factors.a Electrostatic surface (positive: blue; negative: red) shows interaction of TACO1 with A-tRNA (red), rRNA (light grey), and bL12m (green)21–23. b TACO1 and YebC2 show a conservation of surface electrostatic charge distribution.   Furthermore, we performed the fluorescent-based Photo-Activatable Ribonucleotide Cross-Linking and Immuno-Precipitation (fPAR-CLIP) analysis, which demonstrated that the majority of TACO1-CLIP hits, relative to the non-FLAG control, correspond to the mitochondrial 16S rRNA, although crosslinking signals were distributed across the entire mitochondrial genome (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b). Notably, two of the top three binding sites aligned precisely with helices H89 and H41–43, in agreement with the cryoEM data, whereas the third region (H97–100) showed lower relevance relative to our structural model (Supplementary Fig. 5c, d).

  The overall structure, surface electrostatic charge distribution, and the residues of TACO1 involved in engagement with A-tRNA, rRNA and bL12m are largely conserved between human TACO1 and bacterial YebC2 (Fig. 2a–c). This suggests that the structural basis of function in the TACO1/YebC2 family of translation factors is evolutionarily conserved. The structures show that TACO1 utilizes a mechanism wherein it directly engages the A-site tRNA. This mechanism differs fundamentally from that of EF-P and eIF5A, which associate with the ribosomal E site and interact with the P-site tRNA at the peptidyl transferase center to promote peptide bond formation5–7,17 (Fig. 2d). Notably, the binding site of TACO1 partially overlaps with the binding sites for general translation factors EF-G1mt and EF-Tu mt, which also engage the SRL and bL12m-CTD18–20 (Fig. 2d). In this configuration TACO1 sterically blocks the A site and stabilizes the A/A-tRNA molecule that is potentially not stably accommodated due to a delayed peptidyl transfer reaction. The two identified states reflect the intrinsic dynamics of domain 1, indicating that the structural flexibility of TACO1 may enable its regulatory role during translation elongation.


### Conformational flexibility of the N-terminal domain contributes to TACO1 function
  A combination of cryo-EM data, computational modeling, and prior biochemical evidence24 reveals how the dynamic nature of domain 1 may contribute to the functional mechanism of TACO1. We could only observe an ordered domain 1 density in about 10% of monosome-TACO1-bound particles (D1-ordered). Consistent with the lack of density for domain 1 in ~90% of TACO1-mitoribosome particle images, the AlphaFold model of TACO1 is associated with lower pLDTT scores for N-terminal residues of helix 1 (up to Pro72) and domain 1 loops, suggesting this region is flexible and may adopt alternative conformations that are functionally meaningful (Fig. 3a). To investigate this, we performed in silico analysis wherein we generated multiple models of human TACO1 using AlphaFold2 at different depths of multiple sequence alignment (MSA) (Fig. 3b, c; Supplementary Fig. 6). This effectively limits the MSA-derived co-evolutionary information available for structure prediction by AlphaFold, leading to greater heterogeneity in predictions. Previous work has demonstrated that by reducing the depth of MSA, ensembles of energetically-viable conformational states can be predicted using AlphaFold221–23,25. Using this strategy, we could identify two distinct motions: flipping of helix 1, and a rotation of domain 1 (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 6a–c). As the MSA depth is reduced from 512 to 16, helix 1 (residues 58-94) is predicted to flip between two distinct conformational states: 1) “helix1-IN” state, wherein its N-terminal residues closely interact with an acidic patch on domain 2; 2) “helix1-OUT” state where it projects out from the body of TACO1 as an extended helix (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 6a–c).Fig. 3Role of domain 1 in TACO1 function.a Human TACO1 model obtained from the AlphaFold database (AF-Q9BSH4-F1) colored by pLDDT scores (inset shows the model rotated at 180°). b Panel shows the superposed models of TACO1 predicted using AlphaFold 2 (ColabFoldv1.6.1) at MSA depth of 16 and 512, revealing flipping of helix 1 between helix1-IN (green) and helix1-OUT (yellow) states, with a rotation of domain 1 that is associated with the helix1-OUT state.   At MSA depth 16, all TACO1 models are in helix1-OUT state and exhibit a rotation of domain 1 (Fig. 3b, c, Supplementary Fig. 6a–c). The predicted aligned error (PAE) plot for the AF-TACO1 model (Q9BSH4), which reflects the confidence in the relative positioning of residue pairs, also suggests flexibility in the N-terminal region, including domain 1 (Supplementary Fig 6d). To visualize domain 1 movement in the context of the ribosome, we superposed TACO1 models from the ensembles obtained above onto the cryo-EM TACO1–monosome structure and traced its center of mass (although this does not account for steric constraints). The plot between the distance of helix 1 (conserved Trp63 as reference) from A/A-tRNA and SRL (colored by degree of domain 1 rotation) shows a trajectory for domain 1 where higher degrees of rotation tend to be associated with proximity to A/A-tRNA. Therefore, this conformational flexibility appears to position helix 1 near A-tRNA to acquire a potentially transient “A/A-tRNA docked” like conformation (Fig. 3b, c, Supplementary Fig. 6f). RMSD analysis of the ensembles obtained using different MSA depths shows a clustering of TACO1 models into distinct conformational states mentioned above (Supplementary Fig. 6a–c). This clustering is also evident in the plots showing the distance of helix 1 from A/A-tRNA and SRL. This manner of distinct conformational clustering between high and low MSA depths has been reported previously for other proteins22.

  We carried out a similar analysis by comparing AlphaFold models of closely related TACO1 homologs (more than 60 % identity) from different species (96 models in the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database). This revealed a similar conformational variability of domain 1 as predicted by lowering of MSA depth, albeit with greater domain 1 rotation (Supplementary Fig. 7).

  Notably, the interface between domain 1 and domain 2 is relatively weak: −1.7 Kcal/mol for helix1-IN conformation; −1.1 Kcal/mol for helix1-OUT conformation (as calculated on PDBePISA server, see methods), consistent with domain 1 flexibility. Qualitatively, these ensembles together appear to represent a swing-like rotation of domain 1 that is accessible when helix 1 is flipped out, triggering a destabilization of a largely polar domain 1-2 interface (Fig. 3b, c; Supplementary Fig 6a, e). Consequently, when TACO1 is bound to the ribosome (via domain 2), the predicted reorientation of domain 1 (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. 6c) together with helix 1 flexibility would allow for close interactions between conserved basic residues on helix 1 (Fig. 2b, c) and the A-tRNA (Supplementary Fig. 6d–f). This aligns well with previous mutational studies that revealed the conserved N-terminal basic patch (residues R77, S78, R79 and K83) to be required for RNA binding and TACO1 function (Fig. 2b, c)24. We observe a similar conformational variability for domain 1 of YebC2 (S. pyogenes) (at a reduced MSA depth), indicating that, akin to TACO1 binding to the mitoribosome, this is also likely to be an evolutionarily conserved feature of the TACO1/YebC2 family of proteins (Supplementary Fig. 8). In summary, AF predictions suggest a conformational rearrangement of TACO1 domain 1 which would potentially allow it to form transient but functionally meaningful interactions with A/A-tRNA and rRNA. We explore this hypothesis further with molecular dynamics simulations.


### Simulations demonstrate how structural reorganization allows TACO1 to interact with A-tRNA
  To establish a mechanistic understanding of TACO1 function, we next applied molecular dynamics simulations to investigate whether electrostatics can facilitate the interaction of TACO1 with A-tRNA. For these calculations, we used an all-atom structure-based model (“SMOG” force field26), with screened electrostatic interactions (Debye-Hückel) between TACO1 and the mitoribosome. This force field explicitly defines the cryo-EM structure of the ribosome and TACO1 as stable. However, stabilizing interactions between domain 1 and domain 2 are not included, which allows domain 1 to respond to the local electrostatic environment of the ribosome.

  Simulations with this model reveal multiple dominant configurations where TACO1 interacts with the A-tRNA. Ten independent simulations were performed for 2 ms (Supplementary Fig. 9) each. This timescale is sufficient for domain 1 to spontaneously rearrange and associate with the A-tRNA (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 9a–j). To describe the distribution of domain orientations, we calculated the distance between the N-terminal tail (Cɑ atom of Trp63) and the acceptor arm of the A-site tRNA (P atom of C70) (Fig. 3d). This coordinate can distinguish between positions in which TACO1 is closer to the helix1-IN (Fig. 3d) and helix1-OUT (and the subset A/A-tRNA docked) -like conformations, where the latter is associated with close interaction with the A-tRNA (Fig. 3g–j). The helix1-OUT-like conformations are most probable, where the N-terminal tail of domain 1 is in close proximity to the A-tRNA acceptor arm to form the A/A-tRNA docked-like state (Fig. 3f, g–j). Here, the basic patch of the tail forms short-range interactions with the A-tRNA (Fig. 3i-l, Supplementary Fig. 10), as suggested by the AF-predicted structures (Fig. 3k, l; Supplementary Fig. 6c, f and 7). Since our model predefines the N-terminal tail as helical, we considered the possibility that increased flexibility of the tail could allow it to reposition and form stronger electrostatic interactions with the A-tRNA. To address this, we repeated the simulations using a model in which the helical propensity of the tail was reduced or eliminated (see methods for details). With these flexible-tail models, TACO1 can still reach A/A-tRNA-docked-like positions; however, the increase in flexibility leads to frequent dissociation of domain 1 from the A-tRNA (Supplementary Fig. 11).

  Taken together, our cryo-EM data, AF analysis and MD simulations - interpreted in the context of previous biochemical measurements24 - provide strong evidence that a large-scale conformational rearrangement of the N-terminal domain allows TACO1 to directly engage the stalled A-tRNA molecule (Fig. 4, Supplementary Movie 1).Fig. 4Schematic representation of the proposed mechanism of TACO1-mediated human mitochondrial translation, based on cryo-EM analyses and simulated dynamics.During elongation, aminoacyl-tRNA (red) is delivered to the A site of the mitoribosomes by EF-Tu mt (orange) (PDB ID: 7A5G)18. ## Discussion
  In this work, we present the mechanism of TACO1 recruitment to the mitoribosome and elucidate the structural basis of TACO1 function in translation elongation. Distinct from EF-P and eIF5A, TACO1 binds near the A site and interacts with A/A-tRNA. This presents an interesting example where the same biological problem may be resolved by employing diverse strategies. EF-P and eIF5A both have a large interface with the uL1m protein of the L1 stalk7,17. Interestingly, in the human mitoribosome, the L1 stalk is relatively rigid and the rRNA component is much shorter compared to its 70S and 80S counterparts27. This potentially results in a disrupted interface for EF-P binding, necessitating TACO1 to take over the role.

  Here, we computationally demonstrate how conformational rearrangement of domain 1, involving helix 1 flexibility and a certain degree of domain 1 rotation, can bring a conserved patch of basic residues in close proximity to A-site tRNA, rRNA H69 and the A-loop. This idea is consistent with the observation that the interface between domains 1 and 2 is relatively weak and that other translation factors have been previously reported to exploit spontaneous inter-domain rearrangements for their functions. For example, the predicted reorientation of TACO1-domain1 is reminiscent of domain IV rearrangements of bacterial EF-G that adopts a similar “extended” conformation, which is independent of GTP hydrolysis28–30. While EF-G-domain IV would clash with A-tRNA, a relatively small N-terminal helix of TACO1 can be accommodated between H69 and A-tRNA, thereby stabilizing the region. Poly-prolines are reported to delay peptidyl transferase reaction by up to 100-fold, i.e., from 20-50 s−1 to 0.14 s−131,32, which could result in destabilization of amino-acylated A/A-tRNA. Therefore, TACO1-mediated stabilization of A/A-tRNA might be important. It remains to be shown if and how TACO1 accesses the PTC as EF-P does in bacterial ribosome. This becomes a relevant speculation if the A/A-tRNA docked state, as proposed here, could potentially allow the N-terminus to reach the PTC. In B. subtilis, replacing domain 1 of YebC2 with that of YrbC (a paralog of YebC2 that plays a role in transcription, not translation) fails to rescue the ΔefpΔyebC2 background, which is otherwise rescued by wild-type YebC24. This demonstrates the functional importance of domain 1. In another study, it was shown with iCLIP that E.coli YebC2 may transiently interact with residues of the PTC12.

  To further explore this potential interaction mode, FCwSS with a spherical mask centered on the TACO1 domain 1 docked conformations (near A/A-tRNA and H69) yielded a small class displaying an unassigned density (Supplementary Fig. 12). The low particle number and limited resolution preclude definitive conclusions; however, its presence is consistent with domain 1 adopting a dispersed ensemble of configurations localized around the A/A-tRNA acceptor arm. Interestingly, in the recently reported cryo-EM map of TACO1-monosome complex (EMD- 70592), consistent with our own data, we observed a relatively weak density for domain1 and its alternate configuration close to A/A-tRNA (at very low threshold)33.

  In summary, we combine the experimental approach of single particle cryo-EM with systematic analysis employing AlphaFold predictions and MD simulations to reveal a distinct and evolutionarily conserved mechanism exploited by TACO1/YebC2 family of general translation factors involved in resolving translation pauses in bacterial and mitochondrial systems.


## Methods

### Generation of stable cell lines
  Stable cell lines expressing FLAG-tagged version of TACO1 and RCC1L in Flp-In T-Rex HEK293T cells (Invitrogen) were generated (primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 2), enabling doxycycline-inducible, dose-dependent gene expression of target genes. Cells were cultured in DMEM (Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium) containing 10% (v/v) tetracycline-free fetal bovine serum, 2 mM Glutamax (Gibco), 1× Penicillin/Streptomycin (Gibco), 50 µg/ml uridine, 10 µg/ml Zeocin (Invitrogen), and 100 µg/ml blasticidin (Gibco) at 37 °C and 5% CO2 atmosphere. HEK293T cells were transfected in a single well of a 6-well culture plate using Lipofectamine 3000 with pcDNA5/FRT/TO constructs encoding TACO1-FLAG or RCC1L-FLAG (with 1X FLAG at the C-terminus), along with the Flp recombinase plasmid pOG44, according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Twenty-four hours post-transfection, selective antibiotics were applied by adding 100 μg/ml hygromycin B and 10 μg/ml blasticidin S, with media changes every 2–3 days.


### Mitochondrial purification
  HEK293T cells overexpressing TACO1 and RCC1L were induced with 50 ng/ml doxycycline for 48 hours. The mitochondria were isolated and stored as previously described 34. Briefly, cells were collected and swollen in an ice-cold hypotonic buffer (0.6 M mannitol, 100 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 10 mM EDTA, 0.05% BSA). Further mechanical disruption of the cells was carried out using a Dounce homogenizer. The solution was first centrifuged at 400 g for 10 min at 4 °C to eliminate cellular debris, followed by a second centrifugation at 11,000 g for 10 min to pellet the mitochondria. The crude mitochondria were further purified by sucrose cushion (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 1 mM EDTA, 1.0 M and 1.5 M sucrose) and centrifuged for 1 h at 25,000 rpm (SW41 Ti rotor, Beckman Coulter). The mitochondrial fraction was collected from the interphase between the sucrose layers, diluted 1:1 with 10 mM Tris-HCl pH7.5, pelleted again, and resuspended in 300 μl freezing buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH7.5, 10 mM KCl, 0.1% BSA, 1 mM EDTA, 200 mM trehalose). Samples were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C.


### Purification of TACO1-bound mitoribosomal complex
  Mitochondria were lysed in 2-3 ml of lysis buffer (25 mM HEPES-KOH pH7.5, 25 mM KCl, 20 mM Mg(OAc)₂, 2% (v/v) β-DDM, complete EDTA-free protease inhibitors [Roche], and 400U RNase inhibitor [Invitrogen]) and incubated for 30 min at 4 °C. The lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 21,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was incubated with 100 μl of pre-washed ANTI-FLAG M2 Affinity Gel (Sigma–Aldrich) for 2 hours at 4 °C. Bound complexes were washed three times with wash buffer (25 mM HEPES-KOH pH7.5, 25 mM KCl, 20 mM Mg(OAc)₂, 0.05% β-DDM) and eluted by incubation with 35 μl of elution buffer (wash buffer supplemented with 0.5 μg/μl 3X FLAG peptide) for 30 min shaking at 800 rpm. Finally, the purified samples were obtained at a concentration of 2–2.5 AU (260 nm) for RCC1L-IP and ~ 0.7 AU (260 nm) for TACO1-IP.


### Cryo-EM and image processing
  Sample was prepared using UltraAufoil R1.2/1.3 Au 300 mesh grids (Quantifoil Micro Tools GMBH) for the TACO1 dataset, and Quantifoil R2/2 Cu 300 mesh grids for the RCC1L dataset. For the RCC1L-IP sample, we prepared grids with or without the cross-linker BS3 (Bissulfosuccinimidyl suberate), which has an 11 Å spacer arm, with the rationale to stabilize transient complexes. The sample was incubated with 0.8 mM BS3 for 30 min on ice before grid freezing to prevent degradation of sensitive complexes as described previously35,36. Prior to sample application, all grids were coated with a 2-3 nm continuous carbon layer and glow-discharged for 2 minutes at 25 mA using a PELCO easiGlow system. The sample (3 µl) was applied to the grids at 4 °C and 100% humidity (wait time 30 s, blot time 3 s, blot force 1) and vitrified by plunge-freezing in liquid ethane using a Vitrobot Mk IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For TACO1-IP samples, since the sample concentration was low, a triple blotting approach was employed wherein 3 µL of the sample was applied onto the grid followed by 30 s wait and 3 s blotting thrice in succession (sample application-wait-blot) before vitrification.

  Overall, we collected 19 212 movies for TACO1-IP sample. Images were acquired on a Titan Krios electron microscope (Thermo Fisher) operated at 300 kV (cold FEG), in nanoprobe EFTEM SA mode with a slit width of 10 eV using a K3 Gatan Bioquantum detector. Movies were collected at a magnification of 105,000x (0.8252 Å/pix), 40 frames/movie and a flux of ~1 e/Å2/frame on (correlated double sampling) CDS mode. Motion correction and CTF estimation were carried out in Relion 5.037. The particles were picked by Gaussian picking, subjected to 2D classification and the resulting 2D class averages were used as references for final particle picking. The picked particles were subjected to 2D classification, and particles corresponding to monosome classes were pooled and extracted using a box size of 640 pixels, then downsampled to 180 pixels. Binned data were further cleaned up in CryoSPARC v4.338 with heterogeneous refinement using classes produced from Ab initio reconstruction, including a junk class. The final monosome particles (101,601) were unbinned to 640 pixels and imported back to Relion 5.0 for further 3D classification. A spherical mask was applied around the weak density next to A-site to carry out partial signal subtraction. The signal subtracted particle set was subjected to 3D classification (Focussed Classification with Signal Subtraction). This gave us 48 458 particles with improved occupancy for the unknown density (TACO1). The other classes were likely from particles where TACO1 dissociated or remained disordered. The occupancy of the density was further improved by carrying out FCwSS on the A-site tRNA which finally produced a class of 36,890 particles at an overall resolution of 3.2 Å (Relion 3D autorefine).

  We collected two datasets for RCC1L-IP sample comprising 36,854 and 54,853 (with BS3) movies, respectively. Similar to TACO1-IP, images were acquired on a Titan Krios electron microscope (Thermo Fisher) operated at 300 kV (cold FEG only for the second dataset), in nanoprobe EFTEM SA mode with a slit width of 10 eV using a K3 Gatan Bioquantum detector. Movies were collected at a magnification of 105,000x (0.8252 Å/pix), 40 frames/movie and a flux of ~1 e/Å2/frame on CDS mode. For the first dataset, motion correction, CTF estimation and particle picking were performed in Relion 5.0 as explained above. The picked (with 3x binning as above) particles were subjected to 2D classification followed by 3D classification to identify monosome particles. In addition to monosome particles, there was also a large number of mtLSU and mtSSU particles in the data. For this work, only monosomes were identified with 2D classification and taken for further processing. For the second dataset, motion correction and CTF estimation followed by particle picking were carried out in CryoSPARC v4.3. After 2D classification, the particles from monosome classes were pooled together and subjected to Ab initio reconstruction followed by heterogeneous refinement, including classes for monosome, mtLSU and a junk class to enrich monosome particles. We saw a faint density for TACO1 in maps from both datasets (with and without BS3) and decided to proceed with merging the data to improve the particle number for subsequent 3D classifications. The monosome particles from the two datasets were unbinned to 640 pixels, pooled together in Relion 5.0 and subjected to 3D-autorefinement. The occupancy for unknown density (TACO1) was expectedly lower than the starting maps from TACO-IP datasets. After performing FCwSS using a mask for TACO1 as described above, we obtained the final map from 24,792 particles at an overall resolution of 3.05 Å (Relion 3D autorefine) (Supplementary Fig. 2). Notably, we did not observe a density for RCC1L. This is likely because RCC1L either does not directly interact with mature monosome or the association is non-specific and weak, leading to dissociation of RCC1L during later stages of sample preparation following elution.

  Finally, all monosome-TACO1 complex particles (61 682) from TACO1-IP and RCC1L-IP datasets were combined and exported back to CryoSPARC v4.3 where the data were subjected to NU-Refine and global and local CTF refinement iteratively to give a final reconstruction of 2.64 Å overall resolution. In parallel, the particle set was subjected to a final round of FCwSS in Relion 5.0 using a spherical mask placed at the predicted position of domain 1 according to the human AlphaFold model of TACO1 (AF-Q9BSH9). This gave 7024 particles with an ordered domain 1 with an overall resolution of 4.05 Å after NU-refine in CryoSPARC v4.3.


### Model building
  The consensus map was used for building the monosome-TACO1 model. Initial coordinates were taken from PDB entry 7QI5 27. The model was rigid body fitted into the map using UCSF Chimera-X39 and coot v0.9.8 40. The model for TACO1 (AF-Q9BSH4) was obtained from the AFDB 15,16and could be accommodated into the unassigned density. Except for the N-terminal domain (residues 1-133), the rest of TACO1 agreed well with the map (Fig. 1; Supplementary Fig. 2). Therefore, residues 1-133 were removed. An additional unmodeled density was unambiguously assigned to bL12m-CTD (Fig. 1; Supplementary Fig. 2). There were clear densities for A/A- and P/P-tRNAs and mRNA, but a weak density for E/E-tRNA and the nascent polypeptide chain. This requires further 3D classification (FCwSS), especially to resolve the nascent chain from the partly overlapping mL45 N-terminus loop in the polypeptide exit tunnel35, for which the current particle set is insufficient. Therefore, the nascent chain was not built in the current model. The model was then real-space refined in Coot v0.9.8 with self reference-restraints (at weight 3.7), Ramachandran and torsion restraints at an optimized map weight. Water molecules were removed, and metal ions were manually checked and retained/removed according to the density. The models were then hydrogenated by ReadySet in the PHENIX suite 41. The resulting model was subjected to stereochemical refinement against the density map with 4 macro-cycles of energy minimization (rotamer, Ramachandran and reference restraints applied) using phenix.real_space_refine 42. The final model was validated with MolProbity43 as a part of the PHENIX suite. Refinement statistics are given in Supplementary Table 1.

  For TACO1-domain 1, the model obtained above was used as the template and fit into the D1-ordered map. Domain 1 residues 72-133 from the AlphaFold model (AF-Q9BSH4) were added to TACO1. We retained alpha helices α1-3 but the remaining N-terminal residues (up to residue 71) and connecting loops (residues 98-99,117 and 132-133) were removed due to lack of support from density. The model was then subjected to refinement in the PHENIX suite as described above.


### Model analysis and visualization
  Structural data was analysed in coot v0.9.840 and UCSF ChimeraX 1.8 44. Interface energy between domain 1 and domain 2-3 of TACO1 was calculated using PDBePISA webserver (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/pdbe/pisa/). Figures were generated with UCSF ChimeraX 1.8 44.


### Fluorescent photo-activatable Ribonucleoside-enhanced cross-linking and immunoprecipitation (fPAR-CLIP)
  fPAR-CLIP was performed as previously described45 with minor modifications. Briefly, FLAG-tagged TACO1 expression in HEK293 cells was induced with 50 ng/ml doxycycline for 24 h. Cells were then UV crosslinked (312 nm, 0.15 J/cm²) and lysed in RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS) supplemented with protease inhibitors. 1.1 mg of protein lysates were used for immunoprecipitation (IP) with anti-FLAG M2 magnetic beads (M8823, Merck Millipore) overnight at 4 °C. After IP, beads were washed 3 times with RIPA buffer and then treated with 1 U/ul RNase T1 (EN0541, ThermoFisher) for 15 min at room temperature to digest unprotected RNA. Beads were washed 3 times with RIPA buffer and once with dephosphorylation buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl₂), followed by dephosphorylation using Quick CIP (M0525S, New England Biolabs) for 10 min at 37 °C. Beads were washed again 2 times each with dephosphorylation and PNK/ligation buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 10 mM MgCl₂), then ligated overnight at 4 °C to 0.5 μM 3′ fluorescent adapter (MultiplexDX) using truncated T4 RNA ligase 2 K227Q (M0351, New England Biolabs). Beads were washed 4 times with PNK/ligation buffer, and RNA footprints were phosphorylated with T4 PNK (M0201S, New England Biolabs) for 30 min at 37 °C. After washing three times with RIPA buffer, protein–RNA complexes were eluted by heating beads in 2× SDS Laemmli buffer at 95 °C for 5 min, separated by SDS-PAGE (4–12%, NW04122BOX, Invitrogen), and visualised using a Chemidoc MP system (Bio-Rad) on the IR 680 channel. The band corresponding to TACO1–RNA complexes was excised from the gel, followed by Proteinase K (RPROTK-RO, Sigma Aldrich) treatment, and RNA was extracted by phenol–chloroform phase separation. Purified RNA was ligated to 0.5 μM 5′ adapter (MultiplexDX) using T4 RNA ligase 1 (EL0021, ThermoFisher) at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by reverse transcription using SuperScript IV (18090010, ThermoFisher). cDNA libraries were amplified with Platinum Taq DNA polymerase (10966034, ThermoFisher) and size-selected using 3% Pippin Prep (CSD3010, Sage Science). Libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw data were processed using Bcl2fastq (v2.20.0), Cutadapt (v1.15, Python 3.6.4)46,47, PARpipe (https://github.com/ohlerlab/PARpipe)48. Adapter sequences and sequencing primers are listed in Supplementary Table 3.

  The “.bam” files, along with the indexed “.bam.bai” files of three replicates of TACO1-FLAG and two replicates of the negative control, were loaded onto IGV with the NC012920 as the reference mitochondrial genome. Average peak counts from the replicates were calculated for the highest peaks evidently visible in IGV. Delta peak count (TACO1-Avg peak count minus Neg control-Avg peak count) was calculated for such peaks. The appropriate RNA footprints of the peaks were mapped to the RNA sequence (via mtDNA annotation) on the cryo-EM structures, which were obtained.


### AlphaFold analysis
  Strategy 1: Ensembles of TACO1 (Homo sapiens) and YebC2 (Streptococcus pyogenes) models were generated on ColabFold v1.6.249,50 using the AlphaFold 2 model. To sample conformational heterogeneity, we used no template, 16 seeds and MSA depths of 16:32, 64:128 or 512:1024 with inference-time dropout enabled. In this manner, ensembles of 80 models were generated for each run. TACO1 sequence was obtained from UniProt ID Q9BSH4 (residues 58-297, excluding the likely MTS). The sequence of YebC2 was taken from UniProt ID P67188 and models were predicted at an MSA depth of 16:32. A representative YebC2 model is displayed in Fig. 2. Predicted models of TACO1and YebC2 are available as ChimeraX sessions (Supplementary Data 1 and 2).

  Strategy 2: To complement the analysis done by manipulating MSA depth, we alternatively sampled TACO1 models from different species downloaded from the AlphaFold2 database. We used human TACO1 as a query to search for TACO1 homologs with AlphaFind v2. All models up to 60 % sequence identity were downloaded. To ensure uniform representation of models, 25–30 models were randomly selected from sequence identity based subgroups (60–70%, 71–80 %, 81–90% and 91–100%), and those with significant size differences from human TACO1 due to insertions/deletions were removed. The remaining 95 models were subjected to further analysis (Source Data).

  The models were superposed with TACO1 in the cryo-EM structure of TACO1-monosome complex (current work) and analysed in ChimeraX40. The center of mass of domain 1 (residues 77-130) was calculated and shown as spheres. To depict movements of helix 1, the distance between highly conserved residue Trp 63 (helix 1) and A/A-tRNA (atom OP1 of residue C70) and the SRL (atom OP1 of residue 3129) was calculated. To calculate RMSD (root mean squared deviation), all models of TACO1 for ensembles from MSA depth 512, 64 and 16 were superposed on domain 2 and RMSD was calculated with the first helix1-IN model from the MSA depth 512 ensembles as the reference. RMSD could not be calculated for AFDB downloaded models as they differed in sequence and residue number. To calculate domain 1 rotation, we defined the axis passing through domain 1 (excluding the flexible segment of helix 1) for all models and measured the angle between the respective domain 1 axis for each model and the domain 1 axis for the reference helix1-IN state.

### TACO1-specific application of the SMOG force field
  We first generated a SMOG model using the structure of the complete mitoribosome with TACO1 bound (183,906 atoms). To introduce the full N-terminal helix, we generated a SMOG force field for the AF structural model of TACO1 (AF-Q9BSH4). The energetic parameters of the N-terminal residues (Lys62 to Glu77) were then added to the full ribosome system, and the tail was manually positioned near Arg78. For all simulations, we removed all stabilizing contacts between domain 1 (Lys62-Glu129) and the rest of the system. Additionally, we removed all contacts and flexible dihedrals associated with the linker region between domains 1 and 2 (Lys130, Ser131). This allows domain 1 to reorient (consistent with lack of EM density) while also maintaining the AF-predicted structure of the N-terminal helix.

  To characterize the potential influence of molecular flexibility, we constructed force fields that have reduced or eliminated the helical propensity of the N-terminal helix. To generate these force fields, we modified the original force field by either removing contacts and dihedrals in the N-terminal helix (Lys62 to Glu77; i.e. “highly flexibility” model; Supplementary Fig. 10) or scaling the dihedrals and contacts by 0.5 (“moderate flexibility” model).

  SMOG v2.6 and SMOG-tools 26 were used to generate all force fields (original, moderate flexibility and high flexibility models). The force field files are available for download at the SMOG2 force field repository (smog-server.org) under accession name AA_charged_Singh26.v1 and all associated files required for force field preparation are registered and released through GitHub/Zenodo.


### Simulation details
  Simulations were performed using OpenSMOG libraries (v1.3)56, which are built upon the OpenMM framework57. A timestep of 0.002 reduced time units was used. Simulations were performed at a reduced temperature of 0.5. At this temperature, the flexibility of proteins and RNA are consistent with the flexibility predicted by all-atom explicit-solvent models58.

  For the original model, ten independent simulations were performed for an effective simulated time of 2 milliseconds. To estimate the effective simulated time, we used the diffusion-coefficient-based scaling reported by Yang et al59., where one reduced time unit corresponds to approximately 50ps–1 ns. Here, we interpret a reduced unit as 1 ns. Each simulation was sufficiently long that domain 1 of TACO1 was able to spontaneously interconvert between flipped-out and A-tRNA- docked conformations. In all simulations, the A-tRNA- docked (flipped-out-like) position was energetically favored. For the moderate and high flexibility models, 2 independent simulations were performed for 1 millisecond each. For both models, flipped-out-like configurations were accessible, however domain 1 would frequently dissociate from the A-tRNA and adopt OUT-like poses (Supplementary Fig. 10).
