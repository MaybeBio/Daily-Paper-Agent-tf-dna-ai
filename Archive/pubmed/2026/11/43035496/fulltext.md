# Decomposing multi-scale dynamic regulation from single-cell multiomics with scMagnify


## Abstract
  Deciphering the highly coupled regulatory circuits that drive cellular dynamics remains a fundamental goal in biology. However, capturing the multi-scale time-lagged dynamics and combinatorial regulatory logic of gene regulation remains challenging. Here we present scMagnify, a deep-learning-based framework that leverages chromatin accessibility and gene expression via Granger causality to reconstruct and decompose multi-scale gene regulatory networks (GRNs). Diverse benchmarks demonstrate scMagnify’s favorable performance. scMagnify employs tensor decomposition to identify combinatorial TF modules and their activation profiles across multiple time-lags. We applied scMagnify to human hematopoiesis and mouse pancreas development, where it recovered known lineage-driving regulators and provided further insights into the combinatorial logic underlying cell fate decisions. Furthermore, in the context of kidney injury, scMagnify’s intercellular communication module mapped key signaling-to-transcription cascades linking microenvironment cues to pathological epithelial cell changes. In summary, scMagnify provides a robust computational framework for dissecting the multi-scale regulatory logic that governs complex biological processes in development and disease.


## Introduction
  Cell fates and complex organismal structures are established through a sophisticated series of molecular cues that orchestrate intricate and highly specific programs of gene expression. These programs are governed by interconnected molecular networks, which are shaped by a repertoire of cell-type-specific transcription factors (TFs)1–3. Importantly, TFs typically bind to cis-regulatory regions containing clusters of diverse TF binding sites. When associated TFs are co-expressed in overlapping spatial domains, their combinatorial binding generates precise spatiotemporal transcriptional activity patterns4,5. This context-dependent regulation allows TFs to integrate multiple signals, activating or repressing target genes (TGs) with high specificity, thereby driving the intricate gene expression programs required for cellular specialization and organismal development6.

  Over the past decades, a variety of computational methods have been proposed to unravel the intricate regulatory mechanisms controlling gene expression7,8. Transcriptome-based methods, such as WGCNA9, GENIE310, GRNBoost211 and PIDC12, infer TF–TG relationships by capturing covariation in gene expression. Although tree-based methods, such as GENIE3 and GRNBoost2, can infer directed regulator–target associations when candidate regulators are specified, expression-only approaches generally remain limited in distinguishing direct from indirect effects and lack explicit causal interpretability13,14.

  To overcome these limitations, recent advances have taken two major directions. First, transcriptional regulation unfolds dynamically during cell-state transitions, where TF activity and TG expression may be temporally offset. Incorporating temporal or pseudotemporal information is therefore important for distinguishing regulatory drivers from downstream responses. Although single-cell profiles are typically measured as static snapshots rather than true time-series observations, pseudotime-ordering15,16 and RNA-velocity17,18 methods provide temporal ordering and transition directionality, offering a basis for modeling dynamic regulatory relationships along cellular transitions (Supplementary Note 1). These approaches, including widely used frameworks such as Monocle19, Slingshot20, Palantir21, and scVelo22, have been systematically reviewed and benchmarked in previous studies. More recently, CellRank223,24 has enabled unified fate mapping in multiview single-cell data by integrating diverse transition information to infer fate probabilities and lineage relationships in complex cellular landscapes. Building on these representations, pseudotime-informed GRN inference methods model regulatory relationships along cellular transitions, often using time-lagged or autoregressive strategies25–28. Among these, classical Granger causality and its nonlinear or deep-learning-based extensions infer directed regulatory relationships by evaluating whether earlier states of candidate regulators improve the prediction of their putative targets29,30. However, these models often assume a reliable ordering and approximately uniform spacing of observations along pseudotime. In real single-cell systems, these assumptions can be challenged by sparse sampling, uneven cell-state densities, branching or convergent trajectories, and feedback-driven or cyclic regulatory interactions26,28,31,32. Recent frameworks, such as Velorama33 and RegVelo34, have begun to address parts of this challenge by integrating dynamic modeling with regulatory information, highlighting the need to bridge cell-state dynamics and gene-regulatory mechanisms.

  Second, complementary molecular modalities, such as chromatin accessibility (e.g., DNase-seq35, ATAC-seq36) or TF binding profiles (e.g., ChIP–seq37), have been integrated to provide mechanistic evidence for regulatory interactions. These allow the identification of regulatory elements (REs), which can be linked to TFs via motif matching and associated with nearby TGs, forming TF–RE–TG connections38. While these strategies offer mechanistic insights, they often rely on separate measurements from different samples or cell populations, limiting their resolution and ability to resolve dynamic regulatory relationships.

  The development of single-cell sequencing has begun to overcome these challenges by profiling the transcriptome and epigenome at cellular resolution, thereby enabling the investigation of causal and temporal relationships among critical regulatory events39–41. Pioneering methods like SCENIC42 reconstructed cell-type-specific GRNs by integrating scRNA-seq data with population-level chromatin information. Subsequent approaches, including Pando43, FigR44, and SCENIC+45, introduced various optimizations by leveraging single-cell chromatin accessibility data in more intricate ways to better define regulatory connections. Furthermore, LINGER46 employs a lifelong learning framework to build highly accurate GRNs by integrating knowledge from atlas-scale external bulk data across diverse cellular contexts. Beyond improving GRN inference accuracy, a new generation of methods has introduced powerful downstream applications to provide more biologically meaningful insights. For example, CellOracle47 utilizes the inferred GRNs to simulate the effects of TF perturbations and predict their function in driving cell state transitions, while Dictys48 moves beyond a static view by modeling how the GRNs dynamically rewire across a continuous cellular process.

  Despite these significant advances, several key limitations remain41. First, for inference accuracy, most methods do not simultaneously leverage multimodal and pseudotemporal information, which are critical for identifying causal regulatory interactions. Second, for downstream application, current approaches tend to focus on individual regulator activity in discrete cell states, overlooking the continuous dynamics of cellular transitions and the combinatorial logic5 of regulatory modules central to complex biological processes.

  Here, we present scMagnify (Multi scAle Gene regulatory Network InFerence and analYsis), an integrated computational framework designed to address these challenges. To improve inference accuracy, scMagnify synergistically integrates single-cell multi-omic data and pseudotemporal information through a Granger causality model49,50 to infer multi-scale GRNs. For downstream analysis, it enables a multi-level investigation of gene regulation, moving from individual regulators to the identification of combinatorial regulatory modules via tensor decomposition51, and further linking these intracellular dynamics to extracellular cues by modeling signaling-to-transcription cascades. We demonstrate the power and versatility of scMagnify in both developmental and disease contexts, where it rediscovers known master regulators and characterizes multi-scale regulatory programs driving cell state transitions.


## Results

### Overview of scMagnify
  scMagnify is a versatile Python toolkit for inferring and analyzing gene regulatory networks (GRNs) from single-cell multi-omic datasets. To ensure a targeted and robust GRN inference, scMagnify first identifies a set of high-confidence genes intrinsically linked to the specified cellular dynamics. It achieves this by establishing cellular trajectories and using generalized additive models (GAMs)52 to select genes whose expression significantly correlates with the pseudotime axis. To further constrain the model with plausible chromatin interactions, scMagnify then constructs a basal TF binding network for each cell state. This structural scaffold is built by linking genes to potential cis-regulatory elements (cCREs) via expression-accessibility correlation and scanning these regions for known TF binding motifs53. Together, these steps yield a focused, biologically relevant search space for the final GRN inference (Fig. 1a and “Methods”).Fig. 1Overview of the scMagnify framework.a Data preprocessing. scMagnify integrates single-cell transcriptomic and chromatin accessibility data.   Within this focused search space from the previous step, scMagnify performs GRN inference using a partial ordering-based33,54 Granger causality framework. This framework is implemented via a self-explaining neural network featuring a multi-scale architecture. More specifically, we consider these lagged TF expression values as predictors for the regulation coefficient matrices in a feedforward neural network. To capture regulatory effects across different time scales, the model uses parallel branches, each examining cellular dynamics over a different retrospective time lag. Finally, an attention mechanism automatically learns to weight the information from each branch, integrating these multi-scale signals to construct the final GRN (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 1, “Methods”).

  scMagnify includes a suite of functions to analyse context-specific GRNs. To isolate the most robust interactions, the network is filtered based on user-defined statistical thresholds (“Methods”). The resulting GRN then serves as a basis for functional and structural analysis. A primary application is the inference of regulatory activity, where the activity of each TF is quantified from the collective expression of its TGs (its regulon) using a multivariate linear model55 (Fig. 1c and “Methods”). Beyond this functional insight, the network structure can be investigated through standard graph-theoretic metrics to identify pivotal hub regulators within the cellular circuitry (Fig. 1e).

  It is well-established that TFs often act in a combinatorial manner to regulate their target genes56,57. To systematically explore these regulatory synergies within dynamic processes, scMagnify employs a modified Tucker decomposition approach. This regfactor decomposition method is applied to a tensor comprising the regulatory coefficients (Reg. scores) derived from the multi-scale Granger causality model. This approach enables the simultaneous identification of co-regulating TF modules and their shared sets of TGs across different time lags (Fig. 1d, e, and Supplementary Fig. 1b, “Methods”).

  Furthermore, scMagnify links intercellular communication to intracellular regulation by dissecting signaling-to-transcription cascades along the pseudotime axis. To achieve this, it first identifies key pathways consisting of ligand-receptor pairs and their downstream TFs from prior knowledge58–60. It then correlates each receptor’s expression with its target TF’s expression across pseudotime and validates these interactions with a permutation test61. This analysis reveals robust signaling axes that are active during specific cellular transitions (Fig. 1e and “Methods”).

  Collectively, scMagnify dissects the multi-level architecture of gene regulation—from the activity of individual regulators, to the combinatorial logic of regulatory modules, and finally to the systemic coordination via intercellular communication—enabling mechanistic insights into the regulation of cellular dynamics.


### Comprehensive benchmarks support the accuracy and robustness of scMagnify
  To provide a comprehensive evaluation, we benchmarked scMagnify against a set of leading GRN inference methods (Supplementary Data 1). The selected methods represent a variety of modeling strategies and differ in their core capabilities. For example, a major class of methods, including SCENIC+, LINGER, Dictys, CellOracle, and FigR, excel at integrating multi-omic data to infer GRNs, but they do so in a static manner without explicitly modeling temporal dynamics. Conversely, another class of methods, such as Velorama33 and SINCERITIES62, can model cellular dynamics using pseudotime information, but they are limited to single-cell transcriptomic data. This benchmark design allows us to specifically highlight the advantages of scMagnify’s integrated framework, which combines multi-modal data with pseudotemporal information to reconstruct GRNs. We also reported the results from scMagnify without the TF binding network (scMagnify-nobasal) to isolate the contribution of the chromatin prior and to enable a fair comparison with methods that rely solely on transcriptomic data (Supplementary Fig. 3a).

  Our evaluation was performed on both synthetic datasets with known ground-truth network structures and well-characterized real-world multi-omic datasets.   First, we applied scMultiSim63 to generate synthetic single-cell multiomics datasets with different parameters (Supplementary Fig. 2a). We used the area under the precision-recall curve (AUPR) and the F1-score to assess the accuracy of ground-truth network reconstruction, as both metrics are well-suited for the sparse nature of GRNs. scMagnify achieved significantly higher AUPR and F1 score than CellOracle in the synthetic benchmark, based on one-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests across simulation replicates (adjusted P = 0.000122 for AUPR and 0.003357 for F1; Fig. 2b and “Methods”). We further expanded the synthetic benchmark by varying cell number, GRN size, GRN density, transcript dropout, and ATAC-derived chromatin-prior noise; scMagnify maintained higher AUPR across most perturbation settings, whereas F1 scores were broadly comparable between the two methods under some conditions (Supplementary Fig. 3c, d).

  Next, to evaluate scMagnify’s real-world performance, we applied our cell state transition module to uniformly processed single-cell multi-omic datasets spanning hematopoietic and pancreas endocrine differentiation trajectories (Supplementary Fig. 2b–g and “Methods”). Each of these lineages represents a distinct dynamic process and serves as an independent test set for GRN inference. We then curated a corresponding gold-standard set of TF–TG interactions from a comprehensive ChIP-seq database to serve as ground truth. Specifically, we collected ChIP-seq experiments from the Cistrome database64 to assess whether each method could successfully recover these experimentally supported TF–TG interactions, with performance quantified by the AUPR and F1-score (“Methods”). Across the three real-data benchmarks, scMagnify generally achieved among the highest AUPR values, although performance differences varied across datasets, metrics, and competing methods. Compared with the best-performing competitor, scMagnify achieved significantly higher AUPR (adjusted P < 0.01), whereas the difference in F1 score was not significant and remained dataset-dependent and broadly comparable among the top-performing methods (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). These results support the competitive edge-recovery performance of scMagnify across diverse real-data benchmarks. In conclusion, our systematic set of benchmarks on real-world data demonstrates the comparable or superior performance of scMagnify, highlighting its reliability and high accuracy across a range of dynamic cellular processes.

  While edge-level accuracy is a fundamental metric for GRN inference, a more stringent test of any inferred GRN is whether its network topology can be used to identify biologically important regulators24,65. We therefore performed a driver TF recovery benchmark to assess if scMagnify could prioritize known lineage-driving TFs (Supplementary Data 2) more effectively than competing methods. For each inferred network, we ranked TFs by their degree centrality and measured how well this ranking recovered a curated gold-standard list of drivers for each lineage. scMagnify achieved the highest aggregate driver-recovery score among the tested methods (adjusted P < 0.01), with individual lineage-level recovery curves provided in the Supplementary Figs. (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 5a, b). We further compared the rank correlation between inferred and reference degree centrality values. scMagnify showed consistently positive Spearman correlations across most lineages and ranked among the top-performing methods, indicating that its inferred networks better preserve regulator-level topological structure (Fig. 2e). This suggests that the topology of the GRN from scMagnify is not only accurate but also structurally meaningful, successfully identifying key regulators based on their network connectivity.

  A critical test for any GRN inference method is its ability to generate networks that are both reproducible and context-specific48. To assess scMagnify against this standard, we compared GRNs generated from 10 independent runs with different random seeds across representative lineages in the T-cell-depleted bone marrow dataset. The resulting Jaccard similarity heatmap revealed a clear block-diagonal structure, a pattern arising from high intra-lineage similarity (reproducibility) and low inter-lineage similarity (specificity) (Fig. 2f). Using the CD34+ bone marrow dataset, scMagnify maintained stable AUPR and F1 scores under moderate cell subsampling down to 40% retained cells and under increasing synthetic transcript dropout, with lineage-specific trends shown alongside the median response (Fig. 2g, h). These results validate that scMagnify can robustly infer GRNs that are both consistent within a given biological context and unique to it.

  Finally, we evaluated the contribution and robustness of scMagnify’s model design. A forward K-scan showed that performance improved as additional pseudotime-lag branches were introduced and plateaued around K = 5, supporting its use as the default setting (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Conversely, reverse ablation of lag-specific graph-diffused TF inputs or coefficient-generating branches reduced AUPR and F1 score, indicating that multiple lag branches contribute to GRN reconstruction (Supplementary Fig. 6b–d). Sensitivity analyses using alternative trajectory inputs and transition-matrix perturbations further showed broadly stable GRN performance and network similarity under reasonable trajectory variations (Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8). Scalability profiling showed that runtime increased approximately linearly with cell number, gene number, and lag depth, with manageable CPU and GPU memory requirements across the tested settings (Supplementary Fig. 9a–c).

  Together, these benchmarks show that scMagnify achieves competitive GRN reconstruction performance, prioritizes lineage-associated regulators, and produces reproducible, context-specific networks across synthetic and real single-cell multiome datasets.


### scMagnify dissects lineage-specific key regulators in human hematopoiesis
  To demonstrate the utility of scMagnify in developmental contexts, we obtained a human blood dataset containing single-cell multi-omic data of CD34+ T-cell-depleted bone marrow cells from healthy donors66, a paradigmatic system where tightly regulated transcriptional programs drive the differentiation of hematopoietic stem cells into a complex hierarchy of distinct lineages67. We first classified the data into three lineages and selected 3281 genes that were significantly associated with pseudotime (FDR < 0.0001, A > 0.3) (Supplementary Fig. 2b–d and “Methods”). As shown in Fig. 3a, HSCs follow three main developmental trajectories—the erythroid, monocyte, and Naïve B cell lineages—which are defined by a progression through distinct intermediate cell states.   By comparing GRNs across different lineages (“Methods”), scMagnify identified major lineage-defining TFs with highly specific target sets, including EBF1, PAX5, IRF4 and SPIB for the B cell lineage68–71; GATA2 and KLF1 for the erythroid lineage72,73; and IRF8, EGR1 and KLF4 for the monocyte lineage74 (Fig. 3b, c, and Supplementary Fig. 10a, b). In contrast, the analysis also identified TFs with broader, non-lineage-specific regulatory roles. For instance, ERG75 and BACH276 regulate a large number of targets that are distributed across two or more lineages, indicating their function as more general regulators in hematopoiesis (Fig. 3c).

  To better understand how transcriptional programs are orchestrated in different lineages, we next analysed the transcriptional and regulatory dynamics along trajectories. This analysis revealed distinct waves of TF expression that define each lineage, with key regulatory modules being activated in a highly specific temporal order (Fig. 3d). Specifically, the erythroid trajectory is characterized by the strong early expression of a module including GATA2 and KLF1, the monocytic (Mono) trajectory is driven by the subsequent induction of pioneer factors, such as CEBPD and CEBPB, while the Naive B-cell lineage is established through the robust co-activation of key B-cell determinants like EBF1, PAX5, and BCL11A77 (Supplementary Data 2).

  Our analysis also highlights the temporal decoupling between the expression of a transcription factor and its regulatory activity45,78 (Fig. 3d and “Methods”). To avoid relying on visual comparison of expression and activity curves, we quantified lagged regulation using three complementary summaries: the center-of-mass shift between TF RNA expression and inferred TF activity, the model-inferred regulatory lag, and the dominant lag usage of TF–TG edges (Fig. 3e, Supplementary Fig. 11, and Supplementary Note 2). By inferring GRNs that operate across multiple time-lag scales, scMagnify provides a framework to quantify the regulatory lag of individual transcription factors. We interpret this regulatory lag as a relative, model-derived local pseudotime-lag index, rather than an absolute molecular response time (“Methods”).

  Consistent with previous studies, we next examined whether the model-derived lag index was associated with network topology and functional annotation33,79. The model-derived lag index showed positive associations with degree centrality across lineages, although these associations were attenuated after controlling for regulon size and target-gene number. (Supplementary Fig. 10c–e). After controlling for target number, regulon-size downsampling, and lag-shuffling controls, the association was attenuated but remained positive in most lineages (Supplementary Fig. 10f). Functionally, these long-lag TFs, which include the B-cell determinants EBF1, PAX5, and TCF3, are highly enriched for foundational processes like “pioneer-like properties80”, “B cell differentiation,” and “Cell fate commitment.” In contrast, TFs with shorter lags are associated with more immediate cellular responses, such as “B cell receptor signaling” and “BCR signaling events” (Fig. 3f).

  In summary, scMagnify systematically dissects the key regulators and their dynamic regulation across different hematopoietic lineages. These analyses suggest that scMagnify can nominate lineage-associated regulators and summarize their relative pseudotime-lag patterns, while the inferred lag should be interpreted as a model-derived regulatory feature rather than direct evidence of absolute biological timing. Building on this principle of temporal regulation, we next investigated the combinatorial regulatory logic governing B cell commitment.


### scMagnify recovers regulatory logic associated with B cell fate commitment
  Deciphering the regulatory logic that drives B cell commitment is challenging, as the process relies on temporally orchestrated combinatorial inputs rather than a simple linear TF cascade81–83. To systematically explore these dynamic regulatory patterns, we therefore applied scMagnify’s regfactor decomposition, hypothesizing that distinct co-regulating TF modules controlling distinct cellular programs can be deconvolved from our multi-scale GRNs.

  By decomposing the multi-scale GRNs, we identified several RegFactors. Analysis revealed that each of these RegFactors is regulated by a different combination of TFs, activated at a different developmental stage, and is associated with distinct biological functions.

  Specifically, our analysis identified 5 RegFactors that are associated with sequential stages of B-cell lineage progression (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 12a–c). RegFactor 1, predominantly active in the earliest progenitor states (HSC/HMP), includes key TFs, such as MECOM, RORA, and PBX1, suggesting its role in stem cell maintenance, proliferation, and alternative lineage priming84–86. From this stage, progenitors in HMP/CLP initiate lineage priming through the activation of RegFactor 2, suggesting a transitional regulatory program. This is followed by RegFactor 3, which is highly enriched in cell-cycle regulators87,88 like E2F3 and TFDP1 and drives the robust proliferation of these primed progenitors, preparing them for subsequent lineage commitment. In contrast, cells that commit to the B-lineage downregulate these programs and instead activate RegFactor 4 during the pro-B and pre-B stages. Governed by B-cell regulators IRF4, PAX5, TCF3, and EBF1, these factors are central to establishing B-cell identity89.   To understand the molecular programs underlying B cell commitment, we focused our investigation on RegFactor 4. Consistent with the overall activity of RegFactor 4, its key TFs showed tightly correlated expression and activity patterns peaking in the pro-B and pre-B cell stages (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 12d). Several high-loading TF pairs exhibited AND-like/synergy-like activity patterns, with the double-high state showing the strongest RegFactor activity (Supplementary Fig. 15a, b). Together, these core TFs orchestrate a broad downstream program of TGs critical for B-cell function, including components of immunoglobulin chains (IGHM, IGKC), B-cell receptor signaling (BLK, LYN)91, and chromatin modification (CHD7, HDAC9)92 (Fig. 4e).

  To examine chromatin-level support for this RegFactor-associated program, we focused on the PAX5 locus, a canonical B-cell identity gene. Highly correlated peaks linked to PAX5 showed increasing accessibility along B-cell differentiation and were associated with PAX5 expression. These linked regions contained motif occurrences for RegFactor 4 TFs, including IRF4, EBF1, TCF3, and SOX4, providing chromatin-level support consistent with a coordinated regulatory program around PAX5 (Fig. 4f).

  Based on this dataset, scMagnify inferred dynamic, multi-scale GRNs that captured regulatory changes along endocrine differentiation trajectories.   To systematically identify the regulatory modules, we employed the RegFactor decomposition. The GRNs were decomposed into 5 distinct RegFactors for each lineage, revealing 3 classes of TFs: alpha-specific, beta-specific, and a larger group of conserved regulators active in both lineages (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Data 4, 5).

  For the alpha cell lineage, Arx emerged as the key hub regulator, supported by Meis2 and Etv1, consistent with its established role in defining alpha-cell identity and maturation93–95. Conversely, Mafa and Mafb were identified as key beta-specific TFs, consistent with their known roles in beta-cell function and maturation96. Alongside these lineage-specifiers, a conserved module of TFs—including Sox4, Neurog3, and Rfx6—exhibited high centrality in both lineages, representing a core endocrine regulatory program97–100.

  We next sought to characterize the temporal dynamics of these RegFactors. By projecting their activities along the pseudotime axis, we found that each factor displayed a distinct activity profile along both the alpha and beta cell lineages (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 13a, b). This revealed that the lineage-specific RegFactor 5 for each fate exhibited the most striking stage-specific pattern. In both lineages, the activity of their respective RegFactor 5 surged dramatically only at the final maturation stage. To identify the specific TFs driving these terminal programs, we examined the TF loadings for each lineage’s RegFactor 5 (Fig. 5d). The analysis confirmed that they are composed of largely distinct TF cohorts: the alpha-specific module is overwhelmingly dominated by Arx101, while the beta-specific module is principally led by Mafa102. Consistently, TF-pair quadrant analysis identified AND-like/synergy-like activity patterns for Isl1–Mafb and Arx–Mafb within the alpha-lineage RegFactor 5 (Supplementary Fig. 15c, d, and Supplementary Data 8). These results nominate lineage-associated TFs that may contribute to late maturation programs.

  To gain a mechanistic view of the alpha-specific RegFactor 5 program, we visualized the Alpha-RegFactor 5 regulatory network (Fig. 5e). This revealed a sophisticated architecture organized around a core of co-regulating TFs, with Arx acting as a central hub in the inferred network. Its dense connections to other module members, such as Pax4 and Sox4, highlight the coordinated nature of this regulatory program.

  Most critically, the network further linked Alpha-RegFactor 5 to alpha-cell functional genes. We identified a regulatory connection from Arx to Gcg (Glucagon), the definitive hormone produced by alpha cells (Fig. 5g). Underscoring this critical link, an analysis of TG loadings showed that Gcg is the highest-weighted gene in this module (Fig. 5f). Beyond direct hormone synthesis, the module coordinates the broader functional machinery required for its activity. For example, it targets the prohormone convertase Pcsk2, essential for processing proglucagon, and other genes involved in secretion modulation like Ffar1103. Epigenetic analysis confirms this link, showing Gcg expression correlates with accessibility at enhancer regions containing motif occurrences for key module TFs, including Arx, Pax4, and Sox4 (Fig. 5h).

  To validate scMagnify’s capability to infer signaling pairs driving pathological cell state transitions in a complex disease setting, we analyzed a single-cell multi-omic dataset from the cortex of patients with acute and/or chronic kidney disease (AKI/CKD)105 (Fig. 6b). While this dataset comprises diverse kidney cell types, our analysis focuses on the epithelial populations to investigate their adaptive responses to injury. The dataset captures the transition of thick ascending limb (TAL) epithelial cells from a healthy, cortical state (C-TAL) to a dysregulated adaptive state (aTAL). Trajectory inference using a diffusion map resolved a continuous progression from healthy (C-TAL/M-TAL) to adaptive TAL cell states (aTAL), establishing a pseudotemporal axis to dissect the dynamics of the injury response (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. 14b).

  Building on the inferred trajectory, we constructed the GRNs for the TAL, allowing us to identify key regulators (Supplementary Fig. 14a), and then decomposed the network into 5 RegFactors (Fig. 6d, Supplementary Fig. 14d–f, and Supplementary Data 6).   To identify the key upstream signaling axes, we prioritized ligand-receptor pairs based on the correlation strength between the receptor’s expression and the expression of the core RegFactor 3 TFs (Fig. 6f, g). These findings illustrate how scMagnify can deconvolve the complex signaling landscape into both broad-acting and cell-type-specific pathological pathways.


## Discussion
  In this manuscript, we present scMagnify, an integrated computational framework designed to reconstruct, decompose, and analyze dynamic, multi-scale gene regulatory networks (GRNs) from single-cell multi-omic datasets. To achieve this, scMagnify synergistically integrates single-cell multi-omic data with pseudotemporal information through a Granger causality model implemented within an interpretable multi-scale neural network. The inference is further constrained by chromatin accessibility-derived priors to enhance accuracy. A key innovation of our framework is its ability to reconstruct the GRN as a multi-scale tensor, explicitly capturing time-lagged regulatory interactions along a dynamic process. This unique structure is pivotal, as it enables subsequent analyses, such as tensor decomposition to identify dynamic regulatory modules and the linking of these modules to upstream signaling-to-transcription cascades.

  Our comprehensive benchmarks on both synthetic and real-world datasets demonstrate that scMagnify achieves competitive or favorable performance across network reconstruction and driver-TF recovery benchmarks and the critical task of identifying driver TFs. Beyond benchmarks, the application of scMagnify to complex biological systems nominated biologically interpretable regulatory programs and testable hypotheses. In developmental contexts, our analyses of human hematopoiesis and mouse pancreas development revealed relative offsets between TF RNA expression and inferred regulatory activity, and nominated lineage- and maturation-associated RegFactor programs linked to cell-state progression. In a disease context, scMagnify successfully mapped the signaling cascades from the microenvironment that were associated with pathological cell-state transitions in kidney injury. These findings, which are often missed by conventional analyses, underscore the power of a multi-scale approach.

  While scMagnify provides a flexible framework for dynamic GRN inference and downstream regulatory analysis, several limitations should be noted. First, scMagnify depends on the quality of the input multi-omic data and on the accuracy of the upstream pseudotime or transition-matrix estimation. Although our sensitivity analyses showed that the inferred networks were broadly stable across reasonable trajectory choices and perturbations, substantial loss of cell-state coverage or inaccurate trajectory ordering may affect GRN reconstruction. Second, the chromatin-derived basal GRN currently relies on peak-to-gene correlations and motif matching, which provide useful regulatory priors but do not directly establish functional enhancer–gene links or TF binding events. Future versions could incorporate improved peak-to-gene linking strategies111,112, TF footprinting79, perturbation-based priors113, or context-specific chromatin interaction data114 to refine these priors.

  Another important limitation is that scMagnify uses a predictive, Granger-style regulatory model rather than a fully mechanistic biochemical model. Therefore, the inferred regulatory relationships should be interpreted as computationally prioritized TF–TG associations, not as direct evidence of causal regulation. Similarly, the inferred regulatory lag represents a relative, model-derived local pseudotime-lag index along the cell-state transition graph, rather than an absolute chronological delay or molecular response time. Although intermediate regulatory coefficients can take positive or negative values, the final reported GRNs are treated as unsigned TF–TG association networks, and scMagnify does not currently assign activating or inhibitory labels to final regulatory edges. Finally, because the current formulation relies on a directed transition structure along pseudotime, it may not fully capture complex feedback loops or cyclic regulatory dynamics28 (Supplementary Fig. 17). Addressing these limitations, together with extending scMagnify to additional modalities, such as single-cell proteomics115, spatial transcriptomics116, or perturbation screens117, represents an important direction for future development.

  The advent of single-cell multi-omics is providing an unprecedented opportunity to understand complex biological processes with high resolution. To translate this rich data into a mechanistic understanding, it is essential to move beyond static, single-scale views of gene regulation. Frameworks like scMagnify, which embrace the dynamic, multi-scale, and combinatorial nature of regulatory networks, are critical for this endeavor. By providing a tool to dissect these complex systems, scMagnify can help generate novel, testable hypotheses and will be a valuable resource for advancing our understanding of development and disease. Ultimately, integrating such dynamic regulatory maps will be an essential step toward realizing the goals of large-scale initiatives aiming to build “virtual cells,” which seek to create predictive, multi-scale computational models of cellular function and behavior118,119.


## Methods

### Overview of scMagnify
  scMagnify is a computational framework to infer gene regulatory networks (GRNs) and explore dynamic regulation synergy from single-cell multiomics data. The construction of GRNs is predominantly achieved through multivariable Granger causal inference, using an interpretable multi-scale neural network. Moreover, scMagnify integrates prior knowledge of chromatin conformation by incorporating a domain-specific constraint into the model structure.

  The scMagnify workflow consists of several steps: (1) multi-omic data preprocessing; (2) multi-scale gene regulatory network inference; and (3) downstream regulation analysis.

  We implemented and tested scMagnify in Python and designed it for use in the Jupyter notebook environment. scMagnify code is open source and available on GitHub (https://github.com/LiHongCSBLab/scMagnify) along with detailed descriptions of functions and tutorials.


### Multi-omic data preprocessing

#### Cell state transition analysis
  Cell state transition can be modeled as a multi-scale process. Existing approaches, such as CellRank, combine similarity-based Markov models with multi-view single-cell data for cellular fate mapping.

##### Cell state selection
  Cells are then assigned to specific fates or lineages through clustering based on their fate probabilities. This step groups cells that are likely to follow similar developmental paths, allowing for the identification of distinct lineages.


##### Feature association test
  Feature association is tested using GAMs, which model gene expression as a function of pseudotime tt with cubic spline regression. This identifies key genes associated with cell state transitions along pseudotime.


#### TF binding network construction
  For each cell state, scMagnify constructs a TF binding network that contains unweighted, directional edges between a TF and its TG. scMagnify uses the peak-gene correlations and TF-binding motifs for this task.


##### Identification of cCREs via peak-gene correlations
  To identify candidate cis-regulatory elements (CREs), scMagnify first aggregates the single-cell multiomics data into metacells using SEACells66. For each gene, the method then computes the Pearson correlation between its normalized metacell expression and the normalized accessibility of each ATAC peak located within 100 kb upstream and 100 kb downstream. The significance of each peak-gene correlation is assessed against an empirical null distribution, which is generated by sampling 100 background peaks matched for GC content and accessibility120. This analysis identifies the subset of regions significantly correlated with gene expression, retaining pairs that meet the criteria of a correlation coefficient >0.1 and an empirical P < 0.1.

### Datasets

#### Simulated data
  To benchmark the performance of existing GRN inference methods, we used scMultiSim to generate simulated single-cell multiomics datasets with the following configurations: 1139-gene GRN, 1000 cells, 50 CIFs, rd = 0.2, σi = 1, and other default parameters. ### Evaluations
  To evaluate whether scMagnify can correctly identify cell-state-specific GRNs, we benchmarked our method against diverse GRN inference algorithms: SCENIC+, LINGER, Dictys, CellOracle, FigR, Pando, SCENIC, Velorama, and SINCERITIES.


#### Preparation of input data for GRN inference
  For each of the three real-data datasets, we first performed a feature-association test to identify significant genes (Bonferroni-adjusted P < 0.001). Next, we subsetted the cells into 13 lineages (Ery/Mono/NaiveB for the T-cell-depleted Bone Marrow dataset; pDC/cDC/Mono/Ery/CLP/Mega for the CD34+ Bone Marrow dataset; Alpha/Beta/Delta/Epsilon for the Pancreas Endocrine dataset).


#### GRN inference methods
  After preprocessing, the same lineage-restricted datasets were used as input for each GRN inference method, following the official tutorials and default hyperparameters unless otherwise specified. For the real-data benchmarks, SCENIC+ was run using lineage-specific consensus open-chromatin peaks as candidate regulatory regions, because its standard pycisTopic topic- and DAR-based feature selection is primarily designed for comparisons across predefined cell groups and was not directly applicable to our continuous lineage setting. Except for CellOracle, SCENIC+ and the other multiome-based GRN inference methods were not included in the scMultiSim benchmark because the simulated regulatory regions lack genomic coordinates and DNA sequences, precluding the motif enrichment, transcription-factor binding-site annotation, and genome-based enhancer-to-gene mapping required by these methods.


#### Ground-truth data preparation
  Cell-type-specific ground-truth GRNs were generated in the same manner as in a previous benchmarking study33. We obtained tissue-specific ChIP–seq peak data from the Cistrome database64. For each experiment (by GSMID), the top 100 peaks (by peak score) were regarded as positive hits and annotated to the nearest gene. The TF binding gold standard was defined as TF–target pairs that were positive hits in at least one of the experiments.
