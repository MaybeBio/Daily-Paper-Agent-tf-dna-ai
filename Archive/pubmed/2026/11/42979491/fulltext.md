# Integrated Transcriptomic Analysis and Experimental Validation Identify ATF3 as a Mesangial Cell-Associated Candidate Regulator in IgA Nephropathy


## Background
  IgA nephropathy (IgAN) is an immune-mediated glomerular disease marked by mesangial proliferation, inflammation, extracellular matrix accumulation, and progressive renal injury. This study aimed to identify IgAN-associated genes and evaluate the relevance of activating transcription factor 3 (ATF3) to mesangial cell activation.


## Methods
  Public bulk transcriptomic datasets were integrated for differential expression, co-expression network, machine learning, and immune infiltration analyses. Single-cell RNA sequencing, mesangial cell-specific high-dimensional weighted gene co-expression network analysis, and single-cell regulatory network inference were used to assess cell type-specific expression and regulatory activity. The findings were further evaluated using Nephroseq and an aggregated IgA1-stimulated human mesangial cell model with ATF3 knockdown.


## Results
  Integrated analysis identified 47 shared differentially expressed genes and a 12-gene candidate set with reduced expression in IgAN. ATF3 was prioritized as a mesangial cell-associated candidate and showed reduced expression and regulon activity in the analyzed single-cell dataset. Nephroseq analysis showed lower glomerular ATF3 expression in IgAN-related kidney disease samples; ATF3 expression was positively associated with estimated glomerular filtration rate and negatively associated with serum creatinine and proteinuria. Aggregated IgA1 reduced ATF3 expression in human mesangial cells, whereas ATF3 knockdown further increased inflammatory cytokine expression and proliferative activity.


## Conclusion
  Reduced ATF3 expression is associated with IgAN-related mesangial cell activation. ATF3 may represent a mesangial cell-associated candidate regulator that warrants further mechanistic and clinical validation.


## Introduction
  Immunoglobulin A nephropathy (IgAN) is the most common primary glomerulonephritis worldwide and a leading cause of end-stage renal disease (ESRD).1,2 The disease is characterized by glomerular deposition of IgA-containing immune complexes, followed by mesangial cell activation, inflammatory responses, extracellular matrix accumulation, and progressive renal injury.3 Although the multi-hit hypothesis has provided an important framework for understanding IgAN pathogenesis,4 the molecular mechanisms that link immune complex deposition to sustained mesangial injury and disease progression remain incompletely defined.

  Among the intrinsic renal cell populations involved in IgAN, mesangial cells are considered central effectors of disease progression.5 Following the deposition of IgA-containing immune complexes in the mesangial area, mesangial cells undergo abnormal activation characterized by increased proliferation, inflammatory mediator release, and extracellular matrix accumulation, thereby contributing to glomerular injury and progressive renal dysfunction.6,7 Therefore, identifying key molecular regulators associated with mesangial cell activation may help clarify IgAN pathogenesis and provide candidate molecular markers and mechanistic clues for IgAN.

  High-throughput transcriptomic analysis has become an important approach for identifying disease-associated genes and pathways in kidney diseases. Bulk transcriptomic datasets can reveal global molecular alterations in renal tissues, whereas single-cell RNA sequencing provides additional resolution for determining cell type-specific expression patterns and regulatory programs.8,9 However, renal biopsy specimens contain multiple intrinsic renal and infiltrating immune cell populations, and bulk-level signals may obscure cell-type-specific transcriptional changes. Integrating bulk transcriptomics with single-cell analysis may therefore improve the identification of robust candidate genes and help define their cellular relevance in IgAN.

  Activating transcription factor 3 (ATF3) is an immediate-early stress-responsive transcription factor implicated in inflammation, tissue injury, and cellular adaptive responses.10–13 Previous studies have shown that ATF3 can exert context-dependent regulatory effects in multiple models of renal injury.10–13 In lupus nephritis, ATF3 has also been linked to changes in the glomerular immune microenvironment, suggesting that its role may vary across different immune-mediated kidney diseases.14 Direct experimental evidence concerning ATF3 in mesangial cell biology remains limited. In a complement-mediated model of mesangioproliferative glomerulonephritis, ATF3 upregulation promoted sublytic C5b-9-induced glomerular mesangial cell apoptosis.15 More recently, transcriptomic and RNA-interactome analyses in human renal mesangial cells identified ATF3 among the genes regulated by TFRC, an IgA1 receptor implicated in IgAN.16 Recent studies in other disease contexts have further linked ATF3 to macrophage metabolic reprogramming17 and cellular senescence,18 whereas a recent renal fibrosis study implicated ATF3 in EGR2-mediated autophagy.19 These findings further illustrate the highly context-dependent functions of ATF3. Previous transcriptomic and machine-learning studies identified ATF3 as a downregulated candidate biomarker or hub gene,20,21 while glomerular spatial transcriptomic profiling showed consistent downregulation of ATF3 in IgAN glomeruli.22 However, these studies were primarily computational or expression-based, and the mesangial cell-specific regulatory activity and functional relevance of ATF3 in IgAN remain insufficiently understood.

  In this study, we integrated multiple public bulk transcriptomic datasets with single-cell RNA sequencing data to identify potential IgAN-associated molecular regulators. Differential expression analysis, WGCNA, machine learning-based feature prioritization, SHAP-based model interpretation, immune infiltration analysis, and transcriptional regulatory analysis were used to screen and characterize candidate genes. External validation and aggregated IgA1-stimulated human mesangial cell experiments were further performed to evaluate ATF3 expression and its functional relevance. This study identified ATF3 as a candidate regulator with reduced expression in IgAN-related conditions and explored its association with mesangial cell inflammatory activation and proliferation. ## Materials and Methods

### Data Collection and Preprocessing
  IgAN-related transcriptomic datasets were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Four bulk transcriptomic datasets, including GSE115857, GSE35487, GSE93798, and GSE37460, were included for integrated analysis, and the single-cell RNA sequencing dataset GSE171314 was used for cell-level analysis.   Probe identifiers were converted to gene symbols according to the corresponding platform annotation files, and the mean expression value was used when multiple probes mapped to the same gene. The bulk datasets were merged after preprocessing, and batch effects were corrected using the ComBat algorithm in the sva package.23 Principal component analysis was used to assess the effect of batch correction.


### Differential Expression and Functional Enrichment Analysis
  Differential expression analysis between IgAN and control samples was performed using the limma package.24 Genes with |log2FC| > 1 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Volcano plots and heatmaps were generated to visualize DEG expression patterns. Functional enrichment analysis was performed using the clusterProfiler package25 based on Gene Ontology Biological Process, KEGG, Reactome, Hallmark, and WikiPathways gene sets. Adjusted P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.


### WGCNA and Machine Learning Analysis
  Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed using the WGCNA package26 to identify disease-associated gene modules. The soft-thresholding power was selected based on the scale-free topology fit index and network connectivity. β = 3 was chosen because it was the lowest power at which the scale-free topology fit index reached the selection threshold of 0.80, while the network retained adequate mean connectivity. Module eigengenes were subsequently correlated with IgAN status to identify disease-associated modules. Functional enrichment analysis was performed for genes in IgAN-associated modules.

  Machine learning analysis was used to prioritize IgAN-associated transcriptomic features. GSE93798 was used as the model development dataset and was randomly divided into training and internal validation sets at a 7:3 ratio. All preprocessing and feature-construction procedures were fitted exclusively using the training subset. Specifically, the scaling parameters and correlation-based gene-cluster definitions were derived from the training data, and principal component analysis was fitted within the resulting training-derived gene clusters. The fitted scaling parameters, gene-cluster definitions, and principal component loadings were subsequently applied, without refitting, to the internal validation subset and the three external validation datasets, GSE37460, GSE115857, and GSE35487. Gene clusters were converted into representative transcriptomic features using principal component analysis. Gaussian naïve Bayes, support vector machine, k-nearest neighbors, random forest, and XGBoost models were evaluated. Model performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC). Because the external datasets were characterized by class imbalance, model selection primarily considered the mean external MCC, with AUC used as a complementary threshold-independent measure. Performance metrics for all five models are provided in Supplementary Table S2. SHAP analysis was used to interpret feature contributions, and candidate genes were prioritized by integrating SHAP-derived feature interpretation, DEG results, and WGCNA-derived genes.


### Immune Infiltration and Regulatory Network Analysis
  Immune cell infiltration was evaluated using CIBERSORT with the LM22 reference matrix. The relative proportions of immune cell types were compared between IgAN and control samples, and Spearman correlation analysis was used to assess relationships between candidate genes and immune cell infiltration.

  To explore potential regulatory relationships, transcription factor-target networks were constructed based on JASPAR 2024 motif information and expression-based correlation analysis.27 Candidate regulatory networks were further analyzed by functional enrichment. Single-gene enrichment analysis was also performed to explore biological pathways associated with candidate genes.


### Single-Gene Enrichment Analysis
  For each target gene, a two-view FGSEA workflow was used to characterize associated pathways. Samples were stratified into high- and low-expression groups according to target gene expression, using the top and bottom 30% of samples, respectively. Two preranked gene lists were generated based on Spearman correlation coefficients and limma-derived t statistics. Gene Ontology Biological Process gene sets were analyzed using fgsea. P values were combined using Fisher’s method followed by Benjamini-Hochberg correction.


### scRNA-Seq Analysis
  Single-cell RNA sequencing data from GSE171314 were analyzed using Scanpy.28 Before quality control, the dataset included five samples, comprising four IgAN samples and one control sample, with 20,570 cells in total. Standard quality-control procedures and doublet removal were performed, followed by exclusion of samples with fewer than 800 retained cells. The final dataset comprised 3378 cells from three samples, including two IgAN samples and one control sample. The retained count matrices were normalized, log-transformed, and used for highly variable gene selection. Principal component analysis and Harmony batch correction were performed,29 followed by Leiden clustering. Cell types were annotated using canonical marker genes. Candidate genes identified from bulk transcriptomic analysis were mapped to the single-cell atlas to determine their cell type-specific expression patterns. The mesangial cell subset was further analyzed to identify mesangial cell-associated DEGs and related biological functions.


### Mesangial Cell-Specific hdWGCNA and SCENIC Analysis
  To further characterize mesangial cell-specific gene programs, hdWGCNA was performed in the mesangial cell subset.30 The soft-thresholding power was set to β = 18, and module eigengenes were compared between IgAN and control groups. Module genes were intersected with mesangial cell DEGs to identify key mesangial cell-associated candidates. Transcription factor regulon activity was inferred using pySCENIC.31 Differential regulon activity between IgAN and control mesangial cells was assessed using |log2FC| > 0.25 and q < 0.05.


### Clinical Correlation Analysis
  Nephroseq v5 (https://v5.nephroseq.org) was used as an external validation resource for kidney disease-related transcriptomic analysis. Exploratory correlations between glomerular ATF3 expression and eGFR, serum creatinine, and proteinuria were assessed using available Nephroseq kidney disease samples with paired gene-expression and clinical data. Spearman’s rank correlation was used for all analyses.


### Cell Culture
  Primary human mesangial cells were purchased from MeisenCTCC (Meisen Cell, China) and cultured according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Aggregated IgA1 (aIgA1) was used to establish an IgAN-related mesangial cell stimulation model. Based on preliminary CCK-8 assays, 25 μg/mL aIgA1 was selected for subsequent experiments. For ATF3 knockdown, human mesangial cells were transduced with lentiviral vectors carrying ATF3 shRNA or empty vector control. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed by qRT-PCR and Western blotting, and the sh3 construct was selected for subsequent functional experiments. Cells were assigned to the Control, aIgA1, aIgA1 + empty vector, and aIgA1 + ATF3 shRNA groups.


### Immunofluorescence Staining
  HMCs were seeded on coverslips and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. After permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100 and blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin, cells were incubated with the primary antibody against Ki67 overnight at 4°C. After washing with PBS, cells were incubated with the corresponding fluorescent secondary antibody at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany).


### CCK-8 Assay
  Cell viability was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 assay according to the manufacturer’s instructions. HMCs were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5000 cells per well and treated with different concentrations of aIgA1 to determine the optimal stimulation concentration. After treatment, CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and cells were incubated at 37°C for 1 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Based on the preliminary screening results, 25 μg/mL aIgA1 was selected for subsequent experiments.


### EdU
  Cell proliferation was evaluated using an EdU incorporation assay according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After treatment, human mesangial cells were incubated with EdU working solution for 2 h. Cells were then fixed, permeabilized, and reacted with the detection solution. Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst. Fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope, and the percentage of EdU-positive nuclei was quantified using ImageJ.


### Long-Term Live-Cell Imaging
  Dynamic changes in cell confluence were monitored using a long-term live-cell imaging system. HMCs were seeded into culture plates and treated according to the experimental design. Images were acquired every 4 h for 72 h. Cell confluence was quantified using the corresponding image analysis software, and growth curves were generated to compare proliferative dynamics among different groups.


### Quantitative Real-Time PCR (RT-qPCR)
  RNA from cells was isolated using TRIzol reagent and reverse-transcribed into cDNA. RT-qPCR utilized SYBR Green kits (Yeasen, China), with primers synthesized by Sangon Biotechnology (Supplementary Table S1).


### Western Blotting
  Total protein was extracted from HMCs using RIPA lysis buffer (Solarbio, China) supplemented with phosphatase inhibitors and PMSF (Solarbio, China). Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Equal amounts of protein were separated by 10% or 12.5% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against β-actin (CST, USA), ATF3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), PCNA (CST, USA), and Cyclin D1 (Boster, China). After incubation with the corresponding secondary antibodies, protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence and quantified using ImageJ.


### Statistical Analysis
  P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.


## Results

### Integrated Transcriptomic Analysis Reveals Core DEGs in IgA Nephropathy
  To identify key molecular alterations associated with IgA nephropathy (IgAN), transcriptomic data from four GEO datasets (GSE115857, GSE35487, GSE37460, and GSE93798) were collected for integrated analysis. Each dataset was first preprocessed individually, and batch effects within datasets were corrected when necessary using the sva package (Supplementary Figure 1A–H). The datasets were then normalized and integrated for downstream analysis. Principal component analysis (PCA) of the integrated matrix showed clear separation between IgAN and control samples (Figure 2A). Differential expression analysis identified 47 common differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including 43 downregulated genes and 4 upregulated genes in IgAN relative to controls (Figure 2B). Hierarchical clustering and heatmap visualization of these 47 DEGs further distinguished IgAN samples from controls, supporting the presence of a robust disease-associated expression signature (Figure 2C). Among the downregulated genes, ATF3, GDF15, FOSB, EGR1, and ZFP36 were particularly notable, whereas the upregulated genes included LUM, HBB, TYROBP, and COL1A2.   To further characterize the biological relevance of these transcriptional alterations, functional enrichment analyses were performed separately for the downregulated and upregulated gene sets. Downregulated genes were significantly enriched in multiple stress- and inflammation-related pathways, including the p38 MAPK cascade, ERK1/2 signaling pathway, TNF-α/NF-κB signaling pathway, TGF-β signaling pathway, and AP-1-related pathways (Figure 2D). In contrast, the upregulated genes were predominantly enriched in pathways related to tissue injury and fibrosis, such as transforming growth factor beta 1 production, collagen fiber formation, reactive oxygen species metabolism, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and extracellular matrix remodeling (Figure 2E). Together, these results suggest that IgAN renal tissues exhibit altered inflammatory, stress-response, and tissue remodeling-related transcriptional programs.


### Construction of Co-Expression Networks and Identification of IgAN-Associated Modules
  To identify disease-associated co-expression modules in IgA nephropathy (IgAN), we performed weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) using the integrated expression matrix. Soft-threshold analysis showed that β = 3 was the lowest power at which the scale-free topology fit index reached the selection threshold of 0.80. At higher powers, the scale-free topology fit index remained high, whereas mean, median, and maximum connectivity progressively decreased. Therefore, β = 3 was selected to balance scale-free topology with the preservation of network connectivity (Figure 3A). Based on the topological overlap measure (TOM) and dynamic tree cutting algorithm, a total of 25 distinct co-expression modules were identified (Figure 3B). Correlation analysis between module eigengenes and disease status revealed that multiple modules were significantly associated with IgAN. Among them, the black, tan, darkorange, skyblue, purple, salmon, white, and yellow modules showed positive correlations with IgAN, whereas the turquoise, red, and royalblue modules were negatively correlated with disease status and relatively associated with controls (Figure 3C).   Functional enrichment analysis showed that the IgAN-associated modules were mainly involved in immune and inflammatory responses, complement activation, extracellular matrix organization, collagen-related processes, and glomerulus-associated biological pathways, whereas the control-associated modules were more enriched in apoptosis regulation and metabolic processes (Figure 3D). These results suggest distinct functional differences between disease-related and control-related modules. To further assess the biological relevance of the identified modules, the WGCNA topological overlap matrix was compared with the protein-protein interaction network. A significant positive correlation was observed between the two networks (r = 0.180, P = 0.001; Figure 3E), supporting the biological credibility of the co-expression modules. Given that differential expression analysis indicated that transcriptional alterations in IgAN were predominantly characterized by downregulated genes, the red module, which showed a significant negative correlation with disease status, was prioritized for subsequent analyses.


### Machine Learning-Based Feature Interpretation and Multi-Layer Integration Identify Core Candidate Genes in IgAN
  To prioritize IgAN-associated transcriptomic features, machine learning analysis was performed using the integrated bulk transcriptomic datasets. This analysis was designed for feature prioritization rather than for establishing a clinically deployable diagnostic model. All five models showed relatively high apparent performance in the small internal validation subset, although their AUC, accuracy, and MCC values varied across models (Supplementary Table S2). Given the limited sample size, these estimates should be interpreted cautiously and were not considered sufficient evidence of model generalizability. Performance varied substantially across the external validation datasets. Among the evaluated models, the naive Bayes model showed the most consistent AUC across the external cohorts and the highest mean external MCC; it was therefore retained for downstream feature interpretation. Nevertheless, the marked variation in accuracy and MCC across the imbalanced external cohorts indicates that the model should be regarded as a feature-prioritization tool rather than a clinically validated diagnostic classifier (Figure 4A–J).   SHAP-based model interpretation was then used to identify transcriptomic features contributing to IgAN-related model output. Among the analyzed features, grp52_pc1 showed the highest mean SHAP contribution, followed by grp178_pc1 (Figure 4K and L). SHAP dependence analysis showed that lower grp52_pc1 values were associated with IgAN-related model output, whereas higher values tended to correspond to control-associated patterns (Figure 4M). Because grp52_pc1 represented a module-level transcriptomic feature rather than a single-gene signal, gene-level contributions were reconstructed according to principal component loadings. Cumulative contribution analysis showed that gene-level contributions within grp52_pc1 increased progressively, suggesting that this feature reflected a coordinated gene expression program (Figure 4N). Ranking genes by loading values showed that multiple immediate-early and stress-responsive genes, including FOSB, ZFP36, JUNB, EGR1, KLF4, JUN, DUSP1, ATF3, FOS, and GDF15, contributed strongly to grp52_pc1 formation (Figure 4O).

### Single-Cell Analysis Identifies ATF3 as a Mesangial Cell-Associated Candidate Gene
  To determine the cell-type distribution of the core candidate genes, single-cell RNA sequencing data from IgAN and control kidney samples were analyzed. After quality control and doublet removal, three samples were retained for downstream analysis. UMAP visualization showed satisfactory data integration across samples, and a Leiden resolution of 0.50 was selected based on the silhouette coefficient (Figure 6A and B). Cell-type annotation based on canonical marker genes identified major renal parenchymal, stromal, and immune cell populations, including proximal tubular cells, loop of Henle cells, podocytes, endothelial cells, principal/distal tubular cells, intercalated cells, mesangial cells, smooth muscle cells, collecting duct cells, fibroblasts, and monocytes/macrophages (Figure 6C and D). Cell composition analysis suggested remodeling of the renal cellular landscape in IgAN, with relative enrichment of mesangial cells and changes in selected tubular, stromal, and immune cell compartments (Figure 6E–G). Given the limited number of single-cell samples, these findings were interpreted as exploratory evidence supporting mesangial cell relevance rather than definitive cell proportion changes. ## Discussion
  In this study, we integrated bulk transcriptomic datasets, single-cell RNA sequencing data, external validation, and in vitro experiments to identify IgAN-associated candidate genes and explore their biological relevance. Multi-layer transcriptomic prioritization identified a 12-gene core candidate set with reduced expression in IgAN. Single-cell mapping further linked ATF3 to the mesangial cell compartment, and mesangial cell-specific hdWGCNA and SCENIC analyses identified an ATF3-containing mesangial cell-associated co-expression module and reduced ATF3 regulon activity. External validation showed lower glomerular ATF3 expression in IgAN, and aIgA1-stimulated mesangial cells also showed reduced ATF3 expression. Functionally, ATF3 knockdown enhanced aIgA1-induced inflammatory cytokine expression and proliferative activity. These findings collectively support the potential relevance of ATF3 as a mesangial cell-associated candidate regulator in IgAN.

  Immunoglobulin A nephropathy (IgAN) is the most prevalent primary glomerulonephritis, characterized by chronic immune-mediated injury leading to progressive renal fibrosis and functional failure.32 Although aberrant IgA1 production and immune complex deposition are widely recognized as key initiating events in IgAN, the transition from immune deposition to sustained renal injury involves a prolonged and complex pathological process. In addition to the initial deposition of IgA-containing immune complexes, the persistence and subsequent clearance of mesangial IgA deposits may influence the course of renal injury.33 Therefore, the biological basis underlying continued progression in a subset of patients cannot be fully explained by immune deposition alone.

  The renal pathological features of IgAN are highly heterogeneous. The Oxford classification and its updated MEST-C scoring system have been widely used to assess mesangial hypercellularity, endocapillary hypercellularity, segmental sclerosis or adhesion, tubular atrophy and interstitial fibrosis, and crescent formation.34 Among these lesions, M, S, and T scores show relatively stable associations with renal outcomes.35 Of these pathological changes, mesangial proliferation is one of the most representative histological hallmarks of IgAN. Previous studies have shown that mesangial lesions constitute a core pathological basis of IgAN and are closely associated with renal function decline and disease progression.35 Patients with mesangial proliferative lesions are more likely to have unfavorable renal outcomes. Against this background, identifying molecules associated with abnormal mesangial phenotypes is of considerable importance for understanding the pathogenesis and progression of IgAN.

  Mesangial cells are major responder cells following immune deposition in IgAN, and their activation status directly influences local inflammatory mediator release, cell proliferation, and extracellular matrix accumulation. Previous studies have shown that pathogenic IgA immune complexes can bind to and be internalized by mesangial cells through surface receptors, among which CD71 has been closely linked to disease activity and progression.36 More recent mechanistic studies have further shown that galactose-deficient IgA1 can be internalized via transferrin receptor 1, form poorly degradable aggregates in lysosomes, induce lysosomal dysfunction, and trigger inflammatory responses.37 This abnormal intracellular processing mechanism provides a new explanation for persistent inflammation after immune deposition. In light of our findings, lower ATF3 expression in IgAN-related analyses suggests that the intrinsic capacity of mesangial cells to buffer stress and inflammatory stimuli may be impaired, making these cells more prone to enhanced proliferation and inflammatory mediator release. Taken together, the reduction in ATF3 expression following aIgA1 stimulation suggests that ATF3 downregulation may occur, at least in part, as a cellular response to IgA1-associated stress. At the same time, the exacerbation of inflammatory and proliferative responses following ATF3 knockdown indicates that reduced ATF3 may weaken the stress-adaptive buffering capacity of mesangial cells, thereby amplifying these responses. Thus, the available evidence is consistent with ATF3 downregulation being both a stress-associated response and a potential contributor to the progression of mesangial cell dysfunction, rather than simply reflecting the loss of an anti-proliferative factor.

  A key strength of this study is that ATF3 was not selected from a single analysis alone, but was prioritized through a sequential analytical framework. Differential expression analysis, WGCNA, machine learning-based feature interpretation, and multi-layer integration first identified a core set of candidate genes with reduced expression in IgAN. Among the 12 core candidate genes, ATF3 was prioritized because it was supported at each successive level of analysis. ATF3 showed reduced expression in the integrated bulk transcriptomic analysis, overlapped with genes downregulated in IgAN mesangial cells, was included in a disease-associated mesangial cell hdWGCNA module, and exhibited reduced regulon activity in the SCENIC analysis. As a stress-responsive transcription factor, ATF3 provides a biologically plausible link between cellular stress, inflammatory activation, and mesangial cell proliferation. Together, the convergence of bulk transcriptomic, cell-type-specific, and regulatory evidence provided the rationale for selecting ATF3 for subsequent functional evaluation in the aIgA1-stimulated mesangial cell model. Our prioritization of ATF3 is also supported by recent spatial transcriptomic evidence in IgAN. Park et al performed glomerular spatial transcriptomic profiling of IgAN according to mesangial proliferation and reported that ATF3, together with several immediate-early response genes such as EGR1, DUSP1, FOS, JUNB, NR4A1, RHOB, and ZFP36, was consistently downregulated in IgAN glomeruli.22 This spatially resolved evidence is highly consistent with our bulk transcriptomic and single-cell findings and further supports the relevance of ATF3 to glomerular and mesangial cell-associated alterations in IgAN. Importantly, our findings should be interpreted as evidence of reduced ATF3 expression in the analyzed IgAN datasets and IgAN-related mesangial cell model, rather than evidence of persistent ATF3 downregulation throughout the full disease course.

  Activating transcription factor 3 (ATF3) is an immediate-early transcription factor gene and a member of the ATF/CREB (cAMP response element-binding protein, CREB) family.38 Beyond regulating stress responses in various cellular biological processes, it can modulate or participate in multiple metabolic pathways, including amino acid metabolism, lipid metabolism, and glucose metabolism, thereby influencing associated metabolic functions.39,40 ATF3 plays diverse and often contradictory roles across different mammalian cell types, exhibiting context-dependent functions in both physiological and pathological conditions.41 Notably, the effect of ATF3 on cell proliferation appears to be highly context dependent. Previous studies have shown that ATF3 often suppresses pathological proliferation. For example, in human endometrial stromal cells, ATF3 deficiency is associated with a hyperproliferative state and impaired proliferative-secretory phase transition, whereas restoration of ATF3 suppresses proliferation at least partly through CDKN1A and promotes decidualization.42 In prostate epithelial-related models, ATF3 loss enhances androgen receptor signaling and promotes epithelial proliferation.43 In bladder cancer models, ATF3 induction enhances the response to HDAC inhibitors, whereas reduced ATF3 expression is associated with increased proliferative and migratory capacities.44,45 In clear cell renal cell carcinoma, ATF3 acts downstream of tumor-suppressive signaling and exerts antiproliferative effects.46 In contrast, during alveolar epithelial repair, increased ATF3 expression can promote type II alveolar epithelial cell proliferation and support regeneration.47 These studies indicate that the proliferative effect of ATF3 is influenced by cell type, mode of stimulation, and local microenvironment. In our study, the aIgA1 stimulation model represents chronic immune complex-related activation, and under this condition ATF3 appears more likely to restrain abnormal mesangial proliferation and preserve local homeostasis. Consistent with this antiproliferative tendency, ATF3 has also been reported to suppress cell proliferation and invasion in bladder cancer models, whereas ATF3 knockdown promoted tumor-related phenotypes.48

  Evidence from other kidney disease models also supports a protective role of ATF3 in renal injury. Chen et al recently reported that ST32da, a Salvia miltiorrhiza-derived ATF3 inducer, restored ATF3 expression and alleviated obesity-related diabetic nephropathy in mouse models.49 ATF3 deficiency aggravated renal inflammation and fibrosis, whereas ATF3 induction reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine expression and suppressed NF-κB-dependent inflammatory gene expression through interaction with HDAC2. Although diabetic nephropathy and IgAN differ in their initiating mechanisms, both involve renal inflammation, cellular stress, and tissue remodeling. These findings provide indirect support for the possibility that reduced ATF3 expression may weaken protective stress-adaptive responses in kidney disease.

  The in vitro findings further support the functional relevance of ATF3 reduction in mesangial cells. In aIgA1-stimulated human mesangial cells, ATF3 expression was decreased, whereas inflammatory cytokines and proliferation-related markers were increased. ATF3 knockdown further enhanced IL-6 and TNF-α expression, increased Ki67 and EdU positivity, promoted cell confluence, and elevated PCNA and Cyclin D1 expression. These findings indicate that reduced ATF3 expression is functionally associated with enhanced mesangial inflammatory activation and proliferation. However, because only ATF3 knockdown was included in the current functional validation, these data support a functional association but do not fully establish a complete mechanistic pathway. ATF3 overexpression or rescue experiments could further strengthen the causal interpretation of these findings and should be considered in future studies.

  This study also supports the potential relevance of ATF3 as a mesangial cell-associated candidate regulator in IgAN. Nephroseq validation showed lower glomerular ATF3 expression in IgAN and significant associations between ATF3 expression and renal function-related clinical parameters, including estimated glomerular filtration rate, serum creatinine, and proteinuria. Because glomerular ATF3 expression is assessed in renal tissue, its potential clinical application is currently dependent on kidney biopsy. The Oxford MEST-C score describes established structural lesions, including mesangial and endocapillary hypercellularity, segmental sclerosis, tubular atrophy/interstitial fibrosis, and crescents.34,35 In contrast, ATF3 expression may provide complementary molecular information regarding stress-adaptive and inflammatory activity within mesangial cells. Thus, ATF3 may potentially complement MEST-C-based histological assessment by facilitating molecular characterization of patients with similar histological lesions. However, whether ATF3 provides additional prognostic or diagnostic value beyond the MEST-C score and conventional clinical variables requires prospective evaluation in larger independent cohorts.

  Beyond functional validation, structure-guided computational approaches may provide a future route from ATF3 identification to mechanistic and translational investigation. AlphaFold 3 can predict biomolecular complexes involving proteins, nucleic acids, and selected ligands, providing opportunities to model potential ATF3-DNA and ATF3-protein interactions and to prioritize candidate binding interfaces for experimental investigation.50,51 AlphaFold-assisted virtual screening has also been combined with experimental validation to identify kidney injury-targeting peptides, illustrating the potential application of structure-guided approaches in kidney research.52 Applied to ATF3, these approaches could help generate hypotheses regarding its DNA-binding and protein-interaction interfaces and guide the screening of candidate molecules that modulate ATF3 activity. However, such structural predictions would require validation through biochemical binding assays, site-directed mutagenesis, rescue experiments, and disease-relevant experimental models.

  Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although multiple bulk transcriptomic datasets were integrated, residual heterogeneity related to platform differences, biopsy composition, and cohort characteristics may remain. Several external validation cohorts also had imbalanced group sizes, particularly GSE115857 and GSE35487, in which the control groups were relatively small, potentially affecting the stability of model performance evaluation. Second, the single-cell RNA sequencing dataset included a limited number of samples; therefore, cell proportion changes and mesangial cell-level findings should be interpreted as exploratory. Third, the machine learning framework was designed for transcriptomic feature prioritization rather than clinical diagnosis. Fourth, the aIgA1-stimulated mesangial cell model only partially reflects the complex renal microenvironment of IgAN. Finally, rescue experiments, ATF3 overexpression, direct downstream target validation, and in vivo studies are needed to further clarify the mechanism by which ATF3 regulates mesangial cell activation in IgAN.


## Conclusions
  This study identifies ATF3 as a mesangial cell-associated candidate regulator with reduced expression in IgAN-related conditions. Reduced ATF3 expression is associated with enhanced mesangial inflammatory activation and proliferation. These findings identify ATF3 as a mesangial cell-associated candidate regulator in IgA nephropathy and provide a basis for further mechanistic and clinical investigation.
