# Integrated Multi-omics Reveals Molecular Signatures of Rett Syndrome, Informing Future Biomarker Discovery


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s12035-026-06296-8.


## Introduction
  Rett syndrome (RTT) is a severe X-linked neurodevelopmental disorder that affects approximately 1 in 10,000 female births and is caused predominantly by pathogenic de novo loss of function variants in the X-linked gene MECP2 that encodes methyl-CpG-binding protein 2 [1, 2]. As a master regulator of chromatin organisation and gene expression, MeCP2 influences multiple biological processes, including neuronal maturation, synaptic plasticity, metabolism, and immune function [2, 3]. Loss of MeCP2 function results in multiple neurological abnormalities and a complex evolution that includes developmental delay, a distinctive developmental regression, and progressive impairments. Core and common clinical manifestations include partial or complete loss of acquired spoken language and hand function, hand stereotypies, gait disturbances, intellectual disability, epilepsy, behavioural issues, autonomic dysfunction, breathing abnormalities, and a range of multisystem comorbidities such as constipation, decreased bone density, and scoliosis.

  While treatment of RTT remains symptomatic, the therapeutic landscape for RTT has changed substantially over the past decade. The FDA approval of trofinetide (DAYBUE®), a synthetic version of a tripeptide derived from IGF-1, which targets core symptoms of the disorder, together with the rapid development of genetic therapies either in clinical trials or moving towards them, has shifted the field from symptomatic management towards disease modification [4]. Consequently, there is an increasing need for objective biomarkers capable of supporting patient subtyping, monitoring early ‘real-time’ response to therapy, and providing mechanistically relevant outcomes for clinical trials. Furthermore, recent pilot newborn screening data on MECP2 suggest that even presumably pathogenic variants are not predictive of a RTT phenotype [5] raising concerns about early diagnosis and implementation of novel therapies. Although promising neurophysiologic biomarkers are under development [6], there are no objective molecular biomarkers that provide insights into RTT’s underlying pathophysiology and can be used as surrogate endpoints in treatment studies. The RTT field still relies on clinician- and caregiver-reported outcome measures, such as the Rett Syndrome Behaviour Questionnaire (RSBQ) and the Clinical Global Impression - Improvement (CGI-I), which are not well integrated and are not particularly suited for evaluating changes in RTT’s evolution as those expected from disease-modifying therapies [7, 8]. The need for early diagnosis in order to administer treatments that can prevent the progression of RTT, in the context of disappointing initial results of newborn screening studies [5], has highlighted the need for identifying biomarkers that are distinctive and, hopefully, also specific of the disorder.

  Blood represents an attractive source for biomarker discovery because it is readily accessible, minimally invasive, and amenable to repeated sampling throughout the course of clinical trials. Although peripheral tissues cannot directly recapitulate neural dysfunction, numerous studies have demonstrated that alterations in immune signalling, mitochondrial function, cellular metabolism, and protein homeostasis observed in the brain are also reflected in circulating blood cells and plasma [9]. Blood-based biomarkers, therefore, have the potential to provide real-time information regarding systemic consequences of MeCP2 dysfunction while offering a practical platform for longitudinal monitoring in both clinical practice and therapeutic studies.

  Historically, many studies have utilised targeted methodologies in the pursuit of identifying biomarkers for RTT. These studies have shown dysregulation in biogenic amine metabolites and oxidative markers in affected individuals and animal models [10–12]. Additionally, studies performing individual untargeted genomics, proteomics, and metabolomics analyses in cohorts of patients (and animal models) with RTT have also been successful in identifying biomarkers and dysregulated pathways [13–18]. While there are some reports of untargeted omics studies being performed in tissue samples obtained by minimally invasive methods [14, 19], it is important to note that a significant portion of these types of studies have used tissue types collected by invasive measures (i.e. lumbar puncture and skin biopsy) [13, 15, 20] or postmortem [21]. Routine and frequent analyses of invasive biosamples are not pragmatic in a clinical care setting, nor in clinical trials.

  Recent advances in high-throughput omics technologies now permit the simultaneous interrogation of the transcriptome, proteome, phosphoproteome, and metabolome from the same individuals [22]. Each omics modality provides complementary biological information, capturing distinct levels of gene regulation, protein abundance, post-translational modification, and metabolic activity. However, analysing each dataset independently often fails to identify the coordinated biological programmes driving disease because important relationships between molecular layers remain obscured. Multi-Omics Factor Analysis version 2 (MOFA2) is an unsupervised machine learning framework specifically developed to integrate heterogeneous multi-omics datasets [22]. Rather than analysing each molecular layer independently, MOFA2 identifies latent factors that capture coordinated sources of biological variation shared across datasets. These latent factors provide biologically meaningful representations of disease-associated molecular programmes while simultaneously identifying the molecules contributing most strongly to each source of variation. Such approaches offer considerable advantages over traditional differential expression analyses by revealing systems-level mechanisms that may underpin disease pathogenesis, patient heterogeneity, and clinical severity.

  Given the role of MECP2 and its protein’s broad impact on CNS and peripheral physiology, a multi-dimensional approach that interrogates the ‘whole of organism’ phenotype is likely needed. Here, we performed an integrated multi-omics analysis of peripheral blood collected from individuals with classic RTT and age-matched controls. Transcriptomic, proteomic, phosphoproteomic, and metabolomic datasets were analysed independently before integrating them using MOFA2 to identify coordinated molecular programmes and latent factors associated with the disorder. We further investigated whether latent molecular factors also correlated with clinical severity and validated a selected candidate biomarker using orthogonal biochemical approaches. We hypothesise that integrated blood multi-omics can identify distinct molecular signatures capable of both distinguishing RTT from healthy controls and quantifying disease severity, thereby providing objective biomarkers to support diagnosis and management of the disorder.


## Methods

### Participants
  Females with a clinical diagnosis of classical RTT [23] carrying pathogenic variants in MECP2 were recruited to participate in the study through the multidisciplinary RTT clinic at the Children’s Hospital at Westmead (Westmead, NSW, Australia) (Table 1). Age- and sex-matched controls with no known significant health conditions (including neurodevelopmental disorders) were also recruited. Peripheral blood samples were collected from nine individuals with pathogenic variants in MECP2 (median age of 12.4 years, range 4.1–18.3 years) and eight age- and sex-matched controls (median age of 15.3 years, range 9–19.4 years). For each participant, venous blood samples were collected during a single visit into an ACD vacutainer tube and an EDTA vacutainer tube. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from the ACD tube by Ficoll-Paque density-gradient centrifugation and divided into aliquots for transcriptomic and proteomic/phosphoproteomic analysis from the same cell pool for each individual; the plasma in the EDTA tube was centrifuged and the supernatant was aliquoted for metabolomic profiling. Genotype, demographics, and clinical severity, based on the Clinical Severity Scale [24] and medication information, of the RTT cohort are summarised in Table 1. At the time of blood collection, all study participants were stable and there were no reported significant medical events (including infections) in the preceding 2 weeks. Written and informed consent was taken. The study was approved by the Sydney Children’s Hospital Network ethics committee (13/SCHN/14). Information on genotype, clinical severity, and current medication use was obtained through the hospital electronic medical record. ### RNA Sequencing and Bioinformatics for Individual Analysis
  PBMCs that were isolated as described above were used for RNA extraction. Illumina sequencing was performed, and the RNA-Seq raw fastq files were quality-checked with FastQC, and adapter trimming and quality filtering were performed with AdapterRemoval. Trimmed and quality-filtered paired-end reads were aligned to the human GRCh38 reference genome using STAR with default parameters [25]. Finally, gene expression was quantified by counting reads aligned to each Ensembl gene model using STAR. Differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis was performed using the Limma-Voom method [26, 27]. The trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) normalisation method from edgeR was applied to normalise read counts according to library size differences between samples [28]. Genes with < 2 count per million (CPM) reads in a minimum of three samples were removed. After filtering and normalisation, removal of unwanted variation was conducted using the remove unwanted variation (RUV) package [29]. A factor of unwanted variation (k = 4) was applied to adjust for technical noise and eliminate genes exhibiting minimal differential expression relative to the designated negative control genes. Genes are considered differentially expressed if their false discovery rate (FDR) adjusted p-value was < 0.05. DEGs were visualised using a volcano plot using the R package ggplot2, and heatmaps were created using the dittoSeq package.


### Proteomic and Phosphoproteomic Sample Preparation
  PBMCs isolated as described above were lysed to generate whole-cell lysates for proteomic and phosphoproteomic analyses. Tandem mass tag (TMT) labelling was performed with TMTpro reagents using approximately 200 μg of tryptic peptides per sample, and the combined TMT-labelled samples were desalted using solid-phase extraction. Phosphopeptide enrichment was performed as previously described [30], and the eluate was subjected to hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography separation as previously described [31]. LC-MS/MS analysis was performed using a Dionex UltiMate 3000 RSLC nano system and Q Exactive Plus hybrid quadrupole-orbitrap mass spectrometer.


### LC-MS/MS Phosphopeptide-Enriched Fractions
  Samples were loaded onto the column in 99% reversed phase buffer A (solution of 0.1% formic acid) and 1% buffer B (solution of 0.1% formic acid, 90% acetonitrile (34,967, Honeywell)). Data-dependent LC-MS/MS acquisition was performed at a resolution of 70,000 with an automatic gain control target of 1,000,000 for a maximum ion time of 100 ms from m/z 375 to 1500. The MS/MS scans were at a resolution of 35,000 with an automatic gain control target of 200,000 and a maximum ion time of 115 ms. The loop count was 12, the isolation window was 1.1 units of the m/z scale, the first mass was fixed at m/z 120, and the normalised collision energy was 34. Singly charged ions and those with charge > 8 were excluded from MS/MS and dynamic exclusion was for 35 s.


### LC-MS/MS Non-phosphopeptide-Enriched Fractions
  Samples were loaded onto the column in 1% buffer B. Data-dependent LC-MS/MS acquisition was performed for 140 min. Scan settings were as for phosphopeptides, except that the normalised collision energy was 31 and dynamic exclusion was set to 37 s.


### Differential Abundance Protein Analysis and Linear Modelling
  The proteomics analysis and linear modelling were performed as previously described unless otherwise stated below [32]. A set of 500 empirical negative control proteins with little or no change in protein expression across samples (q-value > 0.05) was identified from an initial ANOVA test. Significant differentially expressed proteins were defined as those with q-values < 0.05. Differentially expressed proteins were analysed for pathway enrichment in Reactome pathways and Gene Ontology (GO) terms using the clusterProfiler R library [33]. Redundant GO terms were removed using REVIGO [34] using the medium redundancy setting of 0.7.


### Differential Abundance Phosphopeptide Analysis and Linear Modelling
  To generate ‘evidence.txt’ files, the raw LC-MS/MS data was processed with MaxQuant v1.6.7.0. Any phosphopeptides were removed from further analysis if the first entry returned by MaxQuant matched any entries in the contaminants or the reverse sequence decoy databases (where the protein accession was prefixed with CON_ or REV_, respectively). Phosphopeptides with any missing values were removed from the analysis. If the phosphopeptide mapped to multiple proteins, the ‘best’ protein annotation was determined for each phosphopeptide, and this was recorded as a multi-mapped phosphopeptide. The best UniProt accessions and the best gene symbol for the multi-mapped proteins were kept for further analysis. The multiple UniProt accessions and multiple gene symbols were separated by a colon ‘:’ and were filtered for high-confidence phosphopeptides and a summary of abundance values.

  Identification of phosphopeptides that were of high confidence involved the following two steps. Firstly, to identify all phosphopeptides in which every major phosphorylation site had a site probability score of ≥ 0.75 (including analysing the site probability scores for all major sites for multiply phosphorylated peptides). All phosphorylation sites identified were called high confidence phosphorylation sites. Secondly, to identify all phosphopeptides in which every major phosphorylation site had a site probability score of > 0.5, and the site has also been previously identified as a high confidence phosphorylation site. The abundance values of all phosphopeptides identified using the above criteria were kept for further differential abundance analysis. The abundance values from different phosphopeptides sharing the same group of phosphorylation sites were summed. This resulted in the total abundance values corresponding to each group of phosphosites shared by multiple phosphopeptides. For simplicity, these groups of co-occurring phosphorylation sites are referred to as phosphosites throughout the “Results” section and figure legends, where phosphosites are mapped to their parent protein for downstream Reactome/GO pathway enrichment analyses, the corresponding entities are referred to as phosphoproteins.

  Data normalisation was similar to the method utilised for the proteomics analysis described above. Between sample normalisation was performed using the ‘cyclicloess’ normalisation from the ‘limma’ R package. The RUVIII package was used to remove batch effects, and the parameters used included selecting 1000 phosphopeptides with the least changes across samples as negative control proteins and four unwanted components (k = 4) were removed by the tool. For host protein normalisation of the phosphopeptide, the host protein’s log fold-change was subtracted from the log fold-change of the phosphopeptide. The host protein normalised phosphopeptide log fold-change was used for all subsequent analysis. If the direction of the fold-change for the phosphopeptide and the direction of the fold-change of the protein were opposite, the q-value of the phosphopeptide and the q-value of the protein were used directly for normalisation using Fisher’s method (resulting in a more statistically significant q-score for the phosphopeptide). If the direction of the log-fold change between the phosphopeptides and proteins were the same, then 1 minus the protein q-value and the original phosphopeptide q-value were used for normalisation using the Fisher’s method (resulting in a less significant q-score for the phosphopeptide).

  Differential abundance analysis of phosphopeptides was performed to compare individuals with RTT to controls. The statistical analysis was similar to that of the quantitative proteomics analysis above, in which the ‘limma’ R package [27], linear modelling, and empirical Bayes methods were used to calculate the moderated p-values for the comparison of individuals with RTT to controls. A FDR correction was applied using q-values [35]. Significantly differentially expressed phosphopeptides were defined as those with q-values < 0.05. Proteins containing significant phosphosites were separated into three subsets for pathway enrichment with those containing only significantly increased phosphosites, only significantly decreased phosphosites, or a mixture of both, and each subset was tested for enrichment against Reactome and GO terms as described above for the proteomics analysis.

  The ‘KinSwingR’ R library [31] was used to perform kinase-substrate enrichment to infer upstream kinases with up- or downregulated activities in the experiment. To de-duplicate multi-phosphorylated peptides containing common phosphorylation sites, for each phosphorylation site present in common across multi-phosphorylated peptides, the greatest log fold-change of the phosphosite across all phosphopeptides and the corresponding q-value were kept for KinSwingR analysis. Known phosphosites annotated with kinase-substrate interactions from all mammalian species (from PhosphositePlus version DB5) were used to train kinase-specific scoring matrices. The KinSwingR analysis was then proceeded with default parameters as previously described [31].


### Metabolomic Analysis and Linear Modelling
  Plasma isolated as described above was aliquoted for shipment to Baylor Genetics (Houston, TX, USA), where clinical untargeted metabolic profiling was performed by Metabolon Inc., Morrisville, NC, USA and Baylor Genetics as previously described [36, 37].

  Metabolomics analysis was performed using a method similar to the proteomics analysis using RUVIII-C and limma, unless stated below. A set of 150 empirical negative control metabolites with little or no change in metabolite abundance across samples (q-value > 0.05) was identified from an initial ANOVA test. The RUVIII method [38] was used to remove unwanted variation across samples, and the tool removed six unwanted components (k = 6). Next, the missForest R package [39] was used to impute missing values. The parameters for missForest used were maximum number of iterations (maxiter) = 10 and random number seed was 12,346. Parallelization of computation was performed using the ‘variables’ method. Significantly differentially expressed metabolites were defined as those with q-values < 0.05. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the ‘camera’ function in the limma R package and the metabolomic pathways from the KEGG database.


### Integrative Multi-omics Factor Analysis v2(MOFA2)

#### Transcriptomics
  For integration, a set of 200 empirical negative control transcripts, with little or no change in protein expression across samples (q-value > 0.05), was identified from an initial ANOVA test. The RUVIII method [38] was used to remove the unwanted variations across the samples and a number of unwanted components (k = 6) were removed prior to input into the multi-omics analysis tool MOFA2.


#### Proteomics and Phosphoproteomics
  The RUVIII normalised phosphosite intensity for each protein in each sample was divided by the RUVIII normalised protein intensity for that protein in that sample. This resulted in a matrix of phosphosite intensities, which had been adjusted for host protein abundance. This matrix was used for input into the integrative multi-omics analysis tool MOFA2.


#### Metabolomics
  The RUVIII normalised metabolite intensity values were used for input into the integrative multi-omics analysis tool MOFA2.


#### MOFA2
  Multi-omics data was merged using the unique patient and control sample identifier as joining criteria. Analysis was performed with transcriptomic, proteomic, phosphoproteomic (adjusted for host protein abundance), and metabolomic data. MOFA2 was run as applied in the R package MOFA2 [22] on the merged data using the basilisk option set to ‘TRUE’ to ensure reproducible dependency management. All other default (gaussian) parameters were maintained. The input into MOFA2 was four data modalities including genes, protein, phosphosites, and metabolites data after preliminary filtering and RUVIII normalisation described above. The primary analyses intended to identify latent factor(s) discriminating patients with RTT from controls. To investigate the relationship between patients’ clinical severity and the latent factors identified by MOFA2, Spearman’s rank correlation was calculated between the Clinical Severity Scale (CSS) and the sample weights for each factor. To identify the specific molecules driving this association, the top 10 features (transcripts, proteins, phosphosites, and metabolites) with the highest loading scores on Factor 2 were identified.


#### MOFA2: Correlation Network
  The functional relationships among the molecules were established by identifying the top 15 molecules from each omics layer based on the absolute weights in Factor 1, the one providing the best discrimination between RTT and control samples, from the MOFA2 analysis. The RUVIII normalised log base-2 quantities for each of these molecules were Z-transformed (mean-centred and scaled to unit variance) such that each molecule had a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one across all samples. For the RTT samples, the Pearson correlation score and associated p-value for the correlation score for each pair of molecules were calculated and the same was done separately for the control samples. For each pair of molecules, the differential correlations between the individuals with RTT and the controls (RTT Pearson correlation − the control Pearson correlation) were calculated. Only pairs of molecules that had differential correlation of < − 1 or > 1 (and p-value < 0.05 for either the RTT correlation or the control correlation) were included in the network.


#### Orthogonal Validation Assays
  Orthogonal validation was performed for the most informative plasma metabolite, in this case glutamate, because of its relevance as circulating systemic (overall) signature of MeCP2 dysfunction. For this purpose, plasma from RTT individuals and controls was analysed in the New South Wales Biochemical Genetics Service’s diagnostic laboratory. Samples were deproteinised in a 1:1 volume of a 5-sulphosalicylic acid-containing internal standard mixture and centrifuged at 18,000 rpm at 4 °C for 5 min. A 1:1:3 ratio of the protein-free supernatant to 6-aminoquinolyl-N-hydroxysuccinimidyl carbamate derivatising reagents to NaOH/Borate buffer was added into an HPLC total recovery vial, vortexed, and left at room temperature for 60 s. The mixture was then incubated at 55 °C for 10 min. Chromatographic separation occurred using a reverse phase BEH C18 column and a water/acetonitrile solvent gradient by ultra-performance liquid chromatography coupled to an ultraviolet light detector (UPLC-UV). GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 was used for all statistical analyses, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. Results are expressed as medians and are displayed with graphs depicting the interquartile ranges. The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare independent continuous variables. The relationship between plasma glutamate concentration and CSS was assessed using Spearman rank correlation and the coefficient of determination (R2) from an ordinary least-squares linear regression (R2 = 0.009).

### Blood Multi-omics Consistently Separates Rett Syndrome from Control Groups Across Transcriptomics, Proteomics, Phosphoproteomics, and Metabolomics Using PCA
  To determine whether peripheral blood of individuals with RTT captured a molecular signature across omics layers, we first examined each omics dataset independently using principal component analysis, allowing us to assess whether transcriptomic, proteomic, phosphoproteomic, and metabolomic profiles could distinguish individuals with RTT from age- and sex-matched controls.

  While the transcriptomic alterations were subtle, they demonstrated biologically meaningful and internally consistent disease-associated signatures. The transcriptomics principal component analysis (PCA) showed a distinct separation between individuals with RTT and controls across the first (21%) and second (11%) principal components (Fig. 2A). The proteomic layer provided a far stronger and more disease-discriminating signal than the transcriptome, where the PCA showed a clear separation between individuals with RTT and controls across the first (31%) and second (13%) principal components (Fig. 2B). Phosphoproteomics captures the dynamic state of intracellular signalling and structural regulation. The PCA separation across the first (28%) and second (13%) phosphoproteomics principal components was robust, though slightly less distinct than that of total proteomics (Fig. 2C). Plasma metabolomics captures the systemic milieu, and it showed clean RTT-control discrimination across the first (24%) and second (14%) principal components (Fig. 2D). ### MOFA2 Latent Factor 1 Identifies a Disease-Associated Molecular Axis That Separates Rett Syndrome from Controls
  Rather than relying on a reductionist approach for biomarker discovery, we then investigated a holistic systems biology model to identify common factors underlying the cellular processes revealed by individual molecular layers. To do this, we used a robust, unsupervised machine-learning matrix factorization analysis tool, Multi-Omics Factor Analysis (MOFA2) [22, 40] to integrate multiple omics datasets from individuals with RTT and controls. MOFA2 factors are latent variables that capture the dominant sources of variation across the omics datasets. Each factor represents a distinct underlying signal, defined by its sample scores, feature loadings, and contribution from each omics modality. By modelling these latent factors, MOFA2 can identify coordinated or modality-specific molecular patterns and provides an integrated view of cross-omics relationships that are not detectable through single-omics analyses. MOFA2 Latent Factor 1 represents the primary ‘disease axis’, perfectly separating RTT from controls without overlapping confidence intervals as observed in the MOFA2 latent factor plot (Fig. 7A and Suppl. 1). ## Discussion
  Rett syndrome is a complex neurodevelopmental disorder with neurological and metabolic manifestations. Although the disorder is primarily caused by pathogenic variants in MECP2, the downstream effects of MeCP2 dysfunction are unlikely to be captured by a single molecular measurement. This has made the identification of molecular biomarkers of RTT challenging, particularly biomarkers that reflect disease state, clinical severity or biological response to emerging therapies. While MeCP2 is most extensively studied in the central nervous system, it is also expressed beyond the brain, and RTT is increasingly recognised as a multisystem disorder. Peripheral blood, therefore, represents a biologically plausible compartment in which to detect disease-associated molecular changes, more than simply a convenient sampling source. In this study, we used an integrated blood-based multi-omics approach, incorporating PBMC transcriptomics, proteomics, and phosphoproteomics and plasma metabolomics, to identify molecular signatures associated with RTT. Rather than identifying one dominant molecule or pathway, using a machine learning bioinformatic approach, our findings indicate that RTT is associated with a complex pattern of abnormalities involving multiple cellular processes across several molecular layers. These included pathways related to ribosomal and translational function, mitochondrial and energetic processes, chromatin and RNA-processing regulation, cytoskeletal phospho-regulation, immune and secretory features, and plasma lipid/cholesterol metabolism and energy-linked amino acids. These findings suggest the presence of peripheral molecular signatures associated with disease-state and phenotypical variability in RTT. Although alterations in some of these processes have been previously reported in RTT, our converging multi-omics data strengthens the biological relevance and potential specificity of the identified molecular signatures of RTT disease state and progression.

  A key finding of this study is that RTT-associated molecular differences were most informative when interpreted as interconnected pathway-level changes rather than as independent omics modalities. Comparisons between RTT and control groups identified changes within each molecular layer, while MOFA2 provided an integrated framework for determining shared sources of variation across the datasets. MOFA2 Latent Factor 1 separated individuals with RTT from controls, supporting the presence of an integrated RTT-associated molecular network in peripheral blood. This latent factor was not restricted to a single modality; although transcriptomic and proteomic features contributed strongly, phosphoproteomic and metabolomic features also added to the integrated signal. This suggests that the RTT blood signature spans gene expression, protein abundance, post-translational regulation, and systemic metabolic layers, rather than being driven by a single omics modality. In addition, MOFA2 Latent Factor 2 identified a severity-associated molecular network, which correlated with CSS scores, with a potential for predicting disease severity in individuals with RTT.

  The cellular processes identified by individual omics analysis as differential in individuals with RTT include ribosomal and translation functions, mitochondrial metabolism, chromatin remodelling, immune signalling and secretory function, cytoskeletal organisation and lipid and cholesterol handling pathways, and energy-linked amino acids. These are discussed in the following sections, in the context of their coordinated variation and interactions across molecular layers.


### Ribosomal and Translational Stress Represents the Strongest Cross-Layer Signal
  The most consistent cross-molecular layer finding involved ribosomal and translation-associated function. Both transcriptomic and proteomic enrichment analyses identified ribosomal pathways to be elevated in individuals with RTT, and MOFA2 loadings further highlighted ribosomal transcripts including RPL36, RPL39, RPLP2 and RPS15, and RPS16 and RPS19 as contributors to the integrated disease-associated factor (Latent Factor 1). This convergence across transcriptomic, proteomic, and integrative analyses suggests that altered ribosomal or translational machinery is an internally consistent feature of the RTT-associated peripheral signature. These findings are consistent with previous reports of altered translational regulation in RTT models, including reduced AKT/mTOR signalling and associated protein synthesis dysregulation in Mecp2-mutant mice, and altered ribosome engagement in RTT patient-derived neurons [41, 42]. Increased abundance of ribosomal transcripts and proteins may reflect altered cellular homeostasis, adaptive remodelling of the protein synthesis machinery, or a compensatory response to chronic cellular stress. Upregulated genes were heavily concentrated in the ribosomal structure category indicating a transcriptional push towards a globally increased protein synthesis machinery. Importantly LTN1 which encodes listerin E3 ubiquitin protein ligase 1, an E3 ubiquitin ligase that acts in ribosome quality control to clear stalled translation products [43], was a major hub in the differential correlation network (Fig. 8). Translational dysregulation has previously been reported in mouse and patient-iPSC derived models, thus supporting this core observation of a defective protein synthesis machinery in RTT [41, 42].


### Mitochondrial and Bioenergetic Changes may Reflect Cellular Adaptation Rather Than Enhanced Mitochondrial Function
  Mitochondrial and bioenergetic pathways also emerged as a major theme, particularly in the transcriptomic and proteomic datasets. Genes and proteins associated with mitochondrial energy production, respiratory chain function, mitochondrial matrix biology, and NADH dehydrogenase activity were enriched among features increased in RTT. These findings are aligned with the multiple studies describing mitochondrial dysfunction in RTT and may reflect a RTT-associated bioenergetic adaptation and compensatory response to impaired mitochondrial efficiency, altered energetic demand, oxidative stress, or broader cellular stress [11, 12, 16]. Importantly, succinate, an intermediate of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, which plays a crucial role in adenosine triphosphate (ATP) generation in mitochondria, was negatively loaded onto the metabolomics component of MOFA2 Latent Factor 1 and was a hub molecule in the differential correlation network (Fig. 8). Together, these findings position succinate as a key metabolic node linking mitochondrial dysfunction with immune activation and inflammatory signalling, thereby providing a plausible mechanism through which altered bioenergetics may contribute to cellular activation and systemic inflammation in RTT [44].


### Chromatin and RNA-Processing Features Provide a Biologically Plausible Link to MeCP2-Associated Regulation
  Chromatin regulation and RNA-processing pathways provide one of the most biologically plausible links between the peripheral multi-omics signature identified in this study and known molecular functions of MeCP2. The proteomic analyses found decreased abundance of proteins involved in chromatin remodelling, chromosome organisation, and RNA splicing such as the major chromatin remodeller, ATRX, which was supported by the increased phosphorylation sites in ATRX at S1082, Y1087, and S1348.

  MOFA2 loadings validated these findings with ATRX negatively loaded in the proteomics and the chromatin/transcriptional regulators CHD8, BCL9L, and RBM15 to be negatively loaded in the phosphoproteomics. The MOFA2 analyses highlighted the dynamic state of these processes, involving phosphorylation, and given MeCP2’s established role as a chromatin-associated methyl-DNA reader and transcriptional regulator [45, 46], these findings are relevant to RTT biology and suggest a suppression of the epigenetic machinery in peripheral blood cells [16, 47]. Given the critical role of MeCP2 in epigenetic regulation [48], this may reflect the primary consequence of MeCP2 dysfunction and a broader failure or suppression of the epigenetic and chromatin-remodelling apparatus in RTT cells.


### Proteomic and Phosphoproteomic Analyses Reveal Cytoskeletal Disorganisation
  Dysregulation of pathways involved in cytoskeletal function has previously been identified in RTT [15, 49, 50]. Additionally, alterations in the neuronal cytoskeleton thought to be contributing to distinctive cortical dendritic anomalies have been previously reported in postmortem samples from individuals with RTT [49]. The individual omics data showing a downregulation in proteins such as DMTN and MYO1G was supported by the downregulation of phosphorylation sites of SPTB and upregulation of phosphorylation sites on DMTN.

  While there was evidence of cytoskeletal deficits across several molecular layers in this study, the most striking evidence came from the prominent negative proteomic and phosphoproteomic MOFA2 loadings for structural and membrane-associated targets, especially SPTB and DMTN. Interestingly, 3 different phosphorylation sites on SPTB were negatively loaded and the phosphorylation of DMTN was positively loaded while its overall protein expression was negatively loaded. SPTB is a major component of red blood cell membranes where it forms a flexible scaffold that acts as a membrane skeleton [51]; thus, the profound decrease in phosphorylation sites suggests an alteration of the cellular cytoskeletal architecture in RTT. DMTN is an actin-binding and -bundling protein that, while it has a relatively high brain expression, is known to also play a structural role in erythrocytes. DMTN’s high levels in blood are linked to the protein phosphorylation-regulated actin-bundling activity [52]. However, the role of DMTN and SPTB in the brain pathology of RTT is uncertain. Thus, these findings suggest altered regulation of membrane-associated cytoskeletal proteins in RTT blood cells supporting a cytoskeletal phospho-regulatory signature that may reflect altered cell structure, signalling, activation state, or immune-cell behaviour.

  In addition, we observed RTN3 to be ranked highly positive in the Latent Factor 2 analysis and correlating with high disease severity (Fig. 9B). RTN3 is an endoplasmic reticulum membrane-shaping protein involved in membrane dynamics and intracellular trafficking. Given the close functional coupling between the endoplasmic reticulum and the cytoskeleton, the concurrent alterations in RTN3, SPTB, and DMTN suggest a disruption of an endoplasmic reticulum-membrane cytoskeletal network in RTT.


### Immune and Secretory Features Suggest Altered PBMC-Associated Immune Biology in RTT
  A downregulation of immune signalling was apparent in the individual omics evidenced by a downregulation in proteins such as PRF1 and PTPRCAP. However, a pronounced downregulation in immune signalling and secretion was revealed predominantly by MOFA2 proteomic profiles characterised by PRF1 and CD40LG negative loading, in addition to PTPRCAP as a hub gene in the differential correlation network. PRF1 is central to cytotoxic lymphocyte function through the perforin-granzyme pathway [53], while CD40LG contributes to T-cell-dependent immune signalling and communication between immune-cell populations [54] and PTPRCAP is linked to lymphocyte activation [55]. Immune and inflammatory pathways have been implicated in RTT models, including evidence of sustained NF-κB signalling in MeCP2-deficient myeloid-derived cells and aberrant NF-κB signalling in Mecp2-null mice [56, 57]. Reduced abundance of immune-effector molecules could reflect altered expression within immune cells as well as differences in PBMC cell-type composition, such as altered proportions of cytotoxic T cells, NK cells, or monocytes.

  The negative ranking of MMP1 in the Latent Factor 2 analysis supports these observations. Being a matrix metalloproteinase, MMP1 is involved in both extracellular matrix remodelling and immune regulation and, beyond its role in collagen degradation, MMP1 influences cytokine signalling, leukocyte migration, and inflammatory responses. The association of MMP1 with lower disease severity may, therefore, reflect preservation of immune and tissue homeostatic processes that become disrupted with increasing RTT severity.


### Plasma Metabolomics Identifies Systemic Lipid/Cholesterol and Energy-Linked Amino Acid Changes
  Metabolomics data provide a systemic biochemical perspective that complements the PBMC-based transcriptomic, proteomic, and phosphoproteomic findings. Plasma metabolomics identified alterations in amino acid-, lipid-, bile acid-, and cholesterol-related metabolites. To date, relatively few studies have examined circulating metabolomic or lipidomic signatures in RTT. Prior work has reported plasma metabolic differences between individuals with RTT and unaffected siblings, alterations in sphingolipid metabolism in blood/plasma samples, and changes in the CSF and plasma lipidome of individuals with RTT [14, 58, 59]. Altered cholesterol and lipid-related metabolites are consistent with previous reports implicating changes in lipid and bile acid expression and cholesterol metabolism in RTT, including studies of peripheral cholesterol profiles, patient-derived fibroblasts, and CSF/plasma lipidomics [59, 60].

  In the individual metabolite analysis, glutamate, aspartate, TUDCA, and muricholate were highly upregulated and succinate downregulated. These findings were supported by a positive loading of glutamate and muricholate and a negative loading of succinate in the MOFA2 analysis, with orthogonal validation using UPLC-UV supporting the untargeted metabolomics finding of elevated glutamate. Higher glutamate levels have previously been reported in RTT in blood [14] and cerebrospinal fluid studies [61, 62], and MeCP2-deficient myeloid-derived cells have been shown to exhibit increased glutamate release at baseline and after stimulation [56]. In addition, in vitro studies using a MeCP2-deficient PBMC model [56, 63] showed increased glutaminase expression. Both elevated glutamate release and glutaminase expression would contribute to higher levels of glutamate in the bloodstream, as well as the central nervous system and peripheral tissues. Interestingly, increased glutaminase mRNA levels in mouse microglial cultures were suppressed by NF-κB inhibition [56] of relevance to the aforementioned NF-κB overactivity in RTT [56, 57]. Hence, the mechanism underlying higher glutamate levels may be related to excessive inflammation and immune dysregulation. While the validated individual glutamate concentrations in the participants with RTT were higher than those of the controls in our cohort, they were within the normative reference range (glutamate 14–113 µmol/L; NSW Biochemical Genetics Service, Westmead, Australia). Although this reference population is not age- or gender-matched to our cohort, the overlap in range of values should be taken into account when contemplating glutamate as a molecular disease-state biomarker. However, the use of glutamate levels as a longitudinal monitoring biomarker within individuals with RTT may still be considered. Studies that obtain baseline levels of amino acids prior to the commencement of a therapeutic intervention may help further elucidate the role of amino acid alterations in RTT.

  As the metabolomic analysis was performed in plasma, this provides evidence of a circulating systemic signature rather than direct measures of PBMC-intrinsic biology and reflects contributions from multiple factors, including liver and gut function, diet, medication exposure, endocrine state, mobility, inflammation, or other systemic processes. Metabolomic profiles and MOFA2 loadings also highlight an increase in bile acids/cholesterol derivatives (glycocholate) and complex lipids, paired with other metabolites such as aspartate, dodecanedioate, eicosenoylcarnitine, and creatinine, which suggest a compensatory response to the aforementioned translation dysfunction and reflect a downstream effect to MeCP2 deficit. Reassuringly, many dysregulated metabolites identified in our study were also reported by Neul and colleagues including elevations in glutamate and decreases in arginine, N-acetyl carnosine and 3,4-hydroxyphenyl lactate [14], thus validating our findings. It is promising that these dysregulated molecules are consistent across both cohorts of patients, as this provides further support for pathogenic metabolic processes underlying the pathophysiology of RTT. However, it is important to note that both studies utilised the same wet-lab and initial bioinformatic platform for sample processing. Reproduction of the same findings on different analytical platforms is needed to provide ancillary confirmation.

  In support of the potential of metabolomics data, we observed sphingomyelin to be ranked highly positive in the Latent Factor 2 analysis putting this lipid as a plausible candidate of disease severity (Fig. 9B). Sphingolipid metabolism has been previously reported to be perturbed in RTT CSF and plasma [58, 59]. While the direction of sphingomyelin change differs between cohorts and biological compartments (CSF versus plasma), these studies collectively support dysregulation of membrane lipid and cholesterol-associated pathways as a feature of RTT.

  Based on the integrated individual omics data into MOFA2 Latent Factor 1 and 2, we can identify a number of biologically robust, clinically relevant molecules, reflective of underlying disease mechanisms that may inform future RTT biomarkers.Translational stress: LTN1: Many ribosomal genes and proteins were found to be dysregulated in the individual omics layers, as well as in the MOFA2 transcript positive loadings. These molecular network findings not only elucidate a unique omics molecular signature but also provide important insights into RTT pathophysiology.

  We conclude that a decrease in MeCP2 function induces a systemic molecular stress response that is detectable in peripheral blood and manifests as coordinated dysregulation of translational control, chromatin organisation, cytoskeletal architecture, immune function, and energy metabolism. Our integrated multi-omics analyses revealed that PBMC’s LTN1, ATRX, CHD8, SPTB, DMTN, PRF1, CD40LG, and PTPRCAP and plasma glutamate are the most promising disease-state (complementary diagnostic) candidate biomarkers while sphingomyelin, RTN3, and MMP1 appear to be the most suitable biomarkers of disease severity. Other components of the most informative latent factors could also be considered individually or in groups in future studies.


## Limitations and Future Directions
  As with all rare conditions, the generalisability of our study is constrained by the small cohort size. While our data show a clear molecular signature, future replication studies involving larger cohorts of individuals with RTT, especially studies stratified by genotype or clinical severity, would be essential for the further development of the candidate biomarkers. Future collection of longitudinal data will also be critical in determining the stability of the reported latent factors and their potential to inform about the evolution of RTT and response to interventions. Future analyses of relationships between candidate biomarkers and clinical severity will need to incorporate more dynamic measures than the CSS [7]. Moreover, addition of samples from individuals with other neurodevelopmental disorders will be important for establishing the specificity of the reported molecular signatures. In addition, in a clinical setting, it is often difficult for patients to fast for prolonged periods of time, and this is particularly likely to have had an impact on the metabolomics component. Therefore, future work would benefit from analysing fasted samples (or correcting for this during analysis by including a comprehensive diet and medication summary). Additionally, it is likely that non-truncating variants in MECP2 will have variable impacts on MeCP2 function, given that different domains have varying effects on transcription, often impacting different downstream pathways. Omics studies in tissues from individuals with RTT and the same genotype would provide better insights into downstream functional impacts relevant to specific patient subgroups.

  Despite the aforementioned shortcomings, the consistency of the molecular processes identified across independent omics platforms supports the robustness of the integrated molecular signatures with potential to serve as complementary diagnostic measures or pharmacodynamic biomarkers. Integration of peripheral blood datasets with patient-derived neuronal models, including induced pluripotent stem cell-derived neurons and cortical organoids, will further strengthen the biological interpretation of these molecular signatures and help in characterising RTT pathophysiology by increasing our understanding of peripheral and central disease mechanisms.


## Conclusion
  In summary, an integrated blood-based multi-omics approach identified convergent molecular signatures associated with RTT disease state and clinical severity, including alterations in translational, mitochondrial, chromatin-regulatory, cytoskeletal, immune, and metabolic processes. The alignment of these findings with pathways previously implicated in RTT supports their validity and relevance to the disorder, while their convergence across multiple molecular layers strengthens their biological significance and provides confidence in their robustness and potential utility as peripheral biomarkers. The present study was primarily directed towards biomarker discovery rather than characterisation of RTT biology. Therefore, it should be interpreted in terms of its initial contribution to the identification of accessible molecular signatures that can serve as biomarkers of diagnosis, patient classification, or therapeutic response. Validation in larger, independent cohorts will be required to refine these candidate biomarkers and to also determine whether distinct profiles are associated with specific MECP2 variants, phenotypic profiles, or patterns of disease progression.

  These findings establish a framework for objective biomarker discovery in RTT and provide an important step towards developing biologically informed measures capable of complementing diagnostic measures, supporting patient subtyping, and monitoring responses to available and novel treatments.

  Integrated omics analysis offers substantial advantages to inform biomarker discovery by providing a comprehensive view of a disorder’s pathophysiology through the integration of transcriptomics, proteomics and metabolomics data. This systems-level approach improves the robustness of candidate biomarkers by cross-validating findings across molecular layers, reducing false positives and increasing clinical relevance. While individual omics data can be powerful, integrated omics analysis enables deeper mechanistic insights by linking biomarkers to disease pathways rather than simple associations (e.g. gene expression), accounting for individual heterogeneity, and supporting molecular subtyping. Significantly, using an integrated omics approach to identify biomarkers in patients’ blood enhances the predictive power to understand disease state and treatment response, increasing the likelihood of identifying accessible peripheral biomarkers that reflect the pathology in less accessible tissues such as the brain. This study also supports the use of omics analytical and statistical techniques to better evaluate and understand the complex biological processes underpinning RTT pathophysiology. This holistic approach may also prove useful for investigating biomarkers for other neurodevelopmental disorders and for providing comprehensive mechanistic insights.

  While these data do not represent molecular changes in the brain, this study aimed to rather identify molecular changes in the circulating blood, using a minimally invasive, widely accessible method of monitoring that provides a more practical, scalable, and reproducible approach for both clinical diagnosis and management and therapeutic trials. These findings lay the groundwork and pave the way for future research where further studies are needed to confirm these peripheral molecular signatures As a whole, this study demonstrates that our identified latent factors may be potential biomarkers for disease state and response to interventions.
