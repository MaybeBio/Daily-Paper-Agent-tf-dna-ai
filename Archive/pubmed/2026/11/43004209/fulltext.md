# Helixpr: a helix-aware periodic representation of DNA sequence for enhancer identification


## Background
  Enhancers are distal cis-regulatory elements that play essential roles in gene regulation, development, and disease. Although numerous computational methods have been developed for enhancer identification, commonly used DNA encoding strategies, such as one-hot and k-mer representations, typically treat DNA as a linear symbolic sequence. This simplification ignores the intrinsic geometry of the DNA double helix, where nucleotides are organized along a periodic three-dimensional structure with a helical repeat of approximately 10–11 base pairs. To address this limitation, we propose HelixPR (Helix-aware Periodic Representation), a biologically motivated DNA sequence representation for enhancer identification.


## Results
  HelixPR augments per-nucleotide one-hot encoding with a sinusoidal periodic encoding using a period of T=10 base pairs. It further incorporates a global nucleotide-composition token to capture sequence-level compositional information complementary to local nucleotide patterns. Together, these components form a compact, interpretable, and information-preserving representation of DNA sequences. Combined with a lightweight one-dimensional convolutional neural network, HelixPR achieved competitive performance across benchmark datasets. On Liu’s benchmark, HelixPR achieved an accuracy of 0.8434 and a Matthews correlation coefficient of 0.6874 for enhancer identification. For enhancer strength classification, HelixPR achieved an accuracy of 0.8400 and an AUC of 0.9346, outperforming baseline methods. On Basith’s benchmark, HelixPR obtained a mean balanced accuracy of 0.9778, substantially outperforming existing methods. Further analysis showed that HelixPR supports closed-form decoding and that HelixPR-highlighted regions are enriched for known transcription-factor binding motifs, supporting both the interpretability and biological relevance of the proposed representation.


## Conclusions
  These results suggest that incorporating the periodic structure of the DNA double helix can improve enhancer modeling. By combining nucleotide identity, helical periodicity, and global compositional information, HelixPR offers a biologically grounded framework for enhancer identification and related regulatory sequence prediction tasks.


## Introduction
  Enhancers are cis-regulatory DNA elements, typically ranging from 50 to 1500 bp in length, that activate the transcription of target genes through the binding of transcription factors (TFs) [1]. They are found in both eukaryotes and prokaryotes and are particularly abundant in the human genome [2]. A typical mammalian cell contains thousands of active enhancers, and more than one million enhancers are estimated to be active across all human cell types [3]. Enhancers are involved in gene regulation during development, evolution, and disease [4, 5] including cancer and heart disease [6]. As a result, accurate enhancer identification remains a central problem in genomics. Unlike proximal regulatory elements such as promoters, enhancers are distal and orientation-independent. They can be located in introns, exons, or intergenic regions, may lie up to one million bp away from target genes, and can even regulate transcription across chromosomes [7]. The positional flexibility makes enhancer identification particularly challenging.

  Experimental approaches, such as chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq) and DNase I sequencing (DNase-seq), can identify enhancer elements by profiling transcription-factor binding, chromatin accessibility, or other regulatory activities. However, these approaches are often time-consuming and expensive. To address these limitations, computational methods have been developed to predict enhancers directly from sequence data. For example, iEnhancer-2L [8] employs Pseudo k-tuple [9] embeddings together with a support vector machine; Enhancer-IF [10], iEnhancer-EL [11], and iEnhancer-XG [12] integrate multiple feature encoding schemes (including EIIP [13] and k-mer [14]) with ensemble learners such as XGBoost, SVM, and random forest to capture different aspects of sequence information; iEnhancer-ELM [15] combines the k-mer method with an attention mechanism for enhancer identification; CapsEnhancer [16] and Caps-ac4C [17] use chaos game representation to encode nucleotide sequences as two-dimensional images, and then apply capsule networks to learn sequence patterns. These studies enable rapid and effective identification of candidate enhancer regions prior to experimental validation.

  However, most existing methods still rely on sequence representations such as one-hot encoding, k-mer encoding, and EIIP. These representations generally model DNA as a one-dimensional ordered string of nucleotides, and therefore do not explicitly characterize the spatial continuity or periodic geometry of the DNA backbone. Better capturing such structural properties may provide additional information for distinguishing enhancer from non-enhancer sequences. In particular, one-hot and EIIP encodings preserve nucleotide identity or physicochemical values at each position, but lack explicit structural priors. Frequency-based k-mer features can capture local compositional patterns, but they discard global nucleotide ordering and are generally non-invertible. Moreover, the k-mer feature space grows exponentially with k, leading to the curse of dimensionality.

  A particularly important structural property missing from these representations is the approximately 10–11 bp helical periodicity observed across eukaryotic and prokaryotic genomes [18]. This periodicity is closely associated with nucleosomal positioning and DNA supercoiling [19], and may provide informative signals for distinguishing regulatory from non-regulatory regions. Recent work has introduced a multi-perspective representation [20] to capture topological properties of DNA in its native three-dimensional conformation, demonstrating that structural encoding can improve downstream classification. Motivated by these findings, we propose the Helix-aware periodic DNA sequence representation (HelixPR), a novel sequence representation that explicitly integrates the three-dimensional helical geometry of the DNA double helix into the feature space for enhancer identification. Unlike conventional encoding schemes that mainly treat DNA as a linear symbolic sequence, HelixPR introduces a biologically motivated periodic prior that reflects the characteristic helical repeat of DNA. The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows. We introduce HelixPR, a sequence representation that explicitly encodes the intrinsic periodic property of the DNA double helix for enhancer classification.HelixPR integrates one-hot encoding, a helix-aware periodic Fourier positional encoding, and a prepended global composition token analogous to the [CLS] token in BERT-based models, thereby combining local nucleotide ordering, helical phase information, and sequence-level compositional statistics within a unified representation.Coupled with a lightweight four-layer one-dimensional CNN, HelixPR achieves strong performance on two benchmark datasets, outperforming existing enhancer identification methods, including methods based on large pre-trained language models.HelixPR is injective and allows exact recovery of the original DNA sequence, thereby preserving sequence information while incorporating biologically meaningful structural priors.


## Materials and methods

### Data source

#### Liu’s dataset
  Enhancers belonging to different subgroups, such as strong versus weak enhancers and poised versus inactive enhancers, exhibit distinct biological activities and regulatory effects on their target genes [4, 8]. Based on chromatin-state information from nine cell lines—H1ES, K562, GM12878, HepG2, HUVEC, HSMM, NHLF, NHEK, and HMEC—the enhancer dataset proposed in [8] consists of DNA sequences segmented into 200 bp fragments. To reduce sequence redundancy, DNA sequences with high similarity (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal}                                                   \usepackage{amsmath}                                                   \usepackage{wasysym}                                                   \usepackage{amsfonts}                                                   \usepackage{amssymb}                                                   \usepackage{amsbsy}                                                   \usepackage{mathrsfs}                                                   \usepackage{upgreek}                                                   \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt}                                                   \begin{document}$$\ge 80\%$$\end{document}) were removed using CD-HIT [21]. The resulting benchmark contains 1,484 enhancers, comprising 742 strong enhancers and 742 weak enhancers, together with 1,484 non-enhancers. For fair comparison, an independent test set was further constructed in [11] following the same protocol; it includes 200 enhancers (100 strong and 100 weak) and 200 non-enhancers. Together, the training set and the independent test set constitute the Liu benchmark.

  Following prior work [11], enhancer classification on Liu’s dataset is evaluated as two sequential binary tasks: (i) distinguishing enhancers from non-enhancers, and (ii) classifying identified enhancers as strong or weak. We train the model for the first task, and then transfer the results to the second task through fine-tuning.


#### Basith’s dataset
  The dataset proposed by [22] consists of eight subsets derived from eight cell lines: epithelial (HEK293), normal human epidermal keratinocyte (NHEK), lymphoblast (K562), B lymphocyte (GM12878), human mammary epithelial cell (HMEC), human skeletal muscle myoblasts (HSMM), normal human lung fibroblasts (NHLF), and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC). Since each cell type exhibits a distinct gene expression signature, which is largely driven by cell type-specific enhancer usage [1], this dataset provides a biologically meaningful benchmark for enhancer identification.

  In the training sets, the numbers of enhancer (positive) and non-enhancer (negative) sequences are balanced, whereas in the test sets, the number of non-enhancer sequences is twice that of enhancer sequences. Compared with a fully balanced setting, Basith’s dataset therefore better reflects real-world classification scenarios with class imbalance. Another major difference between the two benchmarks lies in sequence length. In Liu’s dataset, all sequences are fixed at 200 bp, whereas in Basith’s dataset, sequence lengths range from 204 bp to 2000 bp across different cell lines. Detailed sample counts and further analyses of dataset characteristics, including sequence length, are provided in the Supplementary Material.

#### K-mer encoding
  The k-mer method represents DNA sequences by counting the occurrence frequencies of all possible contiguous subsequences of length k. Given a sequence of length L, it can be tokenized into (L - k + 1) overlapping k-mers. For example, for the sequence \documentclass[12pt]{minimal}                                                   \usepackage{amsmath}                                                   \usepackage{wasysym}                                                   \usepackage{amsfonts}                                                   \usepackage{amssymb}                                                   \usepackage{amsbsy}                                                   \usepackage{mathrsfs}                                                   \usepackage{upgreek}                                                   \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt}                                                   \begin{document}$$S = \texttt {ATCGAT}$$\end{document} with k = 3, the vocabulary size is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal}                                                   \usepackage{amsmath}                                                   \usepackage{wasysym}                                                   \usepackage{amsfonts}                                                   \usepackage{amssymb}                                                   \usepackage{amsbsy}                                                   \usepackage{mathrsfs}                                                   \usepackage{upgreek}                                                   \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt}                                                   \begin{document}$$4^3 = 64$$\end{document}, and only the subsequences {ATC, TCG, CGA, GAT} have non-zero frequencies.

  As k increases, k-mer encoding can capture longer-range local dependencies; however, it also leads to exponential growth of the feature space, resulting in the curse of dimensionality. Conversely, when k is small, multiple distinct sequences may map to identical k-mer embeddings [20]. Formally, k-mer encoding is a many-to-one projection that maps a sequence of length L to a fixed-dimensional frequency vector, thereby irreversibly discarding ordering information. As a result, the original sequence generally cannot be recovered from its k-mer representation, meaning that the encoding is not injective. Moreover, like one-hot encoding, k-mer methods treat DNA as a purely linear string and capture limited information about the three-dimensional structure of the double helix and its periodic property.


#### Electron–ion interaction pseudopotentials(EIIP)
  EIIP encoding represents a DNA sequence as a single numerical signal by assigning each nucleotide its Electron–Ion Interaction Pseudopotential value. Specifically, EIIP maps each nucleotide to a scalar corresponding to the energy of its free electrons: A ↦ 0.1260, T ↦ 0.1335, C ↦ 0.1340 and G ↦ 0.0806.

  Compared with one-hot encoding, EIIP provides a more compact representation and introduces physicochemical information into the sequence encoding. However, it still reduces each nucleotide to a single scalar and does not explicitly preserve richer structural relationships or helical geometric information.


### HelixPR: a helix-aware periodic representation
  The preceding methods treat DNA as a linear sequence of characters, thereby neglecting its three-dimensional physical structure. In reality, a single strand of the DNA double helix traces a regular cylindrical helix in three-dimensional space, which can be parameterized as2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal}                                                   \usepackage{amsmath}                                                   \usepackage{wasysym}                                                   \usepackage{amsfonts}                                                   \usepackage{amssymb}                                                   \usepackage{amsbsy}                                                   \usepackage{mathrsfs}                                                   \usepackage{upgreek}                                                   \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt}                                                   \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} x(t) = r\cos (t), \\ y(t) = r\sin (t), \\ z(t) = ct, \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where r denotes the helical radius and c is the axial rise per unit increase in t. This geometry naturally induces periodic patterns along the sequence axis. Studies in both eukaryotic and prokaryotic genomes have consistently reported a characteristic periodicity of approximately 10–11 bp [18], which has been associated with nucleosomal positioning and DNA supercoiling [19].

  To explicitly encode this periodic three-dimensional structure, we propose HelixPR, a hybrid representation that integrates three complementary components:  a global nucleotide composition token (CLS), per-nucleotide one-hot content, helix-aware periodic Fourier positional encoding.

#### Fourier positional encoding
  As described in Eq. 2, a single strand of the DNA double helix can be approximated by a cylindrical helical trajectory in three-dimensional space. Conventional sequence representations can be viewed as considering only the projection of this trajectory onto the helical axis, namely the linear order along the z-axis, while omitting the periodic phase information revealed by its projection onto the xOy plane.   In this work, we set T=10 to approximate the well-established 10∼ 11 bp helical repeat of the B-form DNA double helix. This formulation allows the positional encoding to reflect the helical phase of nucleotides, rather than merely serving as an abstract index-based position embedding. Biologically, nucleotides or regulatory motifs separated by approximately one helical turn may have similar spatial orientations on the DNA helix, which may influence transcription factor binding and enhancer regulatory organization. Unlike generic positional encodings that mainly provide position indices, our objective is not to construct a general-purpose position representation. Instead, we aim to inject a biologically motivated periodic prior into the feature space and concentrate the encoding on the characteristic structural scale of the DNA double helix. The effect of this design choice is empirically validated in “Parameter analysis and control experiments” section.

### Prediction framework for enhancer identification

#### Data pre-processing
  Since sequence lengths vary across different cell lines in Basith’s dataset, all sequences were zero-padded to a unified length \documentclass[12pt]{minimal}                                                   \usepackage{amsmath}                                                   \usepackage{wasysym}                                                   \usepackage{amsfonts}                                                   \usepackage{amssymb}                                                   \usepackage{amsbsy}                                                   \usepackage{mathrsfs}                                                   \usepackage{upgreek}                                                   \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt}                                                   \begin{document}$$L_{\max }$$\end{document}, defined as the maximum sequence length observed in the corresponding training set. Padding was appended to the end of each sequence to preserve the original order of the valid positions. In contrast, no padding was required for Liu’s dataset, since all sequences in that benchmark have the same fixed length.

  Feature-wise standardization was then applied. Specifically, the channel-wise mean and standard deviation were computed over all valid, non-padded positions in the training set. The resulting statistics were then used to normalize the training, validation, and test sets, thereby avoiding data leakage from the test sets.


#### CNN-based sequence modeling

The model is designed to capture local sequential patterns from the input feature and progressively transform them into a compact global representation for downstream binary classification.

  The proposed CNN consists of four convolutional feature extraction blocks, followed by a global pooling layer and a fully connected classifier. This design enables the model to learn hierarchical local sequence patterns and aggregate them into a fixed-dimensional representation for binary classification. Each block contains three components: a one-dimensional convolution layer, a rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation function, and a batch normalization layer. #### Training strategy and optimization
  The model was optimized using the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal}                                                   \usepackage{amsmath}                                                   \usepackage{wasysym}                                                   \usepackage{amsfonts}                                                   \usepackage{amssymb}                                                   \usepackage{amsbsy}                                                   \usepackage{mathrsfs}                                                   \usepackage{upgreek}                                                   \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt}                                                   \begin{document}$$1\times 10^{-4}$$\end{document}, and cross-entropy loss was adopted as the objective function. Considering the differences in dataset size, sequence length between the two benchmark datasets, dataset-specific training strategies were adopted.

  For Liu’s benchmark dataset, all models were trained for 40 epochs. This relatively short training schedule is appropriate because Liu’s dataset is small, containing only 1,484 training sequences, and all sequences have the same fixed length. These properties make the optimization process less challenging, while limiting the number of epochs helps reduce the risk of overfitting.

  For Basith’s benchmark dataset, the original training set of each cell line was further split into a training set and a validation set at a ratio of 9:1. All models were trained for 100 epochs, and the final model for each of the eight cell lines was selected according to its performance on the corresponding validation set. Stratified sampling was used for the validation split to preserve the positive-to-negative ratio.

  To assess the stability of the proposed representation under the same model architecture, we repeated the CNN experiments with different embeddings using five random seeds and reported the results as the mean ± standard deviation. The same set of random seeds was used for all embeddings to ensure a fair comparison.

However, it was not uniformly better than k-mer or EIIP on every metric. For instance, on GM12878, 4-mer achieved a slightly higher BACC than one-hot, and EIIP achieved a higher MCC. On HSMM and K562, EIIP showed higher sensitivity than one-hot, although this was accompanied by lower specificity. Similarly, k-mer representations sometimes achieved higher specificity, such as on GM12878, K562, and NHLF. These results indicate that different encodings produce different sensitivity–specificity trade-offs, while HelixPR consistently improves the overall balance.

  The performance of HelixPR+CNN was not uniform across all Basith subsets, suggesting that enhancer prediction difficulty varies among cell-line-specific datasets. Some subsets, such as GM12878, HSMM, and HUVEC, reached very high BACC and AUC values, whereas HMEC and NHLF remained relatively more challenging. For example, on HMEC, HelixPR improved BACC from 0.7515 with one-hot encoding to 0.8931 and increased AUC from 0.8504 to 0.9758, but its final performance was still lower than that observed in the easier subsets. These results indicate that HelixPR consistently improves the baseline representation, while the achievable performance still depends on the intrinsic separability of each cell-line-specific dataset.

  To better understand the striking results on several Basith subsets, we added analyses of train–test redundancy, sequence length, GC content, CpG frequency, and nucleotide/dinucleotide composition in Supplementary Material. The results did not reveal direct train–test leakage, and simple sequence features alone were insufficient to explain the CNN performance. Length showed relatively strong discriminative power in some subsets, such as GM12878 and HEK293, but was much weaker in harder subsets such as HMEC. GC content, CpG frequency, and nucleotide/dinucleotide frequencies generally showed lower discriminative power. These findings suggest that sequence length, composition bias, and cell-line-specific sequence properties may affect the difficulty of individual Basith subsets, but they do not fully explain the performance gains. The consistent improvement over one-hot encoding supports the effectiveness of HelixPR in providing additional helix-aware sequence information for enhancer prediction.

### Comparison between results on Liu’s dataset and Basith’s dataset
  Notably, the improvement of HelixPR over baseline methods is moderate on Liu’s dataset but more pronounced on Basith’s dataset. The two benchmarks differ in several aspects. Liu’s dataset is constructed as a general enhancer prediction benchmark by combining enhancer sequences from multiple cell lines, and all sequences have a fixed length of 200 bp. In contrast, the Basith benchmark evaluates enhancer prediction separately for each cell line, with larger training sets and variable sequence lengths ranging from 204 bp to 2000 bp.

  These differences may affect the relative contribution of different sequence representations. On Liu’s dataset, one-hot encoding with CNN already provides competitive performance, and HelixPR mainly offers an additional improvement. On Basith’s dataset, the longer sequences and cell-line-specific subsets provide a setting in which positional and periodic sequence information can contribute more substantially.

  Overall, the results on the two datasets indicate that HelixPR is effective across different benchmark settings. The comparison further suggests that helical positional information is a useful complement to conventional nucleotide representations, especially in datasets with longer sequences, larger sample sizes, and cell-line-specific enhancer prediction tasks.

### Transfer learning to enhancer strength classification
  Building on the enhancer identification results, we further evaluate whether the HelixPR-based representation learned for enhancer identification can be transferred to enhancer strength classification, which aims to distinguish strong enhancers from weak enhancers. This task is more challenging than binary enhancer identification because both strong and weak enhancers belong to the enhancer class and therefore share many common sequence characteristics. As a result, the discriminative patterns between them are more subtle and harder to capture. In addition, the available enhancer-strength training set is relatively small, containing 742 strong and 742 weak enhancer sequences. Training a model from scratch on this limited dataset may not fully exploit the general enhancer-related features learned from the enhancer identification task.

  To address this issue, we adopt a transfer learning strategy. The convolutional backbone pretrained on enhancer identification is used to initialize the strength classification model, while the original output layer is replaced with a new task-specific classification head for distinguishing strong and weak enhancers. A two-phase fine-tuning procedure is then applied. In the first phase, the pretrained backbone is frozen, and only the newly initialized classification head is optimized to adapt to the strength classification task. In the second phase, all parameters are unfrozen and jointly optimized with a smaller learning rate, allowing the model to refine the transferred feature representations in an end-to-end manner. ### Parameter analysis and control experiments
  To determine the two key parameters used in HelixPR, namely the scaling factor α  and the periodicity T, we conducted a hyperparameter sensitivity analysis on Liu’s dataset. A controlled one-factor-at-a-time strategy was adopted. Specifically, when the effect of α  was analyzed, T was fixed at 10; when the effect of T was analyzed, α  was fixed at 0.05. This setting allows the influence of each hyperparameter to be evaluated independently.

  The corresponding performance on the Liu’s test set is shown in Fig. 4. For the scaling factor α , HelixPR achieves the most favorable overall performance at α =0.05. Although larger values of α , such as 0.1 and 0.5, lead to slightly higher AUC values, their ACC and MCC decrease, indicating less balanced classification performance.Fig. 4Parameter comparison on Liu’s dataset

  For the periodicity parameter T, we further evaluated several values around and beyond the DNA helical periodicity, including T=3, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 20, and 50. The results show that T=10 achieves the best overall performance, with the highest ACC, SN, MCC, and AUC among the tested periods. In comparison, nearby periods such as T=8 and T=9 also produce competitive AUC values, but their ACC and MCC are lower than those obtained with T=10. Periods farther from 10, such as T=12, 20, and 50, generally lead to weaker performance. These observations are consistent with the biological prior that one DNA helical turn is approximately 10 base pairs, supporting the use of T=10 in HelixPR. Based on the above analysis, we set α =0.05 and T=10 in all experiments.

## Conclusions
  In this study, we proposed HelixPR, a helix-aware periodic DNA sequence representation for enhancer identification. Unlike conventional encoding methods that mainly treat DNA as a linear symbolic sequence, HelixPR explicitly incorporates the periodic geometry of the DNA double helix into the input representation. By combining per-nucleotide one-hot content, helix-aware Fourier positional encoding, and a global nucleotide-composition token, HelixPR provides a compact, interpretable, and biologically motivated representation for regulatory sequence modeling.

  Experimental results on two standard benchmark datasets demonstrate that these design advantages translate into practical predictive gains. Under the same CNN architecture, HelixPR consistently outperformed one-hot, EIIP, and frequency-based k-mer representations, highlighting the benefit of incorporating helical periodic information into DNA sequence features. Moreover, HelixPR combined with a lightweight four-layer CNN achieved better overall performance than previously reported methods, including models based on large pre-trained language models. On Liu’s benchmark, the HelixPR-based CNN also showed strong transferability when applied to enhancer strength classification. These results suggest that accurate enhancer prediction and downstream strength classification can be achieved without relying on complex architectures, emphasizing the importance of biologically meaningful representation design in genomic prediction tasks. PCA-based visualizations further showed that HelixPR helps the CNN learn more discriminative feature spaces across different benchmarks. In addition, attribution-guided motif enrichment analysis showed that HelixPR-highlighted regions were enriched for known transcription-factor binding motifs, supporting the biological interpretability of the learned sequence patterns.

  The ablation and parameter analyses further confirmed the effectiveness of the main components of HelixPR. The Fourier positional encoding improved the one-hot baseline, and the global composition token provided complementary sequence-level information. Notably, the best performance was obtained when the helical period was set to T=10, consistent with the known 10–11 bp periodicity of the DNA double helix. HelixPR also outperformed alternative positional encoding strategies, suggesting that its advantage comes from the biologically motivated helical prior rather than from simply adding positional information.

  Another important property of HelixPR is its injectivity. In contrast to frequency-based k-mer representations, which cannot establish a one-to-one correspondence with the original sequence, HelixPR retains the invertibility of one-hot encoding and supports exact sequence recovery. Beyond one-hot encoding, HelixPR further incorporates biologically meaningful periodic information while preserving nucleotide identity.

  Future work may explore adaptive periodic encoding schemes that can account for variations in DNA helical structure across different genomic contexts. In addition, integrating HelixPR with other regulatory genomic features, such as chromatin accessibility and transcription factor binding profiles, may further improve enhancer modeling. Overall, this study suggests that incorporating the periodic geometry of the DNA double helix into sequence representations is a promising direction for computational enhancer analysis and broader regulatory sequence modeling.
