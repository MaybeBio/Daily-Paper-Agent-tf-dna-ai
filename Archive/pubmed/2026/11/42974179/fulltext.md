# A Dual Mechanism-Based Physiologically Based Pharmacokinetic Model for Predicting Mitoxantrone and Liposomal Mitoxantrone Disposition: Preclinical Validation in Rats and Human Extrapolation with Clinical Evaluation


## Purpose
  This study aimed to develop a physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) model to quantitatively predict plasma and tissue concentrations of both liposomal-encapsulated and free mitoxantrone in rats and further extrapolate the model to human clinical scenarios.


## Methods
  A dual-compartment PBPK framework was constructed to separately describe the disposition of liposomal carriers and released free mitoxantrone. Global sensitivity analysis (GSA) based on partial rank correlation coefficient (PRCC) was performed to identify key parameters. The external validation was evaluated using rat plasma pharmacokinetic and human clinical data from previous reported studies. Allometric scaling was applied to extrapolate the model to adult humans, and Monte Carlo simulation was utilized to predict clinical plasma concentrations under fixed clinical doses of 20, 30, and 40 mg in lymphoma patients.


## Results
  PRCC analysis results demonstrated that tissue DNA binding capacity (T_DNA) and tissue-to-plasma liposome partition coefficient (Kp_n) were the most dominant parameters governing free drug and liposomal exposure, respectively (PRCC > 0.9). Overall average absolute fold error (AAFE) for tissue predictions was below 2.0. A slightly higher AAFE of 2.16 was observed for free drug submodel, which still complied with the widely accepted 3-fold error threshold for PBPK validation. Model external validation results also demonstrated acceptable predictive capacity of the model for both total and free mitoxantrone concentrations across preclinical and clinical dose levels (AAFE < 3). For human extrapolation, over 92% of clinically observed plasma concentrations from 37 lymphoma patients fell within the model’s 90% predictive interval.


## Conclusion
  This study established a robust, mechanism-driven PBPK model that distinguished encapsulated and free mitoxantrone disposition. The model achieved reliable prediction performance and effectively interprets pharmacokinetic characteristics of PEGylated liposomal mitoxantrone. The present framework provided a valuable mechanistic reference for the construction and optimization of liposomal mitoxantrone PBPK models and supported future translational pharmacokinetic studies of liposomal formulations.


## Introduction
  Mitoxantrone is a synthetic anthracenedione cytotoxic agent with broad-spectrum anti-tumor activity against breast cancer, acute leukemia, and non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma, which is also approved as a disease-modifying treatment for multiple sclerosis.1–3 However, systemic administration of mitoxantrone is severely restricted by dose-limiting toxicities, including myelosuppression, gastrointestinal irritation, and irreversible cumulative cardiotoxicity. Especially, cardiac adverse events originate from sustained retention of mitoxantrone within cardiomyocytes mediated by high-affinity DNA binding, which imposes strict limitations on total cumulative clinical dosage and impairs long-term therapeutic benefits.1 Therefore, PEGylated liposomal mitoxantrone was developed by encapsulating drug within liposomes that are approximately 60 nm in size and modified with polyethylene glycol (PEG) which could mitigate systemic toxicities and prolong circulation time in body.4

  By forming a steric barrier, PEGylation reduces the process of opsonization and the uptake by the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS), thereby extending the systemic circulation duration of the therapeutic agents.5 Liposomal mitoxantrone, as a PEGylated liposomal formulation, leverages these benefits to enhance the pharmacokinetic properties of mitoxantrone, a powerful anthracenedione anticancer compound. Compared to traditional mitoxantrone injection, the liposomal encapsulation is designed to reduce systemic toxicity while maintaining its antitumor effectiveness. Despite clinical approval, the in vivo disposition kinetics of liposomal mitoxantrone remain incompletely characterized due to multi-step complex kinetic processes, including nanoparticle transcapillary extravasation, macrophage phagocytosis in tissue, intracellular liposome degradation, and sustained slow release of free mitoxantrone. Conventional non-compartmental analysis (NCA) cannot separate encapsulated and free drug concentrations, failing to distinguish tissue distribution behaviors in different formulation or quantify tissue free drug exposure related to therapeutic efficacy and organ-specific toxicity. A thorough comprehension of mitoxantrone pharmacokinetics is crucial for distinguishing behaviors and therapeutic impacts that are specific to liposomal formulations.

  Physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) model provides a mechanistic framework to link drug physicochemical properties, species-specific physiological anatomy and in vivo tissue distribution. By combining drug physicochemical characteristics with physiological parameters unique to each system, including organ blood flow rates and volume of tissues, PBPK models can adeptly simulate how drugs distributed dynamically within particular tissues. Specifically, in the case of nanomedicines that are susceptible to immune system recognition, integrating particular MPS sub-compartments into the model allows for a more precise representation of the uptake and degradation processes that immune cells mediate in the liver, spleen, and other reticuloendothelial organs.6–8

  The mechanistic PBPK model of free mitoxantrone reported by An and Morris incorporated DNA and macrophage binding, which substantially outperformed conventional tissue partition coefficient-based models and provided a foundational framework for describing tissue sequestration of mitoxantrone. Furthermore, the prior work did not include quantitative goodness-of-fit assessment or global sensitivity analysis to characterize model uncertainty. Therefore, it is not applicable for simulating the dual disposition patterns of liposome-encapsulated and subsequently-released free mitoxantrone.9

  In this study, we developed and validated a novel dual-mechanism PBPK model for PEGylated liposomal mitoxantrone which refers to two coupled disposition subsystems within the model. The liposomal carrier subsystem, which described the disposition of nanoparticle-associated mitoxantrone through MPS uptake, liposomal degradation; The drug release and the free drug subsystem, which described the disposition of released mitoxantrone through transcapillary transport, DNA binding, macromolecular binding, and elimination. These two subsystems are coupled through the release and degradation processes. This PBPK model has three core innovations. Firstly, dual independent sub-systems to separately simulate liposomal carrier and released free mitoxantrone. Accurate differentiation between total and free drug concentrations carries critical clinical implications for liposomal anthracycline therapy, as highlighted in the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) regulatory guidance for nanocarrier-based drug products.10 Neglecting the distinction between these two drug forms may lead to misleading interpretation of pharmacokinetic profiles, exposure-response relationships outcomes. Accordingly, this dual-subsystem modelling strategy, which separately describes carrier-associated and released-free drug kinetics, represents a core conceptual advantage of the present PBPK framework. Secondly, tissue-specific MPS subcompartments in liver, spleen and lung to reproduce liposome sequestration and degradation mediated by macrophage. Thirdly, saturable intracellular DNA and macromolecule binding module to recapitulate mitoxantrone long term tissue retention. The model was calibrated and validated using rat plasma and multi-organ concentration-time data, followed by global sensitivity analysis (GSA) to screen dominant regulatory parameters. After allometric interspecies scaling, Monte Carlo simulation combined with clinical plasma data from lymphoma patients was applied to verify human predictive performance. This work aims to establish a generalizable construction strategy for liposomal nanomedicine PBPK models and deliver a quantitative tool to support rational clinical dose design and hypothesis generation regarding tissue exposure and potential toxicity risk.


## Methods

### Chemicals, Reference Standards and Formulations and Animals
  Reference standards and chemicals were obtained as follows: mitoxantrone hydrochloride (purity > 99%; Lot: 52010301) and mitoxantrone hydrochloride liposome injection (10 mg/10 mL; Lot: 069220807) were gifted by the CSPC Pharmaceutical Group (Shijiazhuang, China), the parameters of mitoxantrone hydrochloride liposome injection were shown in Table S1; HPLC-grade methanol and acetonitrile were purchased from Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Formic acid and ammonium acetate were obtained from Mreda Technology Inc. (Dallas, TX, USA). Ultra-pure water was provided by the Watson and Company (Guangzhou, China). The blank plasma utilized was sourced from healthy volunteers at the Fourth Hospital of Hebei Medical University (Shijiazhuang, China).


### Preclinical Animal Experiments and Sample Collection
  Male Sprague–Dawley rats (250 ± 20 g) were supplied by the Experimental Animal Research Center (Shijiazhuang, China). All rats were housed in a temperature-controlled room (20–25 °C) with relative humidity maintained at 50%–60%, with free access to standard chow and drinking water. Animals were fasted for 12 h before dosing with unrestricted water supply. All animal procedures were conducted in strict compliance with the National Research Council’s Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of The Fourth Hospital of Hebei Medical University (Approval No. IACUC-4th Hos Hebmu-20250207).

  Two intravenous bolus administration groups were set: free mitoxantrone solution (5 mg/kg) and liposomal mitoxantrone (5 mg/kg). Subsequently, the rats were maintained under deep anesthesia with isoflurane and euthanized by cervical dislocation, in accordance with the guidelines of the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA). Death was confirmed by cessation of respiration and absence of reflexes. Then, blood and tissue samples (brain, lung, heart, liver, spleen, kidney) were collected at predefined time points. For tissue samples, three independent rats were used at each timepoint and six independent rats were collected per timepoint. For free mitoxantrone group: 0.083, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 24 h after dosing and 0.25, 1, 4, 8, 12, 24, 48 h for liposomal mitoxantrone group.


### Patient Plasma Sample from Clinical Patients
  The human plasma samples were collected from clinical patients with lymphoma, including T-cell lymphoma, NK/T cell lymphoma and other lymphoma subtypes, who received liposomal mitoxantrone-based chemotherapy regimens, the dosage of liposomal mitoxantrone administered was 20 mg, 30mg or 40mg. All the procedures were undertaken by the Declaration of Helsinki, and the study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Ethics Committee of the Fourth Hospital of Hebei Medical University (No. 2026KY021, Hebei, China). Written informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study.


### Bioanalytical Assays for Total and Free Mitoxantrone
  Total mitoxantrone concentrations in plasma and tissue homogenates were quantified via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) following complete liposome disruption through methanol precipitation and ultrasonic treatment, while free mitoxantrone, defined as the drug unbound to liposomal carriers, was measured by LC-MS/MS analysis of elution solution isolated using a solid-phase extraction (SPE) method. The concentration of encapsulated mitoxantrone was indirectly calculated by subtracting the quantified free mitoxantrone concentration from the total mitoxantrone concentration. Detailed pretreatment procedures and instrumental parameters were described in a previously validated analytical method published by our group.11 The PK parameters were analyzed based on a noncompartmental analysis (NCA) using WinNonlin software (version 8.3.1, Pharsight, USA). The terminal elimination rate constant (λz) was automatically identified by the software by screening contiguous post Tmax time segments and selecting the segment yielding the largest adjusted R2 value; a minimum of three quantifiable concentration points was required for λz estimation. The terminal half life was subsequently calculated as ln(2)/λz. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed on log-transformed AUC0→∞ and Cmax.


### Structure of Dual-Mechanism PBPK Model
  Multiple preliminary model architectures were pre-evaluated, including single perfusion-limited tissue compartments and simplified permeability-limited nanoparticle models. Final model selection was comprehensively judged by Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), coefficient of variation (CV%) of fitted parameters and physiological plausibility. In this research, the dual-mechanism PBPK model framework was built according to physiological characteristic and disposition process of liposomal medicine, and compartment structure in specific organ was evaluated by the lowest AIC and optimal fitting performance across all tested tissues. The whole-body model contained two interconnected sub-models: liposomal carrier disposition and free mitoxantrone disposition, linked by MPS-mediated liposome degradation and free drug release (Figure 1). #### PBPK Submodel for PEGylated Liposomal Mitoxantrone
  Nanoparticle liposome transport across capillary endothelium follows permeability-limited kinetics, rather than perfusion-limited small-molecule transport. Each tissue was divided into vascular and extravascular subcompartments, this structure is essential because the distribution of nanoparticles from blood to tissue is limited by membrane permeability rather than blood flow.6 Additional MPS subcompartments were embedded within liver, spleen, and lung to describe macrophage uptake, reversible liposome release, and intracellular degradation to free mitoxantrone. Key assumptions as follows: intact liposomes cannot be directly cleared by glomerular filtration or hepatic sinusoidal excretion; all carrier elimination relies on MPS degradation; released free mitoxantrone directly enters corresponding tissue compartments of the free drug submodel.

### Global Sensitivity Analysis via PRCC Method
  Global sensitivity analysis was conducted using Latin hypercube sampling (LHS) combined with partial rank correlation coefficient (PRCC). Uncertain PBPK parameters were independently sampled using LHS), each parameter was assigned a log-uniform distribution ranging from 0.5-fold to 2-fold of baseline value. LHS was applied to generate 1000 parameter sets to efficiently and evenly cover the full parameter uncertainty space. The PBPK model was run for all sampled parameter combinations, and the total plasma AUC from 0 to 48 h (Plasma_Total_AUC0_48) was selected as the primary output endpoint. Parameters were stratified by influence strength: strong (|PRCC| > 0.5), moderate (0.3 < |PRCC| ≤ 0.5), weak (|PRCC| ≤ 0.3). Parameters with weak sensitivity were fixed during final optimization to reduce computational complexity without altering core prediction outcomes.


### Human PBPK Model and Monte Carlo Simulation
  Rat-to-human model extrapolation was strictly implemented based on physiological scaling and predefined parameter classification. MPS kinetic parameters (Kup, Krel, and Kdeg) were directly inherited from the calibrated rat PBPK model and further humanized according to human organ weight and MPS cell abundance per gram of tissue. Most tissue-binding parameters, including T_DNA, T_macro, K_DNA and K_macro, were directly transferred from the rat model. Tissue permeability (PS) and clearance (CL) parameters were allometrically scaled from rat values using a fixed power exponent of 0.75 based on human organ mass. Monte Carlo simulation was performed with 1000 iterations to characterize interindividual variability and prediction uncertainty, sampling physiological and kinetic parameters from log-normal distributions with specified CVs. Only parameters with |PRCC| ≥0.3 were included in random sampling. Partition coefficient CV was set to 20%, while physiological and drug-specific kinetic parameters adopted CV = 30%, appropriate for Monte Carlo protocols for liposomal nanoparticle PBPK models which was selected to represent moderate interindividual variability and parameter uncertainty.21–25 All parameter distributions were truncated between the 5th and 95th percentiles to eliminate extreme outliers, the simulated concentrations were used as the 90% prediction interval. Simulations of single and repeated multi-cycle administration (6 cycles, total 168 days) were conducted at clinical fixed doses of 20, 30, and 40 mg.


### Model Validation
  Model validation was performed at both preclinical and clinical levels. First, rat plasma concentration–time profiles of mitoxantrone simulated by the PBPK model were validated against in vivo experimental data in rats at dosages at 0.5mg/kg and 2mg/kg.26 For clinical external validation, human plasma total and free mitoxantrone concentrations were assessed at two dose levels of 12 mg/m2 and 18 mg/m2.27 Model predictive performance was quantitatively evaluated by comparing simulated concentration–time profiles with observed data. The AAFE and the percentage of observed time points within 3-fold prediction error were calculated. Predicted concentration–time profiles were overlaid with observed data to assess the model’s ability to reproduce formulation-specific plasma exposure patterns.


## Results

### Plasma and Tissue Noncompartmental Pharmacokinetic Analysis in Rats
  Compared to the free drug formulation, the administration of liposomal mitoxantrone resulted in a significant increase in systemic exposure, as evidenced by higher AUC0-∞ and Cmax values for both total and free mitoxantrone (Table 4). The apparent volume of distribution at Vss of total mitoxantrone was substantially reduced after liposomal administration. In contrast, the calculated Vss of released free mitoxantrone after liposomal administration was substantially larger (21,422 mL/kg), reflecting the extensive tissue distribution of the released free drug. Following intravenous administration of 5 mg/kg mitoxantrone solution, mitoxantrone was rapidly eliminated from plasma, exhibiting a Cmax of 2.13 µg/mL, an AUC0-∞ of 0.508 µg·h/mL, and a terminal half-life of 1.38 hours. In contrast, liposomal mitoxantrone significantly increased the systemic exposure of total mitoxantrone, with a Cmax of 87.13 µg/mL and an AUC0-∞ of 1431.12 µg·h/mL, resulting in a prolonged terminal half-life (T1/2) of 23.14 hours. Additionally, total systemic clearance (CLtotal) for mitoxantrone was consistently lower following liposomal administration, leading to prolonged systemic exposure and an extended terminal half-life. In addition, the tissue distribution profiles of liposomal drugs differed markedly from those of conventional formulations. Significant differences in tissue exposure were observed between the two formulations. In the kidney, the Cmax of mitoxantrone was significantly lower in the liposomal group compared to the solution group (22.6 ± 7.2 μg/mL vs 45.3 ± 8.1 μg/mL, p < 0.05), while the AUC0-∞ remained comparable (p > 0.05). In the liver, the solution group exhibited a significantly higher Cmax (26.1 ± 4.3 μg/mL vs 11.5 ± 2.8 μg/mL, p < 0.05) but a lower AUC0-∞ (152.8 ± 26.8 μg·h/mL vs 282.1 ± 61.0 μg·h/mL, p < 0.05). ## Discussion
  In this research we developed a novel dedicated PBPK model for liposomal mitoxantrone and pharmacokinetic parameters were assessed to compare the tissue distribution characteristics of the two formulations which could describe drug disposition in vivo. PEGylated liposomal encapsulation drastically reshaped mitoxantrone in vivo distribution kinetics by three core mechanisms: steric PEG shielding reduces rapid MPS clearance to prolong systemic circulation; nanoparticle transcapillary permeability modulates tissue extravasation; MPS macrophage-mediated slow degradation sustains low level free drug release over extended periods.31–33 The 60 nm particle size of liposomal mitoxantrone exceeded the threshold for glomerular filtration and hepatic sinusoidal transcellular transport; all intact liposome elimination depends entirely on MPS phagocytosis and intracellular degradation, consistent with established nanoparticle elimination principles.

  NCA data demonstrated drastically elevated plasma AUC and prolonged half-life for liposomal mitoxantrone, with reduced early-stage non-specific peripheral tissue distribution compared with free mitoxantrone solution. Organs abundant in vascular fenestrations and tissue macrophages (liver, spleen, lung) exhibited enhanced liposome accumulation, consistent with nanoparticle extravasation and MPS phagocytosis rules.8 The highest total drug exposure observed in the kidney for both formulations is consistent with prior anthracycline studies.4,9 Relatively high renal DNA content provides a plausible mechanistic basis for intracellular trapping of mitoxantrone via high-affinity DNA binding, though this causal relationship cannot be definitively verified using the present data, and other renal processes may also contribute.34 Clinically relevant low free mitoxantrone fraction in plasma (<2%) arises from two synergistic factors: high plasma stability of intact liposomes and rapid tissue sequestration of released free drug driven by strong DNA binding affinity, consistent with report in the literature.35 The 48h sampling window for the rat experiments was adequate for characterizing the free drug disposition, but may slightly underestimate the terminal tissue retention of liposomal mitoxantrone, which has a terminal half life of approximately 23 hours and shows significant accumulation in MPS-rich organs (liver, spleen). Longer terminal sampling would strengthen the validation of the liposomal submodel, particularly for MPS-rich tissue compartments. Consistent with previous clinical observations, free mitoxantrone exhibits extremely low intrinsic permeability across the intact blood-brain barrier (BBB), which supports the low brain partition coefficient adopted in the current PBPK model.36 Nevertheless, our in vivo rat data demonstrated markedly higher brain accumulation after liposomal mitoxantrone administration compared with the free drug formulation, several complementary mechanisms collectively contribute to the enhanced brain exposure of liposomal mitoxantrone observed in this study. First, PEGylated long circulating liposomes effectively reduce reticuloendothelial system mediated clearance and mitigate the rapid distribution, metabolism, and elimination of free drug, thereby substantially elevating systemic plasma AUC and prolonging drug residence within brain microvessels. Liposomal encapsulation also minimizes nonspecific plasma protein binding and peripheral tissue sequestration, sustaining higher circulating drug levels and indirectly increasing brain microvascular drug exposure even without overt structural disruption of the BBB.37 Second, nanocarrier administration may induce subtle and transient alterations in BBB integrity via mild endothelial responses, further facilitating peripheral drug access to brain microvascular compartments.38 Third, brain microvascular endothelial cells can internalize liposomal nanoparticles through multiple endocytic pathways, including clathrin- and caveolae-mediated uptake, leading to intracellular nanoparticle accumulation within the vascular endothelium without deep parenchymal penetration.39 Finally, liposomal encapsulation temporarily shields mitoxantrone from recognition and extrusion by apical efflux transporters such as P-gp and BCRP, effectively reducing transporter-mediated brain clearance. Endocytic internalization further enables partial bypass of membrane efflux activity, although released cytoplasmic drug remains susceptible to efflux elimination, rendering this protective mechanism dependent on liposomal release kinetics and intracellular trafficking patterns.40 This elevated microvascular drug burden within the brain may increase the risk of local endothelial toxicity and subclinical central nervous system adverse events, which warrants careful consideration during clinical application of liposomal mitoxantrone.

  Mitoxantrone, similar to other anticancer drugs, is known to bind tightly to DNA, resulting in cytotoxicity. In addition to DNA binding, mitoxantrone has also been reported to bind to macromolecules in tissue, including microsomal protein. Previous studies have shown that models incorporating traditional tissue partition coefficients did not fit the plasma distribution of mitoxantrone well.17 Based on published research, we constructed a mechanism-based PBPK model that incorporates macromolecule binding to describe the mitoxantrone solution model.41,42 The fitting results align with the pharmacokinetic characteristics of mitoxantrone, with an estimated intrinsic hepatic clearance of 37.75 mL/min; this value is significantly larger than the renal clearance (2.14 mL/min), which is consistent with reports indicating that biliary excretion is the primary excretory pathway for mitoxantrone.43 Additionally, earlier pharmacokinetic investigations conducted in rats demonstrated that the observed biliary clearance of mitoxantrone fell within the range of 35.4 to 50.74 mL/min, a result consistent with that observed in the current study.26 It is worth noting that the sample size in the tissue distribution study was relatively small, and the non-significant differences observed in other tissue may be attributable to limited statistical power rather than true equivalence between formulations.

  Existing PEGylated liposome PBPK research mainly focuses on liposomal doxorubicin, with two major limitations: absence of integrated intracellular DNA binding modules and inability to separately quantify encapsulated and free drug concentrations.6,7 Prior mitoxantrone PBPK model only simulated free drug disposition without liposomal carrier sub-systems.9 This work established a dedicated dual-subsystem architecture tailored to mitoxantrone’s unique DNA-binding cytotoxicity. Unlike simplified single global MPS compartments in previous publications, this model embedded tissue-specific MPS subcompartments with independent macrophage density values for liver, spleen, and lung, significantly improving prediction accuracy of nanoparticle uptake and intracellular degradation kinetics. Sensitivity analysis results showed that Kp_n and kup_MPS exert a significant influence on the outputs of the liposome submodel. As Kp_n increasing, the drug exposure in the corresponding tissue rises. However, Kp_n is not the sole factor governing the distribution of liposomes. Liposomes can also enter tissues by MPS cells uptaking. Kup_MPS determines the rate at which nanoparticles were transported from plasma to MPS-rich tissues. The drug accumulated in MPS compartments and further degraded into free drug. The overall validation results of both submodel were good, which supports the validity of incorporating macromolecule binding and the MPS compartment in model development. Table 3 contains several parameters with CV values greater than 50%, for distinguishing between parameter insensitivity and structural/practical non-identifiability, a local Fisher Information Matrix (FIM) and sensitivity analysis were implemented (Figure S1). All pairwise parameter correlations were weak-to-moderate (|r| ≤ 0.51), below the (|r| > 0.80–0.90) cutoff for severe parameter compensation confirmed that the high CVs (>50%) parameters (CT_2, Krel_MPS and Kdeg_MPS) were not attributable to collinearity. Rather, the uncertainty of these parameters reflected practical non-identifiability stemming from low local sensitivity and limited information in macroscopic PK data. The Kdeg_MPS was >5 orders of magnitude larger than Krel_MPS, so liposome degradation dominates after MPS internalization; model outputs are largely insensitive to Krel_MPS, causing wide parameter uncertainty. For CT_2, liver and spleen sinusoidal fenestrations provide a minimal barrier to nanoparticle extravasation, meaning CT_2 was not rate-limiting in our model; Tissue perfusion and MPS uptake govern uptake, resulting in low sensitivity of PK profiles to CT_2 changes. Considering the inherent difficulties in measuring free drug concentrations, we employed a fully validated UPLC-MS/MS assay to quantify plasma free mitoxantrone in the present work from our research group.11 Notably, free drug concentration validation was for the first time integrated into the liposomal PBPK model, making a substantial contribution to enhancing the model’s prediction performance. However, the validation accuracy of the liposomal PBPK model was slightly lower than the solution PBPK model based on AFE and AAFE results (Figure 3), which could be attributed to the unique and complex in vivo behaviors of liposomes, as well as the limitations of model structure and parameters. The liposomal PBPK model tended to underestimate the free mitoxantrone concentration‑time profile in plasma. The most probable cause lies in the great challenges in determining the free concentration of nanomedicines, as the analytical method demands higher precision and stricter testing conditions.

  A significant advantage of the PBPK model is its capacity to facilitate species extrapolation. Specifically, the PBPK model enables the prediction of drug concentration at the site of action, which correlates more directly with drug efficacy than the drug concentration–time profile observed in blood.44 Determining the drug concentration at the pharmacodynamic site within human bodies often poses challenges when relying solely on experimental methods. Extrapolating preclinical PBPK outputs to human clinical scenarios is a powerful strategy to overcome the bottleneck that tissue-level drug exposure cannot be directly quantified via clinical sampling. In this interspecies scaling workflow, T_DNA and T_macro of tissue were fixed across rat and human compartments. DNA and intracellular macromolecular binding potentials represent intrinsic biochemical properties of mammalian tissues, with minimal interspecies divergence reported for anthracenedione anticancer agents in prior mechanistic PK studies, justifying our fixed-parameter scaling strategy.12,45 PRCC sensitivity analysis results in human model showed that renal T_DNA was the dominant parameter negatively regulating systemic drug AUC0_48, as enhanced renal DNA binding sequesters mitoxantrone in renal tissue and reduces circulating drug levels, with hepatic and splenic T_DNA exerting similar moderate inhibitory effects. Differently, pulmonary T_DNA specifically governed plasma Cmax rather than AUC0_48, which is biologically reasonable: the lung receives full cardiac output immediately after intravenous administration, serving as a primary early retention organ for circulating liposomal mitoxantrone, and rapid pulmonary DNA binding efficiently traps drugs in the initial distribution phase to limit peak plasma concentration. In contrast, T_DNA and T_macro in the brain, heart and other peripheral tissues exhibited relatively weak impacts on plasma AUC0_48 and Cmax (|PRCC| < 0.5) which validated our cross-species extrapolation strategy (Figure S2). At present, the extrapolation of pharmacokinetic parameters of nanomedicines from animals to humans still faces major challenges, including nanomaterial MPS sequestration scales and size independent species differences between different species which could not be described by traditional allometric scaling approaches.46 For better extrapolation, we adopted the concentration-dependent saturable uptake mechanism and available human data from literature. Nanomaterial-specific parameters were calibrated using rat experimental data simulated and physiologically scaled to humans using human organ weight and MPS cell abundance information to obtain a high correlation between predictions and measurements. Leveraging this mechanism-informed PBPK framework calibrated with rat plasma and multi-tissue concentration-time datasets, we successfully simulated human systemic and organ-specific pharmacokinetic profiles following intravenous liposomal mitoxantrone administration.

  Notably, while the guidelines recommend administering the drug at a dosage of 12–20 mg/m2, clinical practice often adopted a fixed dosing regimen for operational convenience. Consequently, this study simulated the pharmacokinetics of fixed dosing (20, 30, and 40 mg) in humans for fitting purposes. The simulation results aligned with previously reported findings, indicating that mitoxantrone exhibits linear pharmacokinetics at dose levels of 20–40 mg, under a regimen of administration every 28 days, multiple dosing does not result in significant drug accumulation.29 Additionally, this study incorporated blood concentration data from 37 actual clinical patients to validate this model, all patients included in this clinical dataset received fixed dose administration. The results demonstrate that the majority of the measured data (over 92%) fall within the predicted range, further indicating that the model possesses good predictive performance and could be utilized for clinical applications. The ability of the current model to successfully extrapolate the PK of mitoxantrone from rat to humans. In clinical applications, mitoxantrone has been shown to be cardiotoxic, although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Similar to other anthracyclines, the incidence of cardiotoxicity following mitoxantrone treatment is associated with its cumulative dose over time, which limits its extended use.47 According to PBPK model, the proportion of free mitoxantrone in plasma, heart, and brain tissues is lower than in other tissues, providing a theoretical basis for the reduced toxicity and adverse reactions associated with liposomal mitoxantrone. However, it is important to note that free mitoxantrone exhibits a higher affinity for cardiac tissue. Consequently, the concentration of the free drug in the heart during the later stages of liposomal administration may be comparable to or even exceed that of conventional formulations. The prolonged exposure of the drug in tissues may also contribute to the occurrence of adverse reactions. Although there is currently a lack of data regarding mitoxantrone liposomes, previous studies have indicated that doxorubicin liposomes, while capable of reducing the incidence of cardiac toxicity, do not eliminate it entirely.48,49 Therefore, the chronic cardiotoxicity potentially induced by the long-circulating properties of the drug should be closely monitored.

  Taking into account the variations in distribution, plasma concentration might not serve as an effective measure for drug exposure at the target site or the organ of toxicity.50 Hence, our model may also possess clinical relevance in evaluating both the efficacy and safety of mitoxantrone, potentially offering further insights for therapy optimization. The PBPK model introduced in this study is a significant resource for understanding the mechanisms that govern liposomal tissue distribution and aids in linking preclinical data.

  This research contains several unavoidable limitations. Parameters for multiple tissue-related processes (liposomal phagocytic uptake, exocytosis and release) were obtained from existing literature or calculated estimates, due to the challenge of determining nanoparticle trafficking and clearance related parameters. In human PBPK model, only plasma concentration was validated by clinical data, human tissue-level validation was not possible with available clinical data, and generalizability to other indications requires further evaluation. While PEGylation is known to facilitate tumor accumulation through the EPR effect, our PBPK model excludes a tumor compartment and cannot predict tumor exposure, as construction of a reliable tumor compartment is hampered by tumor physiological heterogeneity, variable EPR effect, and limited in vivo kinetic data of liposomal mitoxantrone in tumors. Tumor tissue compartments were not incorporated into the model; the current framework cannot directly simulate EPR-mediated intratumoral nanoparticle accumulation, restricting its application for anti-tumor efficacy quantitative prediction.


## Conclusion
  This study established and validated a novel mechanism-based PBPK model for mitoxantrone solution and liposomal mitoxantrone, which integrated macromolecule binding and MPS compartments to accurately describe their in vivo tissue distribution in rats. The established PBPK framework enabled mechanistic interspecies extrapolation from rats to humans, and external validation was performed using an independent clinical patient dataset. In summary, this established model can effectively predict organ-specific drug exposure, provides a mechanistic framework for investigating formulation-dependent tissue exposure and generating hypotheses regarding efficacy and safety.
