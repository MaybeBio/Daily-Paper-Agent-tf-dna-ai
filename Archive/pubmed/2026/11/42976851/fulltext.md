# Redox-dependent remodeling of mitochondrial nucleoids contributes to bioenergetic decline during neuronal aging


## Summary
  Why mitochondria progressively fail in aging neurons remains a fundamental question in neurobiology. Although bioenergetic decline and oxidative stress are hallmarks of aging, whether redox signaling remodels mitochondrial nucleoids (mt-nucleoids) to impair neuronal bioenergetics remains unclear. Using aged mouse neurons and human iPSC-derived neurons, we demonstrate that redox-driven mt-nucleoid remodeling underlies reduced mtDNA transcript abundance and bioenergetic decline. Combining MINFLUX nanoscopy with single-molecule transcription mapping, we identify distinct mt-nucleoid architectures governed by TFAM-mediated mtDNA compaction. Aging-associated oxidative stress drives mt-nucleoid remodeling from compact, transcriptionally active structures toward decompacted forms with reduced mtDNA transcript abundance and impaired mitochondrial ATP production. This remodeling involves species-specific TFAM redox mechanisms: cysteine oxidation promotes human TFAM polymerization, whereas methionine oxidation weakens mouse TFAM-mtDNA interactions. Notably, expressing an oxidation-resistant TFAM variant restores mt-nucleoid organization, mtDNA transcript abundance, and ATP production in aged neurons, identifying mt-nucleoid architecture as a potential target for preserving neuronal bioenergetics during aging.


## Introduction
  A central challenge in aging research is understanding why mitochondria—the primary source of cellular energy—progressively fail in long-lived tissues. This question is especially important for neurons, which are uniquely vulnerable to bioenergetic deficits because of their high metabolic demands, elaborate morphologies, and postmitotic longevity.1,2 Recent advances underscore this problem: (i) mitochondrial transcripts encoding electron transport chain (ETC) subunits decline broadly with age,3,4 (ii) mitochondrial dysfunction accelerates aging,5,6 whereas preserving mitochondrial integrity delays functional decline,7 and (iii) brain-wide respiration atlases identify aging as a major determinant of mitochondrial performance.8 Together, these findings highlight a fundamental gap in understanding how age-associated changes in mitochondrial genome organization and regulation—beyond mitochondrial genome (mtDNA) mutations9,10—drive bioenergetic decline. Although mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress are defining hallmarks of aging,11–13 how redox imbalance disrupts mitochondrial genome organization over the lifespan remain largely unknown.

  The mtDNA, which encodes 13 essential ETC subunits,14 is packaged with its transcriptional machinery into ~100-nm mt-nucleoids.15–17 Mitochondrial Transcription Factor A (TFAM), the principal nucleoid-associated protein, binds, bends, loops, and compacts mtDNA through dimerization, thereby organizing the genome for transcription.18–20 Disruption of TFAM-dependent mtDNA packaging and transcription has been implicated in aging and neurodegeneration.21 Individual mt-nucleoids can exist in transcriptionally active or quiescent states,22 but how these structural states are regulated and affect mitochondrial gene expression and bioenergetics remains poorly understood. Because mitochondria are a major source of reactive oxygen species (ROS),23,24 mt-nucleoids are chronically exposed to oxidative stress, which increases in aging neurons.25 Although reversible protein oxidation contributes to physiological redox signaling, persistent oxidative stress disrupts redox homeostasis and promotes protein dysfunction and tissue aging.26 Whether age-associated oxidative stress remodels mt-nucleoid architecture to impair mitochondrial transcription and bioenergetic function, and whether these changes can be reversed, remains unknown. Addressing this question has been limited by the lack of mammalian neuronal aging models that integrate nanoscale mt-nucleoid analysis with measurements of mitochondrial ATP production, redox homeostasis, and mtDNA transcript abundance at single-organelle and single-molecule resolution.

  Here, using dorsal root ganglion neurons (DRGNs) from young adult and aged mice, we combine MINFLUX nanoscopy with live ATP and redox imaging, in situ transcription mapping, biomolecular phase-separation assays, and machine-learning modeling to define a mitochondrial aging signature. Aged neurons exhibit disorganized mt-nucleoids, reduced mitochondrial respiration and ATP production, elevated oxidative stress, and reduced mtDNA transcript abundance. Similar remodeling occurs in aged mouse hippocampal neurons. MINFLUX imaging (~3-nm localization precision) reveals an aging-associated mt-nucleoid architecture characterized by decompacted mtDNA and reduced TFAM localization density. Mechanistically, oxidative stress drives this transition through species-specific TFAM redox mechanisms: disulfide-mediated polymerization of human TFAM and methionine oxidation–dependent weakening of mouse TFAM–mtDNA interactions. Despite these distinct mechanisms, both converge on mt-nucleoid decompaction. These redox-dependent changes are reproduced in human iPSC-derived cortical neurons exposed to oxidative stress, whereas expression of a redox-resistant, mtDNA-packaging-competent TFAM variant restores mt-nucleoid compaction, mtDNA transcript abundance, and ATP production in aged neurons. Together, our findings establish redox-dependent mt-nucleoid remodeling as a conserved mechanism linking oxidative stress to mitochondrial dysfunction during neuronal aging and identify TFAM redox regulation as a potential therapeutic target for age-associated neurodegeneration.


## Results

### Establishing an aging mouse neuron model
  To investigate how aging remodels mt-nucleoids, we established a primary neuronal aging model using DRGNs isolated from young adult and aged mice. Because postmitotic neurons do not divide, DRGNs from aged mice provide a tractable system for investigating age-dependent remodeling of mt-nucleoids and mitochondrial bioenergetics. Unlike intact aged brain tissue, cultured DRGNs enable nanoscale visualization of individual mt-nucleoids together with measurements of mitochondrial ATP production, redox dynamics, and mtDNA transcripts. Although many neuronal populations from aged animals exhibit poor survival in culture,27,28 DRGNs are unusually resilient and retain key physiological properties.29–35 We therefore compared DRGNs from young adult (1 month, P1M) and aged (24 months, P24M) mice. Neurons from both age groups extended elaborate axonal arbors and maintained dense mitochondrial networks throughout the soma and distal processes (Figure S1A). Thus, this preparation provides a tractable model for examining mt-nucleoid architecture, mitochondrial bioenergetics, redox homeostasis, and mtDNA transcript abundance during neuronal aging.


### Aging-associated mt-nucleoid decompaction occurs in peripheral and central neurons
  Mitochondrial genome instability contributes to aging,13,36 yet whether mt-nucleoid architecture is preserved during neuronal aging remains unknown. Aberrant mt-nucleoid morphology has been reported in skin fibroblasts from individuals with Hutchinson-Gilford Progeria Syndrome (HGPS), a premature aging disorder,18 suggesting that mt-nucleoid disorganization may contribute to age-associated mitochondrial dysfunction. Because TFAM is the principal organizer of mt-nucleoids and is required for mtDNA compaction and transcription,19 we asked whether physiological aging disrupts TFAM-dependent mtDNA packaging in neurons.

  We first examined mt-nucleoid organization in DRGNs from young adult (P1M) and aged (P24M) mice. In young adult neurons, mt-nucleoids appeared as compact, sharply defined circular puncta, with extensive colocalization of mtDNA and TFAM (Figure 1A). In contrast, aged neurons exhibited enlarged, dispersed, and irregular mtDNA- and TFAM-positive structures. Despite these morphological changes, overall mt-nucleoid density did not differ significantly between young adult and aged neurons (Figure S1B). Consistent with these observations, while young adult neurons contained compact, well-defined mt-nucleoids within the mitochondrial network in the soma, aged neurons displayed enlarged and decondensed mt-nucleoids, with increased mtDNA- and TFAM-positive area, reduced circularity, and lower fluorescence intensity (Figures 1B–1E).

  We next asked whether neuronal aging similarly affects other mitochondrial biomolecular condensates, including mitochondrial RNA granules (MRGs), which mediate mitochondrial RNA processing. Using GRSF1 as an MRG marker,37 we found that, in contrast to the pronounced mt-nucleoid decompaction in aged neurons, MRG morphology was largely preserved, with no significant age-associated changes in granule area, circularity, or fluorescence intensity (Figures S1C and S1D). These findings indicate that aging-associated remodeling selectively affects mt-nucleoids rather than reflecting a generalized reorganization of mitochondrial biomolecular condensates.

  To determine whether this remodeling extends beyond peripheral neurons, we examined hippocampal sections from adult (P3M) and aged (P24M) mice. The soma-enriched pyramidal cell layer and the adjacent neurite-enriched stratum radiatum were identified based on nuclear staining (Figure S2A). Compared with adult mice, aged hippocampal neurons exhibited enlarged and decompacted mtDNA and TFAM signals in both somatic and neuritic compartments (Figures S2B and S2C), consistent with the mt-nucleoid remodeling observed in aged DRGNs (Figure 1). TFAM signals were confirmed to localize to mitochondria (Figure S2D). Thus, aging-associated mt-nucleoid remodeling occurs in both neuronal somata and neurites, supporting the conclusion that mt-nucleoid decompaction is a conserved feature of neuronal aging in both peripheral and central neurons.


### Machine-learning segmentation identifies a decompacted mt-nucleoid population in aged neurons
  To distinguish compact from decompacted mt-nucleoid populations, we developed a machine-learning semantic segmentation framework integrating an InceptionV3 backbone38 into a U-Net architecture (iUNet). Trained on manually annotated confocal images (Figures S3A and S3B), iUNet outperformed conventional threshold-based segmentation and showed high concordance with expert annotations (Figures S3C–S3H). Quantitative analysis revealed that the age-associated increase in mt-nucleoid area arose predominantly from expansion of the decompacted population (Figures 1F and S3I), whereas reduced circularity was most pronounced among compact nucleoids (Figures 1G and S3J). Both mtDNA and TFAM fluorescence intensities were reduced in aged neurons (Figures 1H and S3K), consistent with decreased TFAM-mtDNA packing density. Thus, young adult neurons were dominated by compact, TFAM-enriched mt-nucleoids, whereas aged neurons exhibited an expanded population of decompacted, TFAM-reduced mt-nucleoids. These findings prompted nanoscale interrogation of mt-nucleoid architecture using MINFLUX nanoscopy.


### MINFLUX nanoscopy reveals aging-associated mt-nucleoid remodeling
  Conventional super-resolution methods, including STED and PALM/STORM, provide ~20-nm resolution, limiting their ability to resolve nanoscale structural transitions within individual mt-nucleoids and quantify TFAM-mtDNA packaging at the single-molecule level. We therefore employed MINFLUX (Minimal Photon Fluxes) nanoscopy, which provides 1–3 nm localization precision and resolves fluorophores separated by ~6 nm.39,40 Two-dimensional MINFLUX imaging revealed striking age-associated remodeling of mt-nucleoid architecture. Individual mt-nucleoids, selected from confocal fields (5 × 5 μm2), were resolved into distinct nanoscale structures. In young adult (P1M) neurons, mt-nucleoids predominantly exhibited compact, TFAM-enriched condensates, whereas aged (P24M) neurons displayed decompacted, irregular TFAM organization (Figures 2A and 2B). Although TFAM localization density spanned a continuous distribution in both age groups, consistent with previous observations using confocal and STED microscopy in human fibroblasts and Hela cells,41 MINFLUX revealed a pronounced age-associated shift toward lower TFAM localization density (Figure 2C), with redistribution from TFAM-dense to TFAM-depleted mt-nucleoids (Figure 2D). Three-dimensional MINFLUX imaging further confirmed these structural transitions (Videos S1–S3).

  Dual-color MINFLUX imaging further revealed age-associated changes in TFAM-mtDNA organization. Although mtDNA and TFAM remained colocalized in both age groups, aged neurons exhibited increased mtDNA area per nucleoid, consistent with mtDNA decompaction (Figures 2E and 2F). Across individual mt-nucleoids, mtDNA area was inversely correlated with TFAM localization density, such that larger, more decompacted nucleoids exhibited lower TFAM density (Figure 2G). Thus, neuronal aging is accompanied by a progressive transition from compact, TFAM-enriched nucleoids to decompacted, TFAM-depleted nucleoids (Figure 2H). Together with previous studies linking distinct mt-nucleoid packaging states to genome maintenance and transcription,18,19,42,43 our MINFLUX analyses identify an aging-associated remodeling characterized by reduced TFAM localization density and impaired mtDNA packaging, potentially compromising mitochondrial gene expression.


### Reduced mtDNA transcript abundance in aged neurons
  To determine the functional consequence of aging-associated mt-nucleoid remodeling, we quantified mtDNA transcript abundance using single-molecule Hybridization Chain Reaction (smHCR).44 We examined four mtDNA-encoded transcripts representing distinct ETC complexes: NADH dehydrogenase 1 (ND1, Complex I), cytochrome b (Cytb, Complex III), cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COXI, Complex IV), and ATPase 6 (ATP6, Complex V). Compared with young adult neurons, aged neurons exhibited a marked reduction in transcript abundance for all four mtDNA-encoded genes (Figures 2I, 2J, and S4A). Probe specificity was validated using mouse COXI probe in mouse DRGNs and human HeLa cells lacking the complementary mouse COXI sequence, which showed only background fluorescence (Figure S4B). Single-cell analyses demonstrated that the decline in mtDNA transcript abundance paralleled aging-associated mt-nucleoid remodeling. Because overall mt-nucleoid density was preserved with age (Figure S1B), reduced transcript abundance is unlikely to result from a loss of mt-nucleoids. Together, these findings suggest that aging-associated mt-nucleoid decompaction is accompanied by reduced mitochondrial transcript abundance.


### Aged neurons exhibit impaired mitochondrial bioenergetics
  Impaired oxidative phosphorylation and elevated oxidative stress are hallmarks of neuronal aging and senescence.45 To determine whether aged DRGNs recapitulate these features, we assessed mitochondrial bioenergetic function in neurons from young adult (P1M) and aged (P24M) mice. Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm), normalized to mitochondrial mass, was markedly reduced in both axons and somas of aged neurons compared with young adult neurons (Figures 3A and 3B), indicating impaired mitochondrial function.

  We next quantified mitochondrial ATP using the mitochondria-targeted FRET-based biosensor mito-Go-ATeam2. ATP binding shifts the sensor emission from green fluorescence (GFP, 510 nm) to orange fluorescence (OFP, 560 nm), and the OFP/GFP ratio—displayed as a heat map—reports mitochondrial matrix ATP levels with minimal pH sensitivity46 (Figure 3C). Based on prior neuronal calibration, a 12% decrease in the OFP/GFP ratio corresponds to an approximately 30% reduction in ATP content.47 Consistent with the ΔΨm measurements, aged neurons exhibited lower mitochondrial ATP levels in both axons and somas (Figures 3D and 3E). Independent assessment of respiratory function using Seahorse extracellular flux analysis further revealed reductions in basal and ATP-linked respiration, maximal respiration, and spare respiratory capacity in aged neurons (Figures 3F and 3G). Together, these findings demonstrate that aged DRGNs exhibit broad bioenergetic decline, characterized by reduced ΔΨm, mitochondrial ATP levels, and respiratory capacity.


### Aging-associated impairment in mitochondrial redox homeostasis
  Chronic mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress are defining hallmarks of aging.48,49 Because elevated ROS has been implicated in neuronal aging, we asked whether the bioenergetic deficits observed in aged neurons are accompanied by impaired mitochondrial redox homeostasis. We used the genetically encoded mitochondria-targeted redox sensor mito-Grx1-roGFP2,48 which reports reversible cysteine oxidation and the mitochondrial glutathione redox state (Figure 4A). Increased oxidation elevates the 405/488-nm ratio, providing a quantitative measure of mitochondrial glutathione redox state, as reflected by the GSSG/GSH ratio. Sensor performance was validated in DRGNs and confirmed to respond robustly to the oxidative challenge (50 μM H2O2) used in subsequent experiments (Figures S5A and S5B). Based on ~390-fold extracellular-to-intracellular H2O2 gradient and endogenous antioxidant buffering systems,49 this treatment is expected to generate intracellular H2O2 concentrations within the physiological nanomolar range associated with redox signaling and adaptive oxidative stress responses.50

  Aged neurons (P24M) exhibited higher basal mitochondrial oxidation than young adult neurons (P1M) in both axons and somas (Figures 4B and 4C), consistent with increased mitochondrial oxidative stress.51 To assess mitochondrial redox adaptability, neurons were challenged with H2O2 (50 μM). Imaging captured basal mitochondrial redox levels and the redox response within 30 min after H2O2-treatment (Figures 4D and 4G), while time-series imaging monitored mitochondrial oxidation for 5 min following a 3-min basal recording (Figures 4E and 4H). In both paradigms, the H2O2-induced change relative to basal oxidation was used to define Redox Response Capacity (RRC), a quantitative measure of mitochondrial adaptation to oxidative stress during aging.52–55 Both within-group and between-group analyses demonstrated reduced RRC in aged neurons (Figures 4F and 4I), indicating impaired redox adaptability. Specifically, a larger increase in the RRC trace observed in young neurons reflects a greater dynamic response of the mitochondrial redox system to an oxidative challenge. In contrast, the blunted RRC traces in aged neurons indicate a diminished capacity to respond to an additional oxidative perturbation from an already oxidized state. Thus, neuronal aging is characterized by elevated basal mitochondrial oxidative stress together with diminished capacity to adapt to additional oxidative challenge, consistent with impaired mitochondrial redox homeostasis.

  To determine whether neuronal aging is accompanied by elevated oxidative stress in vivo, we administered the cell-permeable dihydroethidium (DHE), a widely used probe for detecting superoxide-sensitive oxidative signals, to mature adult (P3M) and aged (P24M) mice.56–58 DHE fluorescence, which reports tissue oxidative status through oxidation products including superoxide-sensitive 2-hydroxyethidium (2-OH-E+) and ethidium (E+),59 was markedly elevated in aged mice in both DRGNs (Figures 4J and 4K) and layer 2/3 cortical neurons in the somatosensory cortex (Figures 4L and 4M). Oxidation-dependent DHE responses were validated using the paraquat-treated adult mice (Figure S6). Together, these findings demonstrate elevated oxidative stress in aged neurons across both the PNS and CNS. This increase in oxidative burden is consistent with the established association between aging, mitochondrial oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction, and further suggests that oxidative modifications may link aging-associated oxidative stress to mt-nucleoid remodeling, reduced mtDNA transcripts, and impaired mitochondrial bioenergetics in the nervous system.


### DRGNs from P1M and P3M mice exhibit comparable mitochondrial phenotypes
  DRGNs undergo terminal differentiation during early postnatal development.60,61 Because P1M mice may represent an earlier stage of adult maturation, we compared mitochondrial function and mt-nucleoid compaction in DRGNs from young adult (P1M) and mature adult (P3M) mice. Mitochondrial ATP levels, basal oxidative stress, mt-nucleoid compaction, and mtDNA transcript abundance were indistinguishable between P1M and P3M neurons (Figure S7). These findings indicate that the pronounced differences observed between P1M and P24M neurons reflect aging-associated remodeling rather than continued postnatal maturation and further validate P1M DRGNs as an appropriate young-adult reference.


### Oxidative stress disrupts TFAM-mtDNA condensates
  TFAM is the principal organizer of the mitochondrial genome and is essential for mtDNA packaging and transcription.17,62–65 TFAM undergoes phase separation with mtDNA to form droplet-like mt-nucleoid condensates that facilitates mtDNA transcription.18,19 Consistent with this model, mt-nucleoids exhibit properties of biomolecular condensates, including spherical morphology, dynamic molecular exchange with the surrounding matrix, and fusion-fission behaviors.17 We therefore asked whether aging-associated oxidative stress disrupts TFAM-mediated mtDNA condensate formation. To address this question, we reconstituted TFAM–mtDNA condensates in vitro following established guidelines.66

  Recombinant human TFAM (hTFAM) was purified by sequential Ni2+-affinity, ion-exchange, and size-exclusion chromatography (Figure S8A, left). An 820-bp human mtDNA fragment (mtDNA-ND1) containing a TFAM-binding region within the ND1 locus was generated as described previously19 (Figure S8A, middle). Alexa Fluor 488-labeled hTFAM (5–20 μM) was incubated with increasing concentrations of DAPI-labeled mtDNA-ND1 (0–30 nM). Neither mtDNA-ND1 alone nor hTFAM alone formed condensates, whereas 5 μM hTFAM robustly induced droplet formation in the presence of mtDNA-ND1 (10 nM), with condensate abundance increasing with mtDNA-ND1 concentrations (Figure 5A). These concentrations were within the estimated physiological range of mitochondrial TFAM.41 Full-length human mtDNA (16 kb; 1–2 nM; Figure S8A, right)18,67 similarly underwent robust condensation with hTFAM at physiological TFAM-to-mtDNA ratios,41 whereas either component alone failed to form droplets (Figure 5B). All droplets contained both TFAM and mtDNA, confirming that phase separation requires both components.

  Given the elevated mitochondrial oxidative stress in aged neurons (Figure 4), we next asked whether oxidative stress disrupts TFAM-mtDNA phase separation. Exposure to H2O2 (50 or 100 μM), a reactive oxygen species and diffusible redox signaling molecule,51 for 2 h markedly reduced both the droplet area and size of TFAM–mtDNA condensates (Figures 5C and 5D). This effect was conserved with mouse TFAM (mTFAM), whose condensation with full-length mtDNA was similarly impaired by H2O2 (Figures 5E and 5F). H2O2 also inhibited hTFAM-mediated condensation of the mtDNA-ND1 fragment across three TFAM concentrations tested (Figures S8B–S8G). Together, these findings demonstrate that oxidative stress suppresses TFAM-mediated mtDNA phase separation across species, supporting a redox-dependent mechanism that may contribute to oxidation-driven mt-nucleoid remodeling during neuronal aging.


### Human neurons recapitulate oxidation-induced mt-nucleoid remodeling
  To determine whether oxidative stress is sufficient to induce mt-nucleoid decompaction in human neurons, we exposed human iPSC-derived cortical neurons to low-dose H2O2 (50 μM) in antioxidant-reduced medium to model sustained oxidative stress. Based on the estimated ~390-fold extracellular-to-intracellular H2O2 gradient,49 this paradigm is expected to generate intracellular H2O2 concentrations within the physiological nanomolar range associated with redox signaling and oxidative stress adaptation.50 Neuronal differentiation and maturation was confirmed by SV2 and β3-tubulin co-immunostaining (Figure S9A). MINFLUX nanoscopy resolved TFAM-labeled mt-nucleoids into distinct nanoscale architectures. Control human iPSC-derived cortical neurons predominantly contained compact, TFAM-dense mt-nucleoids, whereas oxidative stress induced enlarged, decompacted mt-nucleoid with reduced TFAM localization density (Figures 5G and 5H). Mitochondrial redox measurements further confirmed sustained oxidative stress following three days of H2O2 treatment (Figures S9B and S9C). These findings demonstrate that physiologically relevant oxidative stress is sufficient to drive mt-nucleoid decompaction in human neurons, recapitulating the aging-associated remodeling observed in aged mouse neurons and supporting a conserved redox-dependent mechanism across species.


### Mitochondria-derived ROS is sufficient to induce mt-nucleoid remodeling
  To further strengthen the physiological relevance of our findings, we applied mitochondria-targeted paraquat (MitoPQ), a redox cycler that selectively elevates mitochondrial superoxide.68 Unlike exogenous H2O2, MitoPQ generates ROS directly within mitochondria and therefore more closely mimics endogenous mitochondrial oxidative stress. Treatment of adult (P3M) DRG neurons with 1 μM MitoPQ for 3 days induced significant mt-nucleoid decompaction detected by MINFLUX nanoscopy. Notably, MitoPQ-induced remodeling featured enlarged mtDNA area with markedly reduced TFAM localization density (Figures 5I and 5J), closely phenocopying the mt-nucleoid remodeling observed in aged DRGNs and human iPSC-derived cortical neurons exposed to low-dose H2O2.


### Cysteine oxidation promotes hTFAM polymerization and disrupts mtDNA condensates
  Structural studies have shown that hTFAM dimerization through the antiparallel helix-3 interface is essential for mtDNA looping and compaction.69 Because cysteine residues are major targets of reversible redox regulation,26 we asked whether cysteine oxidation alters hTFAM-mediated mtDNA condensates. In a reconstituted phase-separation system, H2O2 markedly suppressed hTFAM-mtDNA condensate formation, whereas subsequent treatment with DTT fully restored phase separation (Figures 6A and 6B). These findings demonstrate that hTFAM-mtDNA condensates are oxidation-sensitive and reversible, consistent with cysteine oxidation and disulfide bond formation as a potential mechanism of hTFAM redox regulation.

  LC-MS/MS analysis identified three intermolecular disulfide bonds induced by H2O2—C49-C49, C246-C246, and C49-C246 (Figure 6C)—consistent with covalent intermolecular crosslinking H2O2. Accordingly, oxidative stress promoted the formation of higher order hTFAM polymers, which reverted to monomers following reduction (Figure 6D). Size-exclusion chromatography further showed that native hTFAM was predominantly dimeric with a minor tetrameric population (Figure 6E, blue trace), consistent with previous structural studies.69 Notably, oxidation depleted the dimeric pool and shifted hTFAM toward higher-order polymers (Figure 6E, red trace). Reducing conditions restored the dimeric population and eliminated tetrameric and polymeric species (Figure 6E, green trace). Together, these findings suggest that oxidation drives hTFAM polymerization and disrupts the dimeric state required for mtDNA compaction.

  To determine whether preventing cysteine oxidation preserves hTFAM-mediated mtDNA condensates, we generated oxidation-resistant hTFAM-C49A and hTFAM-C246A variants. Unlike wild-type hTFAM, both mutants maintained robust TFAM-mtDNA condensates under increasing oxidative conditions, with condensate area largely preserved even at the highest H2O2 concentration tested (Figures 6F–6I). Size-exclusion chromatography further showed that substitution of either cysteine markedly reduced oxidative stress-induced hTFAM polymerization (Figures 6J and 6K). Notably, C246 exhibited greater oxidation sensitivity than C49: tetrameric species persisted in C49A, consistent with C246-dependent intermolecular crosslinking, whereas C246A largely abolished tetramer formation.

  We further validated these oxidation-dependent changes in human iPSC-derived neurons. Oxidative stress induced the formation of higher-order hTFAM polymers in neurons expressing wild-type hTFAM (Figure 6L). In contrast, polymer formation was markedly reduced in neurons expressing hTFAM-C246A or completely abolished by treatment with DTT. Because TFAM dimers are required for mtDNA looping and compaction,69 oxidation-induced polymerization is predicted to deplete the functional dimeric pool, whereas preventing C246 oxidation maintains hTFAM-mediated mtDNA condensates under oxidative stress.

  Based on these findings, we propose an oxidation-sensitive model of human TFAM remodeling during neuronal aging. Under native conditions, hTFAM exists predominantly as dimers, with a minor tetrameric population stabilized by hydrophobic interactions.69 These hTFAM species engage in multivalent interactions with mtDNA to promote mt-nucleoid condensate formation. During neuronal aging, chronic oxidative stress induces disulfide-linked hTFAM polymerization, depleting the functional dimeric pool and driving mt-nucleoid decompaction (Figure 6M). Thus, redox-dependent TFAM remodeling provides a mechanistic link between oxidative stress, mt-nucleoid decompaction, impaired mitochondrial transcription, and bioenergetic decline during neuronal aging.


### Methionine oxidation weakens mouse TFAM-mtDNA interactions
  Unlike hTFAM, mature mouse TFAM (mTFAM) lacks cysteine residues and therefore cannot undergo disulfide-mediated polymerization. The redox-sensitive cysteines C49 and C246 are conserved only in higher primates, suggesting that rodents employ a distinct redox mechanism. LC-MS/MS identified two oxidation-sensitive methionine residues, M53 and M128, in mTFAM. These residues, absent from hTFAM, flank the HMG1 DNA-binding domain (Figure 7A). Label-free quantification demonstrated increased oxidation of M53- and M128-containing peptides following H2O2 exposure (Figure 7B). Oxidative stress impaired mTFAM-mediated mtDNA condensates (Figures 5E and 5F), whereas the oxidation-resistant M53A/M128A mutant fully preserved mTFAM–mtDNA phase-separation under oxidative conditions (Figures 7C and 7D). These findings demonstrate that methionine oxidation at M53 and M128 mediates the oxidation-dependent disruption of mTFAM-mtDNA condensation.

  Unlike hTFAM, mTFAM remained predominantly dimeric following oxidative treatment (Figure 7E). We therefore asked whether oxidation instead alters TFAM–mtDNA binding. Electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSA), as described previously,70 revealed robust mtDNA binding by mTFAM under native conditions, whereas substantially higher mTFAM concentrations were required to detect mtDNA binding following oxidation (Figures 7F and 7G). Thus, oxidative stress disrupts mTFAM-mediated mtDNA condensates by weakening TFAM–mtDNA interactions. At the molecular level, oxidation converts methionine to methionine sulfoxide, increasing side-chain polarity and potentially disrupting hydrophobic interactions that contribute to TFAM–mtDNA binding. Similar methionine-dependent redox regulation has been described for other DNA-binding proteins.71,72 We therefore propose a cross-species model in which human and mouse TFAM employ distinct redox switches—cysteine oxidation in hTFAM and methionine oxidation in mTFAM—that converge on mt-nucleoid decompaction (Figure 7H).


### Oxidation-resistant hTFAM restores mt-nucleoid compaction and bioenergetics in aged neurons
  The species-specific redox signaling switches provide a mechanistic framework for intervention. To test whether preventing TFAM oxidation restores mt-nucleoid organization, we expressed the oxidation-resistant hTFAM-C246A variant in aged neurons. We selected this mutant because it provided greater protection against oxidation-induced disruption of TFAM–mtDNA condensates than hTFAM-C49A. Wild-type and mutant hTFAM were expressed at comparable levels (Figure 8A), while hTFAM-C246A significantly restored mt-nucleoid compaction in aged DRGNs, increasing compacted nucleoid abundance compared with aged control neurons or neurons expressing hTFAM (Figures 8B–8E). These findings parallel the in vitro results showing hTFAM-C246A preserving TFAM–mtDNA condensates under oxidative stress (Figures 6H and 6I). Thus, preventing hTFAM oxidation is sufficient to restore mt-nucleoid architecture in aged neurons. We next asked whether restored mt-nucleoid compaction rescues mtDNA transcript abundance. smHCR analysis revealed significantly higher COXI transcript abundance in aged neurons expressing hTFAM-C246A than in neurons expressing hTFAM or control (Figures 8F and 8G). Thus, preventing TFAM oxidative modification restores both mt-nucleoid compaction and mitochondrial gene expression under oxidative stress.

  We next determined whether these improvements were accompanied by recovery of mitochondrial bioenergetic capacity. Using the mitochondrial ATP sensor mito-iATPSnFR2,73 we found that expression of either hTFAM or hTFAM-C246A increased mitochondrial ATP levels in aged DRGNs, with hTFAM-C246A producing the greatest rescue (Figures 8H and 8I). Relative ATP changes were concordant with those measured independently using mito-GoATeam2 (Figure 3E). The enhanced rescue effect by hTFAM-C246A likely reflects its resistance to ongoing oxidative modification, preserving a larger pool of mtDNA-packaging-competent TFAM dimers and thereby maintaining mt-nucleoid organization more effectively than hTFAM under oxidative stress. Collectively, these findings support a model in which oxidative TFAM modification drives mt-nucleoid remodeling associated with impaired mtDNA transcription and mitochondrial bioenergetics, consistent with previous studies linking disruption of TFAM-dependent mtDNA packaging to impaired mtDNA transcription.19,20,43,65,69,74,75


### Oxidation-resistant TFAM-C246A partially restores aged DRGN excitability
  To determine whether aging-associated, redox-dependent mt-nucleoid remodeling is linked to altered neuronal physiology, we performed whole-cell current-clamp recordings from adult (P3M) and aged (P24M) DRGNs. Focusing on phasic-firing neurons, which exhibit relatively homogeneous electrophysiological properties, we found that aged phasic-firing neurons exhibited increased rheobase and action potential (AP) threshold, together with reduced AP amplitude, AP overshoot, and maximal AP upstroke velocity (max dV/dt), whereas passive membrane properties and AP half-width were unchanged (Figures S10A–S10J). These findings demonstrate that neuronal aging is accompanied by deficits in intrinsic excitability that parallel mitochondrial oxidative stress and bioenergetic decline. To determine whether these deficits are associated with redox-dependent mt-nucleoid remodeling, we expressed the oxidation-resistant hTFAM-C246A variant in aged DRGNs. TFAM-C246A reduced rheobase, indicating partial restoration of neuronal excitability (Figures S10K and S10L). This improvement paralleled restoration of mt-nucleoid compaction, mitochondrial gene expression, and ATP production, supporting a contribution of bioenergetic deficits to reduced excitability in aged neurons. However, TFAM-C246A did not rescue AP threshold, amplitude, overshoot, or maximal upstroke velocity (Figures S10M–S10T), indicating that bioenergetic decline is an important, but not sole, contributor to age-associated deficits in neuronal excitability.


## Discussion
  Our findings extend the free radical theory of aging by highlighting reversible, redox-dependent remodeling of mitochondrial genome organization alongside irreversible oxidative damage. TFAM redox regulation provides a mechanistic link between mitochondrial redox state and mt-nucleoid architecture, identifying mitochondrial genome organization as a dynamic component of neuronal bioenergetic homeostasis and a potential target for preserving mitochondrial function during aging and neurodegenerative disease. Human and mouse TFAM use distinct redox-sensitive residues—cysteines and methionines, respectively—yet converge on disruption of TFAM-mediated mtDNA organization. Conservation of this response across peripheral and central neurons and its recapitulation in human neurons further suggests that redox-dependent mt-nucleoid remodeling is a broadly relevant feature of neuronal aging.


### mt-nucleoid disorganization contributes to bioenergetic decline in aged neurons
  Mitochondrial genome instability has long been implicated in aging and age-related diseases. Aging is accompanied by accumulation of mtDNA point mutations and deletions, likely reflecting continuous replication, limited DNA repair, oxidative stress, and the absence of histone-based DNA protection.36,76 However, despite widespread somatic mtDNA mutations in aging tissues, their direct contribution to mitochondrial bioenergetic decline remains unresolved.13 Because individual cells harbor hundreds to thousands of mtDNA copies, heteroplasmy can buffer mitochondrial function, with pathogenic mutations typically impairing oxidative phosphorylation only after exceeding ~60–90% heteroplasmy.10 Similarly, mice with proofreading-deficient mtDNA polymerase accumulate extensive mtDNA mutations and exhibit premature aging,5,6 yet whether these mutations are primary drivers of aging or consequences of mitochondrial dysfunction remains debated. These observations support the concept that alterations in mitochondrial genome organization, in addition to sequence mutation, contribute to age-associated mitochondrial dysfunction. This framework raises the possibility that altered mt-nucleoid architecture contributes to the broad decline in mitochondrial gene expression reported during brain aging.3,4 Whether individual mtDNA-encoded genes or specific genomic regions differ in their susceptibility to altered nucleoid organization remains unknown. Future studies integrating gene-resolved mitochondrial transcriptomics with high-resolution mtDNA lesion mapping will help determine how disrupted mt-nucleoid architecture influences mitochondrial gene expression during neuronal aging.

  Primary DRGN cultures comprise diverse sensory neuron subtypes with distinct physiological and metabolic properties. Because live-cell imaging and MINFLUX nanoscopy are not readily compatible with simultaneous subtype-specific molecular profiling, we could not determine whether individual sensory neuron subtypes differ in susceptibility to aging-associated mt-nucleoid remodeling. Nevertheless, comparable remodeling in aged hippocampal neurons in vivo, together with findings in human iPSC-derived cortical neurons and biochemical reconstitution, supports the broader relevance of this mechanism beyond specific DRGN subtypes. Future studies combining subtype-specific markers with high-resolution imaging will help define differential neuronal vulnerability to oxidative stress–associated mt-nucleoid remodeling during aging.


### Species-specific TFAM redox mechanisms converge on mt-nucleoid remodeling
  Human and mouse TFAM appear to have evolved distinct redox-sensitive mechanisms that converge on mtDNA decompaction (Figure 7H). In humans, oxidative stress promotes cysteine-dependent TFAM polymerization, whereas in mice it weakens TFAM–mtDNA interactions through methionine oxidation. Cysteine oxidation is a well-established redox signaling mechanism in which sulfenic acid intermediates can form reversible intra- or intermolecular disulfide bonds.51,77 Because biomolecular condensates are stabilized by multivalent weak interactions,66,78 oxidative crosslinking can alter protein conformation, interaction valency, and condensate properties.79,80 Consistent with this principle, oxidation shifted hTFAM from its functional dimeric state toward higher-order polymers with reduced mtDNA-condensing activity. The reducing mitochondrial matrix normally favors the dimeric, mtDNA-compacting state of TFAM,81 whereas chronic oxidative stress may shift this equilibrium toward TFAM polymerization and mt-nucleoid remodeling.

  Comparative analysis of TFAM orthologs from human, non-human primates, and rodents revealed evolutionary divergence in these redox-sensitive residues (Figure S11A). The cysteine corresponding to human TFAM C49 is conserved in human and non-human primate Chlorocebus sabaeus but absent from mouse and rat TFAM. Conversely, methionine corresponding to mouse TFAM M53 is conserved in mouse and rat but absent from human and non-human primate TFAM. This pattern suggests that distinct evolutionary lineages have adopted different redox-sensing mechanisms to regulate TFAM-mediated mtDNA compaction. Consistent with this model, oxidation-resistant mTFAM-M53A/M128A preserved mtDNA condensates under oxidative stress (Figures 7C and 7D), whereas substitution of linker methionines in human TFAM (hTFAM-M127A/M134A/M143A) did not alter its oxidative sensitivity (Figures S11B and S11C). Thus, the redox responses of human and mouse TFAM reflect residue-specific mechanisms rather than nonspecific oxidative protein damage. Because methionine sulfoxide can be enzymatically reduced by methionine sulfoxide reductases, this mechanism may also be reversible. Thus, although human and mouse TFAM employ distinct redox-sensitive switches, both converge on impaired mtDNA condensation and mt-nucleoid remodeling, providing a conserved molecular link between mitochondrial redox state and mitochondrial function during neuronal aging.


### MINFLUX reveals nanoscale remodeling of mt-nucleoid architecture during neuronal aging
  Biophysical models of TFAM-mediated mtDNA compaction are well established.62–64 Beyond packaging mtDNA, TFAM organizes mt-nucleoid architecture and promotes efficient transcription initiation.19,43,65,74 Structural and electron microscopy studies further show that mtDNA is compacted through multivalent TFAM binding, DNA looping, and intermolecular bridging.17 However, direct visualization of TFAM-mtDNA organization within individual mt-nucleoids at nanometer resolution in intact cells has remained challenging, limiting our understanding of how mt-nucleoid architecture relates to mitochondrial bioenergetics.

  Previous STED and SIM studies established that mt-nucleoids are multiphasic condensates of approximately 116×80×75 nm,17–19,43 but their spatial resolution (~20 nm)40 cannot resolve molecular organization within mt-nucleoids. Using dual-color MINFLUX with ~3-nm localization precision,39,40,82 together with single-molecule transcription mapping, we resolve aging-associated mt-nucleoid remodeling characterized by reduced TFAM localization density and mtDNA decompaction, accompanied by reduced mitochondrial transcript abundance. TFAM-mediated mtDNA packaging is required for active transcription.19,20,43,65,69,74,75 Our MINFLUX findings extend previous work identifying heterogeneous mt-nucleoid states,64 including highly compacted, transcriptionally inactive mt-nucleoids and more accessible nucleoids with relatively lower TFAM occupancy associated with active mtDNA transcription.43,63,83 Our data suggest that aging shifts mt-nucleoid architecture toward a more decompacted state accompanied by reduced TFAM localization density and decreased mtDNA transcript abundance. However, reduced transcript abundance could reflect altered transcription, RNA processing, or transcript stability. Direct measurements of transcriptional activity within compacted and decompacted mt-nucleoids will be needed to establish causality.

  An important technical consideration is whether reduced TFAM localization density reflects decreased TFAM abundance within individual mt-nucleoids. Absolute TFAM quantification by in situ dual-color MINFLUX imaging remains challenging because Trace IDs represent fluorophore localization clusters from antibody-labeled TFAM and mtDNA rather than direct molecular counts. Variable fluorophore labeling by primary and secondary antibodies therefore precludes conversion of localization counts into absolute TFAM copy numbers. TFAM localization density should thus be interpreted as a quantitative measure of nanoscale TFAM organization rather than absolute TFAM abundance. Nevertheless, the concordance among MINFLUX imaging, biochemical reconstitution, TFAM oxidation assays, and functional rescue supports a model in which aging-associated oxidative stress remodels TFAM–mtDNA organization and contributes to mt-nucleoid decompaction.


### Redox-dependent mt-nucleoid remodeling during neuronal aging
  Redox signaling regulates protein function through reversible oxidative modifications, whereas oxidative stress occurs when ROS production exceeds cellular redox capacity. Although oxidative stress is a hallmark of aging,84–86 how chronic redox imbalance affects mitochondrial genome organization and bioenergetic remains poorly understood. Our findings support a framework in which oxidative modification of TFAM remodels mt-nucleoid architecture, linking mitochondrial redox state with mitochondrial gene expression and bioenergetic function during neuronal aging.

  By integrating mitochondria-targeted ATP and redox biosensors with MINFLUX nanoscopy, we resolved aging-associated changes at sub-organelle and single-molecule resolution. Our data support a model in which chronic oxidative stress shifts mt-nucleoids from compact, TFAM-enriched organizations toward decompacted configurations with reduced TFAM localization density, accompanied by reduced mitochondrial transcript abundance and bioenergetic decline. In young adult neurons, robust mitochondrial redox homeostasis is expected to maintain TFAM in its functional, mtDNA-compacting state, whereas chronic oxidative stress during aging promotes TFAM oxidation and mt-nucleoid remodeling. Consistent with this model, enlarged mt-nucleoids accompanied by reduced ATP production have been reported in Hutchinson–Gilford progeria syndrome fibroblasts,18 and impaired activity-induced dendritic mtDNA transcription in aged mouse brain slices87 suggests a diminished pool of transcriptionally competent mt-nucleoids during aging.

  Several independent observations support this framework. The oxidation-resistant TFAM variant preserved mt-nucleoid organization while restoring mitochondrial transcript abundance and ATP production in aged neurons, indicating that these changes are reversible. Oxidative stress induced similar mt-nucleoid remodeling in human iPSC-derived neurons, whereas selective mitochondrial ROS induction by MitoPQ phenocopied aging-associated remodeling in young mouse neurons. Together with observations in aged hippocampal neurons and biochemical TFAM-mtDNA reconstitution, these findings support an association between chronic oxidative stress and mt-nucleoid remodeling across neuronal contexts. However, the chronic low-dose H2O2 paradigm cannot completely exclude secondary stress responses and should therefore be viewed as modeling a sustained oxidative environment rather than directly recapitulating endogenous mitochondrial ROS dynamics during aging.

  A further limitation is that mitochondrial redox dynamics and mt-nucleoid organization could not be monitored simultaneously in living neuron. Live-cell redox measurements require genetically encoded or chemical ROS reporters, whereas nanoscale mt-nucleoid architecture currently relies on fixation-dependent super-resolution imaging. Nevertheless, complementary evidence from naturally aged neurons, human iPSC-derived neurons, MitoPQ-induced mitochondrial ROS, and biochemical TFAM–mtDNA reconstitution consistently supports a strong association between mitochondrial oxidative stress and mt-nucleoid remodeling.

  Mitochondrial dysfunction has been implicated in age-associated sensory deficits.88,89 Consistent with this concept, aged phasic DRGNs exhibited reduced intrinsic excitability together with diminished mitochondrial ATP production. TFAM-C246A partially restored neuronal excitability by reducing rheobase but did not normalize other action potential parameters, indicating that mitochondrial ATP deficiency contributes to, but does not fully account for, age-associated functional decline. Chronic oxidative stress may additionally affect ion channels90–92 and membrane protein homeostasis,90,93,94 potentially limiting electrophysiological recovery despite restored mitochondrial bioenergetics.

#### DRG neuron transfection
  For genetic manipulation experiments, DRG neurons isolated from adult and aged mice were either transfected with plasmid DNA prior to plating using a Nucleofector (Lonza), following the manufacturer’s instructions, or transduced with lentiviruses at DIV4. For mitochondrial ATP or ROS measurements, neurons were transfected with mitochondria-targeted biosensors: 1 μg mito-GO-ATeam2 for monitoring ATP production or 2.5 μg mito-roGFP for assessing redox status. In rescue experiments assessing mitochondrial mt-nucleoid compaction and mtDNA transcripts, aging neurons were transfected with 0.5 μg of GFP-mito (control), wild-type hTFAM, or oxidation-resistant hTFAM-C246A. In rescue experiments assessing mitochondrial ATP, aging neurons were transduced with 1 μg mito-iATPSnFR2 lentivirus alone, or together with 2 μg hTFAM or hTFAM-C246A lentivirus at DIV4.


#### HiPSC neuronal mitochondrial fractionation
  Human iPSC-derived cortical neurons were harvested at DPI22–24, washed in ice-cold PBS, and briefly centrifuged. The cell pellets were resuspended in 3x volumes of lysis buffer (10 mM NaCl, 10 mM HEPES, 1.5 mM MgCl2, and a protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche)) and incubated on ice for 15 min. Cells were mechanically disrupted by passing through a 30-gauge needles 15 times. The lysates were centrifuge at 2,000 × g for 5 min at 4°C. The supernatants were collected and centrifuged again at 7,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. After removing the supernatants, the pellets were washed with 1 ml of ice-cold PBS and re-centrifuged at 11,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The resulting pellets, representing the mitochondrial fraction, were used for further analysis via mass spectrometry or immunoblotting.


#### Immunofluorescence
  For tissue sample preparation, Animals were anesthetized and transcardially perfused with 1× PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences). Tissues were cryoprotected in 30% sucrose (Sigma-Aldrich), sectioned at 10 μm, and subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0; Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min at 80°C. For cell sample preparation, cultured mouse and human neurons were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences) with 4% sucrose (Sigma-Aldrich) at room temperature (RT) for 20 min, followed by three washes with PBS (5 min each). Neurons were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 15 min. Tissue sections and neuron samples were then blocked for 1 hour in blocking buffer (PBS containing 10% normal goat serum, Sigma-Aldrich), followed by incubation overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer. The following primary antibodies and dilutions were used: mouse anti-DNA (1:200–1:400, Millipore), rabbit anti-TFAM (1:500 for confocal microscopy, 1:200 for MINFLUX nanoscopy, Abcam), mouse/rabbit anti-TOM20 (1:500, Santa Cruz), mouse anti-ATP5A (1:400, Santa Cruz) and rabbit anti-GRSF1 (1:500, Abcam). After three 5-min washes with PBS at RT, samples were incubated for 60 min with secondary fluorescent antibodies diluted in blocking buffer. For confocal microscopy, Alexa Fluor 488 or 546 conjugated antibodies (ThermoFisher Scientific) were used, while abberior Flux 647, Flux 640 and Flux 680 were used for MINFLUX nanoscopy. Nuclei were stained with To-Pro-3 (1 μM, Invitrogen). Following three 10-min washes with 0.05% Triton X-100 in PBS, the samples were mounted with ProLong™ Diamond Antifade Mountant (Invitrogen) before imaging.


#### Measurement of mitochondrial ATP levels

##### Mito-GO-ATeam2
  Mitochondrial ATP levels were measured with mito-GO-ATeam2 as described in our previous study47. Briefly, DRG neurons were nucleofected with 1 μg mito-GO-ATeam2 at DIV0 and imaged at DIV4–5 using live-cell imaging buffer (Hibernate A low fluorescence medium (BrainBits) supplemented with 2% B22 and 0.5 mM L-GlutaMAX). Imaging was performed on a Zeiss 880 Airyscan confocal microscope. The mito-GO-ATeam2 sensor was excited at 488 nm, and emissions were collected at 510 nm (GFP) and above 560 nm (OFP). To quantify ATP levels, ImageJ (NIH) was used. A selection was defined within the axon or cell body region. Within this selection, the OFP signal was divided by the GFP signal to generate ratiometric intensities for quantification. To create a visual heatmap of the ratiometric signal, background signal was removed using the “clear outside” function to set background signal to zero. The Image Calculator function was used to divide the OFP signal by the GFP signal, producing a representative image. This image was then converted into a heatmap using the “fire” lookup table. All side-by-side heatmaps within an experiment were presented using the same brightness and contrast settings. For time-series analysis, each ATP measurement was normalized to the mean of the baseline.


##### Mito-iATPSnFR2
  Mitochondrial matrix-targeted iATPSnFR2 fused to HaloTag (referred to as mito-iATPSnFR2) was used to assess mitochondrial ATP levels in aged neurons under oxidative stress. Aged DRG neurons were transduced with mito-iATPSnFR2 lentivirus at DIV4 either alone or together with other lentiviruses as indicated. Neurons were incubated with HaloTag-JFX650 (100 nM) for 30 min before imaging in live-cell imaging buffer. The mito-iATPSnFR2 sensor and HaloTag-JFX650 were excited at 488 and 633 nm respectively, and emissions were collected at 510–530 nm (green) and above 650 nm (far-red). To quantify ATP levels, ImageJ (NIH) was used. A selection within the cell body region was defined using the far-red channel. Within this selection, the green signal was divided by the far-red signal (to normalize for expression) to generate ratiometric intensities for quantification. To create a visual heatmap, the background signal was first removed using the “clear outside” function. The image calculator function was then used to divide the green signal by the far-red signal, producing a ratiometric image. This image was then converted into a heatmap using the “fire” lookup table. All side-by-side heatmaps within an experiment were presented using the same brightness and contrast settings.


#### Measurement of mitochondrial redox state
  To assess mitochondrial ROS levels, DRG neurons were nucleofected with 2.5 μg mito-roGFP at DIV0 and imaged at DIV4 using a Zeiss 880 Airyscan confocal microscope. Live imaging was performed in Hibernate A low fluorescence media (BrainBits) supplemented with 2% B22 and 0.5 mM L-GlutaMAX. The mito-roGFP sensor was excited at 405 nm and 488 nm simultaneously, with emission collected at 510 nm. Oxidation of cysteine residues in mito-roGFP was indicated by increased emission at 405 nm excitation, while reduction of cysteines was indicated by increased emission at 488 nm excitation. The mitochondrial redox state was quantified as the 510 nm emission ratio at 405 nm (oxidized) to 488 nm (reduced) excitation. For neurons treated with 50 μM H2O2, imaging was conducted for 30 min immediately after treatment. Time-series imaging involved capturing basal mitochondrial redox levels in individual axons or cell bodies every 10 sec for 180 sec (18 frames), followed by imaging every 10 sec for 300 sec (30 frames) during H2O2 treatment. To analyze time-series images, each redox measurement was normalized to the mean of the basal condition. Representative heatmap images were generated using the ImageJ Image Calculator function, with consistent brightness and contrast settings applied to all side-by-side heatmaps.


#### Measurement of mitochondrial membrane potential
  To assess mitochondrial membrane potential, DRG neurons were sequentially stained with 25 nM TMRE for 20 min, followed by 10 nM MitoTracker Green for 30 min at 37°C. Coverslips were rinsed three times with pre-warmed live-cell imaging buffer containing Hibernate A low fluorescence media (BrainBits) supplemented with 2% B22, 0.5 mM L-GlutaMAX, and 5nM TMRE) before imaging on a Zeiss 880 Airyscan confocal microscope. To analyze mitochondrial membrane potential, the integrated density of TMRE fluorescence was calculated by dividing the raw integrated density of TMRE by the mitochondrial area, as determined using MitoTracker Green. For time-series analysis, the integrated density of TMRE was normalized to the mean value of the basal condition.


#### Seahorse analysis
  The oxygen consumption rate (OCR) of DRG neurons isolated from P1M and P24M mice was measured using a Seahorse Bioscience Extracellular Flux Analyzer (XFe96) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Neurons were seeded at a density of 3000–5000 cells per well, determined through cell titration experiments. The cells were maintained in regular DRG medium, with media changes every 2–3 days, and were analyzed at DIV5. Before the assay, neurons were washed with pre-warmed Seahorse assay medium (120 mM NaCl, 3.5 mM KCl, 1.3 mM CaCl2, 0.4 mM KH2PO4, 1 mM MgCl2, 5 mM HEPES, 10 mM sodium pyruvate, and 10 mM glucose in dH2O, pH 7.4) and incubated in a CO2-free 37°C incubator for 1 hour. OCR was measured at the basal level and following sequential injections of oligomycin, FCCP, and rotenone/antimycin-A at a final concentration of 2.0 μM, 1.0 μM, and 1.5 μM, respectively. The Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test Report Generator (Agilent) was used to analyze the data, providing values for basal respiration, ATP production, maximal respiration, and spare respiratory capacity. Consistent with previous methodology98, wells with OCR measurements below zero were considered erroneous and excluded from analysis.


#### Detection in vivo neuronal O2−
  Neuronal superoxide (O2−) levels were measured in vivo using Dihydroethidium (DHE) fluorescence. DHE is a cell-permeable compound that reacts with O2−, converting into ethidium, which exhibits fluorescence. Briefly, a DHE stock solution was prepared at a concentration of 10 μg/μL in DMSO and stored at −20°C. Mice at P3M and P24M received two intraperitoneal injections of DHE working solution (final volume 500 μL diluted in PBS), with a total dose of 27 μg/g body weight administered at one-hour intervals. Aged mice without DHE injection were used as controls to evaluate the age-dependent autofluorescence increase in these tissues. Paraquat (PQ) was also used to induce ROS in vivo as a positive control. Briefly, a PQ stock solution (20 mg/mL in PBS) was administered to mice by intraperitoneal (IP) injection at a dose of 20 mg/kg for three consecutive days. Following three injections, DHE was administered as described above. Eighteen hours after injection, the animals were then anesthetized and perfused transcardially with 1X PBS, followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) fixation and equilibration in 30% sucrose. DRG and brain cryosections (5 μm thick) were then processed for counterstaining with NeuroTrace™ 435/455 Blue Fluorescent Nissl Stain to detect the neuron. Samples were imaged using a Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope with excitation at 514 nm and emission at 640 ± 60 nm. Ethidium fluorescence intensity and area were measured for analysis using ImageJ.


#### MINFLUX imaging
  To prepare for MINFLUX imaging, 100 μl of undiluted gold nanoparticles (EM.GC150/4, BBI Solutions) was added to DRG neurons on an 18-mm coverslip. The sample was incubated for 10 min, followed by immersion in PBS for 5 min to remove unbound nanoparticles. Coverslips were then mounted onto single concave glass slides (ML1438, Flinn Scientific) using MINFLUX imaging buffer containing 200 mM Tris/HCl, 10 mM NaCl, 10% (w/v) glucose, 64 μg/ml catalase, 0.4 mg/ml glucose oxidase, and MEA (9.5 mM for one-color, 30.9 mM for two-color) at pH 8.0. Coverslips were sealed with Elite double 22 dental epoxy (Zhermack) to prevent leakage during imaging.

  A commercial 3D MINFLUX microscope was used for imaging, with data acquisition managed by Imspector software (Abberior Instruments)99,100. After selecting the desired cell, sample stabilization in x, y and z axes was activated by locking onto multiple gold nanoparticles within the field of view, maintaining a relative standard deviation below 2 nm in all axes during measurements. Regions of interest (ROIs) were selected for imaging. Prior to MINFLUX data acquisition, fluorophores were driven into the dark state through iterative confocal scanning using a 640 nm excitation laser at 8–15% power (maximum power: 1.94 mW). For one-color MINFLUX, standard MINFLUX 2D or 3D imaging sequences were conducted with fixed 640 nm laser power at 3% and 8% for 2D and 3D acquisitions, respectively. During imaging, the 405 nm activation laser power was gradually increased from 0% to 50% over several hours (maximum power: 27 μW). Each 5 × 5 μm2 area was imaged with beamline monitoring activated to correct the drift during long-term imaging. Similarly, for two-color MINFLUX, standard MINFLUX 2D imaging sequence was conducted with fixed 640 nm laser power at 4.5%. During imaging, the 405 nm activation laser power was gradually increased from 0% to 50% over several hours (maximum power: 27 μW). Each 5 × 5 μm2 area was imaged with beamline monitoring activated to correct the drift during long-term imaging. For one-color MINFLUX 2D image data, raw valid localization positions were exported from Imspector software as a .json file. Custom Python scripts were used to identify and segregate localized clusters. Localization traces with fewer than three points (considered noise) were filtered out82,101 and denoised images were generated using the custom Python code.


#### MINFLUX data analysis

##### TFAM localization density:
  For randomly selected 5 × 5 μm2 areas, all valid localization traces (Trace ID) were extracted from individual mt-nucleoids and denoised as described above. Trace IDs were counted, and the nucleoid surface area was delineated. The relative TFAM localization density was calculated as the number of unique Trace ID divided by the outlined mt-nucleoid surface area (nm2). The distribution of relative TFAM localization density in young and aged neurons was visualized using violin plots and statistically compared via the Mann-Whitney test.


##### mtDNA area:
  For randomly selected 5 × 5 μm2 areas, all valid localization traces (Trace ID) were visualized in the pyMINFLUX software102. For two-color MINFLUX datasets, valid localizations were filtered and assigned to a specific fluorophore based on its detector channel ratio (DCR) value using automatic DCR unmixing tool. Individual mt-nucleoids were registered, and the data points of the mtDNA channel were extracted. For each mt-nucleoid, the area of mtDNA (nm2) was calculated using the Convex Hull Area in MATLAB. The distribution of mtDNA area in young and aging neurons was generated using violin plots and statistically compared via the Mann-Whitney test.


#### 3D visualization of MINFLUX data
  Videos of 3D MINFLUX data were generated by modified MATLAB code from previous literatures82,101. Briefly, raw valid localization positions were exported from Imspector software as a mat file. The custom MATLAB scripts were used to identify and segregate localized clusters. Localization traces with fewer than three points (considered noise) were filtered out. Filtered localizations were undergone two-step density-based clustering (dbscan2 in MATLAB)78 followed by an expectation maximization GMM to assign 3D localizations to the position of fluorophores101. The first DBSCAN step had an epsilon of 10 nm and minpts = 2. The second DBSCAN step had an epsilon of 7 nm and minpts = 2. The initial GMM fit sigma was set to 5 nm. The clustered data in a ROI were selected and plotted into a 3D scatter plot (300 × 300 × 300 nm3) with alphaShape in MATLAB for 3D volume estimation. All videos were generated with alphaRadius = 100 revolving around the z-axis.


#### Recombinant protein purification
  For protein expression, mature forms of hTFAM (43–246), mTFAM (45–243) and their mutants were transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) cells (Invitrogen) and cultured in LB medium supplemented with 50 μg/ml kanamycin at 37°C. When cultures reached an OD600 of 0.6–0.8, protein expression was induced by adding 0.5 mM isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), followed by incubation overnight at 16°C. The bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation and resuspended in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris, 500 mM NaCl, 1 mM PMSF). Cell pellets were lysed by sonication, and the lysates were clarified by centrifugation. The supernatants were subjected to affinity purification using High-Affinity Ni-Charged Resin (Genscript) and eluted with elution buffer (20 mM Tris, 500 mM NaCl, 500 mM imidazole, pH 7.4). Proteins were further purified by ion-exchange chromatography on a 5 ml HiTrap Heparin column (Cytiva), followed by size-exclusion chromatography using a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 column (Cytiva). The final purified proteins were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C in storage buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 500 mM NaCl). For protein labeling, Alexa Fluor™ 488 NHS Ester (Invitrogen) was dissolved in DMSO and incubated with purified TFAM proteins at a 1:1 molar ratio at room temperature for 1 hour. Excess dye was removed using MicroSpin G-50 Columns (Cytiva). For fluorescence imaging, 5% of the labeled protein was mixed with unlabeled protein to achieve optimal signal-to-background ratios.


#### mtDNA amplification and purification
  Total DNA was extracted from human HEK 293 cells. For the amplification of mtDNA-ND1, PCR was performed using the following primers: forward, CTACGGGCTACTACAACCCTTC; reverse, GGGGGAATGCTGGAGATTGTAA as previously reported19. To amplify full-length mtDNA, long-range PCR was conducted using total DNA as the template. Two overlapping fragments of 9 kb and 7 kb were amplified using the following primer pairs: for 9 kb fragment, forward: AACCAAACCCCAAAGACACC; reverse: GCCAATAATGACGTGAAGTCC; for 7 kb fragment, forward: TCCCACTCCTAAACACATCC; reverse: ATGGGGTGATGTGAGCC as previously reported18,67. The mtDNA-ND1 fragment was purified using a Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen), while full-length mtDNA was purified using the Zymoclean Large Fragment DNA Recovery Kit (Zymo Research). Purified mtDNA was quantified and stored at −20 °C.


#### Phase separation assays
  TFAM–mtDNA phase separation assays were performed following established guidelines for biomolecular condensate assays66. The mtDNA-ND1 and full-length mtDNA were diluted to the appropriate concentrations in the phase separation buffer containing 20 mM HEPES and 500 mM NaCl, then labeled with DAPI (final concentration: 50 μg/ml). For fluorescence imaging of TFAM, 5% Alexa Fluor™ 488-labeled TFAM was mixed with 95% unlabeled TFAM to achieve an optimal signal-to-background ratio and then diluted to the appropriate concentrations in the phase separation buffer. For oxidative stress experiments, H2O2 was diluted to the desired concentrations in the phase separation buffer. Phase separation assays were conducted by mixing 5 μl of protein solution and 5 μl of DNA solution, or 5 μl of protein solution with 4 μl of DNA solution and 1 μl of H2O2. These mixtures were prepared in 384-well low-binding microscopy plates with 0.17-mm glass bottoms (Cellvis). The samples were incubated at room temperature for 2 hours before imaging using a Zeiss LSM 880 Airyscan confocal microscope. For the reductive recovery assays, DTT was diluted to a final concentration of 1 mM in 20 mM HEPES and 500 mM NaCl. TFAM proteins were mixed with mtDNA-ND1 and H2O2 and incubated for 45 min. Following this, 1 μl of DTT was added, and the reaction was incubated for an additional 20 min before imaging.


#### Gel-filtration chromatography and SDS-PAGE
  TFAM and its mutants were treated with either 0.3 mM H2O2 or 1 mM DTT for 1 hour at room temperature. To analyze molecular weight changes, samples were subjected to 4–12% NuPAGE™ Bis-Tris Mini Protein Gels (Invitrogen). Gels were run according to the manufacturer’s protocol to resolve protein bands. For gel-filtration chromatography, 500 μl of TFAM or mutant protein solution (2.5 mg/ml) was loaded onto a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 column pre-equilibrated with phase separation buffer. Chromatography data were recorded, analyzed, and acquired using the UNICORN™ 7.1 software.


#### mTFAM-LSP Binding Assay
  mTFAM–LSP binding was analyzed by EMSA (Electrophoretic Mobility-Shift Assay Kit, Thermofisher). Reactions (10 μl) contained 25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.0, 90 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 0.01% Tween, 0.3 μM LSP DNA and recombinant mTFAM at 0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.8 or 2 μM. For oxidized TFAM, 10 μM mTFAM was incubated with 2 mM H2O2 at room temperature for 1 h and then diluted to the indicated concentrations immediately before use in the binding reactions. Binding reactions were assembled on ice, incubated for 1 h at room temperature and mixed with 6× native loading dye (Thermofisher). Complexes were resolved on 6% DNA Retardation Gels (Thermofisher) in 0.5× TBE at 4 °C and stained with SYBR Gold (Thermofisher). Band intensities were quantified in Fiji, and the fraction of DNA bound was calculated as shifted DNA over total DNA (shifted + free) after background subtraction. For each biological replicate, control and oxidized TFAM were analyzed over the same series of protein concentrations. Data were analyzed by two-way ANOVA, and, where indicated, pairwise comparisons between control and oxidized TFAM at each concentration were performed using unpaired Student’s t-tests. LSP double strand sequence: 5’-TAACAGTCACCCCCCAACTAAC-3’


#### Immunoblotting
  DRG or i3 neurons were lysed in RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% SDS, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate) supplemented with protease inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using a Pierce BCA Protein Assay kit, and equal amounts of protein were resolved on NuPAGE 4–12% Bis-Tris gels. Proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes at 250 mA constant current for 70 minutes at 4°C. Membranes were blocked in 10% milk in TBS containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST) for 1 hour at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies (1:1000 in 5% milk in TBST) overnight at 4°C. Membranes were washed four times for 10 minutes each in TBST, then incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-linked secondary antibodies (1:5000 in 5% milk in TBST) for 1 hour at room temperature. After four additional 10-minute washes in TBST, proteins were detected using SuperSignal chemiluminescent substrates, imaged with an Amersham ImageQuant 800 system, and analyzed using ImageJ. The following primary antibodies were used: human TFAM (ab176558; Abcam), mouse TFAM (ab273508; Abcam), TOM20 (42406; Cell Signaling or sc11415; Santa Cruz), and β3-tubulin (T8578; Sigma-Aldrich). The following secondary antibodies were used: anti-rabbit IgG, HRP-linked (NA934; Cytiva) and anti-mouse IgG, HRP-linked (NA931; Cytiva).


#### Mass Spectrometry
  The purified His-tagged hTFAM or His-tagged mTFAM was exposed to 2 mM H2O2 for two hours, followed by digestion directly with trypsin, without prior reduction or alkylation. Disulfide bond analysis was performed on a nano-LC-MS/MS system where an Ultimate 3000 HPLC was coupled to an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos Tribrid Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). Peptides were separated on an ES902 column. Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid in LC-MS-grade water. Mobile phase B (0.1% formic acid in LC-MS-grade acetonitrile) was increased from 3% to 22% over 40 min, then from 22% to 80% over 10 min. LC-MS/MS data were acquired in data-dependent mode using the EThcD fragmentation method. TFAM sequence was added to a custom in-house database. TFAM tryptic peptides containing Cys residues were used to generate house-built modifications. Raw data were processed using Mascot Distiller and searched with Mascot Daemon software (Matrix Science) against both Sprot Human and house-built databases. Oxidation (M), dioxidation (C), dioxidation (M), trioxidation (C), and house-built modifications were set as variable modifications. Crosslinked peptides identified by database search were manually curated.

  Methionine oxidation analysis was performed on an Orbitrap Ascend Tribrid Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) connected with Neo Vanquish HPLC (Thermo Scientific). Other conditions were the same as described above except that MPB was increased from 4% to 28% over 29 min, then from 28% to 80% over 4.5 min. LC-MS/MS data were acquired in data-dependent mode using the CID fragmentation method. House-built database consisting of mouse TFAM sequence was used during Mascot search. Proteome Discoverer software version 3.2 was used for protein quantitation. Up to 1 missed cleavage was allowed for trypsin digestion. Variable modifications include Oxidation (M), dioxidation (M), Met-loss (Protein-N-term), and Acetyl (Protein N-term). Mass tolerances for MS1 and MS2 scans were set to 5 ppm and 0.5 Da, respectively. A percolator was used for PSM validation. The search results were filtered by a false discovery rate of 1% at the protein level. Protein abundance values were calculated for all proteins identified by summing the abundance of unique peptides matched to that protein, then normalized to total peptide abundances. Protein/peptide ratios were calculated by comparing the normalized protein/peptide abundances between control and H2O2 treatment. p-values were calculated with the unpaired Student’s t-test.


#### In situ hybridization (smFISH) analysis
  The smFISH analysis was performed using a kit from Molecular Instruments, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, DRG neurons were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences) and 4% sucrose (Sigma-Aldrich) at RT for 20 min. After fixation, the neurons were washed three times with PBS (5 min per wash) and permeabilized overnight at −20°C in 70% ethanol. After a brief rinse with 2X SSC buffer, neurons were pre-hybridized in probe hybridization buffer at 37°C for 30 min. They were then incubated overnight at 37°C with a 16-nM DNA probe mixture targeting NADH dehydrogenase 1 (ND1, Complex I), cytochrome b (Cytb, Complex III), cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI, Complex IV), or ATPase 6 (ATP6, ATP synthase) in probe hybridization buffer. Following hybridization, the samples underwent a series of washes with pre-warmed probe wash buffer (4 washes, 5 min each at 37°C) and 5X SSC buffer (5 min at RT). The neurons were then pre-amplified in amplification buffer for 30 min at RT, followed by overnight incubation at RT with a 60-nM solution of metastable fluorescent hairpins (h1 and h2; B2–488 or B2–546). After additional washes (5 washes, 5 min each in 5X SSC), the samples were mounted using ProLong™ Diamond Antifade Mountant (Invitrogen). Images were acquired using a Zeiss 880 Airyscan confocal microscope. The fluorescent density of single mRNA molecules (number of mRNA puncta per cell body) was quantified using ImageJ with thresholding.


#### Machine learning-based analysis

##### Manual annotation of mtDNA and TFAM fluorescent signals.
  Prior image annotation, criteria were established to classify punctate and diffuse mt-nucleoid phenotypes in Airyscan imaging. Punctate signals were defined as approximately circular regions with high fluorescent intensity resembling a 2D Gaussian distribution. Diffuse signals were defined as morphologically irregular regions with relatively dim, heterogeneous fluorescence intensity that deviates from a Gaussian profile. Annotations were performed using the Annotator FIJI plugin38.


##### iUnet architecture and training.
  Semantic segmentation was performed using an iUNet-based architecture trained on paired Airyscan confocal images and corresponding human-annotated ground truths of punctate and diffuse signals. The network is based on UNet backbone with encoder layers replaced with InceptionV3 modules103, enabling multi-scale feature extraction via parallel convolutional kernels. Hybrid pooling layers combining max pooling and spectral pooling were used to capture both fluorescent intensity and spatial frequency features104. Training data consisted of six manually annotated Airyscan images (three from P1M and three from P28M DRG neurons. To increase data diversity, images were subdivided into patches and augmented extensively. Specifically, 96 × 96 pixel patches were randomly extracted, generating millions of training samples. Data augmentation included random flipping, rotation, cropping, shear, blur, noise addition, contrast adjustment, and intensity inversion. Ground-truth masks were randomly eroded or dilated to simulate annotation variability. Patches were split into training (90%) and validation (10%) sets. Hyperparameters were optimized across multiple training runs (Table S1).

  Model weights were optimized on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU (80GB) from the NIH’s Biowulf HPC cluster using Stochastic Gradient Descent and a weighted Dice Loss objective function105 over 38 hours utilizing 15-epoch early stopping to prevent overfitting.

  In L, n is defined as the number of phenotypic categories plus background (punctate, diffuse, and neither in this study). The ratio of overlapping signals between predicted regions and true regions is calculated for each ith category and weighted by parametrically optimized weights wi (Table S1).


##### Model inference and ROI quantification.
  For inference, input images were normalized and processed using a sliding-window approach with 96 × 96 patches. Each pixel was evaluated across multiple overlapping patches, and class probabilities were aggregated to assign final labels. ROIs corresponding to punctate and diffuse mt-nucleoids were identified. Quantitative analysis of ROIs was performed using FIJI particle analysis. Metrics including ROI size, circularity, and mean fluorescence intensity were extracted and analyzed in GraphPad Prism. Outliers were removed prior to visualization, and data were presented as SuperPlots.


##### iUnet model evaluation.
  Model performance was assessed using both quantitative metrics and qualitative validation. The final model achieved high segmentation accuracy (Dice score ~0.88 and pixel-level accuracy ~96%). Performance was validated on independent images not used during training. iUNet segmentation showed high concordance with expert human annotations (Figure S3). To further benchmark performance, iUNet segmentation was compared with manual annotations from three independent experts and a threshold-based segmentation pipeline. These comparisons confirmed the robustness and reliability of the model.


##### Summary of model training strategy.
  Although only limited numbers of images were manually annotated, patch-based training generated millions of training patches, and extensive data augmentation increased dataset diversity while reducing overfitting. Model performance was validated on independent, non-training images and showed strong agreement with expert manual annotations, supporting robust segmentation within the experimental domain. This approach is widely used in biomedical image segmentation, where manual annotation is often the primary limiting factor.


#### Whole-cell current-clamp recordings
  Whole-cell current-clamp recordings were performed on cultured DRG neurons at DIV4–5. Coverslips containing neurons were transferred to a submerged recording chamber mounted on an upright microscope (BX50WI, Olympus) and continuously superfused at room temperature with artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) containing (in mM): 125 NaCl, 25 NaHCO3, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 10 D-glucose, 2 MgCl2, and 2 CaCl2, equilibrated with 95% O2/5% CO2. Patch pipettes (3–5 MΩ; Sutter Instrument) were filled with intracellular solution containing (in mM): 146.5 K-gluconate, 7.5 KCl, 9 NaCl, 1 MgCl2, 10 HEPES, and 0.2 EGTA (pH 7.3, adjusted with KOH; 295 mOsm). Recordings were obtained using a MultiClamp 700B amplifier (Molecular Devices). Signals were low-pass filtered at 2 kHz and digitized at 20 kHz with a Digidata 1440A interface controlled by pCLAMP 10.2 software (Molecular Devices). Cells exhibiting >20% changes in series resistance during recording were excluded from analysis. Immediately after establishing the whole-cell configuration, the resting membrane potential (RMP) was recorded at 0 pA. Membrane potential was then adjusted to −70 mV by constant current injection to standardize the initial membrane potential across neurons. APs were evoked by depolarizing current injections delivered in 20-pA increments. Neurons were classified as phasic-firing if they generated a single AP throughout the depolarizing current pulse regardless of stimulus intensity, or tonic-firing if they generated repetitive APs during sustained depolarization. Because tonic-firing neurons exhibited substantially greater electrophysiological heterogeneity, quantitative analyses were restricted to phasic-firing neurons.

  Rheobase was defined as the minimum depolarizing current required to evoke the first AP. AP threshold was defined as the membrane potential at which the first derivative of membrane voltage (dV/dt) exceeded 10 mV/ms. AP amplitude was calculated as the difference between the AP peak membrane potential and AP threshold. AP overshoot was defined as the membrane potential above 0 mV at the AP peak. AP half-width was measured at 50% of AP amplitude, and maximal AP upstroke velocity (max dV/dt) was defined as the maximum first derivative of the AP upstroke. All AP waveform parameters were quantified from the first AP evoked at rheobase. Membrane capacitance (Cm) was obtained from the whole-cell capacitance reported by the amplifier. Input resistance (Rin) was calculated from the mean steady-state voltage responses to 300-ms hyperpolarizing current injections of −20, −40, and −60 pA.


### QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS
  All quantifications were conducted without blinding. Statistical parameters, including definitions of n values (e.g., number of experiments, replications, axons, organelles, or neurons), standard errors, and P values, are provided in the figures and their corresponding legends. Statistical analyses were performed using Prism 9 (GraphPad), with the choice of test determined by sample size and experimental design. For comparisons between two groups: an unpaired Student’s t-test was used when the sample size was ≥ 30; a Mann–Whitney test was applied when the sample size < 30. For comparisons among three or more groups: a one-way ANOVA with post hoc testing was used, while Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was applied to compare all groups. Data are presented as mean ± s.e.m., and differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times.

  SuperPlots were used to simultaneously visualize cell-to-cell variability and reproducibility across biological replicates. To account for the clustering of individual cellular measurements within biological replicates and avoid treating individual cellular measurements as independent biological replicates, statistical models were fitted using linear mixed-effects models in R, with experimental group specified as a fixed effect and biological replicate incorporated as a random effect. To accommodate differences in experimental design, two modeling approaches were used. For experiments in which control and treatment conditions were paired within the same biological replicate, replicate identity was modeled as a shared random intercept to account for the paired design. For experiments in which biological replicates were independent between experimental groups, replicate identity was defined separately within each treatment group, and a random-intercept model was fitted using lmer from the lme4/lmerTest packages. In addition, when within-replicate variability differed between treatment groups, models allowing treatment-specific residual variances were fitted using lme from the nlme package, where appropriate. When a mixed-effects model resulted in a singular fit, individual cellular measurements were averaged within each biological replicate and treatment condition, and the resulting biological-replicate means were analyzed using a linear model with treatment and replicate included as fixed effects. Mean differences between treatment groups were evaluated using estimated marginal means and linear contrasts implemented with the emmeans package, without adjustment for multiple comparisons where specified.
