# Identification of YL064 as a novel inhibitor of ATXN3 to suppress tumor growth and metastasis by promoting degradation of oncogenic substrates


## Abstract
  ATXN3 has recently emerged as a pro-oncogenic deubiquitinating enzyme (DUB) involved in the progression of multiple cancers, including breast and prostate cancer. However, the therapeutic potential of targeting ATXN3 remains largely unexplored due to the lack of effective inhibitors. In this study, we performed an Ub-AMC-based high-throughput screening assay using full-length ATXN3 and identified YL064, a derivative of the natural product sinomenine isolated from Sinomenium acutum, as a novel ATXN3 inhibitor. Direct interaction between YL064 and ATXN3 was confirmed by thermal shift assay, cellular thermal shift assay, surface plasmon resonance, and molecular docking analysis. Functionally, YL064 suppressed cancer cell proliferation, induced cell-cycle arrest, and reduced metastatic potential. Mechanistically, YL064 inhibited ATXN3 deubiquitinating activity and promoted the ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of ATXN3 substrates, including KLF4 and YAP. Furthermore, YL064 significantly inhibited tumor growth in an orthotopic breast cancer model in vivo. Collectively, our findings establish YL064 as a promising ATXN3 inhibitor that targets the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway to exert anti-tumor effects, highlighting its potential as a novel therapeutic agent for ATXN3-driven cancers.

  


## Introduction
  The deubiquitinating enzyme ATXN3 (Ataxin-3), a member of the Machado-Joseph disease (MJD) protease family, is a highly conserved and ubiquitously expressed protein encoded on chromosome 14q32.1 [1, 2]. Initially characterized for its role in spinocerebellar ataxia type 3 (SCA3) due to polyglutamine (polyQ) expansion [3], ATXN3 is now recognized as a multifunctional protein involved in diverse cellular processes, including maintaining genome integrity by regulating various key DNA damage response proteins [4, 5], acting as a transcriptional repressor [6], regulating DNA replication [7], regulating the cytoskeleton [8], apoptosis [8, 9], and cell-cycle progression [5, 10]. Beyond its physiological roles, ATXN3 has garnered increasing attention for its context-dependent functions in cancer. While it may act as a tumor suppressor in certain contexts, such as colorectal cancer [11], accumulating evidence highlights its pro-oncogenic role across various malignancies. Notably, its DUB activity promotes tumor progression by stabilizing key oncogenic substrates: it deubiquitinates and stabilizes Krüppel-like factor 4 (KLF4) in breast cancer [12], inhibits the degradation of Histone Deacetylase 6 (HDAC6) via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway (UPP) in pancreatic cancer [13], and stabilizes Yes-associated protein (YAP) in prostate cancer [14]. These findings collectively nominate ATXN3 as a promising therapeutic target in these cancers.

  Sinomenine, an alkaloid extracted from the medicinal plant Sinomenium acutum, is clinically used for treating rheumatoid arthritis [15] and has shown potential anti-tumor activity in preclinical models, such as glioblastoma and colon cancer [16, 17]. Its clinical translation in oncology is hampered by limitations including the requirement for high doses and associated side effects. However, it retains significant development potential owing to its involvement in a wide range of signaling pathways and favorable predictive results for multiple target sites [18]. To overcome these drawbacks, derivative synthesis has emerged as a promising strategy. The derivatives that have undergone structural modifications mainly at the A ring and C ring have shown excellent anti-tumor function [19]. A series of sinomenine derivatives synthesized by our group, undergoing modification on the B ring, have also presented better biological activity. As one of these derivatives, YL064 demonstrated superior therapeutic efficacy, therefore, exploring its functions and therapeutic effects will further promote the research progress of sinomenine and its derivatives in disease treatment.

  In this study, we aimed to identify and characterize a novel inhibitor of ATXN3 for cancer treatment. Through screening a series of small molecular and natural compounds, we identified YL064 as a potential inhibitor of ATXN3. Using a combination of biochemical, cellular, and in vivo models, we demonstrated that YL064 exerted potent anti-tumor effects by inhibiting ATXN3’s DUB activity, thereby promoting the degradation of its key oncogenic substrate. Our work thus not only reports YL064 as a promising lead compound but also validates the therapeutic strategy of targeting ATXN3 in tumors.


## Results

### YL064 is a promising inhibitor of ATXN3
  To identify potential inhibitors targeting ATXN3, we reconstituted its DUB activity in vitro by purifying the full-length ATXN3 protein. Using the fluorogenic substrate ubiquitin-AMC (Ub-AMC), a compound library of natural products and their analogs, such as bardoxolone methyl (CDDO-Me), oridonin, and isoalantolactone, was screened (Fig. 1A).   To further evaluate the stability and pharmacological potential of these compounds, dose–response assays were conducted for comparative analysis (Fig. 1C & Supplementary Fig. S1). The results revealed that YL064 suppressed ATXN3 enzymatic activity in a concentration-dependent manner and achieved substantial inhibition at relatively low concentrations, indicating both high potency and stable efficacy (Fig. 1C).


### YL064 directly interacts with ATXN3
  Given that YL064 markedly suppressed ATXN3 enzymatic activity in vitro, we hypothesized that this compound might interact directly with ATXN3. To examine this possibility, we performed Thermal shift assay (TSA) and Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA). The TSA result provided clear evidence that YL064 binds directly to the purified ATXN3 protein (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, CETSA analyses conducted across both concentration and temperature gradients demonstrated that YL064 decreased the thermal stability of ATXN3 relative to the DMSO control, further supporting a direct interaction between YL064 and ATXN3 within the cellular environment of breast cancer cells (Fig. 2B, C).   To further analyze the binding mechanisms, molecular docking was performed to predict the binding pose of YL064 in ATXN3. In the docked pose, YL064 formed π-π interaction with F10 and a hydrogen bond with H124. The benzyl group inserted into a hydrophobic pocket composed of residues F10, L138, L142, and P145. Additionally, YL064 formed hydrophobic interactions with the side chain methylene groups of S17, H22 and S140 (Fig. 2F).

  To determine whether the predicted residues are critical for the interaction between YL064 and ATXN3, site-directed mutagenesis was performed to generate the single mutants F10A and H124A, as well as the double mutant F10A/H124A. Recombinant mutant ATXN3 proteins were subsequently purified and subjected to TSA analysis (Supplementary Fig. S2A, B). The thermal stability of the mutant proteins was reduced by YL064 even more markedly than that of the wild-type (WT) protein, indicating that YL064 was still able to bind ATXN3 despite these mutations (Supplementary Fig. S2A, B). However, this might be due to the changes of the intrinsic stability of the mutant proteins, as TSA results showed that the thermal stability of the mutant ATXN3 proteins was substantially impaired (Supplementary Fig. S2C). Interestingly, compared with wild-type ATXN3, all mutant proteins exhibited markedly reduced deubiquitinating activity, indicating that these residues are critical for maintaining the catalytic function of ATXN3 (Fig. 2G). These findings suggest that F10 and H124 are critical for the catalytic activity of ATXN3 and may also participate in the interaction between ATXN3 and YL064.


### YL064 exhibits greater potency than sinomenine in inhibiting ATXN3
  As described in the introduction, YL064 is a derivative of sinomenine. To determine whether the inhibitory effect of YL064 originated from its parent compound, we examined the potential interactions between ATXN3 and sinomenine, as well as its derivatives. TSA confirmed that sinomenine was capable of binding to the purified ATXN3 protein (Fig. 3A). In contrast, CETSA result indicated that sinomenine did not alter the thermal stability of ATXN3 at the same concentration used for YL064, suggesting a relatively weak interaction in the cellular context (Fig. 3B, C).Fig. 3Sinomenine inhibits ATXN3 with lower activity than YL064.A The TSA result of the interaction between purified Flag-ATXN3 protein and sinomenine (100 μM).   Subsequently, enzymatic activity assays were performed to systematically assess the inhibitory effects of sinomenine and its derivatives. Interestingly, both sinomenine and the majority of its analogs displayed measurable inhibition of ATXN3 (Fig. 3D & Supplementary Table S3). However, when comparing their potencies, sinomenine required substantially higher concentrations to achieve a level of inhibition comparable to that induced by low doses of YL064 (Fig. 3E).

  To gain structural insight into their distinct binding mode, we compared the docking conformations of sinomenine (Fig. 3F) and YL064. Unlike YL064, sinomenine exhibited an alternative binding pose that lacked the hydrogen bond and π-π interaction observed with YL064 (Fig. 3G). Additionally, sinomenine was unable to extend into the hydrophobic pocket. These structural differences in binding mode might account for the observed activity differences between YL064 and sinomenine.


### YL064 promotes the degradation of ATXN3 substrate KLF4 in breast cancer through UPP
  As ATXN3 is highly expressed in breast and prostate cancers (Supplementary Fig. S3), we next investigated the functional effects and mechanisms of YL064 in breast and prostate cancer cell lines. To determine whether YL064 suppresses the deubiquitinating activity of ATXN3, we examined KLF4, a known substrate of ATXN3 in breast cancer. First, we assessed the effect of YL064 on KLF4 expression across a range of concentrations. The results demonstrated that KLF4 protein levels decreased in a dose-dependent manner following YL064 treatment (Fig. 4A, B). To determine whether this down-regulation occurred at the transcriptional level, RT-qPCR analysis was conducted. The mRNA levels of KLF4 showed no significant change in either Py8119 or MCF-7 cells (Fig. 4C), indicating that the YL064-induced suppression of KLF4 occurred post-transcriptionally. We further investigated whether YL064 promoted KLF4 degradation. Measurement of KLF4 protein stability revealed that its half-life was shortened upon YL064 exposure (Fig. 4D–G). Treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 effectively restored KLF4 protein levels reduced by YL064 (Fig. 4H, I). Moreover, ubiquitination assays demonstrated that YL064 treatment enhanced KLF4 ubiquitination (Fig. 4J). ### YL064 inhibits the proliferation and metastasis of breast cancer cells
  We evaluated the cytotoxic potency of YL064 in the breast cancer cell lines Py8119, MCF-7, and MDA-MB-231. Dose–response curves were generated, yielding an average IC50 value of ~39.71 μM (Fig. 5A). However, the proliferation inhibitory activity of YL064 was unlikely to result from apoptosis induction. In parental and ATXN3-knockdown MCF-7 cells with or without YL064 treatment, there was no obvious increase in the percentage of apoptotic cells under these conditions (Supplementary Fig. S4A), and cleaved PARP1 was not detected following YL064 treatment (Supplementary Fig. S4B). To further assess its anti-proliferative effect, we conducted clonogenic assays. Compared with the control group, YL064-treated cells formed markedly fewer and smaller colonies (Fig. 5B, C). EdU incorporation analysis showed that YL064 treatment significantly reduced the percentage of EdU-positive cells, indicating impaired DNA synthesis and suppressed cell proliferation (Fig. 5D). We further examined the expression of proliferation-related markers and found that PCNA levels decreased in a dose-dependent manner following YL064 treatment (Fig. 5E). In addition, the cell-cycle regulators CDK1 and Cyclin B1 were markedly downregulated after drug treatment (Fig. 5E). Cell-cycle analysis additionally revealed that YL064 induced an accumulation of cells in the G2/M phase (Fig. 5F, G), indicating a blockade of cell-cycle progression.   To determine whether YL064 also affects metastatic behaviors, breast cancer cells were exposed to 20 μM YL064 for 24 h and subsequently subjected to migration and invasion assays. The number of cells traversing the transwell membrane was substantially reduced following treatment (Fig. 5H, I), suggesting that YL064 suppresses both migratory and invasive capabilities. These data indicate that YL064 exerts potent inhibitory effects on breast cancer cell growth, migration, and invasion in vitro.


### YL064 exerts anticancer effects in prostate cancer

Previous studies have demonstrated that ATXN3 facilitates prostate tumor progression by deubiquitinating and stabilizing YAP. In alignment with our observations in breast cancer models, treatment of PC-3 cells with increasing concentrations of YL064 resulted in a dose-dependent reduction of YAP protein expression (Fig. 6A).   Dose–response evaluation of YL064 in PC-3 cells yielded an IC50 value of 44.71 μM (Fig. 6G). Similar to the observations in breast cancer cells, the growth-inhibitory effect of YL064 in PC-3 cells was not associated with apoptosis induction, as no cleaved PARP1 was detected following treatment (Supplementary Fig. S4B). Colony formation assays showed a marked decrease in clonogenic survival following YL064 exposure, indicating strong suppression of PC-3 cell proliferative capacity (Fig. 6H, I). Moreover, in PC-3 cells, YL064 treatment similarly reduced the percentage of EdU-positive cells (Fig. 6J) and downregulated PCNA and Cyclin D1 expression (Fig. 6K). Flow cytometry analysis further demonstrated that YL064-induced G0/G1 cell-cycle arrest (Fig. 6L, M), consistent with the decreased Cyclin D1 expression and previous reports on the role of ATXN3 in cell-cycle regulation [14]. Collectively, these findings indicate that YL064 also inhibits ATXN3 activity in prostate cancer cells and impedes tumor cell proliferation by promoting YAP degradation.


### The cellular effect of YL064 is mediated by its inhibitory effect on ATXN3 activity
  The findings described above suggested that YL064 facilitated the degradation of ATXN3 substrates through UPP in both breast and prostate cancer cells, thereby exerting its anti-tumor activity. Notably, this effect was not attributed to a decrease in ATXN3 protein levels. To further confirm whether the enzyme activity of ATXN3 is critical for the anti-tumor effect of YL064, a rescue experiment was conducted. After overexpressing either the catalytically active ATXN3 WT or the catalytically inactive mutant ATXN3 C14A in Py8119, MCF-7, and PC-3 cells, the overexpression efficiency was detected (Fig. 7A). Then, the cells were treated with YL064 to test cell viability with CCK-8. The results demonstrated that overexpression of ATXN3 WT, but not ATXN3 C14A, significantly restored the cell viability suppressed by YL064 treatment (Fig. 7B).   We next knocked down ATXN3 in MCF-7 and PC-3 cells (Fig. 7C) to further determine whether the inhibitory effect of YL064 depends on ATXN3. YL064 was then applied to parental and ATXN3-knockdown cells, followed by examination of ATXN3 substrates. Upon YL064 treatment, KLF4 and YAP expression were reduced only in parental cells, but not in ATXN3-knockdown cells (Fig. 7D–G), suggesting that the regulation of these substrates by YL064 is ATXN3-dependent. However, CCK-8 analysis showed that YL064 still suppressed cell growth in ATXN3-depleted cells (Fig. 7H). In parallel, cell-cycle arrest was further enhanced by YL064 treatment. Both ATXN3 knockdown and YL064 treatment individually induced G2/M arrest, whereas YL064-treated ATXN3-knockdown MCF-7 cells exhibited a marked decrease in the G0/G1 population, accompanied by S-phase accumulation and enhanced G2/M arrest (Fig. 7I). These findings suggest that combined ATXN3 depletion and YL064 treatment disrupt cell-cycle progression. Overall, these results demonstrated that ATXN3 is a direct target of YL064 and that the anticancer effect of YL064 is partially dependent on ATXN3.


### YL064 suppressed the growth and metastasis of breast cancer cells in vivo

Tumor nodules became palpable ~5 days post-inoculation, at which point the animals were randomly assigned to either a control group or a YL064-treated group.   Comparative analysis of tumor size (Fig. 8B) and mass (Fig. 8C) revealed that YL064 administration suppressed tumor growth in treated mice. Consistent with the in vitro findings, YL064 retained its tumor-suppressive effect even after Atxn3 knockdown. Protein and mRNA analyses from excised tumors showed that KLF4 protein levels were substantially reduced in the YL064-treated group (Fig. 8D, E), while Klf4 mRNA expression remained unchanged (Fig. 8F), indicating the post-translational regulation involved. Meanwhile, in Atxn3-knockdown tumors, YL064 was unable to further down-regulate KLF4, suggesting that ATXN3 is the target of YL064 in vivo (Fig. 8D, E). Immunohistochemical staining further confirmed these observations, showing increased E-cadherin expression together with diminished levels of KLF4 and the proliferation marker Ki-67 in response to YL064 administration (Fig. 8G). These alterations indicated that YL064 effectively restrained tumor progression and metastatic potential.


## Discussion
  Despite the well-established oncogenic role of ATXN3 in multiple cancers, effective inhibitors targeting this DUB remain undeveloped. Our study addresses this critical gap by identifying YL064, a novel sinomenine derivative, as a potent and selective ATXN3 inhibitor. Through comprehensive biochemical and cellular analyses, we demonstrate that YL064 directly binds ATXN3, inhibits its DUB activity, and promotes proteasomal degradation of key oncogenic substrates, including KLF4 [12] in breast cancer and YAP in prostate cancer [14]. These molecular effects translate to significant suppression of tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion.

  Building on our previous work characterizing YL064 as a multi-target inhibitor of STAT3 [20] and c-MYC [21], we now establish ATXN3 as another important molecular target through multiple lines of evidence: [1] direct inhibition of ATXN3 catalytic activity in biochemical assays; [2] confirmation of physical interaction using thermal shift assays (TSA/CETSA); and [3] functional validation through ATXN3 substrate degradation. Importantly, the anti-tumor effects of YL064 were partially rescued by ATXN3 overexpression, establishing a clear mechanism of action. Meanwhile, knockdown of ATXN3 abolished the regulatory effects of YL064 on KLF4 and YAP protein stability, confirming that these downstream effects are largely ATXN3-dependent.

  Structural optimization of YL064, particularly through B-ring modification, confers significant pharmacological advantages over its parent compound sinomenine. Molecular docking reveals enhanced binding interactions including a critical hydrogen bond with H124 and π-π stacking with F10 in ATXN3’s Josephin domain, along with improved hydrophobic contacts. We generated mutant ATXN3 proteins and carried out the TSA assay to see if the interaction was hindered (Supplementary Fig. S2A, B). It should be noted that TSA primarily reflects changes in protein thermal stability and provides indirect evidence of protein-ligand interactions. Therefore, the current results are insufficient to definitively conclude that F10 and H124 are the direct binding sites between ATXN3 and YL064. Moreover, the mutations themselves significantly altered the intrinsic stability (Supplementary Fig. S2C) and deubiquitinating activity (Fig. 2G) of ATXN3, which may also influence the TSA outcomes. Nevertheless, the observed effects of these mutations on protein stability and enzymatic activity (Fig. 2G) suggest that F10 and H124 may play non-negligible roles in maintaining ATXN3 function and mediating its interaction with YL064. Further structural and biochemical studies are required to elucidate the precise mechanisms and binding mode involved.

  Our findings have particular relevance for breast and prostate cancers—the most prevalent malignancies in women and men, respectively. In breast cancer models, YL064 specifically promoted KLF4 degradation without affecting mRNA levels, while similar effects were observed for YAP in prostate cancer. Given ATXN3’s documented roles in additional malignancies, including thyroid [22] and hepatocellular carcinomas [23], YL064 may have broader therapeutic applications against ATXN3-dependent cancers. While our data clearly establish ATXN3 inhibition as a major mechanism underlying YL064 activity, YL064 still retained partial anti-proliferative effects in ATXN3-depleted cells both in vitro and in vivo, suggesting that its multi-target properties, including previously reported effects on STAT3 [20] and c-MYC [21], may also contribute to its overall anti-tumor efficacy.

  In conclusion, our study provides the first pharmacological evidence that targeted inhibition of ATXN3 can effectively disrupt oncoprotein stability and suppress tumor progression. YL064 represents a structurally and mechanistically novel lead compound in the growing field of DUB inhibitors. Future development should focus on optimizing YL064’s selectivity and pharmacokinetic properties while exploring its potential in combination therapies and additional ATXN3-driven malignancies. These findings open new avenues for targeting protein degradation in cancer therapy.


## Materials and methods

### Protein purification
  Transformed full-length ATXN3 with pET-SUMO as a carrier and His as a tag into Escherichia coli BL21 and amplified in YT medium. Stop the amplification when the O.D. value arrived at 0.6–1.0 induced by 0.2 mM Isopropyl β-D-Thiogalactoside (IPTG), 12 h, 24 °C for protein generation. Under high pressure, bacteria were crushed, and His-ATXN3 was purified by affinity chromatography through a nickel column. His-tag was enzymatically cut from ATXN3 by SENP2, and the purified protein ATXN3 was obtained.


### In vitro enzymatic assays
  The DUB activity of ATXN3 was reconstructed by using ubiquitin-7-amido-4-methylcoumarin (Ub-AMC) as substrate. With enzyme activity, ATXN3 could cleave and release dissociative Ub. Through detecting the fluorescence of Ub, the activity was indirectly presented. Similarly, the potent inhibitors were filtered in this way.


### Cell lines and cell culture
  Human breast cancer cell lines MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 were obtained from ATCC and cultured in DMEM (G4511-500ML, Servicebio, China) with 10% FBS (F101-01, Vazyme, China) and 1% Penicillin Streptomycin (MA0110, MeilunBio, China). Mouse breast cancer cell line Py8119 was obtained from ATCC and cultured in DMEM/F-12 (G4610-500ML, Servicebio, China) with 10% FBS and 1% Penicillin Streptomycin. Human prostate cancer cell line PC-3 was obtained from ATCC and cultured in DMEM/F-12 with 10% FBS and 1% Penicillin Streptomycin. HEK-293T cells were obtained from ATCC and cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% Penicillin Streptomycin. All cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. All cell stocks were authenticated by STR profiling and routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination and showed negative.


### Western blot analysis
  Purified proteins or proteins extracted from cells were split by using lysis buffer, which contains detergents and protease inhibitors, and heated in SDS loading buffer for 5–10 min at 98 °C three times. Equal protein samples were SDS-PAGE separated, then transferred to nitrocellulose membranes using an electro-blotting apparatus. Membranes were blocked in PBS-T with 5% skimmed milk, then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After PBS-T washing, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein expression was visualized using the ECL kit.

  Vinculin and α-Tubulin served as internal references for normalization of protein expression in the corresponding experiments. The antibodies used in the present study were as follows: ATXN3 antibody (Proteintech, 13505-1-AP; 1:2000), α-Tubulin antibody (Proteintech, 80762-1-RR; 1:2000), YAP antibody (Cell signaling technology, 4912; 1:2000), PCNA antibody (Proteintech, 10205-2-AP; 1:2000), Vinculin antibody (ABclonal, A2752; 1:1000), KLF4 antibody (HUABIO, ET1702-71; 1:2000), His-Tag antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 2365; 1:2000), Cyclin B1 antibody (Proteintech, 55004-1-AP; 1:1000), Cyclin D1 antibody (Proteintech, 60186-1-Ig; 1:2500), CDK1 antibody (HUABIO, ER31213; 1:500), Flag-Tag antibody (Smart-Lifescience, SLAB0102; 1:2000).


### Thermal shift assay (TSA) and cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)
  TSA and CETSA utilize the concept that target proteins would get stabilized or unstable when drug molecules bind in vitro or in cells to explore the interaction between target proteins and drug molecules [24]. For CETSA, cells were lysed in PBS, involving protease inhibitors, by ultrasound, and the cell suspension was centrifuged at 4 °C, 12,000 rpm. The supernatant was incubated with dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) and target drug molecules, respectively, at 37 °C for 2 h to bring the target protein and drug molecules into full alignment. The mixture was heated in a thermal gradient and tested by Western Blot analysis. Unlike CETSA, TSA was performed using purified proteins rather than cell lysates, including proteins obtained from prokaryotic expression systems or eukaryotic expression systems (with the eukaryotic-purified protein designated as Flag-ATXN3).


### Biacore surface plasmon resonance (SPR) assay
  The interaction between YL064 and ATXN3 was characterized using a Biacore 8 K SPR system (Cytiva). Recombinant ATXN3 protein was prepared in acetate buffer (pH 4.0) and covalently coupled to a CM5 sensor chip via an amine-coupling strategy using the manufacturer’s protocol (Cytiva, BR100050). All measurements were conducted at 25 °C using PBS-P+ as the running buffer. A blank flow cell without immobilized protein served as the reference control. Increasing concentrations of YL064 were then flowed across the chip surface to monitor real-time binding responses. After each injection cycle, the sensor surface was regenerated with 50% DMSO. Binding kinetics were analyzed using the Biacore evaluation software, and the equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) was calculated by nonlinear fitting of the response curves.


### RT-qPCR
  With the help of FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (RC112-01, Vazyme) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, total RNA was extracted from breast cancer cells and tumor tissues. Complementary DNA was transcribed through a reverse transcription kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. ACTB was applied as an internal control, while the relative mRNA expression was analyzed by the 2−ΔΔCt method. The relative sequences have been listed in the supplementary materials.


### CCK-8 proliferation assay
  Tumor cells were incubated with different concentrations of YL064 or DMSO for 24 h. Cell growth was assayed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cell’s response to each concentration is measured, and the data are plotted to generate a dose–response curve. The IC50 value is then determined from this curve.


### Clonogenic cell survival assay
  YL064-treated or control MCF-7, Py8119 and PC-3 cells were seeded onto 6-well cell culture dishes. The culture medium was refreshed with fresh medium every 3 days. After 10 days of treatment, the medium was aspirated, and cell colonies were fixed and stained with crystal violet (0.1% in 20% methanol). Surviving colonies were enumerated by ImageJ.


### EdU incorporation assay
  Cell proliferation was assessed using an EdU Cell Proliferation Kit (CX002, Epizyme, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were treated with YL064 (15 μM) or DMSO control overnight, followed by incubation with EdU for 2 h at 37 °C. The percentage of EdU-positive cells was analyzed by flow cytometry, and data were processed using FlowJo software.


### Transwell assay
  MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cells were treated with 20 μM YL064 or DMSO for 24 h in serum-free culture medium. After the treatment, cells were digested and mixed with serum-free culture medium at a density of 5 × 106 cells/mL. 100 μL of cell suspension was plated in the upper insert of a Transwell chamber containing an 8 μm polycarbonate membrane, with or without Matrigel coating. In the meantime, in the lower Transwell chamber, 600 μL culture medium with serum was filled. After seeding for 24 h, the inserts were fixed and stained with crystal violet (0.1% in 20% methanol). Cells on the upper side of the membrane were removed and we counted the cells on the bottom side of the pore membrane across 3 random microscopic fields. The migrated and invaded cells were enumerated by ImageJ and analyzed by GraphPad Prism 9.5.


### Plasmids and transfection
  The full-length WT and C14A mutant constructs of ATXN3 were obtained by PCR amplification of the full-length sequence of human ATXN3 and subcloned into pLVX.1-puro vector. Sequences of the small hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) used for ATXN3 knockdown in this study are listed in the Supplementary Table S2. To produce lentiviruses, HEK-293T cells were co-transfected with the plasmids above and the packaging plasmids, psPAX2 and PMD2G. PEI served as the transfection reagent. Supernatants were collected 48–72 h after transfection and concentrated by ultra-high-speed centrifugation at 4 °C, 28,000 rpm. Then, Py8119 and PC-3 cells were infected by the above lentivirus in the presence of 8 μg/mL of polybrene. Stable cell clones were selected by 2.0 μg/mL of puromycin 48 h postinfection.


### Molecular docking
  YL064 was prepared and optimized using LigPrep (LigPrep, Schrödinger, LLC, New York, NY, 2021) with OPLS4 force field [25]. Appropriate protonation and ionization states of YL064 were assigned at pH 7.4 ± 1.0 using Epik [26].

  The three-dimensional structure of ATXN3 (PDB ID: 2AGA, https://www.rcsb.org/) was used for docking. It was submitted to protein preparation wizard [26] of Schrödinger Suite for hydrogen atom addition, bond order assignment, protonation state generation, hydrogen bond sampling at pH 7.4 using PROPKA [27, 28], and energy minimization with OPLS4 force field [25]. Docking grids were generated using the centroid of Josephin binding site [29] (residues including H11, Q14, E15, L18, C19, A20, H22, N25, N26, F33, H124, W125, L141, and T143) of ATXN3 with a bounding box size of 14 Å in X, Y, and Z axes. Both Standard Precision (SP) and Extra Precision (XP) docking modes were employed to thoroughly explore the binding conformations. And the most favorable pose was identified based on their positions and interactions from all the outputs. UCSF ChimeraX [30] was utilized for structure visualization and figure preparation.


### In vivo experiments
  C57BL/6J mice, around 6–8 weeks old, were adopted as the subjects of in vivo experiments. All mice dwelt in individually ventilated cages with 12-h light and dark cycles at 20–24 °C and 30–70% relative humidity, had free access to water and food. Py8119 cells with or without Atxn3 knockdown (5 × 105 cells per mouse) were injected into mice. 17 days after tumor implantation, tumors were harvested for further analysis. For drug efficacy studies, five days after tumor implantation, mice were grouped randomly. YL064 dissolved in a vehicle containing 5% DMSO, 30% PEG300, 10% Tween 80, and 55% ddH2O was administered via intraperitoneal injection. Mice received a total of 6 treatments.


### Statistical analysis
  All the experiments were repeated at least 3 times. The statistical methods used for each experiment are specified in the corresponding figure legends. All images of Wound-healing assay, clonogenic survival assay, and western blot were analyzed with the help of ImageJ, and the data were presented by GraphPad Prism 9.5. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001.
