# Exploring the mechanism of oxfendazole in inhibiting osteosarcoma-induced osteolysis based on single-cell RNA seqsuencing


## Abstract
  Osteosarcoma-associated osteolysis represents a major obstacle in the management of osteosarcoma. Within the osteolytic microenvironment, interactions between osteoclasts and tumor cells facilitate tumor proliferation and metastatic dissemination, and contribute to the development of pathological fractures. However, conventional osteoclast-targeting agents are insufficient to effectively suppress osteosarcoma-driven bone resorption. In this study, single-cell RNA sequencing was performed to identify critical regulators involved in osteoclast differentiation in the context of osteosarcoma. Using the Beyondcell platform, drugs targeting osteoclast-related signaling pathways were screened, and oxfendazole (OFN) was identified as a potential therapeutic candidate, with ANXA2 recognized as a key associated target. In vitro osteoclastogenesis assays and an in vivo tibial orthotopic osteosarcoma model demonstrated that oral administration of OFN via gavage effectively suppressed osteoclast formation and osteolytic activity, as confirmed by micro-CT and histopathological analyses. But OFN alone did not significantly inhibit osteosarcoma growth but effectively reduced osteoclast-mediated osteolysis. In addition, qRT-PCR and immunohistochemistry confirmed that OFN downregulates ANXA2 expression in vitro and in vivo, thereby impairing osteoclast differentiation. Molecular dynamics simulations combined with in-vitro validation further suggested that OFN may interact with the transcription factor ZNF530, leading to reduced ANXA2 transcription. In conclusion, the integration of single-cell RNA sequencing with the Beyondcell algorithm enabled the identification of OFN as a potent candidate agent for osteosarcoma-induced osteolysis. OFN attenuates osteoclastogenesis and bone resorption by suppressing ANXA2 expression. These findings provide preclinical evidence supporting the therapeutic potential of OFN in the treatment of osteosarcoma-associated osteolysis.


## 1. Introduction
  Osteosarcoma is a malignant neoplasm originating from bone tissue and is believed to arise from the malignant transformation of mesenchymal stem cells during osteoblastic differentiation. It predominantly affects adolescents and is characterized by aggressive biological behavior, including strong local invasiveness and a high propensity for distant metastasis [1]. Despite substantial advances in therapeutic approaches over the past two decades, clinical outcomes remain suboptimal, particularly for patients with metastatic or recurrent disease. With current first-line treatment—consisting of neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by surgical resection—patients who respond favorably to neoadjuvant chemotherapy achieve a 5-year survival rate of approximately 75%, whereas those with a poor response have a 5-year survival rate of roughly 45% [2]. Another hallmark of osteosarcoma is its pronounced capacity to induce malignant osteolysis. Tumor-associated bone destruction not only disrupts the native skeletal architecture but also liberates pro-tumorigenic cytokines that foster tumor proliferation and metastatic dissemination. Once metastasis develops, therapeutic resistance typically intensifies, thereby complicating clinical management and resulting in poor prognosis. In addition, osteolysis weakens bone mechanical integrity, markedly increasing the risk of pathological fractures during treatment. Such fractures further complicate treatment and may increase the risk of tumor dissemination [3]. Therefore, the inhibition of osteosarcoma-induced osteolysis during therapy is critical for improving patient prognosis and preserving quality of life.

  In osteosarcoma, osteolysis arises from the dynamic interaction between tumor cells and osteoclasts. Studies have shown that osteosarcoma cells secrete Monocyte Chemoattractant Protein-1 (MCP-1) to recruit monocytes to the tumor microenvironment and release RANKL to stimulate osteoclast differentiation and bone resorption [4]. Furthermore, the uncontrolled proliferation of osteosarcoma cells, along with their secretion of collagenases and gelatinases, contributes directly to osteolysis. The resulting bone resorption releases growth factors that further promote osteosarcoma cell proliferation, creating a vicious cycle that disrupts normal bone remodeling and drives malignant osteolysis [5,6]. Targeting this mechanism, the combination of chemotherapy with anti-osteoclast agents has been extensively investigated for the treatment of osteosarcoma-associated osteolysis. In chemotherapy-sensitive osteosarcoma, chemotherapeutic agents can partially suppress osteolysis and facilitate bone repair. In cases where osteolysis remains insufficiently controlled, the addition of bisphosphonates to chemotherapy has been widely explored over the past decade [7–10]. However, the use of bisphosphonates in osteosarcoma-induced osteolysis remains controversial. Although they can partially inhibit bone resorption, studies have shown that bisphosphonates fail to suppress osteosarcoma progression and may even promote tumor growth in vivo [11,12]. Notably, bisphosphonate treatment in osteosarcoma-bearing mice has been reported to significantly increase pulmonary metastasis [13]. In addition, bisphosphonate use can cause adverse effects, including osteonecrosis [14]. Consequently, the clinical application of bisphosphonates in combination with chemotherapy for osteosarcoma-associated osteolysis remains limited. The RANKL inhibitor denosumab has also been investigated as a combinatorial therapy; however, it was reported to reduce the efficacy of cyclophosphamide [15]. Other strategies, including small-molecule inhibitors (e.g., targeting the PI3K pathway) and natural compounds, have been explored [16,17]), but unclear mechanisms of action or uncertain efficacy continue to impede their clinical translation.

  The limitations of current therapies highlight the urgent need to further elucidate the mechanisms underlying osteosarcoma-induced osteolysis and to develop novel therapeutics targeting these pathways. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) offers a powerful approach to systematically dissect the cellular and molecular interactions between tumor cells and osteoclasts at single-cell resolution. This technology facilitates refined tumor subtyping, characterization of therapy-induced changes, prediction of treatment responses, and identification of potential therapeutic targets [18]. scRNA-seq analysis was performed on osteosarcoma patient samples, revealing that osteoclasts within the tumor microenvironment undergo a complete metabolic trajectory, encompassing differentiation, maturation, and functional exhaustion. Furthermore, it was observed that osteoclast formation is promoted through communication between osteosarcoma cells and osteoclasts via the TNFSF11-TNFRSF11A ligand receptor axis [19]. This mechanism is distinctly different from normal bone metabolic processes, providing a critical foundation for further investigation into osteosarcoma-induced osteolysis. In the context of therapeutic development, Beyondcell—a drug screening platform that predicts drug sensitivity based on single-cell subpopulation analysis was developed by Fustero-Torre et al. Originally designed to identify therapeutic targets and novel agents for resistant tumor subpopulations by exploiting cellular heterogeneity, the platform’s feasibility was rigorously validated through both in vitro and in vivo experiments [20]. However, this methodology has not yet been applied to predict drug sensitivity in non-malignant cell populations.

  Building upon this foundation, scRNA-seq was employed in the present study to systematically characterize osteoclasts within the osteosarcoma microenvironment and to investigate the regulatory mechanisms governing their behavior. Leveraging the Beyondcell platform, sensitive therapeutic agents were screened, and key molecular targets were identified. The efficacy of the selected drug against osteosarcoma-induced osteolysis was subsequently validated at the cellular, tissue, and animal levels. Finally, molecular dynamics simulations were conducted to elucidate the mechanistic basis by which the drug regulates critical molecules involved in osteoclastogenesis. Collectively, these findings provide novel insights into potential therapeutic strategies for osteosarcoma-induced osteolysis.


## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1. Ethics statement
  All animal experimental protocols were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University (Ethics Approval No.:2025-IACUC(001)). Clinical trial number: not applicable


### 2.2. Data source and processing
  Single-cell RNA-seq data from six osteosarcoma samples (GSE162454) were obtained from the GEO database and processed using the Seurat package. Cells expressing 300–4,500 genes were retained to exclude doublets and low-quality cells. Cells with mitochondrial gene content greater than 15% or hemoglobin gene content greater than 5% were removed. A total of 4,500 highly variable genes were selected for downstream analyses. Batch effects across samples were corrected using the Harmony package. Cell clustering was performed using the “FindNeighbors” and “FindClusters” functions (resolution = 0.05), and the resulting clusters were visualized using UMAP. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) used to define cell populations were identified with “FindAllMarkers” (logFC threshold = 0.25, adjusted P < 0.05). Subsequent analyses focused on osteoclasts. The osteoclast cluster was extracted and subjected to sub-clustering with a resolution of 0.1. DEGs for defining osteoclast subpopulations were identified again using “FindAllMarkers” (logFC threshold = 0.25, adjusted P < 0.05), and sub-cluster distribution was visualized by UMAP. To distinguish malignant osteosarcoma cells from non-malignant reactive osteoblasts within the osteoblast/osteosarcoma cluster, copy number variation (CNV) analysis was performed using the inferCNV package (v1.8.0). The normal cell populations (NK/T cells, B cells, plasma cells, and endothelial cells) served as the reference set. CNV scores were calculated for each cell based on the relative expression levels of genes across chromosomal regions, and cells with extensive chromosomal amplifications or deletions were classified as malignant.


### 2.3. Pseudotime analysis
  Monocle2 (v 2.22.0) [21], one of the most widely used tools for single-cell pseudotime analysis, was employed to investigate the temporal trajectory among osteoclast subpopulations. The raw count matrix was extracted from the Seurat object, and a Monocle CellDataSet object was constructed using the newCellDataSet() function. This was followed by normalization and dispersion estimation: library size normalization was performed using estimateSizeFactors(), and gene expression dispersion was estimated using estimateDispersions(). Ordering genes for trajectory construction were then selected based on their expression level and dispersion, with genes exhibiting a mean expression ≥ 0.1 chosen as the feature gene set. Finally, dimensionality reduction and trajectory inference were conducted using the DDRTree algorithm. This method was used to reconstruct the cell developmental trajectory, establishing a continuous model of cell state transition. Further analysis of gene expression dynamics during cell differentiation was performed using the BEAM (Branched Expression Analysis Modeling) method.To validate the robustness of the Monocle2 trajectory, we performed independent trajectory inference using Slingshot (v2.6.0), which constructs lineage structures via a minimum spanning tree and fits simultaneous principal curves. Additionally, we applied CytoTRACE2 to predict cellular developmental potential based on the gene expression matrix, with potency scores ranging from 0 (differentiated) to 1 (totipotent). Patient-level consistency was assessed by visualizing the distribution of cells from each sample across the pseudotime trajectory.


### 2.4. Drug sensitivity analysis
  To evaluate drug sensitivity at the single-cell level, the Beyondcell R package (v2.2.2) was employed [20]. This package enables the identification of drug sensitivities from scRNA-seq data. Specifically, a Perturbation Sensitivity (PSc) analysis was performed using Beyondcell. The perturbation sensitivity scores were first computed based on the gene expression matrix using the bcScore function and visualized via the bcUMAP function. Subsequently, differential analysis between groups was conducted using the bcRanks function with default parameters. Drugs exhibiting high sensitivity in osteoclast precursors were identified and calculated using the bc4Squares function, and the therapeutic targets of the different drugs were obtained. Finally, the distribution histogram and spatial expression patterns of signature drugs across the cell population were visualized using the bcHistogram and bcSignatures functions, respectively.


### 2.5. Osteoclast differentiation assay
  The osteoclast differentiation assay was performed in 96-well plates.

  ANXA2 Functional Experiment: RAW264.7 cells from the oe-ANXA2, oe-NC, and si-ANXA2 groups were harvested and resuspended in complete differentiation medium (α-MEM(Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS(Gibco, USA), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Solarbio, China), and 100 ng/mL RANKL(Amizona, China)) at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells/mL. Cells were seeded at 1,000 cells per well.

  OFN(MCE, USA) Functional Experiment:Harvested RAW264.7 cells were resuspended in differentiation medium at 1 × 10⁴ cells/mL and divided into the following groups:RANKL group: RAW264.7 cells seeded at 1,000 cells per well.RANKL+K7M2 group:RAW264.7 and K7M2 cells mixed at a 5:1 ratio, with RAW264.7 cell density maintained at 1,000 cells per well.RANKL + K7M2 + OFN group:RAW264.7 and K7M2 cells mixed at a 5:1 ratio, treated with 6.5 μM OFN, and seeded with RAW264.7 cell density at 1,000 cells per well.

  Rescue Experiment: RAW264.7 cells from the normal control, oe-ANXA2, and si-ANXA2 groups were harvested and resuspended in complete differentiation medium at 1 × 10⁴ cells/mL. All groups involved mixing RAW264.7 cells with K7M2 cells at a 5:1 ratio:si-ANXA2 group: si-ANXA2 RAW264.7 cells seeded at 1,000 cells per well.oe-ANXA2 group: oe-ANXA2 RAW264.7 cells seeded at 1000 cells per well.NC group:Normal control RAW264.7 cells seeded at 1,000 cells per well.oe-ANXA2 + OFN group:oe-ANXA2 RAW264.7 cells treated with 6.5 μM OFN and seeded at 1000 cells per well.

  For all experiments, the medium was replaced every other day. After 7 days of culture, osteoclast formation was assessed by TRAP staining.


### 2.6. TRAP staining
  Osteoclasts were stained for Tartrate-Resistant Acid Phosphatase (TRAP) (Solarbio, China) activity using a commercial TRAP staining kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, after the intervention period, the culture medium was removed, and cells were washed twice with PBS(Solarbio, China). Cells were fixed with rapid fixative solution for 15 minutes. After removing the fixative, cells were washed twice with PBS. TRAP staining solution was prepared and added to the wells (100 μL per well). The plates were incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 1 hour. Subsequently, the staining solution was removed, PBS was added, and osteoclasts were observed and recorded under an optical microscope.


### 2.7. Animal experiments
  All animal experimental procedures were carried out in strict accordance with the Basel Declaration and the ARRIVE guidelines (Version 2.0). The protocols were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University (Approval No.: 2025-IACUC(001)). 4-week-old nude mice used for modeling were obtained from the Animal Experiment Center of Guangxi Medical University. The mice were housed in an SPF facility for 1 week to acclimate. Human osteosarcoma 143B cells in the logarithmic growth phase were harvested and resuspended in complete medium at a concentration of 8 × 10⁷ cells/mL. The cell suspension was then thoroughly mixed with Matrigel at a 1:1 volume ratio. A 50 μL aliquot of the 143B cell suspension (containing 4 × 10⁶ cells) was drawn into a sterile syringe and injected into the bone marrow cavity of the right tibial plateau.1 week after inoculation, tumor formation was confirmed, and the mice were randomly divided into two groups: the Control group and the OFN group. Mice in the OFN group received OFN administration via oral gavage at a dosage of 2 μg/g body weight every two days. Mice in the Control group received an equal volume of normal saline on the same schedule. After 2 weeks of intervention, all mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation. Tumor specimens were collected for subsequent analysis. Anesthetize nude mice by intraperitoneal injection of Avertin at a dose of 200 mg/kg. At the end of the experiment, all mice were humanely euthanized by cervical dislocation.


### 2.8. Micro-CT analysis
  Bone erosion was assessed using a micro-CT system (Scanco Medical, μCT100, Switzerland). The tibiae for scanning were placed in scanning tubes. The specimen coordinates were adjusted along the X, Y, and Z axes and sequentially loaded into the scanner. The resolution was selected based on the specimen size by choosing an appropriate tube and corresponding resolution and Field of View (FOV). Scanning was performed using the following parameters: 70 kVp, 200 μA, 10 μm isotropic voxel size, and 300 ms integration time. The region of interest encompassing the mouse tibia was defined by contouring, and the scan was initiated. The scanning software automatically generated DICOM files.The raw data were analyzed using DataViewer (v1.5.4.0, Bruker), CTvox (v3.3.0 r1403, Bruker), and CTAn software (v1.15.2.2, Bruker). First, bone tissue was selected using DataViewer software to generate TIFF files. Subsequently, the region of interest (ROI) outlining the proximal tibia was defined in CTAn software. 2D and 3D analysis functions were then used to calculate bone volume (BV), bone volume fraction (BV/TV). Finally, three-dimensional morphological reconstruction of the tibia was performed using CTvox software.


### 2.9. Molecular dynamics simulation
  Following the prediction of transcription factors for ANXA2 using JASPAR, molecular docking was performed between the top 10 scoring transcription factors and OFN using AutoDock software to calculate the binding energy.The three-dimensional structure of OFN was obtained from the PubChem database (Compound ID: 40854; https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The predicted structures of all candidate transcription factors were obtained from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/), and the corresponding UniProt IDs were used to retrieve the protein structure files. The SDF file of oxfendazole was obtained and converted into the PDBQT format using OpenBabel (Version 3.1.1). The PDB structure files of the predicted transcription factors were converted into PDBQT format using AutoDock Tools (Version 1.5.7). Blind molecular docking between the predicted transcription factors and OFN was performed using QVina-W, with the exhaustiveness parameter set to 16 and the random seed set to 2025. The docking results were visualized and analyzed using BIOVIA Discovery Studio 2025 and PyMOL software.

  The transcription factor with the lowest binding energy was selected for molecular dynamics (MD) simulation. Subsequently, a 100000 ps MD simulation of the complex was conducted in Gromacs 2023. The CHARMM36 force field was applied for ZNF530, while the topology of OFN was generated using the GAFF2 force field. Atomic charges were derived via the AM1-BCC method, and rotatable bonds were manually verified. The system was placed in a cubic periodic boundary box and solvated with TIP3P water molecules. Energy minimization was followed by equilibration under NVT and NPT ensembles for 500,000 steps each, with the van der Waals and Coulomb interaction cutoffs set to 1.0 nm. Finally, a production simulation of 100,000 ps was performed at 300 K and 1 bar. All analyses, including the root mean square deviation (RMSD), the root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (Rg), solvent accessible surface area (SASA), and hydrogen bond calculations, were carried out using built-in Gromacs tools.Each MD simulation was independently repeated three times to ensure reproducibility.


### 2.10. ZNF530 knockdown and overexpression assays
  RAW264.7 cells were stimulated with RANKL (100 ng/mL) and transfected with ZNF530-specific siRNA or ZNF530 overexpression plasmids, which were custom-designed and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After the indicated treatments, total proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF; Beyotime, China). Protein samples were mixed with 5 × loading buffer (Yeasen Biotechnology, China) at a ratio of 1:5 and subjected to Western blotting analysis.Protein separation was performed using 10% Bis-Tris precast gels. The primary antibodies used were rabbit anti-ZNF530 (Abclonal, China) and rabbit anti-ANXA2 (Immunoway, China), followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (ZhenBio, China). All Western blot experiments were independently repeated three times.


### 2.11. Statistical methods
  Bioinformatics analyses were performed using the R software(v4.4.1). Statistical analyses and graph generation for the experimental components were conducted with GraphPad Prism 9 software. For comparisons between two groups, a Student’s t-test was applied when data followed a normal distribution. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA was used for normally distributed data, while the Kruskal-Wallis test was employed for non-normally distributed data. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.


## 3. Results

### 3.1. ScRNA-seq reveals ANXA2 as a key gene mediating osteosarcoma-induced osteolysis by osteoclasts
  ScRNA-seq data from 6 osteosarcoma samples were processed by removing doublets and filtering lowly expressed genes. After batch effect correction using the Harmony package, a total of 29,278 cells were retained for subsequent analysis. Principal component analysis (PCA) and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) were performed for dimensionality reduction and clustering. At a resolution of 0.05, seven distinct cell clusters were identified. These clusters were annotated into seven major cell populations based on canonical marker genes: osteoblast (highly expressing ALPL,IBSP,CLEC11A,RUNX2,CDH11), NK/T cells (highly expressing NKG7,GZMK,CD3D,CD3E), B cells (highly expressing CD79A,CD79B,MS4A1), myeloid cells (highly expressing C1QA,C1QB,LYZ,FCN1), plasma cells (highly expressing IGHM,JCHAIN,MZB1), osteoclasts (highly expressing CTSK,MMP9,ACP5), and endothelial cells (highly expressing VWF, PLVAP,PECAM1,EGFL7) (Fig 1A, 1B). To resolve the cellular identity of this cluster, we performed CNV analysis using the inferCNV package. The results revealed widespread chromosomal amplifications and deletions characteristic of malignant cells, confirming the presence of osteosarcoma cells within this cluster (S2D Fig).

  The osteoclast population was extracted for secondary clustering. Following additional filtering of low-expression genes and dimensionality reduction, three osteoclast subsets were identified at a resolution of 0.1: osteoclast precursors (characterized by high expression of C1QA, C1QB, C1QC, CD74, and HLA-DRA), mature osteoclasts (marked by high ATP6V0D2 expression), and dysfunctional osteoclasts (defined by low ATP6V0D2 expression) (Fig 1C, 1D). Pseudotime trajectory analysis of the osteoclast population was performed using Monocle2. The results illustrated a dynamic differentiation process, originating from osteoclast precursors and branching towards either mature osteoclasts or dysfunctional osteoclasts. Branch point 3 was identified as the critical node determining the fate decision towards the mature or dysfunctional lineage (Fig 1E). CytoTRACE 2 and Slingshot also proved this (S2A–S2F Fig).

  To identify key genes regulating osteoclast differentiation and maturation, 2,295 genes associated with branch point 3 were extracted. Additionally, 2,593 genes related to osteoclast differentiation were obtained from the GeneCards database (S1 Table). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the osteoclast cluster and other cell clusters were identified using the “FindAllMarkers” function with thresholds of |log2FC| > 1 and adjusted p < 0.05 (S2 Table). The intersection of these DEGs with the two gene sets yielded 77 key genes, designated as Osteoclast Differentiation and Maturation (OSDM) genes, which are putatively crucial for osteoclast maturation (Fig 1F; S3 Table). To assess whether the observed osteoclast heterogeneity and ANXA2 expression patterns were consistent across patients, we performed patient-level stratification analyses. All three osteoclast subtypes (precursors, mature, and dysfunctional) were present in each of the six osteosarcoma samples, though with some inter-patient variability in relative abundances (S3A Fig). To further validate our findings at the patient level and mitigate pseudo-replication concerns, we performed pseudobulk differential expression analysis by aggregating cells from each patient and osteoclast subtype; ANXA2 remained significantly upregulated in mature osteoclasts compared to precursors (log2FC > 1, adjusted p < 0.05; S4 Table, S3B–S3C Fig).


### 3.2. Screening for inhibitors of osteoclast formation based on the Beyondcell platform
  Osteoclasts represent the sole cell population responsible for osteosarcoma-associated bone resorption. Accordingly, inhibition of osteoclast formation constitutes a critical therapeutic strategy for osteosarcoma-induced osteolysis. To evaluate osteoclast drug sensitivity, osteoclasts were re-clustered based on therapeutic response profiles using Beyondcell (Fig 2A). Beyondcell scoring of conventional anti-osteoclast agents (diethylstilbestrol, clodronic acid, and etidronic acid) subsequently demonstrated limited efficacy against osteoclasts within the osteosarcoma microenvironment (Figs 2B, 2D; S1).

  To identify potential inhibitors specifically targeting osteosarcoma-derived osteoclasts, we applied Beyondcell scoring to the osteoclast population. Using the drug sensitivity four-quadrant analysis, 150 drugs were identified as highly specific against osteoclast precursors. We then extracted the downregulated target genes of these 150 drugs that were also highly expressed in precursor cells and intersected them with the OSDM gene set. This analysis identified a single overlapping gene, ANXA2, shared specifically with the drug OFN (S5 Table).

  Further analysis of OFN demonstrated sensitivity across all osteoclast subsets within osteosarcoma. The highest sensitivity was observed in osteoclast precursors, followed by mature osteoclasts, while dysfunctional osteoclasts showed the lowest sensitivity (Fig 2E, 2F). In summary, these results suggest that OFN may inhibit osteosarcoma-related bone resorption by targeting ANXA2 and consequently suppressing osteoclast formation.


### 3.3. In vitro validation of ANXA2 as a key regulator of osteoclast formation
  To elucidate the role of ANXA2 as a key regulator in osteoclast differentiation, RAW264.7 cells were transduced with a lentiviral vector carrying ANXA2. High transfection efficiency was confirmed by GFP fluorescence observation and further validated by qRT-PCR (Fig 3A, 3B). For ANXA2 knockdown, siRNA-mediated silencing was performed. qRT-PCR analysis identified si-568 as the most effective construct (Fig 3C), which was subsequently used in further experiments.Osteoclast formation was then induced in vitro across groups with differential ANXA2 expression levels. The results demonstrated that ANXA2 knockdown significantly reduced the osteoclast area percentage compared to the control group. Conversely, ANXA2 overexpression led to a marked increase in osteoclast area (Fig 3D, 3E). These findings collectively indicate that ANXA2 is a critical molecular regulator of osteoclast formation.


### 3.4. OFN inhibits osteosarcoma-induced osteoclast formation in vitro by suppressing ANXA2 expression
  To investigate the inhibitory effect of OFN on osteosarcoma-induced osteoclast formation in vitro, we first assessed its impact on RAW264.7 cell viability using the CCK-8 assay. Results demonstrated that OFN suppressed RAW264.7 cell viability with an IC₅₀ of 25.42 μM (Fig 4A). A subtoxic concentration of 6.5 μM OFN (approximately 1/4 IC₅₀) was subsequently used for further cellular experiments.

  We then established a direct co-culture system with K7M2 and RAW264.7 cells to simulate the cellular interplay between osteosarcoma cells and osteoclast precursors. Compared to the RANKL group, co-culture with K7M2 (RANKL+K7M2 group) significantly increased the osteoclast area percentage. In contrast, OFN treatment (RANKL+K7M2+OFN group) markedly reduced osteoclast formation (Fig 4B, 4C). These findings indicate that the interaction between osteosarcoma cells and osteoclast precursors promotes osteoclast formation, whereas OFN effectively inhibits this process in the osteosarcoma context.

  To validate the mechanism by which OFN suppresses osteoclast formation via ANXA2 inhibition, we examined ANXA2 mRNA expression levels across different treatment groups using qRT-PCR. ANXA2 expression was significantly upregulated in the RANKL group compared to the negative control(RAW264.7 alone). Furthermore, the RANKL+K7M2 co-culture group showed a significant increase in ANXA2 expression relative to the RANKL group. Importantly, OFN treatment (RANKL+K7M2+OFN group) reversed this increase, resulting in decreased ANXA2 mRNA expression compared to the RANKL+K7M2 group (Fig 4D). This suggests that osteosarcoma cells enhance osteoclast formation by promoting ANXA2 expression in osteoclast precursors, and that OFN inhibits this process by suppressing ANXA2 expression.

  Finally, a rescue experiment revealed that ANXA2 overexpression attenuated the inhibitory effect of OFN on osteoclast formation (Fig 4E, 4F). Collectively, these results demonstrate that OFN inhibits osteosarcoma-related osteoclast formation by targeting ANXA2.


### 3.5. In vivo validation of the inhibitory effect of OFN on osteosarcoma-induced osteolysis
  To evaluate the efficacy of OFN against osteosarcoma-induced osteolysis in vivo, an orthotopic tumor model was established by injecting 143B cells into the tibial marrow cavity of nude mice.After 2 weeks of intervention, the mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation (Fig 5A, 5B). Analysis of tumor volume revealed no significant difference between the control and OFN-treated groups (Fig 5C).To assess bone osteolysis in the 2 groups, micro-CT scanning was performed on 3 tibiae from each group. The OFN-treated group exhibited better preservation of bone tissue, particularly in the tibial plateau region, compared to the control group (Fig 5D). Quantitative analysis further demonstrated that the OFN-treated group had significantly greater bone volume (BV) and bone volume fraction (BV/TV) (Fig 5E). These results collectively demonstrate that OFN effectively inhibits osteosarcoma-induced bone osteolysis in vivo.


### 3.6. OFN downregulates ANXA2 expression and inhibits osteoclast formation in osteosarcoma tissues in vivo
  Paraffin sections were prepared from the harvested tumor tissues. H&E staining revealed the presence of numerous atypical cells in sections from both groups (Fig 6A). TRAP staining of the sections demonstrated that the OFN-treated group exhibited a significantly smaller TRAP-positive area compared to the control group (Fig 6A, 6B).Furthermore, IHC was performed to evaluate the effect of OFN on the in vivo expression of the key gene, ANXA2. The results indicated that ANXA2 protein was readily detectable in both groups; however, its expression was moderately reduced in the OFN-treated group relative to the control group (Fig 6A, 6C).To further investigate the effects of OFN on osteoclast-related factors in vivo, IHC was performed to evaluate the expression of RANKL, a key mediator promoting osteoclastogenesis, and CTSK, a critical marker associated with osteolytic activity. No significant differences in RANKL or CTSK expression were observed between the Control and OFN groups (Fig 6A, 6D and 6E). These findings suggest that OFN suppresses osteoclast formation in osteosarcoma tissues in vivo, but does not significantly affect RANKL secretion or CTSK expression.


### 3.7. OFN may suppress ANXA2 expression by interact with the transcription factor ZNF530
  We further investigated the mechanism by which OFN inhibits ANXA2 expression. Given that OFN suppresses ANXA2 mRNA levels, we focused on its potential regulatory interaction with the transcription factors of ANXA2. First, we retrieved the sequence encompassing 2,000 bp upstream of the ANXA2 promoter from the NCBI database and used JASPAR to predict its potential transcription factors. The top 10 scoring transcription factors were selected, and their binding energies with OFN were calculated (Fig 7A). ZNF530 exhibited the lowest binding energy (-6.8 kcal/mol) with OFN. Molecular docking analysis further revealed favorable binding interactions between OFN and ZNF530 (Fig 7B, 7C). These results suggest that OFN may inhibit ANXA2 transcription by interact within ZNF530 function.

  To further investigate the potential interaction between OFN and ZNF530, MD simulations were performed. The RMSD analysis was used to evaluate the overall conformational stability of the OFN–ZNF530 complex during the simulation. The complex reached a relatively stable conformational state during the 100,000 ps simulation period, with RMSD values fluctuating around 2.6 nm (Fig 7D), suggesting that the predicted complex maintained an overall stable conformation.

  The RMSF analysis was performed to evaluate residue-level flexibility within the complex. The OFN–ZNF530 complex exhibited variable residue fluctuations, with relatively higher flexibility mainly observed in the C-terminal regions (Fig 7E), which may reflect intrinsic flexibility of these regions. The radius of gyration (Rg) was analyzed to assess changes in overall structural compactness. The Rg value remained relatively stable throughout the simulation, indicating that no major global structural expansion or collapse occurred during the simulation period (Fig 7F). Similarly, the SASA profile remained stable, suggesting that the overall solvent exposure of the complex was maintained during the simulation (Fig 7G).

  Hydrogen-bond analysis was performed to characterize potential intermolecular interactions between OFN and ZNF530. A limited number of hydrogen-bond interactions were observed during the simulation (Fig 7H), providing additional structural information regarding the predicted interaction interface. Collectively, these MD results provide preliminary computational evidence supporting the potential interaction between OFN and ZNF530.

  In summary, these results suggest that ZNF530 may be involved in the regulation of ANXA2 expression during OFN treatment. MD analyses predict a potential interaction between OFN and ZNF530, providing a possible mechanistic basis for the regulation of the ZNF530-ANXA2 axis by OFN.

  To further validate the regulatory relationship between OFN and the ZNF530-ANXA2 axis under RANKL stimulation (100 ng/mL). RAW264.7 cells were transfected with ZNF530-specific siRNAs. WB analysis demonstrated that among the three candidate siRNAs, si3 exhibited the highest silencing efficiency and effectively reduced ZNF530 expression (Fig 8A, 8B, S1 Raw Gel). Conversely, overexpression of ZNF530 was achieved by transfecting RAW264.7 cells with a ZNF530 overexpression plasmid (Fig 8C, 8D).Subsequently, the effect of ZNF530 modulation on ANXA2 expression was examined. Compared with the NC group, ZNF530 knockdown significantly decreased ANXA2 protein levels, whereas ZNF530 overexpression restored ANXA2 expression compared with the si-ZNF530 group. However, the difference between the ZNF530 overexpression group and the NC group was not statistically significant (p = 0.5687) (Fig 8E, 8F, 8G). To further determine whether OFN regulates ANXA2 expression through ZNF530, rescue assay were performed. Compared with the OFN + NC group, ZNF530 overexpression significantly increased ANXA2 protein expression in OFN + oe-ZNF530 RAW264.7 cells (Fig 8H, 8I, 8J). Collectively, these findings indicate that OFN does not directly affect ZNF530 expression, but ZNF530 overexpression attenuates the inhibitory effect of OFN on ANXA2 expression, suggesting that ZNF530 mediates the regulatory effect of OFN on the ANXA2 pathway.


## 4. Discussion
  Osteolysis is a critical pathological feature of osteosarcoma that not only facilitates tumor progression and metastasis but also contributes to pathological fractures, thereby substantially compromising patients’ quality of life. Previous studies have demonstrated that osteosarcoma cells continuously secrete receptor activator of RANKL, which promotes osteoclast differentiation and activation, leading to excessive bone resorption [22]. This tumor-driven osteolytic process represents a hijacking of physiological bone remodeling and may explain why currently available anti-osteoporotic therapies fail to effectively prevent osteosarcoma-associated bone destruction [13,15].

  In the present study, we focused on the osteoclast population within the osteosarcoma microenvironment and investigated its potential response to OFN treatment. Using the Beyondcell algorithm, we predicted that tumor-associated osteoclasts exhibit potential sensitivity to OFN and identified ANXA2 as a candidate molecule associated with OFN responsiveness. These findings provide a potential molecular basis for understanding the anti-osteolytic activity of OFN in osteosarcoma and highlight osteoclast-targeting strategies as a promising therapeutic direction.

  OFN, a benzimidazole anthelmintic widely used in veterinary medicine, has recently attracted attention beyond its original indication. OFN exerts its anti-parasitic effect by disrupting tubulin polymerization, leading to cytoskeletal destabilization and mitotic arrest [23–26]. Notably, this mechanism is relevant to osteoclast biology, as cytoskeletal reorganization and cell fusion are essential steps during osteoclast differentiation and maturation. Beyond its anti-parasitic activity, OFN has demonstrated anti-cancer properties in several malignancies. In ovarian cancer, OFN induces apoptosis through activation of the JNK/MAPK pathway and accumulation of reactive oxygen species, and it retains efficacy against cisplatin-resistant cells [27]. In non-small cell lung cancer, OFN acts as a novel c-Src inhibitor, downregulating CDK-4, CDK-6, p-Rb, and E2F1 while upregulating p53 and p21, thereby inducing G0/G1 cell cycle arrest and enhancing cisplatin cytotoxicity [28]. These findings suggest that OFN is a pleiotropic compound whose biological effects are context-dependent, and its repurposing for bone-related disorders is further supported by recent evidence that benzimidazole derivatives can modulate osteoclast activity. In the present study, we provide the first evidence, to our knowledge, that OFN effectively suppresses osteoclast formation and osteosarcoma-induced osteolysis, positioning it as a candidate adjunctive therapy for bone protection in osteosarcoma.

  The identification of ANXA2 as a key functional target in our study is strongly supported by its established roles in cell membrane fusion, cytoskeletal dynamics, and endocytosis—processes that are intimately linked to osteoclastogenesis. ANXA2 is a calcium-dependent phospholipid-binding protein that interacts with actin and membrane proteins, and its involvement in osteoclast formation has been documented in multiple bone pathologies. In osteoporosis, ANXA2 expression is closely correlated with osteoclast activity and bone resorption markers [29–31]. In multiple myeloma, ANXA2 has been implicated in osteoclast activation and bone lesion formation [32]. In prostate cancer, ANXA2 contributes to the establishment of bone metastatic niches by promoting osteoclast recruitment and activity [33]. Notably, ANXA2 functions as an autocrine factor that directly increases osteoclast formation and bone resorption, and reduced ANXA2 expression has been associated with osteosarcoma metastases. Our scRNA-seq analysis revealed that ANXA2 is upregulated during osteoclast maturation in osteosarcoma, and our functional experiments confirmed that ANXA2 knockdown suppresses osteoclast formation while its overexpression promotes it. Importantly, OFN treatment downregulated ANXA2 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels, and ANXA2 overexpression partially rescued OFN-mediated inhibition of osteoclastogenesis. These findings establish ANXA2 as a functionally relevant mediator bridging OFN treatment and osteoclast suppression in the osteosarcoma context.

  To further probe the mechanism by which OFN downregulates ANXA2, we performed MD which nominated the transcription factor ZNF530 as a potential interacting partner. ZNF530 is a zinc-finger transcription factor predicted to localize to the nucleus and regulate RNA polymerase II-dependent transcription. Zinc-finger proteins constitute one of the largest families of transcription factors in eukaryotes and are frequently implicated in cancer pathogenesis through dysregulation of downstream target genes. Our subsequent experiments provided preliminary evidence supporting the potential involvement of ZNF530 in the regulation of ANXA2 during osteoclastogenesis. ZNF530 silencing decreased ANXA2 expression, while ZNF530 overexpression partially rescued the OFN-mediated suppression of ANXA2. Since OFN did not significantly affect ZNF530 expression, these findings suggest that OFN may influence the ZNF530-ANXA2 regulatory axis through mechanisms that remain to be further elucidated.

  Notably, our study further revealed that although OFN reduced the generation of TRAP-positive osteoclasts in vivo, it did not significantly alter the expression of RANKL or CTSK within the osteosarcoma microenvironment. Osteosarcoma cells continuously release osteoclastogenic factors, particularly RANKL, to promote osteoclast differentiation and activation. In addition, osteosarcoma cells have been reported to express bone-resorptive molecules, such as CTSK, which may further contribute to tumor-associated osteolytic destruction. Therefore, targeting osteoclast formation alone may not be sufficient to completely block osteosarcoma-induced bone loss. Therefore, OFN may achieve greater therapeutic efficacy when combined with strategies targeting osteosarcoma cells, such as conventional chemotherapy, to simultaneously inhibit tumor progression and osteolytic microenvironment remodeling.

  Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, the discovery cohort was derived from publicly available human osteosarcoma scRNA-seq data, while functional validation was performed in murine cell lines and xenograft models. Although these are standard preclinical systems, species-specific differences in transcription factor binding, promoter architecture, and drug metabolism may affect the quantitative translatability of our mechanistic findings. Validation in human osteoclast precursors derived from peripheral blood monocytes would further strengthen the conclusions. Second, while our micro-CT and histopathological data demonstrate significant bone preservation, the sample size (n = 3 per group) limits the power to detect subtle structural differences. Third, It should be noted that our discovery data were derived from human osteosarcoma samples, while functional validation was performed in murine cell lines and xenograft models. Although these are standard preclinical systems, transcription factor binding and promoter regulation may differ between human and mouse orthologs. Thus, extrapolation of the ZNF530-ANXA2 regulatory mechanism to the human system warrants caution. Additionally, Although Beyondcell was originally developed to assess drug sensitivity in malignant cancer subpopulations [20], its core principle—predicting drug sensitivity from baseline transcriptional signatures—is not inherently restricted to malignant cells. Recent studies have successfully applied Beyondcell to non-malignant cell populations within the tumor microenvironment. In the present study, we extend this application to osteoclast precursors, a non-malignant cell population, leveraging the same transcriptome-based sensitivity prediction framework.

  In summary, this study identifies OFN as a repurposed agent that effectively inhibits osteosarcoma-induced osteolysis through downregulation of ZNF530-ANXA2 axis Our findings support further preclinical and clinical investigation of OFN, particularly in combination with standard chemotherapy, as a bone-protective adjunctive therapy for osteosarcoma patients.


## 5. Conclusion
  Oxfendazole, identified through single-cell RNA sequencing combined with the Beyondcell algorithm, inhibits osteoclastogenesis and osteosarcoma osteolysis by suppressing ANXA2 expression via binding to transcription factor ZNF530.
