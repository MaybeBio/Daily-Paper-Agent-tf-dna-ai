# Microglial NCAM1 attenuates ischemic brain injury by inhibiting NF-κB-driven neuroinflammation through IκBα stabilization

## Introduction
  Acute ischemic stroke is a severe acute cerebrovascular condition characterized by rapid onset of symptoms such as muscle weakness, disorientation, ataxia, syncope, and paralysis [1–3]. Despite progress in medical and surgical technologies, stroke remains the second most common cause of death worldwide, responsible for 11.6% of total deaths, and the third leading cause of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), accounting for about 5.7% of the global DALY burden [4]. Intravenous thrombolysis using recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (r-tPA) is currently the only universally approved pharmacological treatment [5]. However, its application is limited by a narrow therapeutic window of approximately 4.5 h and other clinical contraindications, with fewer than 3% of ischemic stroke patients eligible to receive it [6]. Although many neuroprotective agents have shown promise in preclinical studies, their translation into clinical success has been disappointing [7]. Thus, identifying novel therapeutic targets and effective interventions for ischemic stroke remains an urgent priority.

  Among the complex cascade of events triggered by cerebral ischemia, neuroinflammation is a pivotal process that exacerbates neuronal damage [8]. Microglia, the resident immune cells of the central nervous system, are rapidly activated following ischemia, releasing a plethora of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines that drive a destructive neuroinflammatory response [9]. The nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathway serves as a central hub regulating neuroinflammation [10–12]. Under resting conditions, NF-κB is sequestered in the cytoplasm in an inactive complex with its inhibitory protein, IκBα. Following stimulation, IκBα undergoes phosphorylation and K48-linked polyubiquitination, leading to its proteasomal degradation. This releases the NF-κB p65 subunit, allowing its nuclear translocation and the initiation of pro-inflammatory gene transcription [13, 14]. Therefore, identifying endogenous molecules that can stabilize IκBα and thereby inhibit the excessive activation of NF-κB is of significant scientific importance for controlling post-stroke neuroinflammation.

  The neural cell adhesion molecule 1 (NCAM1), also known as CD56, is a prominent member of the immunoglobulin superfamily, traditionally recognized for its crucial roles in neuronal development, synaptic plasticity, and learning and memory [15, 16]. However, accumulating evidence suggests that the functions of NCAM1 extend far beyond these realms. Genome-wide association study (GWAS) data indicate that single nucleotide polymorphisms in the NCAM1 gene are associated with the risk of neuropsychiatric disorders such as schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, conditions often accompanied by features of neuropathology [17, 18]. Particularly in the context of cerebral ischemia, studies have shown that NCAM1 is cleaved by matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and β-site app-cleaving enzyme 1 (BACE1), potentially leading to a loss of its function [19, 20]. Nevertheless, the specific role of NCAM1, particularly microglial NCAM1, in the pathophysiology of ischemic stroke, and specifically whether and how it modulates neuroinflammation, remains poorly understood.

  In this study, we investigated that NCAM1 expression is specifically downregulated in microglia during the acute phase of stroke. Using CX3CR1Cre/ERT2 mice with microglia/macrophage-specific overexpression of NCAM1, we demonstrated that NCAM1 significantly reduces cerebral infarct volume, improves neurological deficits, and suppresses long-term motor dysfunction. Mechanistically, utilizing proteomic screening and biochemical validation, we found that NCAM1 directly interacts via its intracellular domain with the E3 ubiquitin ligase TRIM67. NCAM1 recruits TRIM67 to IκBα via its cytoplasmic domain, promoting K63-linked ubiquitination predominantly at lysine residues K21/K22, while simultaneously competitively excluding the K48-specific E3 ligase β-TrCP from binding to IκBα. This competitive substrate editing strategy—enhancing protective ubiquitination while blocking degradative ubiquitination—stabilizes IκBα, prevents p65 nuclear translocation, and ultimately alleviates NF-κB-driven neuroinflammation and cell apoptosis. Building upon this newly discovered mechanism, we further identified the small-molecule compound N3-[3-(1H-indol-6-yl)benzyl]pyridine-2,3-diamine (DrugBank ID: DB07993) as a potent NCAM1 agonist through molecular docking and high-throughput screening. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments validated its neuroprotective effects via activation of the NCAM1-TRIM67-IκBα axis. In brief, Our study provide a solid theoretical foundation and a highly promising lead compound for developing novel stroke treatment strategies targeting NCAM1.


## Materials and methods

### Key resources in this study
  Antibody, chemicals, primers and siRNA/sgRNA sequence information used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1-S3.


### Human sample collection and processing
  A total of 37 individuals with acute ischemic stroke and 22 age-matched healthy controls were enrolled in this study between June 2022 and June 2025. The protocol received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Shangrao People's Hospital, and written informed consent was provided by all participants prior to enrollment. The study adhered to the ethical guidelines set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki. Diagnosis of acute ischemic stroke was initially based on clinical symptoms and subsequently confirmed by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI). Prior to the administration of any therapeutic agents, peripheral blood samples (4–5 mL) were drawn from each patient into EDTA-K2 anticoagulant tubes. The samples were then lysed using TRIzol reagent and stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis. Exclusion criteria for both stroke patients and healthy controls included: (1) presence of other intracranial pathologies; (2) history of autoimmune, acute or chronic infectious, or hematologic diseases, or surgery within the preceding year; and (3) use of hormonal or immunosuppressive medications within the last six months. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated using a commercial PBMC isolation kit (Famacs, Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol, as described in previous studies [21, 22]. Monocyte purity was verified by flow cytometry to exceed 90%. Demographic and clinical characteristics of the patients—such as age, sex, comorbidities, infarct location, and time from stroke onset to blood sampling—are summarized in Supplementary Table S4.


### Animals
  Adult CX3CR1Cre/ERT2 male mice and wild-type (WT) C57BL/6 J male mice (8–12 weeks old) were obtained from the Shanghai Nanfang Research Center for Model Organisms and Slake Company (Shanghai, China), respectively. All experimental procedures, approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shangrao People's Hospital, adhered to the National Institutes of Health (NIH) guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals.


### Isolation of primary mouse cortical neurons, astrocytes and microglia
  Primary mouse cortical neurons, astrocytes, and microglia were isolated using established protocols [23, 24]. Briefly, cortical neurons were obtained from embryonic day (E) 15–16 mice [23, 24]. The fetal cerebral cortices were dissected and placed in Hanks' balanced salt solution (HBSS), minced into 2 mm pieces, and digested with 2 mg/mL papain at 37 °C for 30 min. The dissociated neurons were then cultured in Neurobasal medium supplemented with 2% B27 and 1% GlutaMAX at 37 °C with 5% CO2, and allowed to mature for 7–9 days. Microglia and astrocytes were isolated from mixed glial cultures based on their differential adhesion properties [25]. Cerebral cortices from postnatal day 1–2 mice were aseptically removed after disinfection with 75% alcohol. The meninges and blood vessels were carefully detached, and the tissue was digested with 0.25% trypsin at 37 °C for 6–10 min. Digestion was terminated by adding DMEM-F12 medium (Corning, USA) containing 10% FBS (Gibco, USA). The resulting mixed glial cells were cultured in DMEM-F12 with 10% FBS until reaching 90–100% confluence (around 10 days). Microglia were harvested by shaking the flasks at 180 × g for 2 h, after which the adherent astrocytes were detached using 0.25% trypsin (Gibco, USA) and seeded into 12-well plates (NEST, China) for subsequent experiments.


### Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)
  Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Vazyme Biotech, China). According to the manufacturer's protocol for the cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme Biotech, China), 1 µg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into first-strand cDNA. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was then performed using the SYBR Green reagent (Vazyme Biotech, China) on an ABI StepOnePlus detection system, with the first-strand cDNA serving as the template and gene-specific primers for amplification. The 18S rRNA was used as an internal control for normalization of gene expression. All primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S3.


### Plasmid constructions
  NCAM1 and TRIM67 mutants were constructed using the Gibson Assembly Kit (New England Biolabs, NEB) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The corresponding primers are listed in Supplementary Table S3. All constructs were confirmed through DNA sequencing.


### Small interfering RNA (siRNA)-mediated gene knockdown
  Primary mouse microglial cells, either subjected to or not subjected to OGD treatment, were transiently transfected with small interfering RNA (siRNA). Cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection, and lysates were collected. Knockdown efficiency was then assessed by Western blotting analysis. The siRNA were synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology Co., and the sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S3.


### CRISPR-Cas9-mediated gene knock-out cell generation
  The pX459 plasmid was used to clone guide oligos targeting the NCAM1 or TRIM67 gene. Knock-out cell clones were screened through western blot analysis and validated via Sanger sequencing. The sequences of the gene-specific sgRNAs are listed in Supplementary Table S3.


### Western blotting
  Western blotting was performed as previously described [26, 27]. Briefly, cell lysates or immunoprecipitates were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (GE Healthcare Sciences). The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST, pH 7.4) and then incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. After washing, the membranes were probed with corresponding secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were detected using an Enhanced Chemiluminescence (ECL) system (Santa Cruz Biotechnology). All experiments were repeated at least twice with 2–3 technical replicates, and representative images are shown.


### Model of transient middle cerebral artery occlusion (tMCAO)
  Focal cerebral ischemia was induced in mice using the well-established transient middle cerebral artery occlusion (tMCAO) model, as previously described [22, 28]. Briefly, anesthesia was initiated with 3% isoflurane in an induction chamber and maintained at 2% via a nose cone. Core body temperature was regulated at 37 ± 0.5 °C using a feedback-controlled heating pad throughout the procedure. Following a ventral neck incision and blunt dissection of the muscles, the right common carotid artery (CCA), external carotid artery (ECA), and internal carotid artery (ICA) were carefully isolated. A silicone-coated monofilament was then introduced into the ECA and advanced to occlude the MCA for 60 min. Successful occlusion was confirmed by a reduction in cerebral blood flow exceeding 70%, as monitored by laser speckle contrast imaging (RWD Biotechnology). Reperfusion was initiated by filament withdrawal. Sham-operated mice underwent identical procedures except for the filament insertion.


### Assessment of cerebral infarct volume and neurological function
  Cerebral infarct volume was evaluated by 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC; TCI, Japan) staining. Briefly, mice under deep isoflurane anesthesia were euthanized, and their brains were rapidly removed and sectioned coronally into five slices (1–2 mm thick). The slices were incubated in 2% TTC solution at 37 °C for 10 min, photographed, and analyzed using ImageJ software. The corrected infarct volume (%) was calculated as: [(contralateral hemisphere volume-non-infarcted ipsilateral volume)/(contralateral hemisphere volume × 2)] × 100%.

  Neurological function was assessed using both short- and long-term tests. Short-term deficits were evaluated with the Longa score, while long-term performance was examined using the modified Neurological Severity Score (mNSS), foot-fault test, wire-hanging test, and rotarod test at days 1, 3, 5, 7, 14, 21, and 28 post-tMCAO, as described previously [22, 28]. All behavioral assessments were conducted in a double-blinded manner.


### Immunofluorescence staining
  Immunofluorescence staining was carried out following established protocols. In brief, dissected brain tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, followed by cryoprotection through sequential incubation in 15% and 30% sucrose solutions at 4 °C until the tissues sank (typically 2–3 days). Coronal sections (16 or 40 μm thick) were obtained using a Leica cryostat microtome. For immunostaining, sections and cultured cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde, washed three times with phosphate‑buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4), and then blocked with 10% normal goat or donkey serum in PBS for 1 h at room temperature to minimize nonspecific binding. Sections were subsequently incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by appropriate secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were captured using an Ds-Ri2 fluorescent microscope (Nikon, Japan). For peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), immunofluorescence staining was performed within 1–2 h after blood collection.


### Adult mouse microglial isolation and flow cytometry examination
  Primary microglia were isolated from adult mouse brains using CD11b MicroBeads (Miltenyi Biotec) per the manufacturer's protocol [29, 30]. The isolated cells were first blocked with an anti-CD16/32 antibody for 20 min to prevent Fc receptor-mediated nonspecific binding. Subsequently, the cells were stained with a fixable viability dye (FVS780) and fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies against CD11b and CD45 in the dark. Flow cytometric analysis was performed on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman, USA).


### Oxygen–glucose deprivation (OGD) treatment
  To simulate ischemia in vitro, primary mouse microglia, astrocytes, and cortical neurons were subjected to oxygen–glucose deprivation (OGD) as previously described [31]. Briefly, the culture medium was replaced with glucose- and serum-free DMEM (Gibco, USA). The cells were then placed in a hypoxia chamber (Billups-Rothenberg Inc, USA) at 37 °C under an atmosphere of 95% N₂ and 5% CO₂ for the indicated durations.


### Microglia-neuron co-culture
  Primary microglia and cortical neurons were isolated from C57BL/6 J mice as described in "Isolation of primary mouse cortical neurons, astrocytes and microglia" section. Neurons were seeded in 24-well plates (5 × 104 cells/well) and cultured for 7 days. Microglia were seeded onto Transwell inserts (0.4 μm pore, 1 × 105 cells/insert) and transduced with lentivirus (Vector or NCAM1-OE) for 48 h or treated with DB07993 (10 μM) for 12 h. Inserts were then placed into neuron-containing wells, and co-cultures were subjected to OGD in glucose-free DMEM under 95% N₂/5% CO₂ for 0–16 h. Neuronal viability was measured using CCK-8 assay (Dojindo) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance at 450 nm was normalized to sham control. Experiments were performed in triplicate with at least three independent replicates.


### Stereotaxic surgery
  For stereotaxic injection, mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane and secured in a stereotactic frame (RWD Life Science, China) in the prone position. A total of 1 μL of either rAAV-CMV-DIO-mCherry-Loxp-Vector-WPRE-polyA or rAAV-CMV-DIO-mCherry-Loxp-NCAM1-WPRE-polyA (5 × 1012 vg/μL; packaged in AAV-MG1.2) was injected into the target region of CX3CR1Cre/ERT2 mice (coordinates: 0.5 mm anterior to bregma, 2.0 mm lateral, 1.0 mm ventral) using a 30-gauge needle connected to a microsyringe pump (RWD Life Science). The virus was delivered at a rate of 0.05 μL/min over 20 min, and the needle was left in place for an additional 10 min before withdrawal. Post-surgery, mice were placed on a heating pad for recovery. Fourteen days later, Cre recombinase expression was induced by intraperitoneal administration of tamoxifen (75 mg/kg; Sigma, USA) for five consecutive days. The AAV transfection area covered approximately 19.25 ± 3.26% of the total infarct area and 43.68 ± 5.13% of the cortical infarct area.


### Cell immunofluorescence
  For immunofluorescence, cells grown on chamber slides were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature. After PBS washing, cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min, followed by blocking with 0.5% BSA in PBS for 1 h. The cells were then incubated with primary antibody in PBS overnight at 4 °C. After another PBS wash, the samples were incubated with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies and counterstained with DAPI for 1 h at room temperature. Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan).


### Cell viability assay
  Cell proliferation was determined with a CCK-8 assay kit (KeyGEN BioTECH, China) as per the provided instructions. In brief, cells were plated at 1 × 104 cells/mL in 96-well plates and allowed to adhere for 24 h under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂). After the designated treatments, each well was supplemented with 10 μL of CCK-8 solution. Following a 4-h incubation, the absorbance at 450 nm was recorded using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad, USA) for cell viability quantification.


### TUNEL staining
  The TUNEL assay was conducted using an In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions [32]. Fluorescence images were acquired with an fluorescent microscope (Nikon, Japan). The percentage of cell death was calculated as the ratio of TUNEL-positive nuclei (green) to the total number of DAPI-stained nuclei (blue) in the ischemic area. Quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software.


### Molecular docking
  For Molecular docking, a total of 6,041 experimentally validated small-molecule drugs from the DrugBank database were selected for molecular docking using AutoDock. The defined binding pocket was centered at coordinates (CenterX: 32.8, CenterY: 56.6, CenterZ: 41.4), with a grid box size of SizeX: 16 Å, SizeY: 14 Å, SizeZ: 17 Å. The top 100 compounds with the lowest binding energies were summarized, and the chemical structures and binding modes of the top 10 candidates are listed in the table below. All top 10 molecules were found to bind to the same pocket, which consists of the following residues: E11, P12, Y13, S14, S15, T16, M66, P70, E71, T97, Q98, P99, V100, R101, E102, D127, D128, G129, G130, S131, P132, I133, N179, Q180, Q181, and G182. The best binding affinity achieved was −4.6 kcal/mol, indicating a strong interaction between the small-molecule drug N3-[3-(1H-indol-6-yl)benzyl]pyridine-2,3-diamine (DrugBank ID: DB07993) and the NCAM1 protein. The structural formulas, binding modes, and docking scores of the top 10 small molecules are provided in Supplementary Table S5.


### Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) assay
  Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis was performed on a Biacore X100 system (GE Healthcare, USA) to characterize the binding of DB07993 to NCAM1. Recombinant NCAM1 protein was immobilized on a CM5 sensor chip. Different concentrations of DB07993 were dissolved in the running buffer, passed over the chip at a specific flow rate, and the equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) value was measured from the affinity curve generated.


### Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)
  Primary mouse microglial cells at the logarithmic growth phase were seeded in 15-cm culture dishes. Prior to the Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA), cells were pretreated with or without 3 μM DB07993 for 12 h. After incubation, cells were trypsinized, collected, and resuspended in PBS supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail. The cell suspension was then aliquoted into seven PCR tubes for subsequent heat treatment at designated temperature points using a Veriti 96-well thermal cycler (3 min per temperature, followed by 3 min at room temperature). Cell lysis was performed through three freeze–thaw cycles using liquid nitrogen and the thermal cycler. Tubes were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatants containing cell lysates were collected for subsequent Western blotting analysis.


### Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
  The levels of IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, CXCL1, and CCL2 in tissue homogenates were quantified using commercial mouse ELISA kits (Multi Sciences, Hangzhou, China), and the results were expressed as picograms per milliliter (pg/mL).


### Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry/mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) analysis
  To identify NCAM1-interacting proteins, co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) coupled with Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry/mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) was employed. Primary microglia from OGD model mice were transfected with a Flag-NCAM1 plasmid for 48 h. Cell lysates were then immunoprecipitated using an anti-Flag antibody, with lysates from Flag-vector transfected cells serving as the negative control. Similarly, brain tissue lysates from tMCAO model mice were immunoprecipitated with an NCAM1-specific antibody, using IgG as a control. All Co-IP samples were analyzed by LC–MS/MS (PTM Bio Co., Ltd., China). Potential NCAM1-binding proteins were selected based on the following criteria: 1) a protein signal ratio (experimental group/control group) greater than 5-fold, and 2) exclusive identification in the experimental group. High-confidence interactors were further filtered by requiring more than 5 unique peptides and a sequence coverage exceeding 10%.


### In vivo ubiquitination assays and co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)
  Primary mouse microglia were transfected with HA-ubiquitin along with the indicated constructs. Forty-eight hours post-transfection, cells were treated with 30 μM MG-132 (Selleckchem, USA) for 6 h, then lysed and denatured in 1% SDS buffer (Tris [pH 7.5], 0.5 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT) at 95 °C for 10 min. For immunoprecipitation, cell lysates were diluted tenfold in Tris–HCl buffer and incubated with anti-Flag/His M2 agarose beads (Sigma, USA) at 4 °C for 4 h. The beads were subsequently washed four times with BC100 buffer (20 mM Tris–Cl, pH 7.9, 100 mM NaCl, 0.2 mM EDTA, 20% glycerol) containing 0.2% Triton X-100. Proteins were eluted at 4 °C for 2 h using a competing Flag/His peptide. Western blotting was performed with anti-HA, anti-ubiquitin, and anti-tag (Flag, Myc, HA, His) antibodies to evaluate IκBα polyubiquitination and to detect specific proteins in co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) complexes.


### GST pull-down assays
  GST-tagged fusion proteins were coupled to glutathione-Sepharose beads (Amersham Biosciences, USA) and equilibrated in pull-down buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% NP-40, 1 mM DTT, 10% glycerol, 1 mM EDTA, 2.5 mM MgCl₂, and 1 μg/ml leupeptin). The beads were subsequently incubated with recombinant His-tagged protein for 2 h at 4 °C, followed by five washes with the same buffer. Proteins were eluted in SDS sample buffer, separated by SDS–PAGE, and analyzed by Western blotting.


### Cycloheximide (CHX) treatment
  To assess the effect of NCAM1 on IκBα protein stability, primary mouse microglia were transfected with the indicated plasmids and then treated with the protein synthesis inhibitor cycloheximide (CHX, 50 μg/mL, Selleckchem, USA) for 8 h. Cell lysates were subsequently collected and subjected to Western blotting analysis.


### Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis
  Study design and data sources: Data were sourced from European populations. Stroke genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics came from the MEGASTROKE consortium (40,585 cases; 406,111 controls). Ischemic stroke GWAS data (ID: ebi-a-GCST005843) included 34,217 cases and 406,111 controls. Plasma protein GWAS data were derived from two studies: the UKB-PPP dataset (validation), measuring 2,923 proteins in 54,219 individuals using the Olink Explore 3072 platform; and the deCODE dataset (discovery), assaying 4,719 proteins in 35,559 individuals via SomaScan v4.

  Selection of instrumental variables (IVs): Genetic instruments were selected under rigorous criteria: single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with exposure at p < 5 × 10⁻5 were clumped (r2 < 0.001, window = 10,000 kb). We excluded SNPs with minor allele frequency < 0.01, ambiguous alleles, or strand alignment issues. Only variants with F-statistic > 10 were retained to ensure strong instruments and limit weak bias. Identical criteria were applied in reverse MR.

  Two-sample mendelian randomization analysis: A two-sample MR design was employed to estimate causal effects. Inverse-variance weighted (IVW) regression with random effects served as the primary method. Sensitivity analyses included MR-Egger, weighted median, and mode-based estimators. Heterogeneity was assessed via Cochran’s Q (p < 0.05 indicating significance). Horizontal pleiotropy was evaluated using MR-Egger intercept (p < 0.05 suggestive of pleiotropy). Outliers were detected and removed via MR-PRESSO, with causal estimates re-calculated afterward. The relevant sensitivity test results are attached in Supplementary Table S6-S8.

  Colocalization analysis: We applied Bayesian colocalization (coloc R package) to test whether trait associations shared a causal variant. Five hypotheses were evaluated; posterior probability for H4 (PP4 > 0.8) indicated a shared variant underlying both traits.

  Mediation analysis: A two-step MR approach assessed metabolite mediation between exposure and outcome. The indirect effect was calculated as β₁ × β₂, and the proportion mediated as (β₁ × β₂)/β₃. Confidence intervals were derived via the delta method. Analyses assumed unidirectional causality.

  Statistical analysis: All analyses used R 4.3.3 with TwoSampleMR, MendelianRandomization, MR-PRESSO, ggplot2, and forestplot packages.


### Quantification and statistical analysis
  Data are presented as mean ± SD and analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8. Statistical comparisons were performed with unpaired Student’s t-test, Mann–Whitney test, log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test, one-way ANOVA, or two-way ANOVA (with or without repeated measures), using Bonferroni’s correction where appropriate. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.


## Results

### Genetic association and colocalization between plasma protein NCAM1 and stroke
  Two-sample MR analyses were conducted to estimate the causal effects of 2,897 plasma proteins of Finngen on stroke risk using data from the MEGASTROKE consortium as the outcome dataset. Among them, NCAM1, a protein previously implicated in neurorepair and metabolic regulation, showed a significant association, the IVW method revealed a negative causal association between genetically predicted NCAM1 levels (Finngen dataset) and stroke risk (OR = 0.954, 95% CI = 0.916–0.994, p = 0.024), consistent across multiple MR approaches (Fig. 1A and B). Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of these findings. The Cochran’s Q test for IVW showed no significant heterogeneity, and MR-Egger intercept tests (p > 0.05) indicated the absence of horizontal pleiotropy. Replication using plasma protein data from the UKB-PPP cohort also demonstrated a consistent inverse causal association between NCAM1 and stroke (OR = 0.951, 95% CI = 0.915–0.988, p = 0.010) (Fig. 1A and B).   To further validate the causal association and rule out linkage disequilibrium-driven confounding, we performed colocalization analyses using cis-variants within 500 kb of the NCAM1 locus. The posterior probability for a shared causal variant (PP.H4) between NCAM1 and stroke was 2.2% in the Finngen dataset and 1.5% in the UKB dataset, suggesting limited evidence for a shared genetic signal Supplementary materials (Fig. 1C and D).

  Given our prior findings that the TyG index is closely associated with ischemic stroke, and the established role of lipid metabolism in stroke pathogenesis, we further explored lipid-related traits as potential mediators. Three lipid traits—apolipoprotein B (ieu-b-108), LDL cholesterol (ieu-b-110), and Triglycerides (ieu-b-111)—were included in a two-step MR framework (Fig. S1A-C). The estimated mediation proportions were 4.4% for apolipoprotein B, 4.0% for LDL cholesterol, and 3.5% for triglycerides, indicating that lipid metabolism partially mediates the causal effect of NCAM1 on stroke risk (Fig. S1A-C).


### Peripheral blood expression of NCAM1 is downregulated in patients with stroke and correlated with infarct volume
  Differences in gene expression variability in peripheral whole blood between healthy subjects and patients with ischemic stroke have long been recognized by researchers as a valuable method for identifying diagnostic or prognostic biomarkers or predictors of ischemic stroke [22, 33, 34]. The precise role of NCAM1 in the pathological process of ischemic stroke, however, remains to be fully elucidated. We assessed NCAM1 gene expression in peripheral whole blood and found it was significantly reduced in ischemic stroke patients compared to control subjects (Fig. 2A). Subsequent analysis of the relationship between NCAM1 gene expression and either infarct volume or National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) scores revealed negative correlations, suggesting that NCAM1 may be inversely associated with stroke severity in patients (Fig. 2B-D). Furthermore, immunofluorescence analysis showed decreased NCAM1 expression in peripheral whole blood following stroke onset relative to control subjects (Fig. 2E and F). ### NCAM1 is a microglia/macrophage-specific regulator after ischemic stroke onset
  Consistent with findings in human peripheral blood, we found that in a mouse tMCAO model, NCAM1 protein expression was significantly reduced at 3 h, reached its nadir at 24 h, and eventually returned to baseline levels by 7 days (Fig. 3A-C). We subsequently investigated which cell type contributed to the reduced NCAM1 expression in ischemic stroke. Immunofluorescence staining results revealed a marked decrease in NCAM1 expression within microglia/macrophages, but not in astrocytes or neurons, following stroke onset (Fig. 3D, E, and S2A-S2C). To further define NCAM1 expression in microglia/macrophages after ischemic insult, we isolated these cells via magnetic bead sorting and assessed NCAM1 mRNA and protein levels. Furthermore, we co-transfected primary mouse microglia with Flag-NCAM1 and Myc-TRIM67 plasmids to verify their interaction.   NCAM1 is composed of extracellular domain—primarily consisting of five membrane-distal N-terminal immunoglobulin (Ig) domains (designated Ig1-5) and two membrane-proximal fibronectin type III domains (FN3 1–2), transmembrane segment, and cytoplasmic segment [43]. Expressed predominantly on the cell surface of neurons and glial cells, NCAM1 binds ligands via its extracellular domain to induce intracellular signaling pathways and cytoskeletal rearrangements [44, 45]. Previous studies have identified kinesin light chain 1 (KLC1) as a novel binding partner of the NCAM1 cytoplasmic segment, with their interaction being a key mechanism promoting NCAM1 trafficking and cell surface delivery [46]. To identify the critical domain responsible for the interaction with TRIM67, we constructed four NCAM1 mutants (Fig. 7L). By co-transfecting TRIM67 with these NCAM1 mutant plasmids into primary mouse microglia and assessing their binding, we found that NCAM1 failed to interact with TRIM67 upon deletion of the cytoplasmic segment (amino acids 730–848), while mutations in other domains did not affect the interaction (Fig. 7M). Co-transfection of NCAM1 with these TRIM67 mutant plasmids into primary mouse microglia and subsequent binding assessment revealed that deletion of the CCoil domain (amino acids 332–369) abolished the TRIM67-NCAM1 interaction, whereas mutations in other domains had no effect (Fig. 7O). ### Interaction of NCAM1-TRIM67 stabilizes IκBα protein via enhancing K63-linked and attenuating K48-linked ubiquitination
  IκBα is a negative regulator of NF-κB signaling, and its degradation via the proteasomal pathway mediated by K48-linked ubiquitination chains represents a critical step in NF-κB activation [13]. A previous study indicated that TRIM67 inhibits β-TrCP-mediated IκBα degradation [48], and subsequent work demonstrated that TRIM67 enhances IκBα protein stability by promoting its K63-linked ubiquitination and suppressing K48-linked ubiquitination [49]. In our study, total IKKα/β and p65 protein levels remained unchanged (Fig. 6A), whereas IκBα increased following NCAM1 overexpression, a finding further validated in primary mouse microglia subjected to OGD (Fig. 8A). Given that TRIM67 is a direct binding partner of NCAM1, we hypothesized that NCAM1 promotes IκBα stability in a TRIM67-dependent manner. As shown in Fig. 8A, compared to NCAM1 overexpression alone, TRIM67 knockdown abolished the upregulation of IκBα protein expression induced by NCAM1. In contrast, TRIM67 overexpression did not provide additional benefit. We next examined IκBα ubiquitination. Results showed that NCAM1 upregulation increased IκBα ubiquitination, and this effect was negated by TRIM67 knockdown. Again, TRIM67 overexpression did not further enhance IκBα ubiquitination (Fig. 8B). We then characterized the types of IκBα ubiquitination. NCAM1 overexpression suppressed K48-linked ubiquitination while enhancing K63-linked ubiquitination of IκBα (Fig. 8C).   We next performed rescue experiments in NCAM1-knockout (KO) primary mouse microglia by overexpressing wild-type (WT) or mutant NCAM1 and assessed IκBα ubiquitination. In NCAM1-KO microglia, reconstitution with WT NCAM1, but not mutant NCAM1, restored the levels of total IκBα ubiquitination (Fig. 8D), K48-linked ubiquitination (Fig. 8E), and K63-linked ubiquitination (Fig. 8F). Similarly, in TRIM67-KO primary mouse microglia, reintroduction of WT TRIM67, but not mutant TRIM67, rescued the levels of total (Fig. 8G), K48-linked (Fig. 8H), and K63-linked (Fig. 8I) ubiquitination of IκBα. In addition, we evaluated the effect of NCAM1 on the TRIM67-IκBα interaction in primary mouse microglia. As shown in Fig. 8J, NCAM1 robustly promoted TRIM67-IκBα binding in a dose-dependent manner. To further dissect the molecular mechanism by which the NCAM1–TRIM67 complex shifts the ubiquitination balance of IκBα, we examined whether NCAM1 influences the recruitment of β‑TrCP, the K48‑specific E3 ligase responsible for IκBα degradation. Co‑immunoprecipitation assays revealed that NCAM1 knockout (KO) significantly reduced the interaction between β‑TrCP and IκBα in primary mouse microglia (Fig. S4A). Re‑expression of wild‑type TRIM67 in NCAM1‑KO cells fully restored β‑TrCP–IκBα binding, whereas re‑expression of TRIM67‑Mut4, which lacks the CCoil domain required for NCAM1 interaction, failed to rescue this interaction (Fig. S4A). These results demonstrate that NCAM1, via its direct binding to TRIM67, promotes TRIM67‑mediated competition with β‑TrCP for IκBα association. This competitive displacement reduces K48‑linked ubiquitination and tilts the balance toward K63‑linked ubiquitination, thereby stabilizing IκBα.

  To determine whether K48- and K63‑linked ubiquitination of IκBα occur on the same lysine residues, we generated an IκBα K21/22R double mutant. In NCAM1‑KO primary microglia expressing wild‑type IκBα, NCAM1‑KO increased K48‑linked (Fig. S4B) and decreased K63‑linked (Fig. S4C) ubiquitination, both of which were fully reversed by re‑expression of NCAM1 or TRIM67. In contrast, these modulatory effects were completely abolished when IκBα K21/22R was expressed (Fig. S4B and S4C). These results demonstrate that both ubiquitination linkages target the same K21/22 residues, and that the NCAM1–TRIM67 complex promotes K63‑linked ubiquitination at these sites, thereby competitively inhibiting K48‑linked ubiquitination and stabilizing IκBα.

  We next asked whether the NCAM1–TRIM67 complex also modulates upstream signaling components of the IKK complex. Given that K63‑linked ubiquitination of NEMO, IKKβ, TAB2, and RIPK1 is critical for IKK activation, we examined their K63 ubiquitination status in NCAM1‑KO primary microglia reconstituted with wild‑type TRIM67 or TRIM67‑Mut4. Neither NCAM1‑KO alone nor reconstitution with WT or Mut4 TRIM67 significantly altered the K63‑linked ubiquitination of these adaptors (Fig. S4D-S4G). These results demonstrate that the NCAM1–TRIM67 complex does not suppress IKK phosphorylation by interfering with K63 ubiquitination of canonical upstream regulators. Instead, the observed reduction in phospho‑IKKα/β (Fig. 6A and B) is most likely attributable to feedback inhibition mediated by increased IκBα stability, as stabilized IκBα can directly associate with IKK complexes and dampen their kinase activity.

  In summary, these findings demonstrate that NCAM1 directly interacts with TRIM67 to facilitate TRIM67-IκBα association, promotes K63-linked ubiquitination while inhibiting K48-linked ubiquitination of IκBα, thereby preventing its proteasomal degradation and ultimately enhancing IκBα protein stability.


### Discovery of DB07993 as a potent NCAM1 agonist that mediates neuroprotection in ischemic stroke through IκBα stabilization and subsequent attenuation of neuroinflammation and cell apoptosis.
  Based on these findings, we propose NCAM1 agonists as promising pharmacological candidates for ischemic stroke treatment. Using Autodock molecular docking software, we screened 6,041 experimentally validated small-molecule drugs from the DrugBank database and identified N3-[3-(1H-indol-6-yl)benzyl]pyridine-2,3-diamine (DrugBank ID: DB07993) as a potent NCAM1 activator (Fig. 9A and S5), with a half-maximal activation concentration (AC₅₀) of 2.53 μM (Fig. 9B). DB07993, an aminopyridine analog, has been characterized as a β-site APP-cleaving enzyme 1 (BACE-1) inhibitor [50], a therapeutic target for Alzheimer’s disease [51]. Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis confirmed strong binding between DB07993 and the NCAM1 protein, with a dissociation constant (KD) value of 278 nM (Fig. 9C). In addition, cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) results showed that DB07993 inhibited the thermal degradation of NCAM1 (Fig. 9D and E), further validating their direct interaction. We next investigated whether DB07993 modulates IκBα protein stability in microglia. As shown in Fig. 9F–H, DB07993 treatment significantly upregulated IκBα protein levels (Fig. 9F), decreased K48-linked ubiquitination (Fig. 9G), and increased K63-linked ubiquitination of IκBα (Fig. 9H) in primary mouse microglia under OGD. Notably, combining NCAM1 overexpression with DB07993 did not yield additional benefits on IκBα expression (Fig. 9I), K48-linked ubiquitination (Fig. 9J), or K63-linked ubiquitination (Fig. 9K) beyond DB07993 treatment alone. We then evaluated the effect of DB07993 on neuroinflammation and cell apoptosis. DB07993 reduced IL-6 release in cell supernatants and cleaved caspase-3 expression in cell lysates from OGD-exposed microglia. Western blot analysis revealed that DB07993 administration significantly downregulated the expression of IL-6 and cleaved caspase-3, while NCAM1 overexpression did not exhibit any additional effect (Fig. S7). ## Discussion
  Ischemic stroke is a complex pathological process involving multiple mechanisms such as neuroinflammation, cell death, and vascular dysfunction. This study reveals the critical protective role of microglial NCAM1 in cerebral ischemia. It directly interacts with TRIM67 to modulate the K63- and K48-linked polyubiquitination status of IκBα, enhancing its stability and subsequently suppressing the excessive activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway, ultimately mitigating neuroinflammation and cellular apoptosis. The discovery of this novel mechanism provides a new theoretical foundation and potential therapeutic target for ischemic stroke.

  In recent years, as research into the pathological mechanisms of stroke has deepened, the roles of various modes of cell death in ischemic brain injury have been progressively elucidated. Beyond classical apoptosis, novel programmed cell death pathways such as PANoptosis [52] and ferroptosis [53] are increasingly recognized for their contributions to stroke-related neuronal damage. Our findings demonstrate that NCAM1 overexpression suppresses apoptosis-related proteins like cleaved caspase-3. Concurrently, emerging studies indicate upregulation of key PANoptosis regulators like TNFRSF1A in neurons post-ischemia [52], and that Moracin N can inhibit neuronal ferroptosis by activating the Keap1/Nrf2 pathway [53]. These insights suggest that the protective effects of NCAM1 may extend beyond inhibiting apoptosis, and exploring its potential role in regulating a broader spectrum of cell death modalities represents a promising future direction.

  Neuroinflammation is a central component of secondary injury following stroke. Our study confirms that NCAM1 effectively inhibits microglial M1 polarization and the release of pro-inflammatory factors. This aligns with the observed dynamic shifts in microglial states post-stroke. Single-cell RNA sequencing studies depict the transition of microglia from a homeostatic state to acute-phase and disease-associated states [54]. Notably, recent research reveals that post-stroke microglia undergo cholesterol metabolism reprogramming, transforming into a unique stroke-associated foam-like microglia (SAM-foamy) phenotype. Intracellular accumulation of cholesterol and crystals in these cells can drive chronic neuroinflammation and impede neural repair [55]. This finding intricately links immunity and metabolism, suggesting that combining NCAM1 intervention with modulation of microglial cholesterol metabolism (e.g., via activation of CYP46A1) might more effectively control neuroinflammation.

  Blood–brain barrier (BBB) disruption is a crucial factor contributing to edema and neurological deterioration after stroke. Our results demonstrate the neuroprotective effects of NCAM1, while recent studies have elucidated a refined mechanism underlying BBB breakdown: the ROCK1/MLC/NMMHC IIA-actin signaling pathway forms a positive feedback loop, directly leading to endothelial cytoskeletal reorganization and tight junction degradation [56]. This discovery provides a clear interventional target for BBB protection. Furthermore, nanotechnology-based drug delivery systems targeting neutrophils show great promise; engineered lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) can significantly attenuate BBB disruption and infarct volume by inhibiting the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) [57]. These advances suggest that the protective effects of NCAM1 might be associated with maintaining neurovascular unit homeostasis, warranting further investigation into the precise mechanisms and potential crosstalk with the aforementioned signaling pathways.

  The role of TRIM family proteins in regulating NF-κB signaling is increasingly recognized, with TRIM67 exhibiting strikingly context-dependent functions. Liu et al. [58] reported that TRIM67 promotes NF-κB activation and apoptosis in GA-13315-treated lung cancer cells, suggesting a pro-inflammatory role in malignant settings. In contrast, Fan et al. [48] demonstrated that TRIM67 suppresses TNFα-triggered NF-κB activation by competitively binding to β-TrCP, thereby preventing β-TrCP-mediated K48-linked ubiquitination and degradation of IκBα. More recently, Yu et al. [49] showed that TRIM67 protects against cerebral ischemia–reperfusion injury by targeting IκBα for K63-linked polyubiquitination, stabilizing IκBα and inhibiting neuroinflammation. Building upon these findings, the present study further demonstrates that microglial NCAM1 forms a complex with the E3 ubiquitin ligase TRIM67 to bidirectionally regulate IκBα ubiquitination, thereby inhibiting NF-κB signaling and attenuating post-ischemic neuroinflammation. The core mechanism is as follows: NCAM1 recruits TRIM67 to IκBα via its cytoplasmic domain, promoting K63-linked ubiquitination predominantly at lysine residues K21/K22, while simultaneously competitively excluding the K48-specific E3 ligase β-TrCP from binding to IκBα. This competitive substrate editing strategy—enhancing protective ubiquitination while blocking degradative ubiquitination—stabilizes IκBα, prevents p65 nuclear translocation, and ultimately alleviates neuroinflammatory injury. This model has been rigorously validated through IκBα lysine point mutation (K21/22R), co-immunoprecipitation, in vivo ubiquitination assays, and functional rescue experiments in NCAM1/TRIM67 knockout microglia. Nevertheless, it remains unclear whether the NCAM1–TRIM67 complex potentiates TRIM67 function solely through physical competition for substrate binding or via allosteric modulation of its intrinsic E3 ligase activity. Future studies should address this question by comparing the enzymatic activity of TRIM67 in the presence or absence of NCAM1 using in vitro ubiquitination assays with purified components. Additionally, NCAM1 overexpression reduces IKKα/β phosphorylation. To definitively establish that this IKK dephosphorylation is an indirect feedback consequence of IκBα stabilization, the following experimental strategies can be employed: (1) genetic or pharmacological manipulation of IκBα (knockdown, expression of degradation-resistant S32/36A or ubiquitination-defective K21/22R mutants) to assess whether IKK phosphorylation is restored; (2) time‑course analyses to determine the temporal precedence of IκBα stabilization relative to IKK dephosphorylation; (3) direct measurement of IKK kinase activity via in vitro kinase assays using immunoprecipitated IKK complexes; (4) evaluation of upstream kinase TAK1 phosphorylation status; and (5) unbiased phosphoproteomic screening to identify additional feedback regulators. Collectively, these approaches will dissect the causal hierarchy and reinforce the conclusion that NCAM1–TRIM67 primarily acts at the level of IκBα.

  Regarding therapeutic strategies, our identification of the NCAM1 small-molecule agonist DB07993 via molecular docking demonstrates compelling neuroprotective efficacy both in vitro and in vivo, providing proof-of-concept for targeting NCAM1. Corroborating this, other studies have identified natural products conferring neuroprotection through specific pathways; for instance, Moracin N inhibits ferroptosis by activating the Keap1/Nrf2 pathway [53], while Niacin modulates microglial function and promotes hematoma resolution and tissue repair via the HCAR2/SIRT1/Nrf2 axis [59]. Collectively, these findings indicate that multi-target interventions addressing different pathological aspects, or combinations of various neuroprotective agents, represent a promising future direction for stroke therapy.

  Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, all experiments were conducted exclusively in young adult male mice. While this approach minimizes confounding variables such as estrous cycle fluctuations, substantial evidence documents profound sex dimorphism and age dependency in both microglial responses and stroke outcomes—including the well-established neuroprotective effects of estrogen and the profound impact of aging on microglial function. Therefore, the neuroprotective efficacy of NCAM1 requires further validation in both female and aged animal models. Second, this study did not interrogate the impact of NCAM1 on post-stroke microglial heterogeneity. Single-cell transcriptomic studies have revealed that ischemic microglia transition through distinct functional states, ranging from pro-inflammatory to repair-associated phenotypes. Whether NCAM1 selectively modulates specific microglial subpopulations warrants in-depth investigation using single-cell or spatial transcriptomic approaches. Third, regarding the molecular mechanism, the precise structural basis of the NCAM1–TRIM67 interaction remains unresolved. Future studies should combine surface plasmon resonance with systematic site-directed mutagenesis (targeting the NCAM1 cytoplasmic domain and the TRIM67 C‑coil domain) to define the binding interface, which could inform the rational design of competitive peptides or small-molecule disruptors. Fourth, although K21/K22 were identified as the primary ubiquitination sites on IκBα, comprehensive mapping of all lysine residues under NCAM1–TRIM67 regulation is still lacking. Quantitative ubiquitin-remnant mass spectrometry (diGly proteomics) should be employed to systematically profile the ubiquitination landscape of IκBα, revealing potential hierarchical site usage and cooperative modifications. Finally, with respect to therapeutic translation, the long-term safety, off-target effects, and optimal therapeutic window of the candidate compound DB07993 remain to be established. In particular, it is unknown whether sustained activation of NCAM1 signaling may chronically impair physiological microglial functions—such as synaptic pruning, neurotrophic support, or immune surveillance—or perturb other cellular processes. Comprehensive preclinical safety pharmacology assessments are urgently needed. Moreover, determining the precise time window (hours to days post-stroke) during which NCAM1 agonism confers maximal benefit with minimal risk will be critical for future clinical application. Addressing these limitations in future studies will be essential to advance NCAM1-targeted therapies toward clinical translation.

  In conclusion, our study establishes microglial NCAM1 as a key negative regulator of post-stroke neuroinflammation and delineates the molecular mechanism by which it suppresses the NF-κB pathway via the NCAM1-TRIM67-IκBα axis. Furthermore, the identified agonist DB07993 demonstrates the therapeutic potential of targeting NCAM1. These findings not only deepen the understanding of endogenous regulatory mechanisms in post-stroke neuroinflammation but also lay a solid foundation for developing novel stroke treatment strategies centered on NCAM1. Future studies should focus on exploring the role of NCAM1 in additional pathological processes and advancing its agonists toward clinical translation.
