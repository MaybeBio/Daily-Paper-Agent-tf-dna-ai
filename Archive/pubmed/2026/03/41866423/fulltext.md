# Identification of key genes in the pathogenesis of hepatic ischemia-reperfusion injury based on bioinformatics and experimental verification


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45495-0.


## Introduction
  Hepatic ischemia-reperfusion injury (HIRI) represents a complex challenge in the field of liver surgery, marked by exacerbation of hepatocyte injury and inflammatory responses upon reperfusion following acute ischemia1. The absence of clinically validated therapeutic interventions for HIRI underscores the critical need for further investigation into its underlying mechanisms2. HIRI arises from a complex interplay of multifactorial mechanisms, which can be divided into three sequential phases: (1) the ischemic injury phase, (2) the reperfusion-triggered inflammatory cascade phase, and (3) the tissue repair phase3. During the acute ischemia phase, oxygen and energy deprivation result in mitochondrial dysfunction and ATP depletion4. Energy depletion-induced dysfunction of membrane ion transport proteins (e.g., Ca²⁺-ATPase) disrupts intracellular ionic homeostasis, leading to pathological calcium influx (calcium overload)5. This process triggers the activation of multiple damage-associated enzymes, culminating in organelle dysfunction and subsequent hepatic cell injury or death6.

  Following the restoration of hepatic inflow, oxygen, nutrients, and inflammatory cells flood into the liver. The reintroduction of oxygen reacts with accumulated reducing agents (e.g., nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate, NADPH) in ischemic cells, producing excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS)7. ROS can compromise cell membrane integrity, inactivate key enzymes to disrupt metabolic pathways, and induce DNA damage8. On the other hand, damaged cells release damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which in turn trigger NF-κB and related pathways in monocytes/macrophages, driving the secretion of inflammatory cytokines9. These cytokines subsequently recruit and activate additional macrophages, neutrophils, and other inflammatory cells, thereby triggering an intrahepatic ‘cytokine storm’ that ultimately gives rise to more severe liver damage than that caused by ischemia alone10. Owing to the liver’s remarkable regenerative potential, the majority of HIRI exhibit spontaneous resolution provided that the ischemic duration is short and ischemic area is limited. Research targeting the regulatory mechanisms of HIRI at different stages may provide critical theoretical insights for the clinical management of this challenging postoperative complication.

  In laboratory HIRI research, the partial warm ischemia model in mice has been extensively employed due to its physiological and pathological similarities to human HIRI processes11. Typically, the portal triad entering the left lobe and middle lobe of the liver (accounting for approximately 70% of the total liver volume‌) is occluded with a clamp or ligation. After a predetermined duration (e.g., 60–90 min) of warm ischemia, the blood supply is restored and the animals are sacrificed at different post-reperfusion time points (e.g., 6–24 h) based on research objectives[12]. With this model, studies identified several signaling pathways and molecules involved in HIRI12, yet the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely elucidated, particularly the critical regulatory genes involved in the transition between different stages of HIRI.

  To investigate key differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across various stages of HIRI, our study employed the mouse HIRI model and performed whole-transcriptome sequencing with liver tissues sampled at sequential HIRI stages.

  Candidate genes were first screened through differential analyses, intersection analyses and protein-protein interaction (PPI), followed by further prioritization via machine learning algorithms. Subsequently, based on the key genes, analyses including expression trend analysis, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), immune infiltration analysis, molecular regulatory network construction, and drug prediction were conducted. Then key genes were verified in mouse HIRI model, which aims to provide theoretical basis for clinical practice.


## Materials and methods

### Animals
  C57BL/6J mice (specific pathogen-free) were purchased from Beijing HFK Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. All experimental and control mice were 8-to10- week-old males with body weights ranging from 20 to 25 g. The animals were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions at the 21–23 °C with 12-hour light/dark cycle and provided unlimited access to food and water.


### Mouse HIRI model
  The data in this study were obtained from self-sequenced samples of a mouse HIRI model, comprising 32 specimens divided into 4 groups based on distinct HIRI phases.

  The HIRI model was established as described before13. Anesthesia was induced in an induction chamber with 3% isoflurane in 100% oxygen at a flow rate of 1.0 L/min for approximately 1–3 min. Then, mice were transferred to a nose cone and anesthesia was maintained with 1.5–2% isoflurane in oxygen throughout the procedure. After administration of anesthesia, a midline abdominal incision was performed. Arterial/portal venous blood supply to the left and median liver lobes (accounting for approximately 70% of the total liver volume‌) was interrupted using non-injurious blood vessel clip. After 1-hour warm ischemia, blood supply was restored by removing the clip. The control group (8 mice) received anesthesia and laparotomy only. Eight mice underwent 1 h of ischemia and were sacrificed without restoring the blood supply (0 h group). This phase represents the period before reperfusion begins, during which liver injury is relatively mild and is solely caused by ischemia without involving reperfusion factors. Eight mice underwent 1 h of hepatic inflow occlusion followed by 6 h of reperfusion (6 h group), a time point corresponding to peak liver injury severity and inflammatory response. The final 8 mice underwent 1 h of portal triad occlusion, after which the blood supply was restored and maintained for 24 h (24 h group). This group represents the recovery phase of HIRI, with significantly reduced tissue damage and attenuated inflammatory response compared to the 6 h group. At predetermined time after reperfusion, animals were euthanized by inhalation of isoflurane followed by cervical dislocation.


### Analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)
  At 6 h after reperfusion (the 6 h group), hepatic injury and inflammatory responses were most severe, corresponding to the clinical peak of postoperative liver dysfunction; therefore, we focused on this stage of HIRI. Three comparative analyses were performed: group 1 (6 h vs. normal), group 2 (6 h vs. 0 h), and group 3 (6 h vs. 24 h), with the aim of identifying regulatory genes active during the reperfusion phase. The “DESeq2” package (v 1.40.2) was used to separately screen the corresponding DEGs for the three groups14, namely DEGs1 for group 1, DEGs2 for group 2, and DEGs3 for group 3. The screening criteria were set as |log2foldchange (FC)| > 1 and adjusted (adj) p < 0.05. Subsequently, the study used the “ggplot2” package (v 3.5.2) to generate volcano plots of DEGs for each group, and labeled the top 10 upregulated and downregulated genes in each group based on adj p15. Meanwhile, using the “ComplexHeatmap” package (v 2.16.0), we graphically depict the expression patterns of the top 10 genes showing the highest upregulation and downregulation in each group according to adj p16.


### Screening of candidate genes
  The “ggvenn” package (v 0.1.10) was employed to intersect the upregulated genes from DEGs1, DEGs2, and DEGs3, resulting in the acquisition of commonly upregulated genes17. Secondly, the intersection of the downregulated genes among DEGs1, DEGs2, and DEGs3 was calculated to obtain commonly downregulated genes. Finally, the union of the commonly upregulated genes and commonly downregulated genes was taken, thereby obtaining the candidate genes.

  To explore the biological roles and signaling mechanisms associated with the identified genes, enrichment analyses for Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) were conducted using the “clusterProfiler” package (version 4.8.1) with a significance threshold of p < 0.0518,19. The GO analysis covered three major categories—biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF)—and the outcomes were illustrated through the “ggplot2” package (version 3.5.2).

  In addition, to investigate potential protein-level interactions among the candidate genes, their encoded proteins were analyzed using the STRING database (http://string-db.org), applying a minimum confidence cutoff of 0.15. The resulting data were then used to generate a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network. Data visualization was accomplished with the “circlize” software (v0.4.16)20. Subsequently, the identification of key candidate genes was further performed using the CytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape (v3.10.2)21. Three ranking algorithms—maximal clique centrality (MCC), edge percolated component (EPC), and closeness—were applied to determine the top 30 hub genes. Meanwhile, Cytoscape software (v 3.10.2) was used to draw the PPI network diagram of these 30 candidate genes. The “ggvenn” package (v0.1.10) was applied to identify the overlapping genes among the top 30 candidates derived from the MCC, EPC, and closeness algorithms. These overlapping genes were subsequently designated as candidate key genes.


### Identification of key genes
  The “glmnet” package (v4.1.8) was applied to conduct Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression analysis as an initial step to determine potential feature genes22. One approach involved the adoption of five-fold cross-validation, with characteristic genes 1 obtained by screening variables associated with the optimal λ value. In the Support Vector Machine Recursive Feature Elimination (SVM-RFE) analysis, the study used the “caret” package (v 6.0.94) to conduct five-fold cross-validation, and characteristic genes 2 were screened based on the highest accuracy rate23. Subsequently, the “Boruta” package (v 8.0.0) was used to evaluate the feature importance of the candidate key genes24. This method was based on the iterative comparison between randomly generated shadow features and original features: through a cyclic process, the highest importance of shadow features was set as the benchmark, and genes in the original features with significantly higher importance than the benchmark were retained, which were finally included as characteristic genes 3. Using the “ggvenn” package (v0.1.10), intersections of the characteristic genes derived from the three analytical methods were computed, with the overlapping ones defined as key genes.

  To visualize the expression distribution of key genes across the normal, 0 h, 6 h, and 24 h groups, box plots were generated using the “ggplot2” package (v3.5.2) to depict their expression patterns among the four sample sets.

  To evaluate feature selection robustness, we applied 100-iteration bootstrap resampling across LASSO, SVM-RFE, and Boruta algorithms, defining highly stable features by a selection frequency threshold of 0.70. Furthermore, a permutation test was conducted by shuffling sample labels to generate a null distribution, allowing for the calculation of an empirical P value to ensure the Boruta model’s performance exceeded random expectation.


### Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)
  Spearman correlations between the key genes and the remaining genes were computed for the 0 h and 6 h (group 2) sequencing samples using the “psych” package (v2.5.3)25. The ranked correlation results were then subjected to GSEA through the “clusterProfiler” package (v4.8.1) to uncover enriched biological pathways.

  The gene set file m5.go.v2024.1.Mm.symbols.gmt was obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB; http://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb) and used as the reference background for enrichment analysis. Significant enrichment was determined using thresholds of p < 0.05 and |Normalized Enrichment Score (NES)| > 1, and the top five pathways ranked by p-value were visualized.


### Immune infiltration analysis
  Since reperfusion recruits immune cells and provokes inflammatory responses, we specifically focused on the immune events that differ between the reperfusion and pre-perfusion phases. Sequencing data from the 0 h and 6 h samples (group 2) were analyzed using single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) via the “GSVA” package (v1.48.2) to estimate the relative infiltration of 36 immune cell populations between the two groups26. The “pheatmap” package (v 1.0.12) was then used to generate and display heatmaps of these results27. To screen for differential immune cells between the 0 h and 6 h groups, the R package “rstatix” (v 0.7.2) was utilized to conduct Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for comparing differences in immune cell infiltration between the two groups of samples (with p < 0.05 as the threshold)28. Spearman correlation analysis was conducted using the “psych” package (v2.5.3) to assess relationships both among differential immune cells and between these cells and key genes. Correlations with |cor| > 0.3 and p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.


### Molecular regulatory network
  To explore the regulatory mechanisms of the key genes, this study first used the MicroCosm database (http://www.ebi.ac.uk/enright-srv/microcosm/htdocs/targets/v5) to predict the microRNAs (miRNAs) targeting the key genes, so as to identify their upstream regulatory factors. Next, the long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) upstream of the target miRNAs were obtained from the miRecords database (http://c1.accurascience.com/miRecords). Subsequently, Cytoscape software (v 3.10.2) was used to integrate the above data and construct a molecular regulatory network containing lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA, thereby systematically analyzing the regulatory mechanisms of the key genes. To further investigate the influence of transcription factors (TFs) on the key genes at the transcriptional level, the encyclopedia of DNA Elements (ENCODE) plugin in the NetworkAnalyst online platform (https://www.networkanalyst.ca/) was used to predict the TFs involved in the regulation of the key genes. Then, Cytoscape software (v 3.10.2) was used to construct the TF-key gene network.


### Potential drug exploration
  To explore potential drugs for treating HIRI, the study first converted mouse genes into their human homologous genes using the “babelgene” package (v 22.9)29. Drugs targeting key genes were then predicted through the Drug Signatures Database (DSIGDB) in Enrichr (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/). Subsequently, the drugs were imported into Cytoscape software (v 3.10.2) for visualization processing.


### External validation in human HIRI-related GEO datasets
  To enhance clinical relevance, Adh4, Akr1c14, and Cxcl1 were validated using independent human HIRI datasets (GSE151648, GSE87487, and GSE112713) from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/; accessed on 05/02/2025). Normalized expression data were analyzed using the limma package in R (v3.54.2), with significance set at an FDR < 0.05. Expression patterns were visualized via boxplots, while datasets with incompatible platform annotations or missing probe information for target genes were excluded.


### Reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction
  Total RNA was extracted from liver tissues and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the reverse transcription kit following the instructions of the manufacturer (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). RT-qPCR was perfoemed on QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR system (ABI) with ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) to evaluate the key genes expression. Relative gene expression levels were calculated with the equation of ΔΔCT.


### Protein immunoblotting
  Liver tissues were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (MedChemExpress, New Jersey, USA).

  Following separation by gel electrophoresis, the total proteins were electroblotted onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. To minimize non-specific binding, the membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 60 min at room temperature (RT). Subsequently, primary antibodies targeting the proteins of interest were applied, and the membranes were incubated at 4 °C overnight. After three washes with Tris-buffered saline containing Tween-20 (TBST), the membranes were exposed to species-specific secondary antibodies for a 60-minute incubation period. Target protein bands were visualized using ChemiScope3400 imaging system (Clinx, Shanghai, China) following enhanced chemiluminescence.


### Measurement of transaminase and cytokine levels
  Mouse serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate transaminase (AST) levels were measured by Hitachi 7020 automatic analyzer (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). The concentrations of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in serum or cell culture supernatants were quantified using commercially available ELISA kits (Dakewe Bioengineering, Shenzhen, China) following the manufacturer’s protocols.


### Histopathological evaluation of liver tissue
  Following euthanasia, liver specimens were collected and preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde solution for subsequent histological analysis. H&E staining was performed on paraffin-embedded liver Sect. (5 μm thickness). Blinded assessment of HIRI severity was conducted by two independent evaluators, employing the Suzuki scoring system30. For immunofluorescence staining, liver paraffin sections underwent deparaffinization, antibody blocking and then exposed to anti-Ly6G primary antibody (ABconal, Wuhan, China) for 16 h at 4 °C. Subsequently, fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies were incubated for 1 h at RT. Nuclear visualization was achieved using DAPI staining. Images were captured using a Zeiss LSM710 confocal microscope and evaluated with ImageJ software. Quantitation was based on the number of positive cells / total nuclei.


### Statistical analysis
  GraphPad Prism 8 software was used to conduct the unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test, Wilcoxon rank-sum test. A significance threshold of P < 0.05 was applied to all analyses. Statistical significance was denoted as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001.


## Results

### Comprehensive identification of DEGs
  In the comparison between the 6-hour experimental group and the normal control group, a total of 238 DEGs were detected. Among these, 117 genes showed increased expression levels, while 121 exhibited decreased expression in the 6-hour experimental group (Fig. 1A-B). Between the 6-hour group and the non-reperfusion (0-hour) group, 583 DEGs were identified, with 259 showing increased expression and 324 showing decreased expression in the 6-hour condition (Fig. 1C-D). Additionally, 287 DEGs were found when comparing the 6-hour group to the 24-hour group, consisting of 134 genes that were upregulated and 153 that were downregulated at 6 h (Fig. 1E-F). These differences were considered significant based on criteria of |log2 fold change| > 1 and adjusted p-value < 0.05.

  By intersecting DEGs1, DEGs2, and DEGs3, we obtained 60 commonly upregulated genes and 55 commonly downregulated genes. Subsequently, the union of these 60 commonly upregulated genes and 55 commonly downregulated genes was taken, yielding 115 candidate genes (Fig. 1G-H) (Supplementary Table 1).

### Screening and expression trend analysis of key genes
  The results of LASSO showed that when the lambda value was 0.008220842, the model performance reached the optimal state with the smallest error, and a total of 18 characteristic genes 1 were screened out, namely Fgb, Fga, Fgg, Serpina3n, Serpina3m, Serpina10, Mlxipl, Akr1c14, Klkb1, Tgm2, Gck, Hsd3b3, Igfals, Akr1d1, Cxcl1, Adh4, Nr0b2, and Serpine1 (Fig. 3A-B). The results of SVM-RFE analysis showed that when the model accuracy was 1, the model achieved the highest prediction accuracy, and thus 3 characteristic genes 2 were screened out, including Adh4, Akr1c14, and Cxcl1 (Fig. 3C). The Boruta algorithm showed that the importance scores of 23 characteristic genes 3 were significantly higher than the baseline, including Mat1a, Nr0b2, Fgg, Fga, Adh4, Srebf1, Serpine1, Cxcl1, Hsd3b5, Cdkn1a, Serpina3m, Hsd3b3, Fgb, Klkb1, Igfals, Plin2, Tgm2, Akr1d1, Mlxipl, Serpina3n, Akr1c14, Serpina10, and Cebpd (Fig. 3D). Subsequently, the intersection of characteristic genes 1, characteristic genes 2, and characteristic genes 3 was taken, and 3 key genes were obtained, namely Adh4, Akr1c14, and Cxcl1 (Fig. 3E). The expression trend analysis showed that the expressions of Adh4 and Akr1c14 were the lowest at 6 h, while the expression of Cxcl1 was the highest at 6 h. The expression trend aligns with the progression of HIRI, both reaching the maximum change at 6 h post-reperfusion (Fig. 3F).

  The 100-iteration bootstrap analysis showed that both LASSO and Boruta demonstrated high stability, with the three key genes reaching mean selection frequencies of 0.955 and 1.000, respectively, while SVM-RFE was excluded due to an extreme selection pattern in this small-sample setting (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Further sensitivity analysis using the Jaccard similarity coefficient (Supplementary Fig. 2B-C) confirmed that the LASSO model was robust to hyperparameter variations (α = 0.4–0.8), consistently retaining Akr1c14, Cxcl1, and Adh4 with similarity scores ≥ 0.72. Additionally, a permutation test (Supplementary Fig. 2D) validated that Boruta’s performance significantly exceeded random expectation (P = 0.020), ensuring the selected features were not driven by noise.

### GSEA pathway enrichment of key genes
  GSEA results showed that Adh4 was enriched in 1030 pathways, and the top five pathways were mitochondrial protein containing complex, inner mitochondrial membrane protein complex, aerobic respiration, energy derivation by oxidation of organic compounds, and cellular respiration (Fig. 4A). Akr1c14 was enriched in 1073 pathways, and the top five pathways were mitochondrial protein containing complex, fatty acid metabolic process, ribonucleoprotein complex biogenesis, small molecule catabolic process, and aerobic respiration (Fig. 4B). Cxcl1 was associated with 1118 significantly enriched pathways, with the top pathways including ribonucleoprotein complex biogenesis, mitochondrial protein containing complex, ribosome biogenesis, messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA) processing, and aerobic respiration (p < 0.05 and |NES| > 1) (Fig. 4C) (Supplementary Table 3). GSEA results indicated that samples with higher expression of Adh4, Akr1c14, and Cxcl1 were characterized by transcriptional enrichment of mitochondria-related and aerobic respiration pathways, as well as pathways involved in fatty acid metabolism, ribonucleoprotein complex biogenesis, and mRNA processing. These findings suggest that the expression of these genes is associated with transcriptional programs related to cellular energy metabolism, substance synthesis, and metabolic regulation, thereby providing preliminary clues for further investigation of their potential roles in HIRI pathology.

### Immune cell infiltration and key gene correlations in HIRI
  Immune cell infiltration analysis revealed that the infiltration levels of most immune cells were relatively high in both the 0 h and 6 h groups, such as naive CD4+ T cells and neutrophils (Fig. 5A). There were 13 differentially infiltrated immune cells between the two groups, namely B1 cells, basophils, CD8-positive cytotoxic T cells (CD8+ Tc cells), CD8-positive exhausted T cells (CD8+ Tex cells), follicular B cells, germinal center B cells, granulocytes, naive CD4+ T cells, neutrophils, plasma cells, T helper cells, type 2 conventional dendritic cells (cDC2 cells), and mast cells (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5B). Spearman correlation analysis found that follicular B cells were significantly positively correlated with B1 cells (cor = 0.94, p = 4.22 × 10− 6), whereas granulocytes exhibited a significant negative correlation with CD8+ Tex cells (cor = -0.541, p = 0.02) (Fig. 5C). The key gene Cxcl1 showed a significant positive correlation with granulocytes (cor = 0.821, p < 0.05), and the crucial gene Akr1c14 demonstrated a significant positive correlation with basophils (cor = -0.84, p = 7.65 × 10− 6) (Fig. 5D) (Supplementary Table 4). The observed variations in immune cell distribution and the identified associations among key genes and immune components offer critical insights into characterizing the immune landscape of HIRI, pinpointing potential immunomodulatory targets, and suggesting how these key genes contribute to the immune response during HIRI.

### Molecular regulatory network & drug prediction for key genes
  In the molecular regulatory network analysis, significant differences were observed in the prediction of regulatory factors for key genes: nine regulatory miRNAs, such as mmu-let-7i-5p and mmu-let-7b-5p, were predicted for Adh4, while only one regulatory miRNA, mmu-miR-216a-5p, was predicted for Cxcl1. Meanwhile, a total of 16 lncRNAs including SOCS1, TLR4, and CCND2 were predicted in the network (Fig. 6A). In terms of TFs, two regulatory TFs, namely Jun and NFKB1, were predicted for Cxcl1, whereas no relevant TFs were predicted for Adh4 and Akr1c14 (Fig. 6B). These results clearly demonstrated the differences in regulatory patterns among different genes: Adh4 was more dependent on miRNA regulation, Cxcl1 was co-regulated by miRNAs and TFs, Adh4 and Akr1c14 might not have relied on transcription factor regulation, and lncRNAs might be involved in the overall network regulation. This provided a core direction for subsequent analysis of gene regulatory mechanisms and screening of key targets.

  In this study, the drug prediction results for the key genes Adh4, Akr1c14, and Cxcl1 had important research value and application potential. From the perspective of differences in the number of predicted drugs, Adh4 obtained 19 potential drugs, Cxcl1 obtained 108 potential drugs, while Akr1c14 predicted no drugs. This difference not only reflected the distinction in drug targeting among different genes—Cxcl1, due to the abundant number of potential drugs, might become a key candidate target for subsequent drug development or screening, and Adh4 could also serve as a supplementary target to provide research directions—but also suggested that Akr1c14 might play a role in non-pharmacological regulatory pathways, which broadened the thinking for exploring the regulatory mechanism of this gene. Particularly importantly, Adh4 and Cxcl1 jointly predicted chemical components such as formaldehyde and copper. This indicated that these chemical components might exert a synergistic regulatory effect by acting on these two key genes, provided crucial clues for subsequent research on combined intervention and revelation of their common regulatory mechanisms in pathological processes, and also opened up new research directions for exploring novel prevention and treatment strategies for related diseases (Fig. 6C) (Supplementary Table 5).

### Validation of 3 key genes expression in mouse HIRI model and human HIRI-related datasets
  According to the results of bioinformatics analysis, 3 key differentially expressed genes were further verified at both transcriptional and translational levels in liver tissues subjected to 1 h ischemia without reperfusion-I/R(1/0 h) and 1 h ischemia followed by 6 h reperfusion-I/R(1/6 h). RT-qPCR analysis revealed that, relative to the I/R(1/0 h) group, the transcript levels of Adh4 and Akr1c14 were markedly reduced in the I/R(1/6 h) group, while the transcript level of Cxcl1 was markedly higher (Fig. 7A). Then, we assessed the protein levels of Adh4, Akr1c14 and Cxcl1 by Western blot. As shown in Fig. 7B,C, the trend of protein expression of 3 key genes was consistent with the qPCR results.

  External validation of ADH4, AKR1C14, and CXCL1 across three human HIRI datasets (GSE151648, GSE87487, and GSE112713) yielded mixed results. In GSE151648, no significant expression differences were observed (Supplementary Fig. 1A), while GSE87487 showed a directionally consistent but non-significant upregulation of CXCL1 (logFC = − 0.35, FDR = 0.887) (Supplementary Fig. 1B). GSE112713 was excluded due to data limitations. These findings underscore the potential heterogeneity of clinical HIRI and the necessity for validation in larger, more robust patient cohorts.

### Inhibition of Cxcl1 by ladarixin alleviates HIRI
  Among the 3 key differentially expressed genes, only Cxcl1 exhibited elevated expression at the time point when HIRI reached its maximum severity and its pro-inflammatory role has been widely reported7,31. Additionally, ladarixin, the inhibitor of CXCR1/CXCR2, has advanced to Phase III clinical trials for the indication of type 1 diabetes (NCT04628481). Therefore, it is worthwhile validating the therapeutic potential of Cxcl1 inhibition via ladarixin in our HIRI model. Ladarixin (20 mg/kg) or saline were administered intraperitoneally 2 h prior to operation and at the time of reperfusion. Liver injury and inflammatory responses were evaluated after 1-hour ischemia and 6-hour reperfusion(I/R-1/6 h). Notably, ladarixin administration led to a significant decline in serum alanine aminotransferase and aspartate aminotransferase levels when compared with vehicle control (Fig. 8A). In addition, the increase in serum inflammatory factors triggered by I/R was significantly suppressed in mice treated with ladarixin (Fig. 8B). Consistent with serological examination, H&E staining demonstrated significantly reduced necrotic areas in liver tissues of ladarixin-treated mice, corresponding to improved Suzuki histological scores (Fig. 8C-E). Furthermore, immunofluorescent staining revealed less accumulation of neutrophils (Ly6G-positive) in the liver tissue of ladarixin-treated mice (Fig. 8F-G). Overall, these data suggested that targeting Cxcl1 could be a potential therapeutic approach for HIRI.

## Discussion
  HIRI, a prevalent risk factor in hepatic surgery, may result in postoperative liver dysfunction, biliary tract complications and extended hospital stays32. Alleviating HIRI could not only improve surgical outcomes but also facilitate the utilization of marginal liver graft33. Despite its clinical significance, there are no specific therapeutic options for HIRI, and management primarily relies on symptomatic and supportive treatment2. HIRI is a multi-step pathological process with various factors involved, yet its underlying regulatory mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated. Identifying potential therapeutic targets for HIRI is the primary objective of recent studies. Thus, in our study, through whole-transcriptome sequencing of liver specimens sampled at different HIRI stages (ischemic phase, reperfusion phase, and recovery phase) combined with differential expression analysis, a total of 115 candidate genes related to the HIRI pathological process were obtained. Through the synergistic application of LASSO, SVM-RFE, and Boruta machine learning models, we identified Adh4, Akr1c14, and Cxcl1 as critical regulators in HIRI progression. This multi-algorithm validation approach enhanced the reliability of the findings.

  Adh4 is primarily expressed in the liver, encoding alcohol dehydrogenase 4. As a member of the short-chain alcohol dehydrogenase family, its main function is to catalyze the dehydrogenation of various substrates, including fatty alcohols, steroids, and lipid peroxidation products, playing a significant role in hepatic lipid metabolism and energy metabolism34. As far as we know, the role of Adh4 in HIRI remains unreported, with limited studies documenting its functions in other pathological conditions. Under hypoxic conditions, HIF-1α promotes the expression of Adh4, which enhances the metabolic efficiency of aerobic respiration-related substrates to assist in maintaining mitochondrial energy production35. In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), Adh4 expression is significantly reduced compared to normal liver tissue, making it a potential biomarker for HCC diagnosis and prognosis36. These studies suggest that pathological conditions can alter Adh4 expression in the liver.

  Our research revealed that in HIRI, Adh4 was transiently upregulated during the ischemic phase but sharply declined to its nadir after 6-hour reperfusion. GSEA analysis showed that Adh4 expression was significantly associated with pathways related to mitochondrial protein complexes, cellular respiration, aerobic respiration, and inner mitochondrial membrane organization, suggesting a potential link between Adh4 dysregulation and alterations in cellular energy metabolism during HIRI. This study is, to our knowledge, the first to characterize the dynamic expression pattern and pathway-level associations of Adh4 in HIRI, providing hypothesis-generating evidence linking Adh4 to energy metabolic disturbances during ischemia–reperfusion injury.

  The function of Akr1c14 in HIRI also remains uncharacterized. Under normal conditions, the 3α-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (HSDs) encoded by Akr1c14 plays a critical role in bile acid synthesis in the liver. It works synergistically with steroid 5β-reductase to produce 5β,3α-tetrahydrocholestanes which are essential intermediates in cholanic acid production37. Akr1c14 can also convert excessive lipid peroxidation products (such as 4-hydroxynonenal) into less toxic or non-toxic alcohols, while modulating the ratio of fatty acid metabolic intermediates to reduce the generation of pro-inflammatory lipid mediators, thereby maintaining the stability of the hepatic metabolic microenvironment38. Our study observed that the expression of Akr1c14 slightly decreased during the ischemic phase, then dived to its lowest level at 6 h post-reperfusion when liver injury peaked. The expression of ‌Akr1c14‌ was most significantly associated with fatty acid metabolism, small molecule catabolism and aerobic respiration, while negatively associated with basophil infiltration. Based on its known metabolic functions and the observed expression changes, ‌ischemia-reperfusion associated downregulation of Akr1c14 may be linked to disturbances in lipid metabolic homeostasis and oxidative stress handling. The gradual recovery of Akr1c14 expression during prolonged reperfusion may reflect a compensatory metabolic response during liver injury resolution. Notably, the drug prediction of our study indicated that ‌Akr1c14 has no potential targeted drugs‌, suggesting that it may participate in ‌HIRI through metabolic regulation. These findings highlight Akr1c14 as an underexplored metabolism-associated candidate gene in HIRI and provide a basis for future mechanistic studies.

  Cxcl1 is a well-characterized inflammatory chemokine, which binds to CXCR1/2 to mediate the directional migration of neutrophils and monocyte-macrophages toward injury sites31. Activated inflammatory cells further release enzymes and reactive oxygen species (ROS), inducing the up-regulated expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby triggering an amplification of inflammatory cascade3. Research has demonstrated that in acute hepatic injury and hepatitis models, the dynamic expression of Cxcl1 exhibits a strong correlation with the severity of inflammatory injury39. Elevated Cxcl1 expression recruits additional neutrophils, triggering a ‘cytokine storm’ that exacerbates hepatocyte necrosis and promotes fibrosis, whereas inhibition of the Cxcl1 pathway significantly mitigates hepatic inflammatory injury40. The regulatory role of Cxcl1 in HIRI also has been reported in recent studies. Ye et al. found that hepatic Cxcl1 expression was significantly upregulated after ischemia-reperfusion, with its levels positively correlating with neutrophil infiltration and serum ALT/AST41. Our study further confirmed that expression of Cxcl1 peaks at 6 h post-ischemia-reperfusion. The molecular regulatory network reveals that Cxcl1 expression is under dual control by TFs and miRNAs, suggesting its elevated levels may result from a synergistic effect of transcriptional activation and relief of post-transcriptional gene silencing (PTGS). On the other hand, GSEA analysis showed Cxcl1 was enriched in “ribosome biogenesis” and “mRNA processing” pathways. This suggests that Cxcl1 is not only an “executor” of inflammatory chemotaxis but also plays a role in the post-transcriptional processing of damage-related genes.

  The immune response is a critical pathogenic factor in HIRI, participating in both inflammatory damage and tissue repair6. Our analysis of immune cell infiltration demonstrated significant variations across 13 distinct cell populations (including basophils, granulocytes, and neutrophils) between the ischemic phase (0 h group) and reperfusion phase (6 h group). Notably, Cxcl1 demonstrated the highest correlation with granulocyte and neutrophil infiltration. These findings, consistent with previous reports, highlight the central pro-inflammatory role of innate immunity in the reperfusion phase of HIRI3. Targeting the innate immune response and inflammatory injury in the reperfusion stage presents a promising strategy for alleviating HIRI.

  To investigate the regulatory mechanism of key genes, we predicted lncRNA, miRNAs and TFs modulating HIRI key genes. For Adh4, its regulatory dependence on mmu-let-7i-5p, mmu-let-7b-5p and other miRNAs may present a fine-tuning mechanism for this gene in energy metabolism and mitochondrial function maintenance during HIRI42. Since Adh4 is involved in cellular respiratory substrate metabolism, in response to dynamic changes in oxygen supply and energy metabolism during HIRI, miRNAs can rapidly regulate Adh4 expression levels to adapt to the distinct physiological demands during the ischemic and reperfusion phases. For example, in alcohol-related liver disease, studies have confirmed that miRNAs can regulate the expression of alcohol-metabolizing enzymes such as Adh443. Cxcl1 is co-regulated by miRNAs (e.g., mmu-miR-216a-5p) and TFs (e.g., Jun, NFKB1), positioning it at a complex regulatory crossroad44. Both Jun and NFKB1 are well-recognized key regulators in inflammatory and stress response pathways45. Therefore, we speculate that their synergistic upregulation of Cxcl1 may serve as a critical regulatory mechanism for immune cell recruitment in HIRI.

  In drug prediction analysis, no corresponding drugs were identified for Akr1c14, a small number of drugs were predicted for Adh4, while Cxcl1 was linked to 108 potential drugs. Moreover, the clear association between Cxcl1 up-regulation and inflammatory injury further underscores its potential as a core candidate target for HIRI treatment. Our animal experiment confirmed that ladarixin, a CXCR1/2 antagonist, significantly alleviated HIRI by reducing neutrophil infiltration and inflammatory cytokine secretion.

  This study identified three key genes—Adh4, Akr1c14, and Cxcl1—through bioinformatics screening. Further analyses, including GSEA and immune infiltration studies, explored the potential regulatory mechanisms of these genes, providing a theoretical foundation for HIRI mechanism research and clinical practice. While the study provides valuable insights, it is important to acknowledge its limitations. The identification and experimental validation of critical genes were based solely on mouse models, lacking confirmation from clinical samples to clarify the relevance of the findings to clinical contexts. Future research should include the collection of clinical serum samples from HIRI patients to measure the expression levels of Adh4, Akr1c14, and Cxcl1. These data should be analyzed for their correlation with ALT, AST, and inflammatory factors (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6), in order to evaluate the clinical utility of these key genes as diagnostic biomarkers or potential therapeutic targets for HIRI. Additionally, although ssGSEA offers insights into immune enrichment, it remains an inferred estimation that may underrepresent the rapid, transient immune dynamics (e.g., neutrophil flux) inherent to acute liver injury. In particular, the predicted roles of Adh4 and Akr1c14 are preliminary and require functional validation via gain- or loss-of-function assays in cellular or animal models.


## Conclusions
  The objective of this study was to identify the key genes implicated in HIRI, with a particular emphasis on those associated with the reperfusion phase causing the most severe hepatic injury. Using a mouse HIRI model and whole-transcriptome sequencing, we profiled gene expression across different stages of HIRI. Differential and intersection analyses identified 115 candidate genes, which were further narrowed down to 3 key genes (Adh4, Akr1c14, Cxcl1) through PPI and machine learning screening. GSEA indicated that these genes were significantly associated with multiple biological pathways related to HIRI progression. The results of immune infiltration analysis showed that Cxcl1 were correlated with granulocyte and neutrophil infiltration. Potential regulators such as the long non-coding RNA Socs1, transcription factor NFKB1, and microRNA mmu-let-7b-5p were identified by molecular regulatory network analysis. Additionally, the expression pattern of the key genes was validated with animal model samples. Moreover, inhibition of Cxcl1 demonstrated potential therapeutic effects in alleviating HIRI. In summary, these findings enhance our understanding of HIRI-associated molecular changes and offer promising directions for future therapeutic development. Notably, the underlying mechanisms necessitate further detailed experimental verification.
