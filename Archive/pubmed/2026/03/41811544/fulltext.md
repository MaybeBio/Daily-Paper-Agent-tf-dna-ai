# Deucravacitinib Inhibits Synovial Fibroblast Activation Via TYK2/STAT3 and PI3K/Akt Signaling Pathway


## Background
  Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a systemic autoimmune disease characterized by chronic synovitis, primarily affecting symmetric small joints. Deucravacitinib, a novel oral highly selective tyrosine kinase 2 (TYK2) inhibitor, has shown promise in treating various immune-mediated diseases. This study aimed to explore the therapeutic effects and underlying mechanisms of deucravacitinib in RA.


## Methods
  The anti-proliferative effects of deucravacitinib were assessed using the CCK-8 assay, while its pro-apoptotic activity in MH7A cells was evaluated via flow cytometry. Cell wound healing assay and transwell assays were employed to determine the migration and invasive capabilities of MH7A cells. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was conducted to identify differentially expressed genes following deucravacitinib treatment. Network pharmacology and molecular docking analyses were utilized to predict potential anti-arthritic targets. The impact of deucravacitinib on the TYK2/STAT3 (signal transducer and activator of transcription) and PI3K(phosphoinositide3-kinase)/AKT(protein kinases B) signaling pathways was investigated. In vivo, a collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) mouse model was treated with low-dose deucravacitinib (5 mg/kg/d) for 21 days, and its anti-arthritic effects were evaluated.


## Results
  In vitro, deucravacitinib significantly inhibited MH7A cell proliferation and induced apoptosis, while also reducing cell migration and invasion. Mechanistically, deucravacitinib upregulated Bax (Bcl-2-associated X protein) expression and downregulated Bcl-2 (B-cell lymphoma 2), MMP-2(Matrix Metalloproteinase-2), and MMP-9(Matrix Metalloproteinase-9) mRNA levels, likely through inhibition of the TYK2/STAT3 and PI3K/AKT pathways. In vivo, deucravacitinib alleviated toe swelling, reduced arthritis scores, and decreased mRNA levels of Bcl-2, MMP-2, and MMP-9 in CIA mice. Histopathological analysis revealed improved joint integrity and reduced bone erosion.


## Conclusion
  Deucravacitinib demonstrates significant anti-arthritic potential by inhibiting synovial fibroblast proliferation, migration, and invasion while promoting apoptosis. These effects are mediated through suppression of the TYK2/STAT3 and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways, suggesting its promise as a therapeutic agent for RA.

## Introduction
  Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a systemic, chronic autoimmune disorder characterized by progressive synovial inflammation and bone destruction [1]. The hallmark pathological features of RA include synovial inflammation, hyperplasia, immune cell infiltration, and the progressive destruction of cartilage and bone within the joint synovium, ultimately leading to joint dysfunction and disability [2–4]. Although the precise etiology of RA remains elusive, its pathogenesis involves multiple factors, including genetic predisposition, environmental triggers, and immune dysregulation.

  In the development and progression of RA, multiple cell types are pivotal, including rheumatoid arthritis fibroblast-like synoviocytes (RA-FLS), inflammatory monocytes, polyclonal B cells, and T cells [5, 6]. Among these, RA-FLS are the predominant cell type in the synovial tissue of patients with RA. Activated RA-FLS exhibit hyperproliferation and resistance to apoptosis. They also contribute to cartilage and bone destruction, angiogenesis, and matrix degradation through the secretion of inflammatory cytokines and matrix-degrading enzymes [7, 8]. Consequently, targeting the malignant phenotype of RA-FLS represents a promising therapeutic strategy for RA.

  Deucravacitinib represents a second-generation tyrosine kinase 2 (TYK2) inhibitor which uniquely targets the pseudokinase (JH2) domain via an allosteric mechanism [9, 10]. Unlike conventional ATP-competitive JAK inhibitors, it confers enhanced selectivity (> 100-fold vs. JAK1-3) and mitigates off-target hematologic toxicities, as demonstrated by its FDA approval for plaque psoriasis [11, 12]. The critical involvement of TYK2 in Interleukin (IL)-12/IL-23 signaling—key drivers of synovial Th17 polarization—suggests untapped potential in RA therapeutics, particularly given the limited exploration of TYK2-specific modulation in RA-FLS pathobiology [13–15].

  In this study, we comprehensively investigated the pharmacodynamic profile and molecular circuitry underlying deucravacitinib’s anti-arthritic actions. We used the MH7A cell line as a model for RA-FLS. This well-established, stable in vitro model retains the core pathological phenotypes of primary RA-FLS—including hyperproliferation, resistance to apoptosis, and secretion of inflammatory cytokines and matrix-degrading enzymes—while offering reproducibility and consistency critical for mechanistic studies. Although primary RA-FLS are physiologically relevant, they exhibit high inter-individual variability and limited availability. Combined with a collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) mouse model, we demonstrated that deucravacitinib inhibits the proliferation, invasion, and migration of RA-FLS while promoting apoptosis. Mechanistically, deucravacitinib suppresses the TYK2/STAT3 and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways, thereby reducing the Bcl-2/Bax protein ratio and inhibiting the secretion of MMPs. These findings suggest that deucravacitinib exerts its anti-arthritic effects through anti-inflammatory and anti-proliferative actions on the synovium, thus providing a novel theoretical basis for RA treatment.


## Materials and Methods

### Reagents and Materials
  Deucravacitinib (purity ≥ 99.87%, CAS No. 1609392-27-9, molecular weight 425.46) was obtained from Med Chem Express. A stock solution of 1 mmol/L was prepared by dissolving the compound in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and stored at − 80 °C. Fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin-streptomycin, and Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) were procured from Gibco (Rockville, MD, USA). The FITC Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit I was sourced from Sigma. Primers for Bcl-2, Bax, STAT3, MMP-2, and MMP-9 were synthesized by Abcam (Cambridge, MA). Primary antibodies targeting PI3K, AKT, and β-actin were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China), along with goat anti-mouse and goat anti-rabbit secondary antibodies. Transwell chambers were acquired from Corning (New York, USA). Bovine type II collagen was supplied by Chondrex, Inc. (Redmond, WA, USA), and Freund’s complete adjuvant (CFA) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).


### Cell Culture and Treatment
  The human RA-FLS cell line MH7A was procured from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 50 µg/mL streptomycin. Upon reaching 80%–90% confluency, cells were observed under a microscope and subjected to subsequent experimental procedures. For deucravacitinib treatment, cells were exposed to varying concentrations of the compound for 0, 24, 48, and 72 h.


### Cell Viability Assay
  Cell viability was evaluated using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Beyotime). Briefly, MH7A cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10³ cells/well and allowed to adhere. All experiments were performed with 3 biological replicates, and each biological replicate included 3 technical replicates, which is standard practice for ensuring the reliability and reproducibility of cell viability assay results. Following adherence, cells were treated with deucravacitinib at the indicated concentrations (0, 1, 10, and 20 µM) for 0, 24, 48, and 72 h. Subsequently, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and plates were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.


### 2.4 Flow cytometry apoptosis analysis
  The effect of deucravacitinib on apoptosis was assessed using an Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology). MH7A cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells/well. After 48 h of treatment, cells were harvested, centrifuged, and resuspended in pre-cooled phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Cells were then stained with 5 µL FITC-labeled Annexin V and 5 µL propidium iodide (PI) and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. Apoptosis rates were quantified using a flow cytometer (CytoFlex, Beckman Coulter, USA). Each experiment was performed in triplicate.


### Wound Healing Assay
  A scratch wound-healing assay was employed to evaluate the effect of deucravacitinib on MH7A cell migration. Cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells/well. Upon reaching > 90% confluency, a scratch was introduced using a sterile 200-µL pipette tip, and cell debris was removed by washing with ice-cold PBS. Cells were then treated with deucravacitinib and cultured for 24 and 48 h. Wound closure was monitored under a microscope (Leica), and the percentage of wound healing was quantified using ImageJ software. All experiments were conducted in triplicate and repeated three times.


### Transwell Invasion Assay
  MH7A cells were seeded in 24-well plates and allowed to reach 60% confluency. Cells were then treated with complete medium containing varying concentrations of deucravacitinib for 24 h. Prior to the assay, the upper chamber was coated with Matrigel (BD Biosciences), which was diluted with serum-free medium at a 1:8 ratio on ice. A volume of 40 µL of the Matrigel mixture was added to each upper chamber and allowed to solidify at 37 °C for 4–6 h. After removing the culture medium and washing twice with PBS, cells were trypsinized and resuspended in serum-free medium at a density of 6,000 cells/µL. Subsequently, 200 µL of cell suspension was added to the upper chamber, while 600 µL of medium supplemented with 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber. Following 48 h of incubation, non-migrated cells were removed from the upper chamber. Migrated cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde for 15 min, stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 15 min, and imaged under a microscope. The number of invasive cells was quantified using ImageJ software. Each experiment was performed in triplicate.


### Western Blotting Analysis
  MH7A cells were seeded in 6-well plates and treated with different concentrations of deucravacitinib for 24 h. Cells were lysed using RIPA buffer supplemented with PMSF and phosphatase inhibitors according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Protein concentrations were quantified using a BCA assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% skim milk for 2 h at room temperature, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection kit (Beyotime Biotechnology), and band intensities were analyzed using ImageJ software.


### Reverse Transcription-quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-qPCR)
  Total RNA was extracted from cultured MH7A cells using TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific), Then extracted RNA received reverse transcription utilizing a Prime Script RT kit. The mRNA levels were performed real-time quantitative PCR with SYBR green on Bio-Rad PCR system (Bio‐Rad). The primers of selected target genes employed are listed in Table 1. Gene expression levels were measured by 2−ΔΔCT method and the data were normalized by GAPDH.

### Induction of Collagen-Induced Arthritis (CIA) Mice
  All animal experiments were conducted in strict adherence to the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals issued by the National Institutes of Health (NIH, USA) and the institutional animal care and use regulations of Xi’an Honghui Hospital; the experimental protocol was reviewed and formally approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Xi’an Honghui Hospital (Approval Number: 202312026), and all experimental procedures strictly followed the relevant national and institutional guidelines to minimize animal suffering and ensure the ethical treatment of animals. Male DBA/1 mice were acclimatized for 7 days prior to experimentation, housed in the animal facility of Xi’an Honghui Hospital under controlled environmental conditions (22 ± 2 °C, 50% humidity, 12-hour light/dark cycle) with free access to standard food and drinking water, and then randomly divided into three groups using a random number table (n = 8 per group): control group, collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) group, and CIA + deucravacitinib group, with the sample size (n = 8 per group) determined based on previous similar studies on CIA mouse models (sufficient to detect significant differences in synovial tissue-related indicators among groups with a statistical power of 80% and a significance level of P < 0.05) and blinding procedures strictly implemented throughout the entire experiment (the researcher responsible for animal immunization, drug administration, and sample collection was blinded to the group allocation, while another independent researcher unaware of the group assignments performed subsequent sample processing and analysis to avoid subjective bias). Chicken type II collagen (Chondrex, Inc.) was dissolved in 0.1 mol/L glacial acetic acid to a final concentration of 2 mg/mL, stored at 4 °C overnight, then emulsified with an equal volume of Complete Freund’s Adjuvant (Chondrex, Inc.) to form a homogeneous emulsion; on days 0 and 21 of the experiment, mice in the CIA and CIA + deucravacitinib groups were immunized via intradermal injection of 0.1 mL emulsion at the tail base and back (3–4 injection sites per mouse), and intervention was initiated on day 21 of modeling: the CIA + deucravacitinib group was given 5 mg/kg of deucravacitinib dissolved in 0.5% sodium carboxymethylcellulose by gavage once a day for 28 consecutive days, while the control group and CIA group were given an equal volume of 0.5% sodium carboxymethylcellulose by gavage under the same conditions to exclude the influence of the solvent.


### Measurement ofArthritis Score and Paw Swelling
  The severity of arthritis was evaluated using a visual scoring system ranging from 0 to 4 scale: 0, no erythema or swelling; 1, mild swelling in the small toe joint; 2, swelling and redness in the toe joint and plantar region; 3, severe swelling from the ankle to metatarsal joints; and 4, extensive swelling and redness in all joints, including the ankle. The arthritis score for each mouse was calculated as the sum of the scores for both hind paws.


### Histopathological Assessments
  On day 50, DBA/1 mice were euthanized, and paw tissues were collected, weighed, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h. Tissues were decalcified in 10% EDTA, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. Section  (5 μm thick) were prepared and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) or safranin O/fast green. Images were captured using a light microscope (Nikon).


### Micro-CT and Analysis
  Paw samples were analyzed using a micro-CT device (SKYSCAN 1172). After removing surrounding tissues, paws were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Scanning parameters were set as follows: 90 kV source voltage, 120 µA source current, 0.5 mm aluminum filter, 8 μm pixel size, and 180° rotation step. Following scanning, 3D reconstructions of the paws were generated, and regions of interest were selected for quantitative analysis. Bone mineral density (BMD) and bone volume/tissue volume (BV/TV) were calculated to evaluate bone integrity.


### Bioinformatics Analysis
  Potential targets of deucravacitinib were predicted using the SwissTargetPrediction database (http://swisstargetprediction.ch/). RA-related targets were identified using the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses were performed using the DAVID database (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/). Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed using STRING (http://string-db.org/, version 10). Molecular docking studies were conducted using the COACH-D server (https://yanglab.nankai.edu.cn/COACH-D).


### Statistical Analysis
  Data are expressed as mean ± SD. GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software) was used for thestatistical analysis and visualization (9.5.0). The 2-tailed Student’s t test was applied for comparisons of 2 groups, and 1-way analysis of variance was implemented for 3 or more group comparisons. The value of P < 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.


## Results

### Deucravacitinib Inhibited Aggressive Behavior Characteristic of RASFs
  The cytotoxicity of deucravacitinib on MH7A cells was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. The results demonstrated that deucravacitinib exhibited no toxicity at concentrations of 20 µM or lower (Fig. 1A). Based on these findings, concentrations of 1, 10, and 20 µM were selected for subsequent experiments to evaluate its effects on the biological functions of MH7A cells. To determine the antiproliferative activity of deucravacitinib on rheumatoid arthritis synovial fibroblasts (RASFs), MH7A cells were treated with deucravacitinib for 24, 48, and 72 h, followed by measurement of cell viability using the CCK-8 assay. Deucravacitinib significantly inhibited MH7A cell growth in a concentration- and time-dependent manner. (Fig. 1B). Further investigation revealed that deucravacitinib induced apoptosis in MH7A cells, as evidenced by increased proportions of apoptotic cells in flow cytometry analysis. (Fig. 1C-D). To assess the migration and invasion capabilities of MH7A cells, wound scratch and transwell chamber assays were performed. Wound scratch assay results at 24 h and 48 h showed that deucravacitinib significantly inhibited wound healing compared to the control group, with the most notable effect observed at 48 h (Fig. 1E-F). Similarly, deucravacitinib markedly suppressed MH7A cell invasion in the transwell assay (Fig. 1G-H). Collectively, these findings indicate that deucravacitinib effectively inhibits the aggressive behaviors characteristic of RASFs in vitro.

### RNA-Seq Transcriptome Profiling of Deucravacitinib-treated MH7A Cells
  To explore the molecular mechanisms underlying the response to deucravacitinib, MH7A cells were treated with 20 µM deucravacitinib. Total RNA was then extracted, followed by RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis. The RNA-seq data revealed 2,425 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), comprising 1,277 upregulated and 1,148 downregulated genes. (Fig. 2A). Notably, several genes associated with inflammation, proliferation, and matrix metalloproteinases, such as IL-6, Bcl-2, MMP-2, and MMP-9, were significantly downregulated (Fig. 2A). Functional enrichment analysis was performed using the DAVID tool to identify key KEGG pathways and GO terms associated with the DEGs. The top 20 KEGG pathways (enrichment factor > 0.12) included Rheumatoid arthritis, Wnt signaling, PI3K-AKT signaling, Hippo signaling, IL-17 signaling, ECM-receptor interaction, and Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction (Fig. 2B). Additionally, the top 10 enriched GO terms for biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF) are illustrated in Fig. 3C. BP terms were primarily related to cell adhesion, inflammatory response, and signal transduction. MF terms were associated with calcium ion binding, extracellular matrix structural integrity, and dynein intermediate chain binding. CC terms were primarily found in the extracellular matrix, external plasma membrane, and receptor complexes.

### Deucravacitinib Modulates RA-FLS Function Via TYK2/STAT3 and PI3K/Akt Signaling Pathway
  The molecular structure of deucravacitinib is illustrated in Fig. 3A. Using the GeneCards database and SwissTargetPrediction platform, we identified 4,210 RA-associated target genes and predicted 100 potential binding targets for deucravacitinib. By cross-referencing these targets with transcriptomic data from deucravacitinib-treated MH7A cells, we identified 10 overlapping targets (Fig. 3B). A PPI network was generated using the STRING database and visualized via Cytoscape. This network elucidates the role of deucravacitinib in RA treatment (Fig. 3C). Among the 10 overlapping genes, JAK3 (a key component of the JAK/STAT3 pathway) and PIK3CG (involved in the PI3K pathway) were identified, consistent with the known pharmacological effects of deucravacitinib on the PI3K pathway. Further analysis using the TargetNet platform revealed a strong binding affinity between SBR and JAK1, which was confirmed through molecular docking simulations on the COACH-D platform (Fig. 3D, G). Western blot analysis showed a concentration-dependent decrease in the phosphorylation levels of STAT3 (Fig. 3E-F), PI3K, Akt, and mTOR (Fig. 3H-I). Additionally, downstream proteins linked to the TYK2/STAT3 and PI3K/AKT pathways, which regulate proliferation, apoptosis, and invasion, were analyzed. With increasing concentrations of deucravacitinib, Bax expression was upregulated, while levels of Bcl-2, MMP-2, and MMP-9 were downregulated. These results suggest that deucravacitinib regulates MH7A cell function through both the JAK-STAT and PI3K/Akt signaling pathways (Fig. 3J-N).


### Deucravacitinib Prevents Arthritis Progression and Attenuates Severity in CIA Mice
  To evaluate the in vivo therapeutic potential of deucravacitinib, we established a collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) model in DBA/1 mice. Arthritis scores were recorded every other day beginning on day 1 post-immunization. Prophylactic administration of 5 mg/kg/d deucravacitinib significantly alleviated clinical symptoms, including joint swelling, toe redness, and thickening, accompanied by a marked reduction in arthritis scores (Fig. 4A-B). Micro-CT imaging of ankle joints revealed less joint space narrowing and improved bone integrity in the deucravacitinib-treated group compared to the CIA model group (Fig. 4C-D). Furthermore, qRT-PCR analysis of ankle tissues showed that deucravacitinib significantly suppressed the mRNA expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6), MMPs (MMP-2 and MMP-9), and the proliferation-related gene Bax, while upregulating the expression of the pro-apoptotic gene Bcl-2 compared to the CIA model group (Fig. 4E). Histopathological analysis using H&E and Safranine O/Fast Green staining, as illustrated in Fig. 4F, demonstrated significant pathological alterations in the CIA group based on macroscopic changes. These alterations included pronounced inflammatory cell infiltration, substantial cartilage degradation, and notable bone erosion within the ankle joints, indicative of severe arthritic progression. In comparison to the CIA model group, the deucravacitinib-treated groups exhibited markedly attenuated histopathological abnormalities. Specifically, these groups showed reduced synovial tissue hyperplasia, diminished bone and cartilage erosion, and a significant decrease in inflammatory cell infiltration. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that deucravacitinib exerts a protective effect on joint integrity, effectively mitigating the pathological hallmarks of arthritis, thereby potentially slowing disease progression.

## Discussion
  RA is a chronic, inflammatory, and erosive autoimmune disease primarily affecting synovial joints [16]. The hallmark pathological features of RA include synovial inflammation, hyperplasia, pannus formation, and cartilage and bone destruction, ultimately leading to joint deformity and functional impairment [17–19]. Although the exact etiology of RA remains unclear, RA-FLS are widely recognized as key effector cells driving disease progression. Unlike normal synovial tissue, RA-FLS exhibit defective apoptosis and undergo tumor-like proliferation and transformation, displaying invasive and adhesive properties similar to those observed in malignant cells [20]. RA-FLS secrete various proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) (e.g., MMP-2, MMP-9, and MMP-13), actively contributing to synovial hyperplasia, pannus formation, cartilage degradation, and bone erosion [21–23]. Beyond being downstream targets of the inflammatory milieu, RA-FLS also play an active role in immune regulation by promoting the abnormal activation and differentiation of immune cells (e.g., macrophages, B cells, and T cells), thereby exacerbating disease progression [20, 24, 25]. Consequently, targeted RA-FLS therapy has emerged as a promising strategy for RA treatment.

  The JAK/STAT signaling pathway is closely associated with RA-FLS dysfunction [26]. Dysregulation of this pathway leads to excessive RA-FLS proliferation, contributing to cartilage erosion and bone destruction [27]. The JAK kinase family consists of four members: JAK1, JAK2, JAK3, and TYK2 [28]. Current research primarily focuses on developing selective inhibitors for JAK1, JAK2, and JAK3. TYK2, a member of the JAK family, binds to STATs to initiate gene transcription and translation, promoting the release of inflammatory factors [29]. While the role of TYK2 in systemic lupus erythematosus, allergic asthma, and psoriasis has been extensively studied, its involvement in RA remains underexplored [15, 30]. Deucravacitinib, a small-molecule selective TYK2 inhibitor, exerts its effects by specifically binding to the pseudokinase domain of TYK2, inducing allosteric inhibition rather than interacting with the active site [31]. Although Deucravacitinib has demonstrated favorable efficacy and safety in psoriasis treatment, its application in RA research remains limited.

  In this study, we demonstrated that deucravacitinib inhibits the migration and invasion of RA-FLS while promoting apoptosis in a dose-dependent manner, as demonstrated by scratch and migration assays. To elucidate the underlying mechanisms, we analyzed transcriptome sequencing data and examined protein expression levels in relevant signaling pathways. Our findings revealed that deucravacitinib suppresses the expression of TYK2/STAT3 and PI3K/Akt pathway-related proteins, further reducing the Bcl-2/Bax ratio and promoting RA-FLS apoptosis. MMP-2 and MMP-9, key mediators of cell migration and invasion, degrade extracellular matrix components such as collagen types IV and V, gelatin, and elastin, contributing to cartilage and bone destruction in RA [32]. Deucravacitinib was found to downregulate MMP-2 and MMP-9 expression, mitigating synovitis and bone destruction in a CIA model. These results suggest that deucravacitinib may represent a promising therapeutic option for RA through its anti-inflammatory and anti-proliferative effects on synovial tissue.

  Collectively, our study provides the first evidence that deucravacitinib demonstrates significant anti-arthritic effects in a CIA-induced RA model. Mechanistically, deucravacitinib inhibits RA-FLS proliferation, invasion, and migration and promotes apoptosis. It also suppresses TYK2/STAT3 and PI3K/Akt signaling pathways, thereby reducing the Bcl-2/Bax ratio and MMP release (Fig. 5). However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the effects of deucravacitinib on other cell types involved in RA pathology, such as T cells and the balance between osteoclasts and osteocytes, remain unclear. Secondly, while we examined the prophylactic effects of deucravacitinib in CIA mice, its therapeutic efficacy post-arthritis onset remains unassessed. These limitations highlight the necessity for further investigation to fully elucidate the mechanisms and therapeutic potential of deucravacitinib in RA treatment.
