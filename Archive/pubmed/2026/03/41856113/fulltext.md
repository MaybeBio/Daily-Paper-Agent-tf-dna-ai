# Thermodynamic Prediction of RNA Cellular Activity from Sequence via Conformational Ensembles


## SUMMARY
  Despite advances in structure prediction from sequence, predicting cellular activity requires conformational ensembles that capture propensities to form functionally active states. Such ensembles remain difficult to measure and even harder to predict. Here, we systematically altered the HIV-1 TAR RNA sequence to change its propensity to adopt a functional versus inactive secondary structure and quantified these propensities using 1H CEST NMR without isotopic labeling. Minor sequence changes shifted the active-state propensity by ~500-fold, quantitatively predicting 125—300-fold changes in binding to the RNA-binding region of Tat and cellular transactivation. These propensities could be inferred from secondary-structure prediction algorithms and incorporated into a thermodynamic framework to quantitatively predict how sequence changes alter protein-binding affinity and cellular activity in this well-characterized system. Our findings establish a quantitative thermodynamic framework that links RNA sequence to cellular activity through conformational ensembles, setting the stage for more generalized predictions as computational ensemble modeling continues to advance.


## INTRODUCTION
  Transformative advances now enable the prediction of protein three-dimensional (3D) structures directly from their amino-acid sequences1–3. Yet, the ultimate goal of biomedical science is far more ambitious: to predict cell physiology directly from the sequences of interacting biomolecules. Not only do we want to be able to predict how a sequence variation alters biological outcomes, but we also want to explain why it does so, by modeling its impact on the multitude of steps that govern biochemical and cellular processes. Attaining this objective requires moving beyond static 3D structures to conformational ensembles that describe the probabilities of adopting often rare yet functionally critical conformations, information essential for quantitatively modeling cellular processes4–10. Unlike structure prediction, however, ensemble prediction remains a major unsolved problem1,11,12. A key obstacle is the paucity of experimentally determined ensembles: while breakthroughs in structure prediction were powered by tens of thousands of static structures currently populating the database13, only a handful of dynamic ensembles have been experimentally determined to date6,9,10,14–16.

  RNAs have biophysical properties that make ensemble prediction more tractable than proteins, offering a powerful entry point for this challenge6,17. Because RNA base-pairing patterns often form independently of tertiary contacts18,19, the prediction problem can be approximated into two simpler tasks: estimating propensities for alternative secondary structures, and atomic-level 3D modelling of the sub-ensembles they form. Here, we focus on the first task, predicting RNA secondary structural ensembles directly from sequence, by leveraging high-throughput NMR methods that now allow measurements of these ensembles at a scale previously unattainable. These data have enabled us to quantitatively test ensemble predictions from secondary structural prediction algorithms and to guide their development in the future.

  RNA secondary structural changes pervade biology, acting as molecular switches that sense and respond to cellular cues to regulate gene expression7,20–34 and as fidelity checkpoints guiding ribonucleoprotein folding, assembly, and catalysis35–39. Quantitative modeling of cellular processes requires knowing the propensities of RNAs to adopt alternative secondary structures, since these are key energetic determinants of interactions and the downstream cellular output4–9. Accurate prediction of RNA secondary structural ensembles directly from sequence is thus not only a critical step toward complete ensemble modeling, but also central to understanding and ultimately controlling RNA cellular behavior.

  Despite simplifying the task, predicting RNA secondary structural ensembles from sequence remains challenging. Hundreds of RNA secondary structures have been determined by integrating chemical probing data with computational modeling40–45. However, these methods identify a single conformation, usually assumed to be the most stable state in the ensemble under experimental conditions, that best satisfies the measured reactivities. While recent advances have started to extract ensemble-level information from these measurements7,46–48, as well as from advances in deep learning-based structure prediction algorithms49,50, they remain insensitive to low-populated states, increasingly shown to be drivers of RNA folding and function4,51–54.

  This historical focus on a single dominant conformation has, in turn, shaped the development and evaluation of programs that predict RNA secondary structure from sequence. Most of these algorithms are trained and benchmarked against sparse datasets of melting energetics with much of the focus being on how well they predict the dominant secondary structure55–61 rather than the underlying conformational energetics. Rarely are they tested against what is arguably the more critical benchmark, capturing the thermodynamic propensities of alternative secondary structures, a prerequisite to solving the ensemble prediction problem. The scarcity of such tests reflects the difficulty of determining RNA secondary structural ensembles, especially quantifying lowly-populated short-lived states within a landscape of alternative conformations. These measurements typically require advanced NMR chemical exchange methods62–64 that are technically demanding, slow, and rely on costly 13C/15N-labeled RNA. A single study can take years to yield one ensemble4,53,54,62,63,65, and only a handful have been quantitatively defined62–64, far too few to benchmark prediction programs or assess how sequence-dependent ensemble changes influence cellular function.

  To overcome this limitation, we used proton chemical exchange saturation transfer (1H CEST) NMR66–69, which obviates isotopic enrichment and quantifies exchange kinetics of rare states using unlabeled samples and sensitive 1D 1H experiments (Figure 1A). We focused on the HIV-1 transactivation response element (TAR) RNA70–73 (Figure 1B), a well-characterized system whose ensemble is well-established15,72,74,75, and systematically designed 27 sequence variants with varied propensities for two competing secondary structures, one of which is functionally inactive. Measuring these propensities with 1H CEST produced the first quantitative dataset of secondary structural ensembles across such a broad sequence space for any RNA (Figure 1B), enabling us to benchmark secondary structure prediction algorithms and directly evaluate how sequence changes impact ensemble energetics and function.

  TAR activates transcriptional elongation of the HIV-1 retroviral genome by binding to the viral transactivating protein Tat, which recruits the host super elongation complex (SEC)76–78 to RNA polymerase II stalled downstream of the HIV-1 promoter71,79,80. In addition to the energetically most favorable functionally active ground-state (GS), TAR can adopt an alternative, transcriptionally inactive secondary structure known as ‘ES2’ (Figure 1B).

  ES2 arises from a secondary structure shift that remodels the bulge, upper stem, and apical loop, replacing GS canonical Watson-Crick bps with closely spaced mismatches (Figure 1B). While no functional role is known for ES281, mutations stabilizing ES2 strongly inhibit cellular transactivation81 and promote retroviral genome dimerization82,83. Another state, ES162, forms by locally zipping up the apical loop but retains wt activity81 and will therefore be incorporated into the GS sub-ensemble (Figure 1B).

  In wt TAR, ES2 exists at an NMR-measurable equilibrium population of ~0.4%63 with an energetic gap relative to the GS (ΔG2y = ΔGminor – ΔGmajor) of ~3.3 ± 0.1 kcal/mol, well within the reach of a single-nucleotide substitution. Indeed, point substitution mutations can shift the equilibrium to favor ES2 as the dominant state81. Thus, the TAR ensemble of the active GS and the inactive ES2 provides a powerful system to dissect how sequence variations reshape RNA secondary structural landscapes and how such changes, in turn, drive changes in protein binding affinity and cellular activity.


## RESULTS

### Benchmarking 1H CEST
  We first verified that 1H CEST can robustly measure GS and ES2 propensities across a broad dynamic range (Figure 1C). To do so, we applied the approach to three well-characterized TAR variants: U2, G28U, and TARES2 (Figure 1C), previously shown by conventional 13C/15N off-resonance R1ρ experiments81,82,84 to exhibit >1000-fold differences in the equilibrium population of ES2 (pES2). U2 predominantly adopts the GS, whereas G28U and TARES2 invert the equilibrium and predominantly form ES2, with the GS present as a minor state81,82. The profiles measured using 1H CEST (Figure 1A, see STAR Methods), can be fit to Bloch–McConnell equations85 to yield the exchange rate (kex = kforward + kreverse), minor-state population (pminor), and difference in chemical shift between the minor and major states (Δω = ωminor - ωmajor).

  We selected the U38-H3 imino proton in TAR as the primary 1H CEST probe because it was well-resolved across variants and exhibited a large difference in chemical shift between the GS and ES2 (Δω ~ −2.9 ppm). For all three variants, the U38-H3 1H CEST profiles were well fit by a 2-state exchange model, yielding chemical shift differences between the minor and major states Δω ~ −2.7 to −3.0 ppm, consistent with GS⇌ES2 exchange (Figure 1E). Notably, these chemical shift differences had similar magnitude but opposite sign in G28U and TARES2, consistent with inversion of the conformational equilibrium (Figure 1E).

  The free energy differences between the minor and major states (ΔG2y) and the exchange rates (kex) deduced from the 2-state fits of the 1H CEST profiles were in excellent agreement with those measured independently using 13C/15N R1ρ relaxation dispersion experiments and isotopically labeled RNA samples81,82,84 with average differences of ~0.2 kcal/mol in ΔG2y and kex within experimental error (Figure 1F).

  As negative controls, the U0 and U1 variants, which effectively prevent formation of an ES2-like conformation63, showed no evidence of GS⇌ES2 exchange in their U38-H3 CEST profiles (see STAR Methods, Figure S1A).


### Truncated TAR recapitulates the full-length TAR ensemble
  Our NMR studies employed a truncated 29-nucleotide (nt) TAR construct with a shortened lower stem relative to the cellular context70,81,86–88. To verify that the truncation does not alter the GS⇌ES2 exchange, we also performed 1H CEST NMR measurements for the full-length 57 nt TAR (Figure S1B). The measured ΔG2y and the exchange rate kex was within experimental error of values measured for truncated TAR (Figure 1G and Figure S1C–E).


### Design of TAR library
  Leveraging the throughput of 1H CEST, we measured ΔG2y for 14 additional designed variants, bringing the total to 20 (Figure 2A, and Table S1). Many carried single-nucleotide substitutions previously known to stabilize ES2 as the dominant conformation, along with compensatory secondary mutations that restore the GS81. Such “ensemble cliffs” (Figure 2A) where minor sequence changes drastically reshape the energy landscape, provide powerful test cases for linking sequence-dependent ensemble remodeling to cellular activity and for benchmarking secondary structure prediction programs. Variants were also designed to avoid disrupting contacts with Tat:SEC in the GS and to maintain the utility of U38-H3 as a 1H CEST probe (see STAR Methods).

  The set spans broad perturbations to GS and ES2: eight bulge substitutions, seven changes to Watson-Crick bps, five bulge length alterations (from 0 to 4 nt), four replacements of Watson-Crick bps with mismatches, and one apical loop residue substitution (Figure 2B). This diversity broadens evaluation of RNA secondary structure prediction programs. For 20 variants, the dominant conformation was previously characterized by NMR81,82,87, facilitating 1H CEST measurements of ΔG2y, while the transcriptional activities for 17 were previously measured quantitatively in cells using a reporter assay81,87, allowing direct tests of how conformational propensities relate to cellular function.

  The library also includes natural variants (Figure 2B) predicted to either favor either GS (A22G, U31C, U25A-ΔC24) or ES2 (G28C, A35C, U38C), providing blind tests of predictions discussed below.


### Measuring sequence-specific conformational propensities for TAR library
  We used 1H CEST to measure ΔG2y for the 20 variants using G26-H1, G28-H1, and G36-H1, as probes of GS⇌ES2 exchange75. Besides the U0 and U1 negative controls (Figure S1A), we were unable to measure GS⇌ES2 exchange for U38A due to extensive line-broadening (Figure S2A and see STAR Methods), and for U25A, because 1H CEST profiles did not exhibit a statistically significant exchange contribution (see STAR Methods).

  For the remaining 16 variants, the U38-H3, G26-H1, G28-H1, and G36-H1 1H CEST profiles (see STAR Methods) could be combined in a global 2-state fit yielding chemical shift differences between the minor and major state Δω = ~−2.7 to −3.1 ppm, ~0.1 to 0.3 ppm, and ~−0.8 to −1.1 ppm for U38-H3, G26-H1, and G28-H1 respectively, consistent with GS⇌ES2 exchange (Figure S2D, and Table S2). We independently verified the ES2 minor state for A27U/U38A and C29A/G36U using an apical loop replacement mutation to stabilize ES2 as the dominant conformation75,82 (Figure S4A–C).

  Despite the subtlety of the mutations in our variant library, their impact on RNA ensemble energetics was dramatic: the propensity to form ES2 varied by one million-fold, corresponding to sequence-specific differences in GS⇌ES2 energetics of ΔΔG2y = 8.3 kcal/mol (Figure 2C). These measurements reveal an extraordinary sensitivity of RNA secondary structural landscapes to single-nucleotide changes.

  1H CEST provided rare insights into how the kinetics of RNA secondary structural changes vary across sequence space. The exchange rate kex = kforward + kreverse varied ~30-fold, with kforward and kreverse each varying by ~20,000-fold and ~500-fold, respectively. The rates exhibited a strong linear (kforward; r = 0.9) or inverse linear (kreverse; r = −0.9) correlation with the conformational equilibrium (Kconf = pES2/pGS), showing that mutations alter GS propensities by shifting both rates (Figure 2D). The ~40-fold larger variation in kforward relative to kreverse is consistent with a late transition state89,90 suggesting that the mutations primarily affect GS stability rather than ES2, a point we return to later.


### NMR-derived ΔG2y predict binding affinities to Tat-ARM
  Our secondary structural ensembles, measured for the 20 TAR variants using 1H CEST, provided the first-of-its-kind dataset needed to dissect sequence-ensemble-function relationships. To accurately and quantitatively link sequence to function, it is crucial to recognize that the mutations in our library not only affect the overall propensity to form the active GS secondary structure, but also the propensities to form specific functionally active 3D conformations within the GS sub-ensemble87,91.

  In the absence of Tat:SEC, the TAR GS sub-ensemble (Figure 1B) comprises a broad distribution of conformations with variable inter-helix orientations, bulge residue U23 predominantly intrahelical, and the other bulge residues flipped out and flexible15,82,92. Among these is a high-energy binding-competent conformation specifically recognized by Tat:SEC where the two helices are coaxially stacked18 and U23 forms a U23•A27-U38 base triple72 (Figure 3A) via a U23•A27 reverse Hoogsteen bp93. The Tat arginine-rich motif (ARM) forms key contacts with this stacked base-triple conformation22,23 through arginine forks94,95 and the arginine sandwich motif72,73,96 (Figure 1B) reinforced by Tat/Cyclin-T1 contacts with the TAR apical loop (Figure 1B).

  We previously showed that the functionally active stacked base-triple conformation within the TAR GS sub-ensemble is exceptionally rare (≤0.01%), carrying a sizeable energetic penalty (>5.0 kcal/mol) for binding97. This penalty reflects the combined energetic cost of (i) stacking the helices from a broad inter-helical distribution (ΔGstack) and (ii) forming the base triple starting from the stacked state (ΔGtriple)87 (Figure 3A). Certain sequence changes in our library alter these propensities. For example, increasing the bulge length decreases the stacking propensities, while altering the A27-U38 bp can decrease the propensities to form the U23•A27-U38 base triple87. For productive binding, this penalty must be offset by favorable intermolecular interactions (ΔGint) with Tat:SEC. For a variant i predominantly forming the GS , the binding energetics ΔGbind,GS(i), is given by the sum of these energetic contributions, assumed to be independent87 (Figure 3A):  (1) ΔGbind,GSi=ΔGstacki+ΔGtriplei+ΔGinti=−RTlogpstack+ΔGtriplei+ΔGinti

  In which pstack is the equilibrium population of the stacked state87. This thermodynamic model was originally developed for TAR variants that adopted the functionally active GS secondary structure87. Here, we extended the framework to additionally account for variants in which the alternative ES2 conformation is favored, and the active GS conformation exists only as a minor population. Assuming Tat:SEC preferentially binds these TAR variants in the GS, even when it is less populated than ES2 in the unbound ensemble84, favorable intermolecular contacts must compensate for the energetic cost (see STAR Methods), ΔGconf,GS, to adopt the GS secondary structure87. Assuming these energetic contributions are independent (Figure 3A):  (2) ΔGbind(i)=ΔGconf,GS(i)+ΔGbind,GS(i)=−RTlnpGS(i)+ΔGbind,GS(i)=RTln1+e−ΔG2y(i)/RT+ΔGbind,GS(i)  in which pGS(i) is the equilibrium population of the active GS conformation for variant i. The difference in binding energetics between variant i and wt is given by:  (3) ΔΔGbind(i−wt)=ΔΔGconf,GS(i−wt)+ΔΔGbind,GS(i−wt)=RTln(1+e−ΔG2y(i)/RT1+e−ΔG2y(wt)/RT)+ΔΔGbind,GS(i−wt)

  This model extends our prior framework87 by incorporating not only sequence effects on forming the stacked base-triple 3D motif recognized by Tat:SEC, but also the energetic cost of forming GS secondary structure when the competing ES2 conformation dominates. While only a narrow set of substitutions can alter the 3D motif propensities87,92, our experimental and computational results (see below) demonstrate that secondary structural energetics can be altered by a broader range of sequence changes. Accounting for this broader contribution provides a necessary generalization to quantitatively bridge RNA sequence to cellular function.

  As mentioned earlier, our TAR library was designed to isolate conformational effects and minimize any impact intermolecular contacts with Tat:SEC such that ΔΔGint (i-wt) ~0 kcal/mol. Under this assumption, the difference in binding energetics between two TAR variants is given by their overall propensities to form the active GS stacked base-triple conformation:  (4) ΔΔGbind,GS(i−wt)=ΔΔGstack(i−wt)+ΔΔGtriple(i−wt)ΔΔGbind(i−wt)=ΔΔGstack(i−wt)+ΔΔGtriple(i−wt)+ΔΔGconf,GS(i−wt)=ΔΔGstack(i−wt)+ΔΔGtriple(i−wt)+RTln1+e−ΔG2y(i)/RT1+e−ΔG2y(wt)/RT

  Before applying our model to directly predict cellular activity, we first tested a simpler prediction: the differences in binding energetics (ΔΔGbind) of TAR variants to the Tat-ARM peptide87,98. Although our model assumes that TAR predominantly binds Tat:SEC in the GS, prior work also showed that the truncated Tat-ARM peptide lacking several native contacts does have a propensity to bind TAR in alternative conformations87. As a result, especially in variants where the energetic penalty to adopt the GS is very high, we cannot rule out some degree of competitive binding to ES2. To account for this possibility, we extended the model to allow binding to both GS and ES2 (see STAR Methods).

  We tested predictions from our model for 14 variants for which propensities could be measured by 1H CEST, not including mutations impacting contacts between the apical loop and the Tat-ARM that are not captured by our model73 (see STAR Methods). Even though the TAR mutations in our library preserve intermolecular contacts with Tat-ARM, our model predicts that the Tat-ARM binding affinities will vary ~80-fold across these variants due to the ~500-fold differences in their propensities to adopt the functionally active GS secondary structure, along with the contributions from differences in their propensities to form the stacked and base triple conformations.

  We measured the binding affinities of TAR variants to the Tat-ARM peptide using a FRET-based assay98 (see STAR Methods, Figures 3A, S5A and Table S3). This Tat-ARM peptide was previously demonstrated to serve as a faithful mimic for the active full Tat:SEC complex across a range of TAR variants87. As predicted by our model (see STAR Methods), the binding affinities spanned a broad range (~20-2000 nM), exhibiting ~100-fold variation across the variants (Figure S5B). Remarkably, the ensemble-derived ΔΔGconf,GS values accurately predicted the differences in binding energetics ΔΔGbindwith an RMSE of 0.3 kcal/mol and an R2 of 0.9 (slope = 0.8, intercept = −0.1, Figure 3C). The prediction accuracy deteriorated substantially for variants predominantly forming ES2 from RMSE = 0.3 kcal/mol to RMSE = 1.6 kcal/mol (Figure S5D) when omitting the contribution from the secondary structural ensemble (ΔΔGconf,GS). Supporting this conclusion, three variant pairs carrying compensatory mutations that restored the GS conformation also rescued the wt binding affinity, showing that the effects of the original mutations acted primarily through changes in conformational propensities rather than direct disruption of sequence-specific intermolecular contacts81.

  These results demonstrate that even subtle sequence changes outside of residues directly involved in intermolecular contacts can continuously modulate the intrinsic propensity of RNA to adopt the active secondary structure, thereby altering protein-binding affinities by up to two orders of magnitude.


### NMR-derived ΔG2y predicts cellular transactivation
  We extended our model to quantitatively relate the sequence-dependent secondary structural ensemble of TAR variants to their transcriptional activity in cells. TAR’s primary role during cellular transactivation is to recruit the Tat:SEC complex to the paused RNA polymerase II71,79,80. In prior work87, we decomposed cellular transactivation (Figure 3B) into two major energetic contributions again assumed to be independent: (i) TAR binding to Tat:SEC (ΔGbind) and (ii) all subsequent steps driving transactivation (ΔGtrans) (Figure 3B).

  Our model87 assumed that transactivation is under thermodynamic control with respect to TAR-Tat:SEC and, thus depends on the equilibrium concentration of the active TAR–Tat:SEC complex87 and ΔGtrans is independent of the TAR sequence87. Under these assumptions, an expression for cellular transactivation is given by the sum of these contributions again assumed to be energetically independent:  (5) ΔGcelli=ΔGbindi+ΔGtransi=ΔGbind,GSi+ΔGtrans

  To account for the propensities to adopt the active secondary structure required for transactivation, we incorporated to our model the conformational penalty to form the GS given by ΔGconf,GS=RTln⁡1+e−ΔG2y(i)/RT, assuming energetic independence:  (6) ΔGcelli=ΔGbindi+ΔGtrans=RTln1+e−ΔG2yi/RT+ΔGbind,GSi+ΔGtrans

  The difference in energetics for cellular transactivation between a variant i and wt, is given by:  (7) ΔΔGcell(i−wt)=ΔΔGconf,GS(i−wt)+ΔΔGbind,GS(i−wt)=RTln(1+e−ΔG2y(i)/RT1+e−ΔG2y(wt)/RT)+ΔΔGbind,GS(i−wt)

  Thus, our model predicts that differences in cellular activity (ΔGcell) across the TAR sequence variants will vary continuously as a function of the propensity to form the functionally active GS.

  We applied our model together with the experimentally measured sequence-specific ΔG2y values, to directly predict the cellular transcriptional activity (ΔΔGcell) of 16 TAR variants. These variants are predicted to span an ~800-fold range in cellular activity, driven primarily by ~500-fold differences in their propensities to adopt the functionally active GS secondary structure.

  We tested these predictions using prior measurements81 of ΔGcell obtained using a gene-reporter assay81,87 that measures the fold increase in transcriptional activity for each variant in the presence versus absence of sub-saturating Tat, normalizing the signal to account for transfection efficiency. Strikingly, for the 16 TAR variants, we were able to quantitatively predict the cellular activities (ΔΔGcell) in very good agreement with the experimentally measured values, with RMSE of 0.5 kcal/mol and R2 of 0.80 (Figure 3D). Excluding the ΔΔGconf,GS contribution led to a marked decrease in prediction accuracy (RMSE = 1.2 kcal/mol; R2 = 0.2; Figure S5D), demonstrating it dominates the variation in ΔΔGcell ~4 kcal/mol. Supporting this conclusion, three variant pairs carrying compensatory mutations that restored the GS conformation also rescued the wt cellular transactivation81. The sequence-specific ΔΔGcell closely matched the corresponding differences in Tat-ARM binding affinity (ΔΔGbind) with RMSE = 0.5 kcal/mol; R2 = 0.8; slope = 1.1, intercept = 0.1 kcal/mol (Figure 3E).

  These results show that continuous tuning of the active-state propensity allows sequence changes to function as a molecular rheostat, shifting cellular activity up to 300-fold without substantially altering key intermolecular contacts.


### Benchmarking secondary structure prediction programs
  Our quantitative measurements of sequence-specific ΔΔG2y values spanning ~8.3 kcal/mol across designed TAR variants provided a distinct opportunity to rigorously evaluate how well existing secondary structure prediction programs quantify the thermodynamic propensities of lowly populated alternative secondary structures, often enriched with non-canonical mismatches (like ES2), within an RNA ensemble directly from the sequence (Figure 4A).

  We evaluated the prediction accuracy of eight commonly used secondary structure prediction programs (Figure 4B and Table S5). These include statistical learning-based models (Eternafold99, CONTRAfold57, MC-Fold100), thermodynamics-based models (ViennaRNA58, RNAstructure56, and NUPACK59), and hybrid models that employ both approaches (MXfold2101 and Vfold2D60). These programs were previously assessed for predicting single minimum-free energy conformation61,102,103, and more recently, ensemble-averaged nucleotide pairing probabilities for canonical Watson-Crick bps99. CONTRAfold was the top-performing program99, while the VFold pipeline104 led 3D RNA structure prediction at CASP-16105.

  To test their ability to predict conformational energetics, we used these programs to compute pseudo-energy scores for the GS (ΔGGS,i) and ES2 (ΔGES2,j) TAR secondary structures (see STAR Methods: Secondary Structural Constraints for TAR) across the 20 variants in our library. Most programs (7 of 8) correctly predicted the dominant minimum free energy secondary structure for over 90% of the variants. MC-Fold showed lower accuracy (~80%), primarily due to the overprediction of ES2.

  We compared the predicted ΔG2y values to experimental values measured using 1H CEST (see STAR Methods). Across the TAR variants, most programs performed comparably, predicting ΔΔG2y with an R2 of ~0.7-0.9 and an RMSE of ~1.3–1.5 kcal/mol (Figure 4B,C, and Figure S6A). MC-Fold100 performed significantly worse (R2 ~0.3, RMSE ~2.9 kcal/mol), consistent with its weak performance in prior benchmarks99,106–108 (Figure 4B,C). Accuracy was improved (R2 ~0.8–0.9 and RMSE ~1.0–1.3 kcal/mol) when incorporating a pH correction, a point we revisit later (Figure 4D, and Figure S6B, and see STAR Methods). Similar performance and predictive accuracy were obtained for five additional lowly populated, short-lived alternative RNA conformations previously characterized by NMR51,53,54,62,63,65 (Figure S6D,E).

  Consistently across all programs (except for MC-Fold), TAR variants that strongly favor ES2 over the GS were predicted to do so primarily by destabilizing the GS, rather than by stabilizing the ES2 (Figure S7A–C). We experimentally tested this prediction from GS destabilization by performing optical melting experiments on a subset of 5 variants chosen to uniformly sample the range of observed ΔG2y within the TAR library. As expected, the variants that predominantly adopt ES2 exhibited significantly lower melting energetics, by ~1.0–3.0 kcal/mol, compared to wt and those favoring the GS conformation (Figure S7D,E). These experimental melting measurements showed reasonable quantitative agreement with the predicted GS destabilization values from the computational models (RMSE ~1.3 kcal/mol, see STAR Methods).

  Although GS destabilization was the main driver of the shift toward ES2 across the TAR library, differences in prediction accuracy among programs largely reflect how well they model the energetics of the mismatch-enriched ES2 conformation (Figure S7B, see STAR Methods: Sources of uncertainty in the predicted ΔG2y). This shortcoming is not unexpected. Most thermodynamics-based models treat mismatches as unpaired internal loops109–111 (e.g., NUPACK, RNAstructure, MXFold2, Vfold2D) or rely on heuristic estimates58 (e.g., ViennaRNA). Nearest-neighbor parameters112–115 further approximate mismatches as energetically equivalent (e.g., A-C, A-A, U-C and C-C), not fully accounting for context-dependent energetic variations (see STAR Methods: Limitations in RNA Secondary Structure Prediction). These approximations reflect the scarcity of optical melting data relative to the vast space of RNA enriched in mismatches, a gap that 1H CEST coupled to library design begins to address.

  In contrast, statistical learning-based programs such as CONTRAfold, trained on secondary structures, can model non-canonical mismatches57, which may explain their marginally better performance for mismatch-rich ES2, especially in variants that strongly favor ES2 (Figure 4B). However, benchmarking showed that CONTRAfold under-penalize mismatches like G-U in mismatch-enriched environments (see STAR Methods: Sources of uncertainty in the predicted ΔG2y), likely due their underrepresentation in training datasets57.

  Another source of error is that most programs do not account for pH dependent conformational energetics, despite many mismatches (e.g. G-A53,62,65, C-A62,74,116–118, U-C119 and C-C75,109,120) being protonatable with pKas near neutrality 53,62,65,74,121. Two such mismatches (C-C and A-C) occur in the GS and/or ES2. Indeed, thermodynamics-based and hybrid programs showed notable and robust improvements in predictive accuracy, reducing the RMSE by ~0.4 kcal/mol and increasing R2 from 0.90 to 0.95, when a pH correction was applied to account for the slightly acidic pH conditions (pH = 6.4) used in the 1H CEST NMR experiments (Figure 4D, and Figure S6B). In contrast, statistical learning-based models showed smaller gains, possibly because their training datasets spanned broader pH ranges.

  While there is a need to improve prediction accuracy, these results show that alternative secondary structure propensities can be predicted directly from sequence for simple stem-loop RNAs like TAR. Prediction errors (~1.0 kcal/mol) are small compared to the measured ΔΔG2y range of ~8.3 kcal/mol from minor sequence changes; with up to ~11 kcal/mol variation predicted across all TAR double mutants.


### Predicting ES2-forming natural TAR variants
  The ability to computationally estimate RNA secondary structure energetics directly from sequence enables large-scale ensemble analyses that are currently infeasible through experimental approaches alone. As a prospective test, we used the suite of RNA secondary structure prediction programs to compute ΔG2y for all 118 naturally occurring HIV-1 and HIV-2 TAR variants (see STAR Methods) curated in the HIV sequence compendium database122 (Figure 5A). Unexpectedly, three natural HIV-1 variants bearing point-substitution mutations, G28C, A35C, and U38C, were predicted to significantly favor the inactive ES2 relative to wt, with some programs predicting ES2 to be the dominant state (Figure 5A and Figure S8A). We tested these predictions using NMR (see STAR Methods).

  The G28C point substitution mutation (Figure 5B) was predicted by all eight programs to substantially increase the ES2 population (pES2 ~40%), with CONTRAfold and MXFold2 predicting ES2 as the dominant state (pES2 ~85%). Remarkably, NMR analysis revealed (Figures 5C, S8E) that G28C adopts ES2 as the dominant conformation (Figure S2). As predicted from its low GS propensity (~15%, ~7-fold lower than wt), G28C exhibited ~50-fold weaker Tat-ARM binding and reduced transactivation, greater than expected from GS loss alone, likely reflecting effects on arginine fork interactions (Figure S5A–C, Table S3, S4). These results suggest G28C may be the first naturally occurring TAR variant that predominantly adopts ES2, though its identification in only a single HIV-1 isolate means additional evidence is needed to exclude sequencing error.

  A35C (Figure 5B), observed in two HIV isolates, stabilizes ES2 by replacing the G28-A35 mismatch with a Watson-Crick pair while minimally affecting GS, as A35 is usually flipped out74. Statistical learning models (CONTRAfold, MC-Fold) predicted ES2 enrichment to ~23% (versus wt ~0.4%), whereas thermodynamic models predicted near-wt levels (~0.6%; Figure 5A). NMR confirmed near-equal GS and ES2 populations (~61% and ~39%) in slow exchange (Figures 5C, S8E), the first TAR variant where both states are directly observable. The measured ΔG2y of 0.3 kcal/mol was in better agreement with CONTRAfold and MC-Fold (~0.7 kcal/mol) compared to thermodynamic models (~2.8 kcal/mol). ES2 was also strongly pH-dependent, shifting from GS to ES2 dominance as pH decreased from 7.4 to 5.4, consistent with protonated C+24-C39 mismatch formation123. As expected, Tat-ARM binding and cellular transactivation were similar to wt (ΔΔGbind = −0.3 kcal/mol and ΔΔGcell = 0.1 kcal/mol, Figure S5A–C, and Table S3, S4) consistent with the small ΔΔGconf ~0.5 kcal/mol predicted from the nearly equally populated GS and ES2 ensemble.

  The U38C point substitution (Figure 5B) was predicted by all eight programs to modestly (~10-fold) increase the ES2 population relative to wt, while still favoring the GS. This mutation replaces the U25-U38 mismatch in ES2 with another U25-C38 mismatch and the A27-U38 Watson-Crick bp in the GS with an A27-C38 mismatch. We observed a single set of resonances consistent with the GS. However, the measured pES2 = 0.5 ± 0.1% was nearly identical to that of wt (pes2~0.4±0.1%) deviating from predictions, once again highlighting the limits of modeling mismatch energetics (Figures S3 and S8E,F). U38C bound Tat ~1.3 kcal/mol weaker and exhibited reduced cellular transactivation by ~2 kcal/mol, consistent with disruption of the U23•A27-U38 base triple (Figure S5A–C, and Table S3, S4).

  As a negative control, we also examined three natural variants (U25A/ΔC24, A22G, U31C) and all predominantly formed the GS, consistent with most prediction programs and 1H CEST measurements (Figure S3, S8G, Table S2).

  Together, these results show that secondary structure prediction programs can be used to probe RNA ensemble energetics directly from sequence, while also highlighting the need to better model mismatch energetics. They further demonstrate the value of 1H CEST NMR in testing divergent predictions and point to the stronger performance of statistical learning-based programs over thermodynamic models.


### Predicting binding affinity and cellular activity directly from sequence
  We tested the possibility of bypassing NMR and predicting the energetics of Tat-ARM binding ΔΔGbind (16 variants) and cellular transcriptional activation ΔΔGcell (19 variants) directly from sequence using our thermodynamic model and ΔΔGconf,GS values calculated using the eight secondary structure prediction algorithms (Figure 6A). Remarkably, from these predicted ΔΔGconf,GS values, we could accurately predict ΔΔGbind with RMSE = 0.3-0.6 kcal/mol and R2 = 0.7-0.9 (Figure 6B,C) and ΔΔGcell with RMSE = 0.7-0.9 kcal/mol and R2 = 0.5-0.7 (Figure 6D,E). As expected, the prediction accuracy deteriorated substantially (RMSE = 0.7 kcal/mol, R2 = 0.5 for ΔΔGbind, and RMSE = 1.2 kcal/mol, R2 = 0.2 for ΔΔGcell) when omitting the energetic contribution from the secondary structural ensemble.

  CONTRAfold and VFold2D at T = 60 °C, yielded the best overall performance, consistent with their superior performance in prior benchmarks99,107 (Figure 6C,E and Figure S9A,B). This improved performance was primarily driven by their more accurate predictions for variants (e.g., G36U, G28U, A27C) that predominantly adopt ES2, which have the largest effects on binding and cellular transactivation (Figure S9C,D).

  To demonstrate the applicability of our approach to predict the cellular activities of other RNAs, we also applied the ensemble-based thermodynamic framework (see STAR Methods) to predict RRE-dependent nuclear RNA export for a library of six previously characterized variants81 of the HIV-1 Rev Response Element (RRE) stem IIB, which mediates the nuclear export of incompletely spliced viral RNAs by binding the viral Rev protein124–128. In contrast to TAR, sequence changes produce more modest shifts in RRE cellular activity. Nonetheless, from the predicted ΔΔGconf,GS values (see STAR Methods) obtained using RNA secondary structure prediction programs, we could predict ΔΔGcell with R2 = 0.3-0.7 (Figure S9E,F), again with MC-Fold performing worse (R2 ~0.02) compared to the remaining programs which gave R2 ~0.3-0.7. The RMSE were similar across programs (0.2-0.5 kcal/mol) due to the smaller energetic span of ΔΔGcell for the RRE variants compared to TAR. Together, these results demonstrate that current structure prediction programs can estimate ensemble-level conformational propensities directly from sequence, at least for simple hairpins such as TAR and RRE stem IIB, enabling modeling (Figure S9G) of how mutations impact protein binding affinity and cellular activity at a practically useful level of accuracy (~5-fold) and providing a platform for future efforts to extend to energetic contributions from the 3D ensemble utilizing the modularity of RNA conformational landscapes17.


### Sensitivity of TAR secondary structural landscape to sequence changes
  Our results reveal that the TAR secondary structural ensemble is extraordinarily sensitive to sequence variations. To more comprehensively explore this sequence sensitivity, we used the suite of secondary structure prediction programs to compute ΔG2y for every single (n = 58) and double-nucleotide (n = 1,595) substitution of 19 TAR residues (from A22 to U40) spanning the bulge, upper stem, and apical loop (Figure 7A–E). These single and double-nucleotide substitutions resulted in substantial variations in the predicted conformational energetics spanning ΔΔG2y ~5.7 kcal/mol and ~12.0 kcal/mol, respectively (Figure 7F). For most doublemutants, energetic effects were approximately additive (RMSE ~0.1 kcal/mol, Figures 7D and S9H,I), consistent with the nearest-neighbor assumptions underlying current prediction algorithms. Deviations from energetic additivity were observed, primarily for spatially adjacent mutations or mutations involving residues that form complementary base pairs in either the GS or ES2 (Figure S9H,I).

  Approximately ~10% of these mutations were predicted to reduce the active GS population by more than 10-fold by stabilizing ES2, resulting in weaker predicted binding affinities to Tat and cellular transactivation by over 10-fold (Figure 7A,B). These mutations were broadly distributed across seven residues in the upper stem and apical loop (Figure 7A). These findings reinforce our experimental measurements showing strong and broad sensitivity of secondary structure propensities on sequence.


### Ensemble-level constraints shape TAR sequence conservation
  Given the strong sequence dependence of TAR’s structural ensemble, we asked whether selective pressure to maintain secondary structural propensities influences sequence conservation, a phenomenon that likely extends to other regulatory RNAs129–132.

  While the bulge and apical loop residues that contact Tat:SEC are highly conserved, some upper stem residues are also highly conserved despite lacking clear structural or contact-related roles72,73,94. For example, C29, G36, C37, and C39 all form canonical Watson-Crick bps in the GS without contributing to Tat:SEC interactions or base triple formation yet are highly conserved across TAR natural variants (Figure 7B). Likewise, A35 is unpaired in the GS, and does not contact Tat:SEC yet shows significant sequence conservation (Figure 7B).

  To assess the contribution of the secondary structure ensemble to the TAR sequence conservation, we asked which sequences across all single and double substitutions are predicted to adopt the GS as the dominant secondary structure with ΔGconf < 0.5 kcal/mol, the largest penalty to form GS measured across the natural TAR variants. Strikingly, these constraints alone discover all 45 naturally occurring TAR variants comprising single and double-point substitution mutations, which represent ~40% of all observed TAR variants (n = 118). Ensemble-based sequence conservation was also evident in the correlation (r ~0.6, p < 0.01, Figure S8B–D) between the frequency of natural substitutions and their predicted impact of GS propensities (Figure S8B–D).

  We further identified residues with unusually high conservation (A27, G28, A35, G36, U38, C37, C39) that, when mutated, favored ES2 (Figure 7A–C). Several variants were experimentally confirmed to adopt ES2 as the dominant state in our TAR library, reducing Tat binding (ΔΔGbind ~1.2-2.2 kcal/mol) and cellular transactivation (ΔΔGcell ~2.4-3.0 kcal/mol). Of these, only G28 directly contacts Tat:SEC73,133, suggesting the conservation of the other residues may reflect at least in part a selective pressure to preserve the secondary structural thermodynamic preferences.

  By contrast, mutations to the least conserved residues (C24, U25, and apical loop residues C30–G34) were not predicted to stabilize ES2 relative to GS (Figure 7B,C) and some of these variants (U3, C24G, A22G-U40C, U31C) were included in our library and behaved like wt in terms of ensemble energetics, binding, and cellular activity.

  Finally, analysis of ΔG2y showed that natural TAR variants occupy a narrow energetic range (ΔG2y ~3.3 ± 0.6 kcal/mol), significantly tighter than the distribution predicted for all possible single or double mutants or measured in our designed library (Figure 7F). Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistical tests further confirmed this difference (p < 10−12 for single and p < 10−31 for double mutations).

  These results support a model in which selection pressures act to preserve the GS–ES2 energetic balance, thereby shaping TAR sequence conservation.


## Discussion
  Our results demonstrate that even subtle sequence changes can dramatically reshape the intrinsic propensity of an RNA to adopt its active secondary structure, enabling graded control of protein-binding affinity and cellular activity across a dynamic range spanning at least two orders of magnitude. This finding directly highlights the need for ensemble-based conformational energetics, since functional output is not determined by a single dominant conformation but rather the probability of forming functionally active conformations within the ensemble. Recent advances in measuring conformational energetics in high-throughput7,48,134,135 promise to further expand the scope of thermodynamic ensemble-based predictions of RNA cellular activity. Ultimately, models based on a single static structure, cannot quantitatively link sequence to function, not only for RNA but for biomolecules more broadly.

  The probability of forming the fully active stacked-base-triple TAR conformation can be astonishingly small, below 0.0001% for some of our variants due to the compounding energetic penalties of forming the GS secondary structure, coaxial helix stacking, and base-triple formation. Yet our data demonstrate that this exceptionally rare state still dominates the observed cellular transcriptional output. Such extreme cases highlight how functional conformations can be effectively buried within an overwhelmingly inactive ensemble. Our quantitative thermodynamic framework unmasked these hidden states and directly links their sequence-specific propensities to cellular activity, providing a generalizable strategy for connecting RNA sequence to function through conformational landscapes.

  This ensemble perspective also adds important insights into how we view RNA evolution. Sequence conservation in regulatory RNAs reflects not only the encoding of binding interfaces or 3D motifs but also the maintenance of an energetic balance between competing secondary structures. Consistent with this view, our mutational profiling and entropy analysis revealed that the most conserved positions in TAR are those where mutations most strongly perturb the secondary structural ensemble. Such “ensemble-based sequence conservation” is likely general, as many RNAs adopt alternative secondary structures with propensities comparable to or greater than that of wt TAR4,46,52–54,62,136, making them similarly vulnerable to point substitution mutations that shift the dominant secondary structure. Harnessing ensemble-based sequence conservation could provide a powerful new approach for functionally annotating regulatory RNAs, exposing evolutionary constraints on conformational landscapes, and enabling the inference of secondary structural ensembles directly from large-scale sequencing data.

  The constrained ΔΔG2y range in natural TAR variants suggests that ES2 may act as a molecular switch, becoming dominant when cellular cues overcome the ΔG2y energetic gap. ES2 formation could facilitate Tat:SEC release to enable multiple rounds of transcriptional activation and/or promote TAR-TAR kissing-loop dimerization proposed to play roles during genome dimerization and packaging137,138. Indeed, ES2-stabalizing mutations (e.g. G28C, G28U and G36U) enhanced dimerization whereas GS-retaining mutations (e.g., A22G, U40C, U31C) had little effect83. Notably, G28C, identified here as an ES2-dominant natural variant , was shown to strongly promote dimerization83. Our TAR variant library, which tunes the GS–ES2 propensities, provides a framework to quantitatively probe these ES2-specific roles in viral replication in the future. The thermodynamic model introduced in our study provides a principled foundation for future efforts to further incorporate alternative conformations and their functional roles in various cellular processes, ultimately resulting in a holistic understanding of the impact of sequence variations on viral activity

  In conclusion, by establishing RNA secondary structural ensembles as a critical energetic bridge between sequence and cell activity, this work marks an important step toward delivering on the long-standing goal of predicting function from sequence.


### Limitations of the Study
  Our thermodynamic model not only predicts how cellular activity varies with sequence, but also explains why, a key distinction from purely statistical or AI-driven models. However, it requires system-specific inputs, including knowledge of the active bound conformation and the conformational ensemble of the free RNA (e.g., ΔGstack and ΔGtriple). These inputs are available for HIV-1 TAR but are not yet broadly accessible for uncharacterized RNAs. At the same time, these inputs define the features that must ultimately be predicted from sequence in order to build a general, quantitative, and mechanistic model of RNA cellular activity. Continued advances in tools such as AlphaFold31 may allow prediction of 3D structures of active RNA-protein complexes; current algorithms provide useful estimates of RNA secondary structural ensemble properties; and methods such as FARFAR-NMR15,74,75 enable rapid determination of RNA 3D ensembles that can be integrated into thermodynamic models. Together, these advances define a path toward building a universal, ready-to-apply thermodynamic model that predicts RNA cellular activity directly from sequence, at least for relatively simple RNAs such as TAR and RRE stem IIB.

  Additionally, our NMR strategy relied on having a suitable, well-resolved imino proton (U38-H3) probe capable of distinguishing between conformational states in TAR. Such probes may not always be available even for RNAs amenable to NMR. Application to new systems further requires defining the dominant secondary structure and the identity of the minor conformation using technical NMR-based methods that are far from routine and difficult to apply to large and complex RNAs. Moreover, our analyses were limited to a subset of TAR conformational states (GS and ES2). However, additional,  lower-population states may also contribute to cellular activity. For instance, our 1H CEST data revealed evidence of a third state (ES3) of unknown structure and activity. Extending ensemble characterization to all states accessible by sequence variation, including states falling outside NMR detection, will be necessary to fully capture the functional landscape. We envision that by systematic design of RNA variant libraries containing suitable imino probes, combined with 1H CEST measurements across sequence space, could generate the critical experimental datasets needed to refine and train secondary structure prediction tools. In this way, future sequence-based prediction of ensemble propensities could ultimately replace the need for direct NMR measurements, making ensemble-level insights more broadly accessible across RNAs.

  While we experimentally tested all single substitutions predicted to stabilize ES2, we did not systematically evaluate combinations of substitutions, insertions, or deletions that may alter the ensemble in more complex ways. Future efforts using larger variant libraries and higher-throughput assays capable of quantifying weak binding and low cellular activity will allow for a more exhaustive evaluation of sequence-encoded ensemble modulation and cellular response.

  Finally, while work remains before such models become broadly generalizable prediction tools, this study demonstrates that thermodynamic models can quantitatively link sequence variation to ensemble energetics and cellular function, providing a conceptual, experimental, and computational framework toward the long-term goal of developing a ready to apply tool for generally predicting RNA cellular activity directly from sequence.

### EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

#### Cell Lines
  Cell lines used in this study include HeLa cells and HEK293T cells. All cell lines were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection. All cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and Antibiotic-Antimycotic at 37°C with 5% CO2.


### METHOD DETAILS

#### RNA Preparation and Purification
  All TAR variants (unlabeled) used in this study were synthesized using a MerMade 12 Oligo Synthesizer (BioAutomation) using standard phosphoramidite chemistry and base and 2′-hydroxyl deprotection protocols as described previously89. All RNA samples were prepared using unlabeled 2’-TBDMS protected phosphoramidites (ChemGenes) with 1 μmol standard (1000 Å) synthesis columns. The 5’-DMT (4,4’-dimethoxytrityl) was removed for DMT-off deprotection and standard PAGE purification as previously described. Nucleobase protecting groups were removed, and oligonucleotides were cleaved from the synthesis columns using 1 ml of 30% ammonium hydroxide and 30% methylamine (1:1), followed by incubation at room temperature for 2 hrs. Deprotected samples were then air-dried, followed by the addition of 100 μL DMSO, incubation at 65°C for 5 min to ensure the samples were fully dissolved, and the addition of 125 μL TEA-3HF, with incubation at 65°C for 2.5 hours. Finally, the samples were precipitated overnight using 3 M sodium acetate and 100% ethanol, air-dried, and then dissolved in water. The precipitated products were purified using a 20% (w/v) denaturing polyacrylamide gel with 8 M urea and 1X TBE (Tris/borate/EDTA). The RNAs were extracted from the excised gel by  electro-elution (Bio-Rad), followed by concentration and ethanol precipitation. The RNAs were then annealed in water at 95°C for 5 minutes and snap-cooled on ice for 1 hour. Finally, RNAs were buffer exchanged using Amicon Ultra-15 centrifugal filters into NMR buffer (15 mM sodium phosphate, 25 mM sodium chloride, 0.1 mM EDTA) at pH=6.4. 10% (v/v) D2O was added to each sample before NMR data collection. The final concentration of RNA samples ranged between ~0.5–1.0 mM. For in vitro binding measurements, the synthesized unlabeled RNAs were buffer exchanged using Amicon Ultra-15 centrifugal filters into assay buffer comprising 50 mM Tris-HCl, 100 mM NaCl and 0.01% (v/v) Triton X-100 at pH 7.4.


#### NMR Spectroscopy
  NMR experiments were performed on spectrometers operating at 600 MHz, 700 MHz, 800 MHz and 900 MHz magnetic fields (Table S8), all equipped with triple-resonance HCN cryogenic probes. NMR spectra were processed with NMRPipe140 and visualized with SPARKY (version 3.115)141.


##### NMR Assignments
  The assignment of imino (H1/H3) and aromatic (H6/H8/H2) resonances was either obtained from prior studies in the case of wt142, G28U63,81,82, TARES2 75,82,143, A22G/U40C144, U0, U1, U2, U3 and U482 or deduced from 2D 1H-1H NOESY and 2D HSQC experiments81 in the case of A22G, C24G, U31C, A27U/U38A, U2/A27U/U38A, C29A/G36U, C29G/G36C, G28C/C37G, A27C, G28C, U38A, U31C, U25A/ΔC24 and G36U (Figures S2). All NOESY and HSQC experiments on the TAR variants (~0.7–1.5 mM) were performed at pH=6.4 in NMR buffer (15 mM sodium phosphate, 25 mM sodium chloride, 0.1 mM EDTA) unless stated otherwise.


##### Determining dominant conformation
  The dominant conformation (GS versus ES2) adopted by new TAR variants (G28C, A35C, U31C, A22G and U25A/ΔC24) was deduced based on comparison of chemical shift fingerprints (imino H1/H3 resonances: G26, G28, G36 and U38 ; aromatic C8/C6/C2 resonances: A22, U23, C24, A35, U40, and C41) for the two states in 1D 1H and 2D HSQC spectra as described previously (Figure S8F–G)81. Additional support for the dominant state of the above-mentioned variants was also obtained from NOE-derived distance connectivity measured using 2D NOESY experiments.


##### 1H CEST Measurements
  In 1H CEST (Figure 1A), the GS magnetization is measured during a relaxation period in which a continuous radiofrequency field (RF) of variable power (ωSL) and frequency (ωRF) is applied66–69. On-resonance irradiation of the minor NMR-invisible state saturates its magnetization, which transfers to the dominant and NMR-observable GS via exchange, reducing GS signal intensity. This produces either a dip at the minor state chemical shift or a distortion of the GS dip at zero offset, depending on exchange rate145 (Figure 1A,D). The resulting CEST profile is fit to the Bloch– McConnell equations85 to obtain the exchange parameters (kex, pminor and Δω).

  1H CEST experiments on the TAR variants (~0.7-1.5 mM) were performed at T = 25 °C and pH=6.4 in NMR buffer (15 mM sodium phosphate, 25 mM sodium chloride, 0.1 mM EDTA) unless stated otherwise. To minimize NOE contributions, we restricted the offset of the applied RF field to range from −6 ppm to 6 ppm, as described previously69,146,147. Since the shaped pulse used for selective excitation is typically 3–4 ms in duration, corresponding to an excitation bandwidth of ~2–3 ppm69, we optimized the excitation window and offset to specifically cover the U38-H3 resonance. In two of the engineered variants, A27U/U38A and U2/A27U/U38A, the U38 residue was mutated thereby prohibiting the use of the U38-H3 as a probe for GS⇌ES2 exchange. In these two cases, we instead targeted the U27-H3 and G36-H1, both of which transition from U27-A38 and G36-C29 Watson-Crick base pairs to a U27-G36 wobble mismatch, resulting in a large Δω of >2 ppm. To increase the precision of our measurements, we used the largest number of unambiguously assigned probes for global fitting of the 1H CEST data (see STAR Methods), with an average of ~2 probes per variant. Further details on the RF fields (ω1/2π), offsets (Ω/2π) and mixing times (Tex) used for the 1H CEST probes are provided in Table S8.

  The peak intensities at each RF power and offset were extracted using NMRPipe140. The experimental uncertainty was obtained based on the standard deviation in peak intensities obtained from triplicate CEST experiments with zero relaxation delay for a given spin-lock power. The RF field inhomogeneity was measured and accounted for during CEST fitting. The exchange parameters of the TAR variants summarized in Table S2 were obtained by fitting the normalized intensity data to a  two-state Bloch-McConnel equation using an in-house Python script. The errors in exchange parameters were set to the standard error (SEM) derived from the square root of the diagonal elements in the covariance matrix of the fitted parameters. The 1H CEST profiles were also fit with and without ( ρes = kex = Δω = 0) exchange. Model selection for fits with and without exchange was performed as previously described148 by computing Akaike (wAIC) and Bayesian information criterion (wBIC).

  The 1H CEST profiles for all variants could be fit to a 2-state exchange model, with the only exception of G26-H1 of U2 and A27C (Figure S3). U38-H3 did not show statistically significant exchange contribution in U25A and U25A/ΔC24 since it forms A-U bps in both GS and ES2 in these variants, likely resulting in significantly smaller Δω. Data for the G26-H1 of U2 and A27C sensed an additional minor contribution from an additional alternative conformation (ES3)82, which required a 3-state exchange model to satisfactorily fit the 1H CEST profiles, yielding exchange parameters in good agreement with those observed for ES3 using prior R1ρ experiments82.

  Among the naturally occurring TAR variants, 1H CEST profiles could not be measured for imino resonances in the upper helix of G28C and A35C, due to significant spectral overlap and exchange broadening (Figure S2). For A35C, the populations of the GS and ES2 were instead inferred from the relative peak volumes of resonances A22-C2H2, C8H8 and U23-C6H6, which showed both GS and ES2 peaks in the 2D aromatic HSQC spectra (Figure 5C).

  We also investigated the effect of divalent cations (Mg2+) on the conformational ensemble by measuring the GS⇌ES2 exchange in 2 mM MgCl2 in NMR buffer (pH=6.4) for a subset of n=3 variants (wt, A27U/U38A and G28U) which represent low, intermediate, and high degree of stabilization of ES2 within the ensemble. Across all three variants, we observed minimal differences (RMSE < 0.3 kcal/mol on average, Figure S6C) in the measured ΔG2y upon comparing measurements with and without Mg2+. Furthermore, the addition of 2 mM Mg2+ to full-length TAR resulted in perturbations very similar to those observed in truncated TAR (Figure S1F). These results indicate that full-length TAR both adopts the canonical TAR structure in both the presence and absence of Mg2+, and the presence of divalent cations has minimal effect on the GS⇌ES2 conformational equilibrium89.

  Note, our approach involving the direct energetic comparisons between competing conformations of the same sequence that can be measured using 1H CEST NMR overcomes key limitations of melting-based approaches18,149, including deviations from  2-state transitions and the challenge of accurately capturing transient, metastable motifs, such as those in ES2, that are not readily stabilized in isolation to allow thermodynamic measurements.


#### Tat-ARM Binding Assay
  We used a fluorescence-based binding assay98 to measure the binding affinities of the variants in our TAR library to the Tat-ARM peptide. The binding assay was performed using unlabeled RNAs (wt and other TAR variants) prepared in-house (see STAR Methods: RNA preparation) and a peptide containing the Tat arginine rich motif (ARM) N-AAARKKRRQRRR-C, an  N-terminal fluorescein label, and a C-terminal carboxytetramethylrhodamine (TAMRA) label (LifeTein). In solution, the unbound flexible peptide allows the two terminal fluorophores to interact, quenching fluorescence. Upon binding to TAR, the peptide adopts a structured conformation that separates the two fluorophores, resulting in fluorescence98.

  For this assay, RNA was plated in 1:3 serial dilutions in a 384-well plate with a constant concentration (20 nM) of Tat-ARM peptide. Both RNA and Tat-ARM peptide were diluted in assay buffer consisting of 50 mM Tris-HCl, 100 mM NaCl, 0.01% (v/v) Triton X-100 at pH 7.4. The plate was left to incubate in the dark for 15 minutes prior to measurement. Fluorescence was measured in triplicate using a Biotek Synergy Neo2 plate reader (Agilent) using a 485 nm excitation wavelength and 590 nm emission wavelengths. Each RNA variant was assayed independently with five biological replicates (n=5). The averages and standard deviations of the Kd values for all variants are reported in Figure S5A and Table S3. The. Kd values were obtained by fitting the binding curves to the following equation using GraphPad Prism (version 9.3.1):  (8) A=Afree+(Abound−Afree)×([TAR]+[Tat−ARM]+Kd−([TAR]+[Tat−ARM]+Kd)2−4[TAR][Tat−ARM]2[Tat−ARM])  where A is the measured fluorescence; Afree is the fluorescence in the absence of TAR-Tat-ARM binding; Abound is the fluorescence at saturated TAR-Tat-ARM binding; Kd is the measured dissociation constant and [TAR] and [Tat-ARM] are the total concentrations of TAR and the peptide in solution, respectively.

#### Measuring cellular trans-activation
  We used a cellular luciferase-based assay to quantify the cellular transactivation for the n = 3 new TAR variants (G28C, A35C and U38C, Table S4). Corresponding cellular transactivation measurements for remaining variants in the TAR library were previously published81,87. HeLa cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 0.1% gentamicin at 37°C and 5% CO2. Cells were plated to 1.5 x 105 cells per well in 12-well plates 24 hours prior to transfection with polyethylenimine PEI (Polysciences). The primary transfection mixtures contained 125 ng pFLuc-TAR-140 reporter plasmid, 10 ng RLuc control plasmid, +/− 20 ng pcTat expression plasmid, and pBC12-CMV filler DNA plasmid up to a total of 1385 ng total DNA per well. Media was replaced at 24 hours post-transfection. Cells were lysed at 48 hours post-transfection with 250 μL passive lysis buffer (Promega) and incubated 20 minutes at room temperature. FLuc and RLuc activity was measured using a Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System. The assay was repeated in duplicate five times for all variants on independent days (n = 5). The FLuc and RLuc activities with and without Tat (+/− Tat) for a given variant ( i ) relative to ( wt) were converted to free energies (Table S4) in cell using the expression below, as previously described87:  (9) ΔΔGcell(i−wt)=−RTlnFLuc+Tati/RLuc+TatiFLuc−Tatwt/RLuc−Tatwt

  luciferase-based assay for RRE-mediated RNA export. #### Thermodynamic Model
  Our thermodynamic model decomposes the differences in the Tat:SEC binding energetics / cellular transactivation between two TAR variants into the following energetic contributions assumed to be independent:  (10) ΔΔGbind/cell(i−wt)=ΔΔGstack(i−wt)+ΔΔGtriple(i−wt)+ΔΔGconf,GS(i−wt)=ΔΔGstack(i−wt)+ΔΔGtriple(i−wt)+RTln1+e−ΔG2y(i)/RT1+e−ΔG2y(wt)/RT

  Note, in our thermodynamic model, we define ΔGconf,GS as the energetic propensity to adopt the GS secondary structure within the RNA conformational ensemble (ΔGconf,GS = −RT ln pGS), which relies on the overall population of the GS (pGS). In contrast, the ΔG2y (measured by NMR 1H CEST or predicted from secondary structural prediction programs) represents the energetic difference between the ES2 and the GS conformational states (ΔG2y = −RT ln pES2/pGS). For the two-state ensemble (GS, ES2) of TAR, ΔGconf,GS can be obtained from ΔG2y as:  (11) ΔGconf,GS=RTln1+e−ΔG2yi/RT

  Most mutations within our library only impact ΔG2y without perturbing ΔGstack and ΔGtriple relative to wtTAR. These variants include A22G, A22G/U40C, A35C, C24G, G28U, G28C, U25A, G36U, C29A/G36U, C29G/G36C and G28C/C37A; they all share the same bulge length as wt and maintain the integrity of the U23•A27–U38 base triple required for forming the active bound protein-RNA complex. For these variants we set ΔΔGstack = ΔΔGtriple = 0 kcal/mol.

  The U2, U3, and U4 variants altered the TAR bulge length thereby perturbing ΔGstack. For these variants, differences in stacking energetics (ΔΔGstack) were estimated using previously measured differences in stacking energetics from chemical shift perturbations87. Other variants in the library (A27C, U38A, U38C, A27U/U38A/wt) disrupted the U23•A27–U38 base triple, resulting in ΔΔGtriple = 1.3 kcal/mol, estimated from the constant impact of the A27U/U38A mutation on binding energetics across various bulge mutants of TAR in prior work87. Finally, variants A27U/U38A/U2 and A27U/U38A/U3 impacted both stacking and base triple energetics, and the sum of the two energetic contributions (ΔΔGstack + ΔΔGtriple) assumed to be independent was used to predict the overall binding energetics and cellular transactivation for these variants. The ΔΔGstack and ΔΔGtriple used for all mutants in the TAR library are summarized in Table S6.

  To accommodate potential contributions from Tat-ARM binding to ES2, we expanded our initial thermodynamic model for binding to four states involving both GS and ES2 in their free and Tat-ARM bound form. We estimated the low-affinity binding affinity for ES2 using the measured ΔGbind for G28U, the variant with the highest ES2 stabilization, adopting the ES2 conformation with a population of ~99.8 %. In contrast, the ΔGbind for wt (adopts GS with population of ~99.8%) was used to estimate the specific binding affinity for the GS. Since our fluorescence-based assay cannot distinguish between either GS or ES2 conformations in the free and in the bound states, in our four-state thermodynamic model, we have:  (12) Ka,app=1/Kd,app=Bound ComplexFree TatFree TAR=GS: Tat+ES2:TatFree TatGS+ES2

  Here [GS] and [ES2] are the concentrations of GS and ES2 in the unbound (free) state, while [GS:Tat] and [ES2:Tat] are the equilibrium concentrations of the GS and ES2 conformations of TAR bound to Tat-ARM, which can be related to the binding affinities of the GS and ES2 conformations to Tat-ARM as:  (13) Ka,GS=[GS−Tat][Tat][GS]Ka,ES2=[ES2−Tat][Tat][ES2]

  The above equations can be combined to yield the following expression for the apparent binding affinity:  (14) Ka,app=Kconf1+Kconf×Ka,GS×1Kconf+Ka,ES2

  Where Kconf = pES2/pGS represents the conformational equilibrium constant between GS and ES2 in the free TAR ensemble.

  Equation (14) combined with the above specified estimates for Ka,GS and Ka,ES2, and the ΔΔGconf,GS predicted from various prediction programs were used to predict ΔΔGbind across all TAR variants. Among the n = 8 variants that predominantly adopt the GS as the dominant state, differences in the propensities to form ES2 (ΔΔGconf,GS ~0.5 kcal/mol) only modestly affected the binding energetics relative to propensities to stack and form the base triple in the GS (ΔΔGstack + ΔΔGtriple ~1.3 kcal/mol).

  Among the variants which increase the propensities to form ES2 > 10-fold relative to wt, G28U and A27C were predicted to bind Tat-ARM predominantly in the ES2 conformation rather than GS (pES2 ~95 % and ~80 %, respectively), while the remaining variants (C29A/G36U, and A27U/U38A and U2/A27U/U38A) predominantly bind the GS but with an enriched ES2 population (pES2 ~10 %).

  To predict cellular activity for the six variants of RRE stem IIB, we assumed the previously characterized65 3-state ensemble in which the ES1 and ES2 conformations sequester key residues involved in the RRE-Rev interaction, thereby rendering them inactive. Assuming that only the GS secondary structure is active, the variations in cellular activity across mutants (ΔΔGcell) is predicted by the difference in propensities to adopt the active GS conformation (ΔΔGconf,GS = −RT ln pGS), similar to the thermodynamic model for TAR variants, which are calculated based on the predicted energy scores from RNA secondary structure prediction programs. As for TAR, all fits were performed to the y=x model, based on the thermodynamic predictions of cellular activity from sequence (ΔΔGcell) using the thermodynamic framework.


#### Expanding library beyond TAR
  In addition to the TAR library, we also benchmarked secondary structure prediction programs using n=3 additional RNAs for which exchange involving lowly-populated and short-lived alternative secondary structures were previously characterized using 13C/15N R1ρ NMR experiments64,150. These included ribosomal A-site (A-site)62, the HIV-1 stem loop 1 (SL1)62, and the HIV-1 Rev response element (RRE)65. In all cases, the proposed secondary structure of the minor state was verified using mutations that render it the dominant state, allowing for structural verification through comparison of chemical shifts62. We did not consider RNAs in which the secondary structure of the minor state was only partially characterized (e.g. those containing long apical loops). We also exclusively focused on hairpins and avoided pseudoknots and triplexes, whose secondary structures are more challenging to model computationally and require special considerations.


#### Predicting RNA secondary structural energetics

##### Description of secondary structure prediction programs
  We used eight commonly used RNA secondary structure prediction programs (Eternafold99, CONTRAfold57, MC-Fold100, MXfold2101, Vfold2D60, ViennaRNA58, RNAstructure56, and NUPACK59, Table S5) to predict ΔG2y across all variants in the TAR library, using the sequence of the variants and their NMR-derived secondary structural constraints in the GS and ES2 sub-ensembles as input, which is further elaborated in STAR Methods and Figure S6D.

  ViennaRNA, NUPACK, MXFold2 and RNAstructure do not support constraining non-canonical mismatches (all bps except A-U, G-C and G-U) as paired. For these programs, all non-canonical mismatches in both the GS and ES2 secondary structures were relaxed to form internal loops (unpaired), while for remaining programs, they were constrained to be paired, to obtain meaningful results. Similarly, all prediction programs tested, except for CONTRAfold and MC-Fold, do not permit the closing bp of tetraloops to be paired, like in ES2, hence these constraints were also relaxed to obtain meaningful predictions. While MC-Fold does not accept secondary structural constraints as input, it provides pseudo-energy score predictions for various alternative secondary structures sampled for a given input sequence. Hence, for MC-Fold, we first predicted the energy scores for a sample of n = 100 alternative secondary structures from the input sequence and subsequently filtered out those secondary structures matching the GS/ES2 constraints from the pool of n = 100 sampled alternative secondary structures, to estimate ΔG2y, across all TAR variants.

  VFold2D, ViennaRNA, NUPACK and RNAstructure also accommodate temperature dependent fluctuations in the pseudo-energy scores in their predictions. Hence, for completeness, we also computed the pseudo-energy scores from these programs at three different temperatures (T = 25°C, 37°C and 60°C). Similarly, we used CONTRAfold and EternaFold, both with and without (denoted as *) the ‘non-complimentary’ flag, to sample predictions under all possible parameters / settings. The exact commands used for computing the pseudo-energy scores from sequence and input secondary structural constraints for each program in each configuration is summarized in Table S5. Note, predictions with the ‘non-complimentary’ flag (CONTRAfold* and EternaFold*) generally resulted in lower accuracy in predictions compared to those without the flag (CONTRAfold, EternaFold).

  Note, in contrast to our approach, prior benchmarks have primarily focused on predicting RNA structures with canonical Watson-Crick bps, while the ability to quantitatively predict the energetics of forming alternative conformational states, often enriched with mismatches, has been largely unexplored.


##### Secondary Structural Constraints for TAR
  We explicitly defined the TAR GS and ES2 secondary structures, used as input for the secondary structure prediction programs, based on the recently determined NMR-derived 3D conformational ensembles of TAR15,74,75. The GS included six Watson–Crick bps in the lower helix, a trinucleotide (UCU) bulge, and four Watson–Crick bps in the upper helix, capped by a six-nucleotide apical loop15,74. We modeled the two different observed conformations of the apical loop in GS: the dominant (~85%) unpaired conformation with a cross-stranded C30-G34 Watson–Crick bp and the minor (~15 %) ES1 conformation, where the apical loop is zipped up by replacing the cross-stranded C30–G34 bp in the GS with two consecutive C30–A+35 and U31–G34syn mismatches74.

  In contrast, the ES2 included a one-nucleotide bulge (U23), a dinucleotide apical loop (U31 and G32), and four non-canonical bps (C24-C39, U25-U38, A27-G36 and G28-A35)75. The U25-U38, A27-G36 and G28-A35 mismatches were all constrained to be paired, while for the C24-C39 mismatch, both paired and unpaired constraints were applied, in line with prior NMR evidence15,75. Finally, in both GS and ES2, the A22-U40 Watson Crick bp was constrained to be both paired and unpaired, based on prior NMR-derived ensembles15,75.


##### Predicting ΔG2y
for TAR variants
  The predicted pseudo-energy score for each TAR sequence and input secondary structural constraint (see STAR Methods) was used to compute the predicted ΔG2y. Briefly, for each variant, we specified as input both the sequence and the secondary structure (using either dot brackets or connectivity tables) of GS and ES2 to obtain predicted pseudo-energy scores for each secondary structure. We then computed the free energy difference from the Boltzmann distribution (see STAR Methods):  (15) ΔG2y,predicted=−RTln∑ie−ΔGES2,iRT∑je−ΔGGS,jRT  where i and j are indices iterating over the input secondary structures associated with GS (numerator) or ES2 (denominator, see STAR Methods: Secondary Structural Constraints for TAR), respectively, T is the temperature and R is the universal gas constant. Among the eight programs tested, three (MXFold2, RNAstructure and NUPACK) do not allow non-canonical mismatches to be specified as bps, in which case they were specified as internal loops instead to obtain meaningful predictions (see STAR Methods). For completeness, we also tested these predictions under alternative parameter settings (CONTRAfold* and EternaFold*) and temperatures (T = 25 °C, 37 °C and 60 °C for VFold2D, ViennaRNA, RNAstructure and NUPACK) (see STAR Methods).

  A uniform scaling factor was applied to the predicted ΔG2y values to allow rescaling of the pseudo energy score (instead of free energies), such as those predicted by MXFold2, as well as the log partition scores by CONTRAfold and EternaFold. While a scaling constant should not be applicable to the thermodynamic models, the scaling was applied since the mismatches across these models were constrained as internal loops. A rescaling also permits for trivial optimizations due to deviations from temperature and pH. Note, the TARES2 sequence variant from the TAR library was not included in the above predictions since we could not determine the exact conformation of its apical loop in the GS, unambiguously, due to the two-nucleotide insertion within the apical loop in this mutant.

  While our benchmark suggests that most programs performed comparably, (R2 ~0.7-0.9 and RMSE ~1.3–1.5 kcal/mol, Figure 4B,C, and Figure S6A), MC-Fold performed significantly worse (R2 ~0.3, RMSE ~2.9 kcal/mol). This underperformance may stem from its use of a coarse-grained nucleotide cyclic motif representation100, in contrast to the nucleotide-level representation used in other probabilistic log-linear models such as CONTRAfold and EternaFold. In particular, the statistical learning-based models (CONTRAfold and EternaFold) and hybrid models (MXFold2 and Vfold2D) performed similarly to thermodynamic models (R2 ~0.7–0.8, RMSE ~1.3–1.5 kcal/mol, Figure 4B,C) over the entire library, but showed different prediction errors across the variants, which could explain earlier findings99 that slightly favored statistical models for predicting ensemble-averaged base-pairing probabilities.

  The above-outlined approach was also used to predict ΔG2y for all naturally occurring variants of TAR (see Table S9), as well as all possible single and double point-substitution mutations of the TAR sequence involving residues 22 to 40 which are involved in the GS⇌ES2 exchange (see Tables S10, S11).


##### Predicting ΔG2y
for other RNAs
  A similar approach was used in the evaluation of secondary structure prediction programs using a library of ~3 RNAs other than TAR (Figure S6D) as well as six mutants of the RRE Stem IIB whose cellular activities were previously characterized81. We specified the GS and ES secondary structures for these RNAs strictly based on the RNA secondary structures presented in their original publication62,65 (Figure S6D). Like TAR, the ΔG2y for these RNAs were computed using the predicted pseudo-energy scores for the different secondary structural constraints, as highlighted in (15).


##### Limitations in RNA Secondary Structure Prediction
  In our analysis, we tested eight commonly used secondary structure prediction programs (Eternafold99, CONTRAfold57, MC-Fold100, MXfold2101, Vfold2D60, ViennaRNA58, RNAstructure56, and NUPACK59). Of these, CONTRAfold, EternaFold and MC-Fold were statistical learning-based programs, that utilize machine learning algorithms to infer linear probabilistic models using large sequence and annotated secondary structural datasets. In contrast, the thermodynamics-based programs ViennaRNA, RNAstructure and NUPACK use Turner’s nearest neighbor thermodynamic parameters derived from UV melting experiments on a dataset of RNA duplexes. Finally, MXFold2 and VFold2D represent hybrid approaches that utilize both statistical learning and thermodynamic parameters for their predictions.

  Except MC-Fold, most popular RNA secondary structure prediction tools such as ViennaRNA, RNAstructure, NUPACK, MXfold2101, Vfold2D, EternaFold, and CONTRAfold do not provide a set of alternative secondary structural conformations in the ensemble and their energetics for a given input RNA sequence. However, they can evaluate the energetic score for a user specified secondary structure, when provided as an input constraint. Thus, by specifying both the RNA sequence and the NMR-derived secondary structures of the GS and ES2 as inputs, we were able to expand the utility of these programs to predict the energetics of alternative conformational states.

  Significant limitations still exist in many prediction programs for modeling non-canonical base pairs in RNA secondary structures. All thermodynamics-based programs do not allow non-canonical mismatches (all bps excluding Watson-Crick bps and G-U wobbles) to be constrained to form bps, despite extensive studies demonstrating that many mismatches form stable hydrogen-bonded bps when embedded in RNA duplex. RNAstructure and NUPACK apply a high energetic penalty (~2000 kcal/mol) to their predictions. While ViennaRNA allows mismatches to be constrained as bps, it uses a purely ad hoc approximation to model their energetics by using the worst energy contribution from the most similar canonical Watson Crick bp, as opposed to using a scoring metric trained to model mismatches, thereby leading to inaccurate predictions. To obtain meaningful predictions using Turner’s nearest neighbor parameters, these programs require non-canonical mismatches to be specified as unpaired internal loops.

  When specified as internal loops, the energetics contributions of non-canonical mismatches are calculated based on an empirical internal loop energetic model, which extrapolates from a handful of thermodynamic measurements reported in Turner’s parameters and does not explicitly account for either hydrogen bonding between the residues forming the mismatched bp or stacking between the mismatch and its neighboring bps. This internal loop model predicts A-C, A-A, C-C, C-U and A-G mismatches to have the same energetic contributions as well as identical sequence context-dependent changes in energetics, despite extensive experimental evidence against the same. Other prediction programs, such as MXFold2, actively relax any base pairing constraints for non-canonical mismatches before energetic predictions, effectively rendering them as internal loops.

  The statistical learning methods CONTRAfold, EternaFold and MC-Fold allow mismatches to be constrained as bps, using inferred probabilistic parameters for these mismatches from extensive sequence and secondary structural datasets to predict their energetic contributions. However, it remains unclear whether the single-model structures used to train these programs carry the energetic information needed to model the structure and energetics of rare conformational states, which are typically underrepresented in these datasets49,151,152. These models also typically require a large number of parameters for accurate predictions, which can easily lead to overfitting to the training data153,154.

  In addition to the above-mentioned limitations in modeling non-canonical mismatches, the presence of tetraloops, such as those in ES1 and ES2, also presents a challenge for many prediction programs. Among those tested, only CONTRAfold and MC-Fold can allow the closing bps of tetraloops to be paired. The remaining programs (NUPACK, RNAstructure, ViennaRNA, Vfold2D, EternaFold, MXfold2) do not allow the closing bps of tetraloops to be paired in the specified secondary structural constraints, despite NMR evidence for the formation of stable hydrogen bonding. Thus, to obtain meaningful results, we specified the closing bp of tetraloops in ES1 and ES2 for these prediction programs to be unpaired, to avoid a blanket high-energy penalty.


##### Sources of uncertainty in the predicted ΔG2y
  Our benchmark using the TAR library suggests that RNA secondary structure prediction programs can predict ΔG2y from sequence with reasonable accuracy (R2 ~0.8-0.9 and RMSE ~1.0-1.3 kcal/mol, see Table S12), especially when compared to the experimentally measured ΔΔG2y range of ~8.3 kcal/mol. However, these errors were significantly higher than the experimental uncertainty in the 1H CEST measurements (~0.2 kcal/mol), highlighting significant room for future improvement. To dissect the underlying reasons for the prediction errors, we decomposed the variations in the predicted ΔG2y into its two underlying contributions representing the changes in the stability of the GS (ΔΔGGS = ΔGGS(i) – ΔGGS(wt)) and the ES2 (ΔΔGES2 = ΔGES2(i) – ΔGES2(wt)) conformations for a given variant (i) relative to wt.

  As previously mentioned, across all eight structure prediction programs, the predicted variations in ΔG2y were more strongly correlated with ΔΔGGS (R2 ~0.7-0.9) as opposed to ΔΔGES2 (R2 ~0.0-0.3), indicating that the predicted variations in ΔG2y is dominated by the relative destabilization of GS and not the ES2. This dissection of the predicted ΔG2y also helped identify potential origins of program-specific prediction errors against certain variants in the library limiting their prediction accuracy.

  Among all eight prediction programs, MC-Fold showed the smallest range of GS destabilization (ΔΔGGS ~0-2 kcal/mol) in contrast to the remaining programs, which predicted over 2-fold higher GS destabilization (ΔΔGGS ~-2-4 kcal/mol). This explained the consistently lower predicted ΔG2y by MC-Fold (~2-3 kcal/mol) relative to NMR measurements distributed across the variants in the library. These differences in the predicted GS destabilization likely arise from the use of a coarse-grained nucleotide cyclic motif representation in MC-Fold, rather than a complete nucleotide-level representation, as in the remaining prediction programs. Such a representation is susceptible to potential ambiguities in motif assignments. CONTRAfold showed errors in the predicted ΔG2y for variants with the A27U/U38A mutations (wt, U2 and U3 with A27U/U38A) could be attributed to overpredicting the ES2 stabilization (by ~2 kcal/mol) in these variants, which replace the A27-G36 mismatch with a U27-G36 wobble bp.

  The hybrid (MXFold2, VFold2D) and thermodynamics-based (NUPACK, RNAstructure, ViennaRNA) programs showed the highest prediction errors for the variants that adopt ES2 as the dominant state (G28U and G36U). These programs underestimated the ES2 stabilization across these variants, where, despite replacing a G-A mismatch in the ES2 with a U-A Watson-Crick bp, the predicted ES2 stabilization was ~0-0.5 kcal/mol, potentially stemming from modeling the mismatches as internal loops.


#### Thermodynamic pH-correction
  NMR 1H CEST measurements were performed at pH = 6.4 while and binding and cellular transactivation assays were carried out at pH = 7.4. To account for this small difference in pH, we applied a correction for each variant which takes into account the pH dependence of ΔG2y90. Prior NMR studies90 demonstrated that A-C and C-C mismatches in stem loops show significantly elevated pKas (~7.5 for A-C and ~7.0 for C-C, respectively), resulting in significant pH-dependent changes in their conformational energetics between pH = 6.0 to 8.0. While other mismatches such as U-C and A-G also undergo protonation, they have significantly lower pKas (5.5-5.8)109,155 in comparison, resulting in negligible impact on ΔG2y within the pH range in our experiments, and hence were ignored in our modeling. Under this simplifying assumption, the pH-dependent contribution to ΔG2y can be written as:  (16) ΔG2ypH=−RT∑i,jn1+10pKaj−pH1+10pKai−pH

  Here i and j represent various protonation sites (A-C and C-C mismatches) in the GS and ES2 respectively, while pKai and pKaj represent the corresponding pKa for these sites. The pH correction due to differences in pH between the NMR (pH = 6.4) and binding / cellular assays (pH = 7.4) experiments can be computed as:  (17) ΔΔG2ypH=ΔG2ypH=6.4−ΔG2ypH=7.4

  Equation 16 was also used to apply pH corrections to the predicted ΔG2y from different RNA secondary structure prediction programs (Figure 4D, S6B).


#### Optical Melting Experiments
  RNA hairpins (3 μM) were prepared in NMR buffer and subjected to thermal denaturation with a 1 °C/min ramp, while absorbance at 260 nm was recorded every 0.5 °C. Melting profiles were fit to the Van’t Hoff equation under a two-state model to obtain the melting temperature (Tm) and enthalpy of melting (ΔHmelt) and as previously described156 (Figure S7D):  (18) A260=mmeltT+bmelt×pmelt+mfoldT+bfold×1−pmelt  where mmelt, bmelt, mfold and bfold are coefficients describing the temperature-dependence of the extinction coefficients for the melted and folded species, respectively, and pmelt is the population of the melted hairpin species, given by:  (19) pmelt=1−e−1Tm−1TΔHmeltoR1+e−1Tm−1TΔHmeltoR

  Where T is the temperature (in units K) and R is the gas constant (kcal mol−1K−1). The standard free energy change of melting was computed as:  (20) ΔGmelto=ΔHmelto−TΔSmelto

  The mean values and uncertainties in Tm, ΔHmelto, ΔSmelto and ΔGmelto were set to be equal to the average and standard deviation from multiple measurements (n ≥ 3, summarized in Table S7).


#### Analysis of TAR natural variants
  We ranked the n = 118 naturally occurring TAR variants122 using the predicted ΔG2y values from the suite of RNA secondary structure prediction programs. To control for false positives, we removed n = 14 variants that differed by more than six nucleotides from wt and were each observed only once in the sequence compendium, likely reflecting alignment/sequencing errors. Excluding such heavily mutated variants also preserved the validity of our thermodynamic framework, as sequences with more than three mutations are prone to forming new conformational states outside the scope of our model. We also removed natural variants with mutations outside of residues 22 to 40, since these residues adopt the same geometry in GS and ES2, and are therefore energetically indistinguishable from wt. Applying these filters resulted in n = 69 unique natural variants, of which G28C, A35C and U38C showed the highest propensity to form ES2 relative to wt. These n = 3 variants correspond to HIV-1 isolates identified from different geographic locations (G28C: Italy; A35C: Uganda, USA; U38C: South Africa), and each variant also showed a corresponding Tat sequence with significant mutations neighboring the ARM region. The GenBank IDs for the deposited sequences corresponding to the above identified natural TAR variants are: GU595153 (G28C), JX236675 (A35C), KF990608 (A35C), and AY162223 (U38C).

  As a negative control, we also tested three natural variants: U25A/ΔC24, A22G and U31C, involving mutations away from the critical upper helix and apical loop residues. Each predominantly formed the GS as predicted by 7 of the 8 prediction programs. Once again, the only exception was MC-Fold, which incorrectly predicted U25A/ΔC24 to adopt ES2 predominantly, overestimating the stabilizing effect of replacing the U25-U38 mismatch in ES2 with an A25-U38 Watson-Crick bp. For A22G and U31C, the predicted ΔG2y averaged across all 8 prediction programs (~3.3 kcal/mol and ~3.4 kcal/mol, respectively) was like wt (~3.3 kcal/mol), and consistent with 1H CEST measurements (~4.5 ± 1.0 kcal/mol and ~3.5 ± 0.1 kcal/mol, respectively, Figure S3 and Table S2). For A22G, the measured ΔG2y had a significant uncertainty and U25A/ΔC24 did not exhibit a substantial exchange contribution, as also observed for U25A (Figure S3).


#### Analysis of potential ensemble-level constraints shaping TAR sequence conservation
  To test for correlations between highly conserved residues in the TAR sequence and the impact of single point substitution mutations on conformational propensities to adopt the active GS, we quantified the degree of sequence conservation for a given residue (i) across the natural variants by computing the information content at residue i denoted by Isequence(i):  (21) Isequence(i)=2−∑j=A,U,G,Cpi,jlnpi,j  where pi,j represents the frequency of nucleotide j (with j = A,U, G, and C) at residue i across all natural variants. Here, Isequence(i) is a positive value ranging between 0 and 2, which represents the number of bits of information encoded in the sequence of residue i. A higher value of Isequence(i) directly corresponds to greater sequence conservation of residue i.

  We also quantified the degree to which single substitution mutations to a residue i perturbs the conformational propensities ΔGconf using σi defined as:  (22) σi=maxj=A,U,G,CΔGconf(i,j)−minj=A,U,G,CΔGconfi,j  where ΔGconf(i, j) denotes the conformational free energy change for adopting the GS when residue i is mutated to nucleotide j (j = A, U, G, C). For residue i, σi represents the maximum predicted perturbation in ΔGconf across the four possible single-nucleotide substitutions (A, U, G, C). By definition, σi is positive, with larger values indicating greater sensitivity of ΔGconf to mutations at that position. We observed a statistically significant Pearson correlation r = 0.6 (p < 0.01) between the Isequence(i) and σi (log scale), indicating that residues whose mutations most strongly perturb ΔGconf are, on average, more highly conserved across naturally occurring TAR variants (Figure S8B–D).
