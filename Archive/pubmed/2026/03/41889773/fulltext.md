# Multisite Phosphorylation Regulates the Structure
and Auto-Inhibitory Function of the Intrinsically Disordered N‑Terminal
Domain of p53


## Abstract
  Multisite phosphorylation plays an important role in essential cellular processes such as cell cycle control and circadian rhythm regulation and is also closely involved in the onset and progression of cancer and neurodegenerative diseases. As one of the most critical human tumor suppressor proteins, p53 is greatly modulated by phosphorylation. It contains numerous phosphorylation sites in its intrinsically disordered terminal domains. Specifically, multisite phosphorylation in the N-terminal domain (NTD) stimulates its autoinhibitory function, thereby repressing its downstream DNA transcriptional activity. However, the molecular mechanisms by which phosphorylation regulates p53 autoinhibition remain largely unclear. In this study, we employed all-atom molecular dynamics simulations combined with enhanced sampling methods to investigate how phosphorylation influences the structural properties of the intrinsically disordered p53-NTD, as well as its interaction with the DNA-binding domain (DBD). Our results show that phosphorylation significantly modulates the structural properties of p53-NTD, including both local structures and long-range residue interactions. T55 phosphorylation promotes the insertion of the aromatic rings of F54 and W53 into the DNA-binding pocket (DBP) of DBD and synergistically stabilizes the NTD-DBD interactions. Although S46 single phosphorylation would not induce NTD binding to the DBP, it can amplify the self-inhibitory ability of pT55 by reducing the dynamic conformational entropy of NTD. This study reveals the detailed molecular mechanism by which phosphorylation on p53-NTD regulates the structure and self-inhibition, providing crucial insights into the molecular basis underlying intrinsically disordered protein functions.


## Introduction
  Phosphorylation is one of the most prevalent post-translational modifications (PTMs) of proteins. ,  By introducing negative charged groups to serine, threonine, or tyrosine residues, it induces conformational changes in proteins and thereby modulates their functions. ,  This modification plays critical roles in cellular signal transduction, gene expression regulation, and metabolic control. ,,  A single protein often contains multiple phosphorylation sites, giving rise to multisite phosphorylation. ,  Compared with single-site phosphorylation, multisite phosphorylation can amplify and enhance the functional performance by rate differentiation, order modulation, and site competition. By the signaling coupling and cascades, multisite phosphorylation modulates essential cellular processes, including cell cycle progression and circadian rhythm maintenance. ,  Importantly, it also serves as a pivotal factor in the initiation and progression of diseases such as cancer and neurodegenerative disorders.

  Most phosphorylation sites are located on intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) or intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs). IDPs and IDRs lack stable secondary and tertiary structures, which expose abundant reactive residues and allow them to exist as a highly dynamic and heterogeneous ensemble of conformations. This structural plasticity endows them with the capacity to mediate multivalent and regulable molecular recognition, thereby rendering them major targets for post-translational modifications. ,

  The tumor suppressor protein p53 is a model protein for studying phosphorylation regulation. As a crucial regulatory protein, p53 can activate transcriptional processes. Under genotoxic or oncogenic stress, p53 protein might lose its normal function, thereby promoting tumorigenesis. ,  p53 protein also participates in key cellular processes such as cell cycle arrest, DNA repair, senescence, and apoptosis. Structurally, p53 protein is a tetrameric multidomain protein composed of both folded structures and IDRs. ,  Its functional domains include the N-terminal transactivation domain (NTD, residues 1–94), the DNA-binding domain (DBD, residues 94–294), the tetramerization domain (TET, residues 323–360), and the C-terminal regulatory domain (CTD, residues 360–393). Among these, NTD and CTD are intrinsically disordered regions that enable dynamic binding with a variety of regulatory proteins. Notably, the NTD contains two acidic segments: AD1 (residues 18–26) and AD2 (residues 40–55), which are functionally important for binding to partner proteins, such as MDM2 and p300/CBP. ,

  In unstressed human cells, some sites on p53-NTD are constitutively phosphorylated. Under this condition, the NTD exerts an autoinhibitory function by binding to the DNA-binding pocket on the DBD via AD2 (activation domain 2). ,  This hinders the binding of p53 to DNA through steric hindrance and prevents the activation of downstream transcriptional pathways. Under non-stress conditions, the autoinhibition of p53 is crucial for ensuring the normal proliferation and metabolism of cells. Based on nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and fluorescence anisotropy, recent studies show that the autoinhibition of p53 is dominated by the phosphorylation of T55 in the AD2 region. This phosphorylation greatly enhances the interaction between the AD2 motif on the NTD domain and DNA-binding pocket of the DBD domain. Moreover, the autoinhibition is further strengthened by multisite phosphorylation of T55/S46, which deepens the autoinhibitory state and diminishes the synergistic DNA-binding capacity. ,,  By using a coarse-grained (CG) protein model, Barethiya et al. have shown that phosphorylation on NTD sites enhances dynamic interactions between NTD and DBD. This leads to conformational rearrangements, which promote trans interactions between p53 dimers, and ultimately drives DNA dissociation. Despite these advances, the atomic-level molecular basis underlying the synergistic effects of multisite phosphorylation on p53 autoinhibition remains unclear.

  To further investigate the molecular mechanisms by which multisite phosphorylation occurs, here we employed the all-atom molecular dynamics simulations combined with enhanced sampling methods to study the effects of phosphorylation on the intrinsically disordered NTD of p53. The replica exchange with solute tempering (REST3) simulations were first performed to characterize the structural ensembles of the p53-NTD before and after phosphorylation. The results show that phosphorylation not only affects the local structures near the phosphorylation sites but also increases the global extensibility and changes the long-range interactions. Moreover, dual phosphorylation at pS46 and pT55 exhibits a mutually compensatory effect on the p53-NTD structure. Single-site phosphorylation at either S46 or T55 significantly promotes the interaction between p53-NTD and DBD. However, only T55 phosphorylation promotes the interaction between the TAD2 and DBP domains, thereby inducing self-inhibition. pT55 forms synergistic interactions with the surrounding hydrophobic residues to jointly stabilize NTD-DBD binding. Dual phosphorylation at pS46 and pT55 acts as a double safeguard, reducing the conformational entropy associated with NTD dynamics and thereby enhancing the self-inhibition ability of p53. This study provides an in-depth molecular mechanism for how p53-NTD phosphorylation regulates structure and function, laying a foundation for understanding the molecular basis of p53 self-inhibition and related diseases.


## Results

### Regulation of the p53 NTD
Structural Ensemble by Phosphorylation
  The structural ensembles of p53-NTD were sampled by replica exchange with REST3. To evaluate the accuracy of the simulated ensembles, the secondary chemical shifts were calculated and compared with the experimental measurements. Chemical shifts are highly sensitive to the local environment and can reflect the local protein structures. Although intrinsically disordered proteins lack stable structures, chemical shifts can capture the time-averaged structural characteristics of the ensembles of these unique proteins. By comparing with NMR experimental data (Figure  A and B), we found a high degree of agreement between the simulation results and experimental data, with root-mean-square errors (RMSEs) of 0.63 ppm (for Cα) and 1.06 ppm (for N), respectively. This result confirms that our model can accurately capture the conformational ensembles of p53-NTD.

  The effects of phosphorylation on the p53-NTD structural ensemble were evaluated by the residue chemical shift differences (Figure  C and D). The results show that in addition to residues near the phosphorylation sites (residues 40–55), chemical shifts also significantly changed in regions far away from the phosphorylation sites S46 and T55. For instance, the residues 20 to 30 exhibited large Cα chemical shift changes (about 0.8 ppm). Compared to WT p53-NTD, the secondary chemical shifts of N atoms at residues D21, L25, and V31 exceeded 0.5 ppm. The results indicate that phosphorylation not only affects the local structure of the disordered p53-NTD domain but also modulates the structures of residues separated along the sequence. This regulation may influence the overall protein structure and the distribution of conformational ensembles.

  The free energy surfaces of WT and phosphorylated p53-NTD are constructed onto two reaction coordinates: the radius of gyration (Rg) and the number of internal residue contacts (Figure  ). The Rg reflects the extensional state of the system, and the number of internal contacts represents the total count of residue pairs that are in close proximity within the system. The results showed that p53-NTD became more extended after phosphorylation. In wild-type (WT) p53-NTD, the Rg of most conformations was less than 2 nm. Phosphorylation at either the S46 or T55 site significantly increased the extensibility of p53-NTD (Figure  C, D). In comparison, for p53-NTD with double phosphorylation at S46 and T55, the probability of conformations having an Rg exceeding 2 nm was over 50%. These findings suggest that phosphorylation favors further extension of p53-NTD, which may increase the structural flexibility and dynamism of the domain. This enhanced flexibility could, in turn, facilitate the functional performance of the p53-NTD.

  The contact frequencies between residue pairs across different systems (Figure S1) were calculated. The results show that single-site phosphorylation at either S46 or T55 alters the internal residue interactions of the protein. Specifically, phosphorylation at S46 leads to a significant reduction in the interactions within the AD1 and AD2, as well as the interactions between AD1 and AD2 regions. In contrast, although the phosphorylation at T55 reduces the interactions within the AD1 and AD2 regions, the interactions between AD1 and AD2 are increased (Figure S1A). Dual phosphorylation of the S46 and T55 sites weakens intradomain contacts to an intermediate degree compared to either single modification, indicating that the effect is not a simple linear sum (Figure S1C). This suggests a complex synergistic regulation where the outcome is a finely balanced state rather than a straightforward cascaded enhancement.


### Regulation of the Binding
Ability between p53-NTD and DBD by
Phosphorylation
  To investigate how phosphorylation affects the function of intrinsically disordered protein p53, we employed the biased-exchange metadynamics (BE-MetaD) method to simulate the interaction process between p53-NTD and the DNA-binding domain (DBD). The 2D free energy surfaces (FES) related to the p53-NTD and DBD binding processes were constructed. The FES are embedded onto two collective variables (CV): the residue contact number between the AD2 region of p53-NTD and the segment of residues 273 to 294 on DBD (contact number CV1), and the distance between the center of mass (COM) of AD2 and DBP (distance CV6). The results show that wild-type p53-NTD tends to remain separate from the DBD. In most of the structures, the AD2 region of p53-NTD is far from the DNA-binding pocket of p53-DBD (97%). Although occasional proximity between AD2 and DBD is observed, WT p53-NTD prefers to contact DBD in the regions outside the DNA-binding pocket (Figure  A).

  Phosphorylation of S46 promotes interactions between p53-NTD and DBD (Figure  B). The lowest free energy state of pS46 p53 corresponds to the NTD closed to the DBD, which suggests that the NTD of the structures in this state cannot move freely (Figure  B). However, the high free energies of the states with a large AD2-DBP contact number mean that the probability of AD2 binding to DBD is very low. This interaction mode would not induce the steric hindrance effect on the DNA binding, and therefore, phosphorylation at S46 would not produce the autoinhibition of p53.

  On the other hand, phosphorylation at T55, as well as dual phosphorylation at S46 and T55, greatly promotes the interactions between p53-NTD and the DBP region. The low free energy associated with small distances between NTD and DBD indicates that these two domains tend to approach each other. Moreover, the free energies of states with large AD2-DBP contact numbers (>10) are relatively low. These results show that the phosphorylation of T55 would enhance the interactions between the AD2 motif and the DBP region, thereby preventing the interaction between DBP with DNA and causing p53 autoinhibition (Figure  C). Compared with single-site phosphorylation at T55, dual phosphorylation at S46 and T55 further strengthens the NTD and DBD interactions. In the pS46pT55 system, the NTD-DBD distances in most conformations are smaller than 3 nm, and the AD2 region exhibits very strong interactions with the DNA-binding pocket on DBD. These findings provide compelling evidence for the regulatory role of phosphorylation in the function of IDPs, particularly its ability to control activity by modulating the conformational ensemble.


### Regulation of the Interdomain Interactions
  Residues in the DBD with high contact probability with NTD residues are highlighted in Figure  . Most of these residues are charged and polar, which indicates that the interactions between NTD and DBD are primarily mediated by electrostatic forces (Figure  ). Although phosphorylation at both S46 and T55 promotes spatial proximity between NTD and DBD, their binding modes differ greatly from each other. Figure   shows the distribution of DBD residues that interact with the NTD. Upon S46 phosphorylation, interaction sites are broadly distributed across the DBD surface, including several charged residues (e.g., K101) and polar residues (e.g., H168). In contrast, T55 phosphorylation results in a more localized interaction pattern (Figure  B), predominantly centered near the DNA-binding pocket. Among these interactions, contacts with positively charged residues in the DNA-binding pocket, particularly R248, are critical to the stabilization of the binding.

  Compared with single-site phosphorylation, simultaneous phosphorylation at S46 and T55 promotes the interaction between the p53 N-terminal domain (NTD) and the DNA-binding domain (DBD) more strongly. Interaction analysis further reveals that the binding interface between dual-phosphorylation NTD and DBD is primarily localized near the DNA-binding pocket of DBD. In comparison with pT55 p53, dual phosphorylation pS46/pT55 stabilizes the interaction between residue T55 and residue R248, leading to stronger competition with DNA. That is why dual phosphorylation further enhances the autoinhibitory capacity of p53-NTD. To further demonstrate the critical role of R248 in the p53-NTD and DBD binding, we performed simulations on the pT55-p53 mutant R248A. By following the same simulation protocol as pT55-p53, we obtained a different binding free energy landscape (Figure S2). The results show that after the mutation, the free energies of the AD2-DBD bound states are greatly increased.


### Molecular Mechanism of p53-NTD and DBD Binding
  To elucidate the binding process of the p53 NTD, we analyzed the key interactions present in different intermediate states identified from the energy minima on the free energy surface. Figure  A depicts the residue interaction probabilities for pT55 in various NTD conformational states. In state 1 ( s1 ), the NTD and DBD remain relatively separated, with few contacts between them, and the detected interactions are primarily transient and involve charged residues near the N-terminus of the NTD and certain residues within the DBD.

  Phosphorylation at T55 markedly increases the propensity of the NTD to associate stably with the DBD. The main interaction interfaces include the AD2 motif of the NTD and the DNA-binding region of the DBD (Figures   and ). This process is mediated by electrostatic interactions between the negatively charged pT55 and basic residues in the DBD (especially R248) effectively acting as a molecular switch that tethers the AD2 to the DBD. In state 2 ( s2,  Figure  B), pT55 and R248 form a stable contact, with an interaction probability exceeding 50%.

  As the binding proceeds, additional interactions further stabilize the NTD-DBD complex. These include salt bridges such as E51-R280 ( s2 ) and D48-R280 ( s3 ), hydrogen bonds such as S46–N288 ( s2 ) and W53-R273 ( s3 ), and hydrophobic interactions such as W53–V274 ( s5 ). Moreover, residues W53 and F54 insert into hydrophobic clefts in the DBD’s DNA-binding pocket (Figure   and D), which contributes as a key step in the phosphorylation-mediated regulation of p53 autoinhibition. F54 forms hydrophobic contacts with M237 and I251, while W53 interacts with V272 and V274 via hydrophobic effects and with R273 through a cation-π interaction. From state 4 ( s4 ) to state 5 ( s5 ), W53 and F54 penetrate deeper into the hydrophobic pocket of the DBD (Figure  C and D), strengthening NTD–DBD binding. Ultimately, the NTD occupies the DNA-binding surface of the DBD, thereby preventing DBD–DNA association and inhibiting p53’s transcriptional activity.


### The Regulation of the Binding Process of p53-NTD and DBD by
Dual-Site Phosphorylation
  Dual-site phosphorylation at S46 and T55 enhances the binding between the p53 NTD and DBD. Previous NMR experiments have shown that dual-site phosphorylation further strengthens the autoinhibitory function of the N-terminal region of p53. Analysis of residue–residue interactions between p53 NTD and DBD across different states reveals that, compared to single phosphorylation at T55, phosphorylated S46 also engages in strong interactions with DBD residues (Figure  A). These interactions are primarily electrostatic, driven by the negative charge introduced by phosphorylation. However, pS46 mainly interacts with positively charged residues in the C-terminal region of the DBD, and these interactions do not synergize directly with the binding of phosphorylated T55 to the DNA-binding pocket of the DBD. That is, regardless of whether pT55 is bound to the DBD (as in  s2,  Figure  B), pS46 can bind to the DBD in a relatively stable manner. Conversely, the binding of pS46 may reduce the conformational entropy of the p53 NTD, bringing it closer to the DBD surface and thereby facilitating the interaction between pT55 and the DNA-binding pocket (Figure  C and D). This mechanism explains how dual phosphorylation at S46 and T55 further enhances the N-terminal autoinhibition of p53.


## Discussion

### Comparison with Experiments and Previous
Computational Results
  Many pioneering experimental works have studied the structural ensemble of p53-NTD, the binding mechanism of p53-NTD and DBD, and the phosphorylation regulation of p53. For example, He et al. demonstrated through NMR that the AD2 motif interacts with the DNA-binding surface of the DBD, competitively inhibiting DNA binding. Their chemical shift perturbation (CSP) analysis revealed significant perturbations for AD2 residues W53 and F54, as well as DBD residues G245 and R248 upon complex formation. Similarly, Krois et al. employed chemical shift analysis and paramagnetic relaxation enhancement (PRE) experiments to show that the amphipathic AD1 and AD2 motifs interact electrostatically with the DBD’s DNA-binding surface, with AD2 dominating the interactions. Subsequent work revealed that Thr55 phosphorylation enhances competitive AD2-DBD interactions and inhibits DNA binding, while dual phosphorylation at S46 and T55 further strengthens this interaction. Pronounced CSPs were observed for backbone amides from W53 to E56 following T55 phosphorylation.

  Our simulation results are consistent with the experimental observations and provide atomistic details of the p53-NTD and DBD binding regulation mechanism by phosphorylation. By using REST3 enhanced sampling simulations, we reconstructed the structural ensemble showing p53 NTD remains largely disordered, with transient helical structures in AD1 and AD2 motifs. The computed chemical shifts show good agreement with experiments, with average deviations of ∼0.6 ppm for Cα atoms and ∼1.0 ppm for backbone amide nitrogen atoms. Phosphorylation reduces intradomain interactions within the NTD and promotes conformational expansion: phosphorylation at S46 leads to greater extension than at T55, and dual phosphorylation results in an even more disordered state, in line with experimental reports that phosphorylation weakens intraNTD contacts. Free-energy landscapes reconstructed via bias-exchange metadynamics show that T55 phosphorylation significantly stabilizes conformations with high NTD-DBD contact frequency, shifting the ensemble toward bound states. Phosphorylation at S46 alone has only a modest effect, whereas dual phosphorylation further lowers the free energy of the bound state.

  The compactness results (reflected by the radius of gyration distribution) from our simulations are consistent with the findings of previous experimental work, with the average values being 1.95 ± 0.61 nm vs 2.04 ± 0.42 nm. To evaluate the effects of force field parameters and initial structures on the simulation results, we carried out REST3 simulations with the Amber14SB force field and TIP4P water model to characterize the structural ensemble of the wild-type p53 NTD. The resulting Rg values are comparable to those from our simulations (Figure S3). While conventional MD simulations are highly susceptible to initial conformations, the above results demonstrate that the enhanced sampling approach used in this work could greatly eliminate the influence of initial structures.

  Analysis of the bound states reveals that stable association between the p53 NTD and DBD is primarily driven by interactions involving the AD2 segment of the NTD and residues on the DNA-binding pocket of the DBD, including the residues at the DNA-binding sites, such as the positively charged residue R248 on the DBD loop L3. The chemical shift perturbation of residue 248 is also observed in the NMR experimental studies. Interactions involving aromatic residues F54 and W53 with the DBD also align with significant CSPs reported for these residues.

  However, some of our results also exhibit subtle discrepancies in comparison to the experimental observations. For instance, the measured helical populations for AD1 and AD2 are approximately 30% and 5% by NMR experiments while our simulations yield values of about 10% for AD1 and 6% for AD2 (Figure S4). This discrepancy likely arises from the significant uncertainty associated with helicity measurements in highly dynamic, intrinsically disordered regions, where subtle spectral variations can substantially influence the derived secondary structure contents. Therefore, obtaining a structural ensemble whose secondary structure distribution exactly matches the experimental data remains challenging. Moreover, the role of PRD remains controversial in experimental studies. For example, He et al. suggested that the PRD promotes NTD-DBD interactions, while Sun et al. reported that removal of the PRD strengthens the AD2-DBD interaction and leads to autoinhibition of DNA binding even in the absence of Thr55 phosphorylation. In our simulations, no stable hydrogen bonds or ionic bonds involving the PRD residue in the NTD-DBD bound state are observed, suggesting a limited regulatory role. Finally, although experimental work proposes that phosphorylation at both S46 and T55 enhances NTAD-DBD interaction via a graded regulatory mechanism, our simulations do not support such a mechanism. Instead, phosphorylation at T55 appears to play the dominant and driving role, while phosphorylation at S46 contributes mainly by reducing the conformational entropy of the N-terminal region, thereby further stabilizing the NTD-DBD complex.

  Compared with the previous computational work to study the phosphorylation effects of p53-NTD, (1) our study was designed to directly address the central question of the p53 autoinhibitory function, i.e., how phosphorylation alters the atomic-level interactions between p53-NTD and DBD. We carefully constructed multiple reaction coordinates that focus on potential interacting residues between NTD and DBD, while considering the possible different binding modes. This research strategy not only allows a comprehensive assessment of the binding between p53-NTD and DBD from a free energy perspective but also unveils the molecular mechanism underlying the binding mode most relevant to the autoinhibitory function of p53-NTD. (2) All-atom model that provides an accurate description of atomic-level interactions. As a result, our simulation results can be compared with experimentally measured values, such as chemical shifts, which are sensitive to local structural environments. Moreover, in the study of the binding mechanism between NTD and DBD, we identified not only phosphorylation-driven electrostatic interactions but also key hydrophobic residues that act cooperatively to stabilize the complex. Such detailed insights are accessible only through atomic-resolution modeling. Combined with the advanced enhanced sampling techniques employed in our work, the overall methodology demonstrates clear advantages in mechanistic precision and biological relevance. (3) Our findings reveal distinct functional roles among phosphorylation sites within AD2 of p53-NTD. Although both S46 and T55 are located in the AD2 motif, pS46 does not promote the autoinhibitory function of p53. Our study provides an atomic-level explanation for this phenomenon, as well as for the ability of pS46 to enhance the autoinhibitory effect mediated by pT55.


### Regulation
of Intrinsically Disordered Protein Structure and
Function by Phosphorylation
  Phosphorylation is a common post-translational modification in intrinsically disordered proteins and regions, which enhances residue polarity and introduces electrostatic charges. The phosphorylation of Thr55 (T55) and Ser46 (S46) reduced the internal inter-residue interactions of p53-NTD. This phenomenon can be attributed to the high abundance of negatively charged residues in p53-NTD: the 61-residue fragment contains 15 negatively charged residues. The introduction of negatively charged phosphate groups enhances the internal electrostatic repulsion, leading to an increase in the radius of gyration (Rg) of the system, a more extended conformational state, and consequently a reduction in the number of internal interactions (Figure S5). However, phosphorylation of T55 was observed to moderately enhance inter-residue interactions, thereby strengthening the binding between AD1 and AD2.

  Experimental studies have confirmed that p53-NTD and DBD interactions are electrostatically driven, as evidenced by their weakening at high salt concentrations. ,  Phosphorylation can increase the negative charge of residues, thereby strengthening electrostatic attraction between the NTD and positively charged regions of the DBD, especially the DNA-binding pocket region. Our analysis of the NTD-DBD binding process further indicates that phosphorylation at T55 plays a critical role during the initial stages of complex formation, supporting the conclusions that phosphorylation modulates protein function by electrostatic interactions.

  However, the electrostatic interactions are not the only factor strengthening the p53-NTD and DBD binding. Although phosphorylation at both S46 and T55 increases the local negative charge within the NTD, our simulations indicate that phosphorylation at S46 has a comparatively modest effect on promoting NTD and DBD association. Previous studies have shown that phosphorylated residues can cooperate with hydrophobic residues, especially aromatic residues, to form local hydrophobic clusters, which in turn regulate the function of intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs). ,  Similarly, we found that phosphorylation at T55 induces reorientation of the side chains of adjacent aromatic residues F54 and W53. This rearrangement promotes the formation of specific local hydrophobic clusters that enhance contact with the DBD, while also positioning F54 and W53 into a parallel orientation conducive to insertion into the hydrophobic DNA-binding pocket of the DBD, thereby stabilizing the bound-state conformation. The evolutionary conservation analysis also shows that W53 and F54 are highly conserved (Figure S6). In support of this, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) experiments by He et al. also reported significant chemical shift perturbations for the resonances of W53 and F54, indicating conformational reorganization upon phosphorylation.


### Roles of Multisite Phosphorylation
  Compared to single-site phosphorylation, multisite phosphorylation further enhances the binding affinity between the NTD and DBD. The changes in structural ensembles of the p53 NTD induced by multisite phosphorylation are comparable with those induced by single-site phosphorylation. Thus, the underlying mechanism for enhanced binding is likely attributable to an overall amplification of electrostatic charge. After phosphorylation at S46, although it does not form stable interactions with residues in the DNA-binding pocket of the DBD, the increased negative surface charge promotes electrostatic attraction to basic residues on the DBD surface. This interaction reduces the conformational entropy of the AD2 motif and its neighboring regions, thereby facilitating the interaction of phosphorylated T55 with residues near the DNA-binding pocket. Functioning like a dual-lock mechanism, this process further stabilizes the p53 NTD within the DNA-binding pocket and prevents p53 from binding DNA. These findings suggest that the functional regulation of intrinsically disordered proteins, such as p53, by multisite phosphorylation involves a complex process.

  The CREB-binding protein (CBP) and p300 play critical roles in the regulation of p53 structure and function. It was found that phosphorylation at the p53-NTD sites around AD1 region (such as S15, T18, and S20) would greatly enhance the CBD/p300 and p53 interactions. Although this study primarily focuses on the phosphorylation of S46 and T55 in the AD2 region and its impact on the interaction with its own DBD, as well as its potential role in the self-inhibitory function, our approach can naturally be extended to investigate how phosphorylation regulates the binding between p53-NTD and CBP/p300 proteins, thereby elucidating the molecular mechanisms by which multiple phosphorylation sites modulate their interaction.


### Model of p53
Autoinhibition Regulated by Phosphorylation
  Based on computational simulation results, we propose a dynamic model illustrating how multiphosphorylation regulates the structure and function of p53 (Figure  ). In wild-type p53, the NTD remains in a freely mobile conformation with a relatively compact overall structure and only weak transient interactions with the DBD of p53. Under such conditions, the NTD exerts minimal autoinhibitory effects, allowing the p53 DBD to bind with DNA. Upon phosphorylation at T55, the introduced negative charge on p53-NTD enables tight binding to the positively charged residue R248 within the DNA-binding pocket via electrostatic interactions. Subsequently, nearby aromatic residues F54 and W53 insert into the hydrophobic pocket of DBP, further strengthening the NTD and DBD association. As a result, pT55 confers autoinhibitory function to p53, partially restricting its DNA-binding and transcriptional activity. However, the N-terminal region remains highly dynamic due to the lack of anchoring, and its mobility may facilitate the dissociation of phosphorylated T55 from the DBP, thereby attenuating the autoinhibition mediated by the NTD. When S46 is also phosphorylated, pS46 establishes stable interactions with residues such as K120 and R280 in the DBD. The p53NTD remains largely disordered after its binding to the DBD promoted by phosphorylation at T55. The high fluctuations of the p53NTD can lead to occasional detachment from the DBD. Although the phosphorylation at S46 does not induce direct interactions between it and residues of DBP, pS46 interacts with positively charged residues on the DBD surface outside the DBP pocket, creating an additional binding anchor-termed a dual-lock mechanism-that further stabilizes the association between the p53NTD and the DBD. This anchoring effect enhances the AD2-DBP binding affinity, further reinforcing the autoinhibitory capacity of p53.


## Conclusions
  Multisite phosphorylation plays important roles in cellular activities such as cell cycle control and circadian rhythm regulation and is critically involved in the onset and progression of cancer as well as neurodegenerative diseases. As one of the most important tumor suppressor proteins in humans, the function of p53 is modulated by phosphorylation, with numerous phosphorylation sites located within its intrinsically disordered terminal domains. Phosphorylation at multiple sites within the N-terminal domain of p53 can enhance its autoinhibitory function, thereby repressing the downstream DNA transcriptional activity. However, the molecular mechanisms of single-site and multisite phosphorylation to regulate p53 autoinhibition remain largely unclear.

  In this study, we employed all-atom molecular dynamics simulations combined with enhanced sampling methods to study the effects of phosphorylation on the intrinsically disordered region of p53-NTD. The REST3 simulations were first performed to characterize the structural ensembles of the p53-NTD before and after phosphorylation. The results show that phosphorylation not only affects the local structures near the phosphorylation sites but also increases the global extensibility and changes the long-range interactions. Moreover, dual phosphorylation at pS46 and pT55 exhibits a mutually compensatory effect on the p53-NTD structure. The single-site phosphorylation at either S46 or T55 significantly promotes the interaction between p53-NTD and DBD. However, only T55 phosphorylation promotes interaction between the AD2 and DBD domains, thereby inducing self-inhibition. pT55 forms synergistic interactions with surrounding hydrophobic residues to jointly stabilize NTD-DBD binding. Dual phosphorylation at pS46 and pT55 acts as a double safeguard, reducing the conformational entropy associated with NTD dynamics and thereby enhancing the self-inhibition ability of p53. This study provides an in-depth molecular mechanism for how p53-NTD phosphorylation regulates structure and function, laying a foundation for understanding the molecular basis of p53 self-inhibition and related diseases.


## Method

### Simulation Setup
  The initial structures of p53-NTD (residues 1–61) were generated using PyMOL to construct a fully extended conformation. Then, they were placed into an aqueous solution and equilibrated for 100 ns by conventional MD simulation. The CHARMM36m force field parameters were utilized for the proteins, including the phosphorylated residues. Each system was placed in a rectangular simulation box filled with TIP4P water, and sodium ions (Na+) were added to neutralize the net charge. The simulation box size is 10.0 × 10.0 × 10.0 nm3, which was large enough to solvate the protein, even in its extended state (Figure S7). Energy minimization was performed by using the steepest descent method for 50,000 steps. The systems were then equilibrated in NVT ensemble, which was run for 100 ps with the Berendsen thermostat to heat the systems, followed by 100 ps NPT ensemble run for pressure and density equilibration using the Parrinello–Rahman barostat at 1 atm. ,  After equilibration, conventional MD simulations were carried out for 100 ns. Throughout the simulations, a time step of 2  fs was used. Long-range electrostatics were computed by the particle mesh Ewald (PME) method, van der Waals interactions were truncated at 1 nm, and all bond lengths were constrained using the LINCS algorithm. All MD simulations were conducted using GROMACS  2022.5.


### REST3 Simulation
  Solute-Tempered Replica Scaling (REST3) is designed to enhance sampling efficiency when simulating biomolecules with explicit solvents. In REST3 simulations, the Hamiltonian of selected “solute” regions is scaled, effectively tuning only the degrees of freedom of interest rather than the entire system, thereby reducing the number of replicas required for a given temperature range. A high-temperature (500 K) MD simulation based on the conformations generated in the 100 ns conventional MD simulations was performed, and then 16 structures were randomly selected from the high-temperature MD simulation to serve as the initial structures for REST3. The conformations sampled by the high-temperature MDs were randomly selected and used as starting structures for REST3 simulations (Figures S8–S11). In our simulations, topology files from 16 randomly generated conformations were merged into a single complete topology file, which was subsequently scaled for REST3. Each replica then proceeded with simulation using the scaled topology. The preprocessing procedures, computational methods, and simulation parameters were identical with those described in the preceding section, including energy minimization, NVT-based preheating, and NPT-based pressure equilibration. Following equilibration, REST3 production simulations were performed with data exchange attempts between replicas every 2 ps. Each system was simulated for a total accumulated time of 33.6 μs, with every replica running for 2,100 ns. The convergence of REST3 was estimated by the time evolution of the free energy landscape projected onto the first two principal components (PCs). As shown in Figure S12, during the last 500 ns of the simulation, the overall shape of the free energy surface and the distribution of the deepest minima remained essentially unchanged. Analysis of the free energy surface difference (Figure S13) further indicates that the free energy landscape reached equilibrium in the final 500 ns, with an average free energy difference of less than 1 kJ/mol.


### Bias-Exchange Metadynamics (BE-MetaD)
  The p53 DBD structure was built based on the X-ray structure of p53 (PDB ID: 2AHI ) The first monomer structure in the PDB file was selected and extended at both termini to build as the initial structures of NTD-DBD binding simulations, and the simulated systems were consisted of p53 residues 1–300. The simulation box size was 12.0 × 12.0 × 12.0 nm3. Bias-exchange metadynamics (BE-MetaD) combines replica exchange dynamics with metadynamics, enabling exchange simulations over multiple predefined collective variables (CVs). In this study, six CVs were defined. CV1: number of residue contacts between AD2 of p53-NTD and residues 273 to 294 on DBD; CV2: number of residue contacts between AD2 of p53-NTD and residues 238 to 249 on DBD; CV3: number of residue contacts between AD2 of p53-NTD and residues 117 to 123 on DBD; CV4: α-RMSD of AD1; CV5: α-RMSD of AD2; CV6: distance between the center of mass (COM) of AD2 and COM of DNA-binding pocket residues. Here, α-RMSD (α root-mean-square deviation) quantifies the similarity between protein’s α-helical region and ideal α-helix structure. When the distance between the centers of mass of the heavy atoms in the side chains of two residues is less than 5 Å, we consider them to be in contact. For the CV involving contacts, it is calculated by selecting residues in the defined regions and summing the contacts between residue pairs: 1 q(R1,R2)=∑i∈R1,j∈R2Cij where R 1 and R 2 are the residue sets in two structural regions. The contact calculation is performed by summing over all pairwise contacts between residues: 2 Cij=1−(rij−d0r0)n1−(rij−d0r0)m where r  ij  is the distance between the center of mass (COM) of the side-chain heavy atoms of residues i and j. Here, r0 is set to be 0.5 nm, n and m are equal to 6 and 12, respectively.

  The Gaussian bias heights for CV1–CV6 were set to 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, and 0.5, respectively, with Gaussian widths of 0.7, 0.4, 0.4, 0.2, 0.2, and 0.05. Replica exchanges were attempted every 2,500 steps, and Gaussian potentials were deposited every 5 ps with a bias factor of 32. All simulations were performed at 300 K and 1 atm using GROMACS 2022.5 interfaced with PLUMED 2.9. The simulation time of the four systems ranged from 500 to 1000 ns, depending on the convergence of the free energy surface. The one-dimensional free energy profiles and corresponding errors for all CVs are presented in Figures S14–S19. Convergence tests of the 2D free energy surfaces are provided in Figure S20. As shown in the figure, the average deviations of free energy states in the final 100 ns are less than 3 kJ/mol. The error estimation of the free energy profiles and free energy surfaces is carried out by evaluating the variation in free energy over different time windows during the simulation. For one-dimensional free energy profiles, the free energy is estimated by dividing the CV coordinate into discrete bins, s  i . The free energy value in each bin s  i  is computed for successive time segments, and the standard deviation across these segments is taken as the error; we used the final 100 ns of the simulation, dividing it into 20 ns blocks and calculating the free energy for each block for 1D free energy error estimation. For two-dimensional free energy surfaces, the surface is divided into grids, each grid is treated as a free energy state. The change in the free energy value of each grid over simulation time is calculated. The changes across all grid points are then averaged to analyze the convergence of the 2D free energy surface as 3 εG=∑s|Fs(t+Δt)−Fs(t)|2N where the free energy surfaces were divided into N small grids, the differences between free energies Fs (t + Δt) – Fs (t)­of grid s obtained in simulation time t and t+Δt (here Δt = 20 ns) are calculated and averaged over all the grids. The free energy F s(t) and Fs(t + Δt) are corresponding to the time periods of 0-t ns and 0-(t+Δt) ns, respectively.


### Structure Analysis
  Secondary chemical shifts originate from variations in protein secondary structures and provide valuable information about the structural organization. In this study, SPARTA+ was employed for the chemical shift prediction. A sufficient number of frames were extracted from the MD trajectories, and each frame was analyzed with SPARTA+. The predicted values were then averaged over all frames. Secondary chemical shifts for individual residues were obtained by comparing these averaged values with random coil reference values reported by Wishart et al.  This approach enabled quantitative evaluation of the conformational characteristics of intrinsically disordered proteins within the MD ensembles.


### Free Energy
Calculation
  Free energies from the BE-MetaD simulations were calculated using the METAGUI 3 plugin in VMD. In this reconstruction scheme, selected collective variables (CVs) were divided into multiple intervals to construct a multidimensional CV grid. Simulation conformations were clustered according to their CV values, yielding coordinate–energy data pairs that defined the free energy landscape. Two-dimensional and three-dimensional free energy maps were subsequently generated by using plotting software. An in-house script was used to visualize BE-MetaD results, allowing for the identification and detailed characterization of the principal conformational families observed during the simulations.
