# Multi-omics integration reveals ANXA6-high γδ T cell–endothelial communication as a potential link between periodontitis and MASLD


## Abstract
  Periodontitis (PD) and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) were highly prevalent inflammatory disorders that frequently coexisted, yet the molecular basis of their comorbidity remained poorly defined. Here, we applied an integrative multi-omics strategy that combined bulk RNA sequencing, weighted gene co-expression network analysis, spatial transcriptomics, single-cell profiling, and machine learning. This approach identified 11 hub genes bridging immune activation and metabolic remodeling, among which Annexin A6 (ANXA6) emerged as a key cross-disease candidate. Spatial transcriptomics supported tissue-specific localization of the hub-gene signature, while single-cell analysis revealed selective enrichment of ANXA6 expression in γδ T cells. Notably, ANXA6-high γδ T cells exhibited enhanced signaling interactions with endothelial cells, suggesting immune-vascular crosstalk in PD-MASLD comorbidity. Upstream regulatory analysis further highlighted transcription factors associated with ANXA6 expression, and Connectivity Map-based prediction suggested candidate compounds with the potential to reverse ANXA6-associated transcriptional programs. Collectively, these findings support the hypothesis that ANXA6-high γδ T cell–endothelial communication represents a shared immunological feature of PD–MASLD comorbidity, offering novel insights into common immune programs and potential therapeutic opportunities.


## Introduction
  Periodontitis (PD), a chronic inflammatory disease of the periodontal tissues, represented a significant global health problem1. It was characterized by progressive destruction of the periodontal ligament and alveolar bone and, if left untreated, ultimately led to tooth loss2. Global estimates indicated that severe periodontitis affected about 1 billion people worldwide, imposing a substantial burden through lost productivity and healthcare costs3. Epidemiological meta-analysis estimated that among dentate adults between 2011 and 2020, the overall prevalence of periodontitis was about 62%, with severe periodontitis at about 24%4,5. PD was increasingly recognized not just as an oral disease but as one with systemic effects6, since its inflammatory mediators, microbial products, and immune cell activation could enter circulation and potentially affect distant organs7,8.

  Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), formerly referred to as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease9, emerged as the most prevalent chronic liver disorder worldwide10. Global epidemiological assessments indicated that MASLD affected roughly 25–30% of the adult population11, with prevalence rising to more than 60% among individuals with obesity or type 2 diabetes12. MASLD encompasses a spectrum of histological lesions, ranging from simple steatosis to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis, progressive fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma13,14. Beyond liver-related morbidity and mortality, MASLD was increasingly recognized as a systemic condition, strongly linked to cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, and extra-hepatic malignancies13,15,16.

  Growing epidemiological evidence suggested a bidirectional association between periodontitis and MASLD17. Individuals with periodontitis showed increased odds of developing hepatic steatosis and significant fibrosis18, and greater periodontal severity was linked to worse liver function indices19. Mechanistically, translocation of periodontal pathogens such as Porphyromonas gingivalis and their virulence factors into the circulation could promote systemic endotoxemia and hepatic immune activation, thereby exacerbating steatosis and inflammation20,21. In parallel, periodontal inflammation contributed to a systemic pro-inflammatory milieu characterized by elevated cytokines22 and oxidative stress23, which have been proposed to synergize with metabolic dysfunction and contribute to liver injury24. Conversely, metabolic factors such as obesity, insulin resistance, and dyslipidemia—the hallmarks of MASLD—were also recognized risk factors for periodontal breakdown25,26, suggesting shared pathogenic pathways and reinforcing the concept of an oral-liver axis27.

  Despite accumulating evidence, the precise molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying the immune-metabolic crosstalk between PD and MASLD remained poorly understood, and current studies had yet to identify conserved candidate determinants and cell-state programs that spanned both diseases. Most existing work emphasized epidemiological associations and systemic biomarkers—such as elevated cytokines, oxidative stress markers, and metabolic disturbances—without delineating integrated, cross-tissue molecular-cellular programs28. Furthermore, investigations typically relied on single-disease models or single-omics approaches, limiting insight into cross-tissue interactions and convergent pathogenic pathways29. Although animal experiments showed that periodontitis exacerbated hepatic steatosis through sphingolipid metabolism and insulin resistance30, the precise cross-tissue molecular determinants and mechanisms remained undefined. Addressing this gap was critical for delineating the biological basis of PD-MASLD comorbidity and for identifying tractable therapeutic targets19.

  To address this research gap, we established a comprehensive multi-omics framework that integrated bulk RNA sequencing, single-cell RNA sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, and machine-learning algorithms. This framework overcame the limitations of previous single-disease studies by enabling systematic analysis of both periodontal and hepatic tissues, thereby uncovering cellular and molecular targets that would have been difficult to identify in isolated investigations. Notably, through integrative multi-omics analysis, we identified Annexin A6 (ANXA6) as a core hub gene shared between PD and MASLD. ANXA6 was consistently expressed at high levels in γδ T cells, a subset that was significantly expanded in both diseases. Importantly, γδ T cells in PD and MASLD showed prominent interactions with endothelial populations, prioritizing ANXA6-high γδ T cells as a convergent candidate cell state associated with PD–MASLD comorbidity and suggesting enhanced immune–endothelial crosstalk. This discovery strengthens the conceptual framework of the oral–liver axis and nominates candidate cell states and molecular features for future mechanistic validation and therapeutic exploration.


## Results

### Single-cell transcriptomic analysis revealed disease-specific immune and stromal remodeling in PD and MASLD
  To delineate the cellular heterogeneity of periodontitis (PD) and MASLD, we analyzed publicly available scRNA-seq datasets from gingival tissues of PD and healthy controls (GSE164241, GSE152042, GSE171213) and from liver tissues of MASLD (GSE202379).   In MASLD livers, single-cell transcriptomic profiling revealed four transcriptionally distinct hepatocyte subsets (Hep1-Hep4), along with non-parenchymal populations including immune cells (macrophages, γδ T cells, B cells, plasma cells), endothelial cells (BECs, LECs), fibroblasts, and epithelial cells (Fig. 2C). Marker-based annotation confirmed hepatocytes by ALB, TTR, APOB, and HNF4A, epithelial cells by EPCAM, fibroblasts by COL1A1, endothelial cells by CDH5 and PECAM1, macrophages and neutrophils by LYZ and CSF3R, B cells by MS4A1, and T-cell subsets by CD4, CD8A, and TRDC (Fig. 2D).

  Comparative analysis revealed disease-specific remodeling of immune and stromal compartments (Fig. 2E, F). In PD, plasma cells, neutrophils, γδ T cells, CD8⁺ T cells, macrophages, and CD4⁺ T cells were significantly expanded, whereas LECs, melanocytes, fibroblasts, and epithelial cells were reduced. In MASLD, Hep4 and Hep3 hepatocytes, fibroblasts, epithelial cells, and γδ T cells were increased, whereas macrophages, neutrophils, CD8⁺ T cells, endothelial cells, and Hep1 hepatocytes were diminished compared with controls.

  Together, these integrative analyzes of public scRNA-seq datasets provided comprehensive single-cell atlases of PD gingival tissues and MASLD livers, revealing disease-specific remodeling across immune, stromal, and parenchymal compartments.


### Differential gene expression and immune deconvolution analyses revealed disease-specific remodeling in PD and MASLD
  Building on the single-cell atlas of PD and MASLD, we next investigated the transcriptional alterations to better understand the molecular factors associated with the observed cellular remodeling. Differential expression analysis revealed extensive transcriptomic reprogramming in both diseases. In gingival tissues from PD lesions, 951 genes were significantly upregulated and 1029 genes were downregulated relative to healthy controls (Fig. 3A). In MASLD livers, 600 genes were upregulated and 625 genes were downregulated compared with healthy livers (Fig. 3B). Despite the large number of DEGs in both datasets, the overlap between diseases was relatively modest (Fig. 3C). Among upregulated DEGs, only 30 genes were shared, while 921 and 570 were uniquely upregulated in PD and MASLD, respectively.   To gain insights into shared transcriptional signatures, we performed Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyzes. Shared upregulated genes were mainly involved in immune-related pathways, including cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, tumor necrosis factor (TNF) signaling, and IL-17 signaling, as well as pathways associated with viral protein interactions and lipoic acid metabolism (Fig. 3D). In contrast, shared downregulated genes were enriched in metabolic and biosynthetic programs, such as biosynthesis of cofactors, phosphatidylinositol signaling, and glycerophospholipid metabolism (Fig. 3E).

  Given the immune-centric transcriptional rewiring, we next examined immune cell composition using CIBERSORT deconvolution. Consistent with the single-cell findings, PD gingival tissues showed increased plasma cells, γδ T cells, and neutrophils, alongside depletion of naïve and memory B cells, regulatory T cells, and M2 macrophages relative to healthy controls (Fig. 3F). MASLD livers exhibited a distinct immune architecture, characterized by elevated γδ T cells, activated NK cells, M1 macrophages, and neutrophils, whereas resting NK cells, naïve B cells, and M2 macrophages were markedly reduced compared with healthy livers (Fig. 3G). Finally, stacked bar plots summarized the distribution of inferred immune subsets across all samples, emphasizing the divergent immune remodeling patterns between PD and MASLD (Fig. 3H).

  Integrative analysis of these key WGCNA modules with DEGs identified 11 overlapping hub genes shared between PD and MASLD (Fig. 4F), including ANXA6, APOE, GAS6, GLDC, HYOU1, IL32, LIME1, MMP9, SLCO2B1, TAPBPL, and TNFSF13. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that these shared hub genes were significantly involved in immune regulation and metabolic pathways, including lipoic acid metabolism, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, folate and glyoxylate metabolism, and the intestinal immune network for IgA production (Fig. 4G), suggesting that these genes linked immune signaling with metabolic remodeling.

  Collectively, these findings defined a core set of shared immune-metabolic candidate genes that may be associated with γδ T-cell expansion and plasma cell activation in both PD and MASLD, providing insights into the possible processes underlying convergent immune remodeling across these distinct tissue contexts.


### Machine learning prioritized ANXA6 and TNFSF13 as cross-tissue biomarkers linking PD and MASLD
  Building on the 11 shared hub genes identified above, we next used a multi-algorithm machine-learning framework as an orthogonal prioritization step to evaluate their discriminative performance and to nominate a compact set of cross-tissue candidates for downstream biological interpretation. We first evaluated the classification performance of eight predictive models, including random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), generalized linear model (GLM), gradient boosting machine (GBM), k-nearest neighbor (KNN), neural network (NNET), least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, and decision tree (DT).

  For PD, the RF model showed the highest area under the curve (AUC) (0.947), followed by SVM (AUC = 0.887) and GBM (AUC = 0.895) (Fig. 5A).   For MASLD, six models (RF, SVM, GBM, KNN, NNET, and LASSO) achieved an apparent AUC of 1.000 in this cohort, while GLM also showed high performance (AUC = 0.978); DT showed a lower AUC (0.809) (Fig. 5D). We note that perfect or near-perfect AUC values in a single cohort can sometimes be influenced by dataset-specific structure (e.g., cohort composition, sample size, class separability, and technical/batch characteristics) rather than biology alone. We assessed performance using a held-out test split and cross-validation, and examined model stability using complementary diagnostics (e.g., RF out-of-bag error curves), together with feature-level analyzes (single-gene ROC and SHAP). Nevertheless, independent external cohorts will be important to further confirm the generalizability of these performance estimates. ANXA6 and TNFSF13 were identified as the strongest predictors by single-gene ROC analysis (AUC = 0.990 and 0.967, respectively), followed by GAS6 and SLCO2B1 (Fig. 5E).

  Random forest error curves showed convergence of out-of-bag (OOB) error in both diseases (Fig. 5C, F). Supplementary Fig. 1 provides further quantitative data, showing the feature importance ranking for PD and MASLD based on the Random Forest model, with ANXA6 ranking highest in both diseases, consistent with its discriminative performance in these datasets (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Leveraging this framework, we integrated RF feature ranking with LASSO and SVM-RFE to refine candidates (Fig. 5G, I). For each model, we first intersected the genes selected independently from PD and MASLD to identify model-specific overlaps, yielding 5 genes for LASSO, 12 genes for SVM-RFE, and 6 genes for random forest. We then intersected these model-specific overlaps across the three methods, which yielded two consistently selected cross-disease candidates, ANXA6 and TNFSF13 (Fig. 5J).

  To further evaluate the association between these candidates and disease status, we trained gradient-boosted decision tree classifiers (XGBoost) in both multiclass (HC/PD/MASLD) and one-versus-rest settings and quantified feature contributions using Shapley additive explanations (SHAP). In PD, ANXA6 and TNFSF13 exhibited the highest global mean absolute SHAP values, followed by GLDC, SLCO2B1, LIME1, and MMP9; in MASLD, TNFSF13 and ANXA6 likewise ranked as the top contributors (Fig. 5K).

  The directional-push heatmap showed a consistently positive directional push toward disease classification, and these genes were consistently upregulated in both PD gingival tissues and MASLD livers compared with HC (Fig. 5K, L). Together, these findings prioritized ANXA6 and TNFSF13 as cross-tissue candidate markers linking PD and MASLD, suggesting that their coordinated upregulation may reflect convergent immunometabolic remodeling observed in both diseases.

In PD gingival tissues, RCTD-based deconvolution revealed spatially distinct immune and stromal compartments, including γδ T cells, macrophages, fibroblasts, epithelial cells, endothelial cells, B cells, and plasma cells (Fig. 6A).   We next analyzed the spatial expression of the 11 hub genes across annotated cell types (Fig. 6C, D). In PD, ANXA6 and TNFSF13 were predominantly expressed in epithelial cells (Epi) and γδ T cells. In MASLD, ANXA6 was mainly localized to γδ T cells and fibroblasts (Fibro), whereas TNFSF13 was enriched in fibroblasts and the Hep1 hepatocyte subset.

  Notably, ANXA6 showed consistently high expression within γδ T cells across both PD and MASLD, suggesting that γδ T cells may represent a shared cellular context in which ANXA6 is consistently upregulated across the two diseases. In contrast, TNFSF13 displayed disease-specific enrichment, being preferentially localized to epithelial cells in PD and to fibroblasts and Hep1 hepatocytes in MASLD, consistent with a context-dependent association with local immune activation and metabolic remodeling.


### ANXA6-high γδ T cell interactions and potential therapeutic candidates in PD and MASLD
  As ANXA6 showed higher expression in γδ T cells in both PD and MASLD, we next focused on this population to explore potential functional implications. Violin plots showed heterogeneity of ANXA6 expression within γδ T cells, allowing stratification into ANXA6-high and ANXA6-low subsets for downstream analyzes (Fig. 7A). CellChat analysis indicated that outgoing signaling patterns differed between ANXA6-high and ANXA6-low subsets (Fig. 7B). In PD, ANXA6-high γδ T cells showed higher inferred signaling scores in immune-related pathways, including PARs, ITGB2, and MHC/chemokine signaling, compared with the low subset. In MASLD, ANXA6-high γδ T cells showed higher inferred signaling activity in SEMA4, TGF-β, and CD45 pathways relative to ANXA6-low cells. Network visualization indicated that in both PD and MASLD, ANXA6-high γδ T cells showed stronger inferred interactions with endothelial cell subsets than with other lineages (Fig. 7C). CellChat analysis of γδ T cell to endothelium communication showed subset-specific signaling differences (Fig. 7D). In PD, ANXA6-high γδ T cells showed higher inferred communication for CCL5-ACKR1 and ITGB2-ICAM1 interactions, whereas in MASLD, higher inferred PTPRC-MRC1 signaling was observed in the ANXA6-high subset.   Upstream regulatory analysis using SCENIC highlighted multiple transcription factors (TFs) positively associated with ANXA6 expression in each disease (Fig. 7E). In PD, regulators such as FOXP1, SOX2, NR5A1, and BHLHE40 showed strong positive associations with ANXA6, while in MASLD, factors including SNAI2, PPARA, and MITF were among the top associated candidates. Comparison of the two diseases further highlighted a subset of overlapping TFs (e.g., ZBTB7A, FOXP1, SOX2, BHLHE40, BCL6) with concordant positive correlations, consistent with a shared transcriptional signature associated with higher ANXA6 expression in γδ T cells (Fig. 7F).

  Building on these insights, a Connectivity Map–style perturbation analysis based on LINCS L1000 chemical perturbation signatures (queried via the Enrichr API) was applied to prioritize small-molecule compounds whose perturbation signatures were inversely associated with the ANXA6-associated transcriptional signature. The top-ranked perturbagens included GSK-461364, withaferin-A, vorinostat, AT-7519, BI-2536, and HKI-272, whose transcriptional signatures across multiple cell lines (e.g., THP1, SKBR3, HepG2) showed consistent inverse associations with the ANXA6-associated gene set (Fig. 7G). Collectively, these results support ANXA6-high γδ T cells as a candidate signaling state in both PD and MASLD, showing context-specific ligand–receptor patterns, transcriptional associations, and candidate drug-perturbation signatures, and nominate ANXA6 for future functional evaluation across these disease contexts.


## Discussion
  Our integrative single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analyzes suggested both convergent and disease-specific patterns of immunometabolic remodeling in PD and MASLD. Despite the distinct tissue environments, γδ T cells consistently emerged as an expanded immune subset, and ANXA6 was among the most prominent markers in this population, nominating it as a cross-disease candidate marker and a candidate component of shared immunometabolic programs across both diseases. Previous studies had established that γδ T cells were essential for barrier immunity and tissue surveillance31, played pathogenic roles in periodontitis through IL-17-mediated neutrophil recruitment and bone resorption32, and were increased and functionally active in metabolic liver disease, where liver-resident IL-17⁺ γδ T cells were sustained by microbiota and contributed to pathology33. Our findings extended these observations by prioritizing ANXA6-high γδ T cells as a convergent immune population associated with inflammatory features in both oral and hepatic contexts.

  At the transcriptional level, we identified 11 hub genes shared between PD and MASLD, including ANXA6, TNFSF13, APOE, and GAS6. These genes were enriched in pathways bridging immune regulation and metabolic remodeling, such as cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction and folate metabolism. This immune-metabolic coupling was consistent with prior evidence demonstrating that chronic inflammation and metabolic dysfunction were tightly interconnected in both periodontal and hepatic diseases34,35. Importantly, machine-learning analyzes consistently prioritized ANXA6 and TNFSF13 as consistently selected candidate markers with high discriminative performance within the analyzed cohorts, supporting potential translational relevance for comorbidity risk assessment pending external validation.

  Analysis of spatial transcriptomic datasets, recognized as a powerful approach for dissecting complex inflammatory niches36, enabled us to investigate the cellular origins of hub genes in PD and MASLD. We observed that ANXA6 was consistently enriched in γδ T cells across both diseases, whereas TNFSF13 displayed tissue-specific localization, being expressed in epithelial cells in PD and in fibroblasts and hepatocytes in MASLD.

  Further stratification of γδ T cells by ANXA6 expression revealed that ANXA6-high subsets preferentially interacted with endothelial populations via CCL5-ACKR1 and ITGB2-ICAM1 in PD, and PTPRC-MRC1 in MASLD, suggesting that endothelial crosstalk may represent a shared axis of tissue inflammation. This was supported by prior evidence showing that γδ T cells influenced endothelial cell function and contributed to vascular inflammation37, and that T cell-endothelial interactions were central regulators of chronic tissue remodeling38.

  In addition to direct ligand–receptor interactions between ANXA6-high γδ T cells and endothelial populations, our CellChat results and pathway-level signatures collectively suggest that soluble mediators may also participate in shaping the inflammatory microenvironment across PD and MASLD. Consistent with the enriched cytokine/chemokine-related programs and the subset-specific communication patterns observed at the endothelial interface, ANXA6-high γδ T cells may represent a candidate source of immunomodulatory signals that facilitate immune recruitment and endothelial activation in both tissues. Notably, because our analyzes are based on transcriptomic and communication inferences, the specific composition of the γδ T cell secretome (e.g., cytokines/chemokines and tissue-remodeling factors) was not directly measured here and will require dedicated secretome/proteomic profiling in future studies.

  These findings are consistent with a hypothesis that ANXA6-high γδ T cells are associated with immune-regulatory programs spanning cytokine/chemokine signaling and antigen-presentation-related pathways. Rather than establishing a direct causal bridge between innate and adaptive immunity, our results nominate antigen-presentation signatures as a candidate component of the ANXA6-high γδ T cell state that warrants targeted validation, for example, by assessing antigen-presentation gene expression at single-cell resolution and functional assays of downstream T-cell activation. In both PD and MASLD, our communication and pathway-level analyzes are consistent with a hypothesis that ANXA6-high γδ T cells may be associated with myeloid cell–enriched inflammatory niches, potentially through chemokine-linked programs and endothelial crosstalk. Together with the enhanced γδ T cell–endothelial interaction patterns, these signatures nominate a shared inflammatory microenvironment as a testable framework that warrants targeted validation (e.g., chemokine expression, myeloid recruitment assays, and endothelial activation readouts).

  Finally, a Connectivity Map–style perturbation analysis based on LINCS L1000 chemical perturbation signatures (via Enrichr) suggested candidate compounds predicted to reverse ANXA6-associated transcriptional signatures, converging on immune and metabolic pathways. While experimental validation was still required, drug repurposing efforts had successfully applied similar strategies in other inflammatory diseases39. These findings raised the possibility of biomarker-guided therapies targeting shared immunometabolic processes in PD and MASLD.

  In conclusion, our integrative analyzes suggested that ANXA6-high γδ T cells represent a conserved candidate immune population associated with both oral and liver inflammatory contexts. By integrating single-cell and spatial multi-omics with machine learning, we prioritized ANXA6 as a cross-tissue biomarker with translational potential and mechanistic implications that warrant functional validation. These results not only deepened our understanding of PD-MASLD comorbidity but also provided a rationale for biomarker-guided strategies that might ultimately have informed precision diagnostics and therapeutic interventions.

  While this study was primarily computational and integrated public transcriptomic, single-cell, and spatial datasets, direct functional validation will be required to test causality. Moreover, because our integrative analyzes relied on heterogeneous public datasets across platforms and cohorts, classification performance (including near-perfect AUCs) may be influenced by cohort structure and technical factors (e.g., platform-specific effects, batch characteristics, and sample-size–dependent separability), underscoring the need for independent external validation. Accordingly, these ROC/AUC estimates should be interpreted as cohort-level performance within the analyzed datasets rather than as definitive evidence of cross-cohort generalizability. Although our analyzes consistently implicated an ANXA6-high γδ T cell–endothelial communication program in both PD and MASLD, these findings should be interpreted as a multi-omics–supported mechanistic hypothesis rather than definitive proof of mechanism. Future work will include in vivo and in vitro experiments using disease-relevant models and mechanistic assays to test whether perturbing ANXA6 and/or γδ T cell–endothelial signaling alters inflammatory and metabolic phenotypes, and to evaluate the therapeutic tractability of this axis.


## Methods

### Experimental design
  This study integrated single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), and spatial transcriptomic datasets, all of which were obtained from public repositories, to investigate shared immunometabolic mechanisms in periodontitis (PD) and MASLD. Raw datasets were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), the Chinese National GeneBank’s CROST platform, and related public spatial transcriptome data portals.


### Single-cell RNA sequencing and analysis
  Public scRNA-seq datasets from gingival tissues of periodontitis (PD) (GSE164241, GSE152042, GSE171213) and liver tissues from MASLD (GSE202379) were downloaded from the GEO database. These datasets were selected based on their relevance to immune cell profiling in PD and MASLD, as well as their inclusion of sufficient metadata to allow for meaningful cell-type identification and disease-related analysis.

  Raw count matrices from the GEO datasets were processed using the Seurat package (v5.0.1) in R (v4.4.3). Initially, cells with fewer than 300 or more than 5000 detected genes, greater than 30% mitochondrial content, or greater than 30% ribosomal transcripts were excluded to eliminate low-quality cells or technical artifacts. To further ensure data quality, doublets (cells representing more than one distinct cell type) were identified and excluded using the DoubletFinder package, with an expected doublet rate of 6% based on the dataset characteristics.

  Data were normalized using SCTransform (glmGamPoi), a method that improves normalization by accounting for technical noise while preserving biological variation. After normalization, batch effects across different datasets were corrected using Harmony (v0.1.1), with 3000 variable features selected for integration to minimize any confounding effects from dataset-specific biases.

  Dimensionality reduction was performed using principal component analysis (PCA), followed by uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) for visualization. Clustering was carried out at a resolution of 0.5 using the Seurat package’s graph-based approach, which allowed us to identify distinct cellular subpopulations. Marker genes for each cluster were identified using the FindAllMarkers function, with the following criteria: logFC > 0.25 and min.pct = 0.25, representing the minimum fraction of cells expressing the marker within the cluster.

  Cells were annotated based on canonical markers for known immune and stromal cell types. Common immune cell markers (e.g., CD4, CD8, CD19, CD14, CD56) and stromal markers (e.g., VIM, COL1A1, PDGFRB) were used to assign cell identities, ensuring accurate annotation of major cell populations in PD and MASLD samples.

  Changes in cell proportions between healthy and disease (MASLD/PD) groups were assessed using edgeR (v3.42.4) and Fisher’s exact test. The results were visualized as log2 fold changes with adjusted p-values to identify significant differences in immune and stromal cell compositions between disease states. This statistical approach ensures that the observed changes in cell proportions are statistically robust and not due to random variations.

  Differential gene expression between clusters and disease conditions was performed using FindMarkers in Seurat, utilizing the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Genes that were significantly differentially expressed (adjusted p-value < 0.05, logFC > 0.25) between disease and control groups were identified. Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway analysis were conducted to assess the functional enrichment of DEGs in immune response, inflammation, and metabolic pathways.

  For integrated analysis across datasets, t-SNE and UMAP visualizations were generated using the Seurat package. These techniques were employed to display the relationships between different cell populations across multiple disease conditions, allowing for a comprehensive understanding of immune cell alterations in PD and MASLD. All plots were generated using ggplot2 (v3.3.6) in R for clarity and aesthetic presentation.


### Bulk transcriptome analysis
  Bulk transcriptomic datasets were obtained from GEO, including gingival tissues from PD patients and controls (GSE10334, GSE16134, Affymetrix HG-U133 Plus 2.0, Affymetrix Inc.) and liver tissues from MASLD patients and controls (GSE135251, RNA-seq). For microarray data, raw CEL files were processed in R using the affy package with robust multi-array average (RMA) for background correction, quantile normalization, and probe summarization. Probe annotations were mapped to gene symbols with hgu133plus2.db, and for genes with multiple probes, the probe with the lowest adjusted p-value was retained. Differential expression was determined using the limma package (v3.58.1) with a linear model including batch effects (~ batch + group). For RNA-seq data, raw reads were aligned to the GRCh38 reference genome using STAR (v2.7.10a), and gene counts were obtained with featureCounts (v2.0.3, Subread package). Normalization and differential expression analysis were performed using DESeq2 (v1.40.2). For both datasets, DEGs were defined as those with |log2FC| > 1 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. Gene-level expression matrices and trait files (HC = 0, Disease = 1) were exported for downstream WGCNA.


### Differential expression visualization
  DEGs from bulk transcriptomic analysis were visualized using volcano plots. Gene-level statistics (log2 fold change and p-values) derived from limma (PD datasets) or DESeq2 (MASLD dataset) were imported into R (v4.4.3). Volcano plots were generated with ggplot2 (v3.5.1), with thresholds set at |log2FC| ≥ 1 and p ≤ 0.05. Genes were classified as upregulated, downregulated, or not significant, and the numbers of up- and downregulated genes were annotated directly on the plots.


### Venn diagram analysis
  Venn diagrams were generated using the VennDiagram R package (v1.7.3) to visualize overlaps among different gene sets. Shared and unique genes across datasets or analytical results were quantified and annotated.


### KEGG enrichment analysis
  DEGs from PD (GSE10334, GSE16134) and MASLD (GSE135251) datasets were subjected to KEGG pathway enrichment using clusterProfiler (v4.10.1). Pathways with adjusted p < 0.05 were considered significant, and enrichment results were visualized using R.


### Immune cell infiltration analysis
  Immune cell composition was estimated from bulk transcriptomic data using the CIBERSORT algorithm with the LM22 leukocyte gene signature and 100 permutations. Quantile normalization was applied to microarray data (PD) but not to RNA-seq data (MASLD). Relative fractions of 22 immune cell subsets were compared between groups using Wilcoxon or t-tests, and results were visualized as boxplots, heatmaps (pheatmap v1.0.12), correlation plots (corrplot v0.92), and stacked bar charts in ggplot2.


### Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)
  WGCNA was performed separately for PD (GSE10334, GSE16134) and MASLD (GSE135251) bulk RNA-seq datasets using the WGCNA R package (v1.72-1). Normalized gene expression matrices were preprocessed by removing low-quality genes and outlier samples, followed by hierarchical clustering to confirm sample integrity. A signed network was constructed using the bicor correlation function, and the optimal soft-thresholding power was determined by the scale-free topology criterion (R² ≥ 0.85). Gene co-expression modules were identified using the blockwiseModules function. Module eigengenes were subsequently correlated with disease status to identify trait-associated modules. Module-trait relationships were visualized by heatmaps, and module membership (MM) versus gene significance (GS) scatter plots were generated for selected modules of interest. For each dataset, gene-to-module assignments and module-trait correlation statistics were exported for downstream integration analyzes.


### Integration of WGCNA modules with immune infiltration
  For both PD and MASLD, we integrated module eigengenes (MEs) from WGCNA with immune-cell fractions inferred by CIBERSORT (LM22). For each disease, the sample intersection between the ME matrix and the CIBERSORT output was taken to ensure 1:1 alignment. Pairwise correlations between MEs and the 22 immune-cell proportions were computed with Spearman’s ρ; p values were obtained with corPvalueStudent (WGCNA) and BH-adjusted to control the false-discovery rate. We focused on the disease-associated modules identified in WGCNA—turquoise (MASLD) and green (PD)—and extracted their ME-immune correlation rows to enable side-by-side comparison. Correlation heatmaps were drawn with pheatmap, displaying ρ values and significance stars (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001). For compact summaries, we further reported immune-cell associations meeting |ρ| ≥ 0.30 and FDR < 0.05.


### Machine-learning benchmarking
  Expression matrices were constructed from the core feature set (the 11 shared genes identified by the WGCNA-DEG intersection) and paired with sample labels (HC/PD or HC/MASLD). For each disease, samples were split into training (70%) and test (30%) sets using stratified partitioning. Models were trained with caret (v6.0-94) under fivefold cross-validation: random forest (randomForest v4.7-1.1), SVM with radial kernel (e1071 v1.7-13), GLM (binomial), gradient boosting machine (gbm v2.1.8), KNNs (class v7.3-22), single-hidden-layer neural network (nnet v7.3-19), LASSO via glmnet (v4.1-8), and decision tree (rpart v4.1-23). The classification performance of each model was assessed on the held-out test set by calculating AUC values, and comparative results were visualized to highlight algorithm-specific differences between PD and MASLD cohorts. Model-agnostic variable importance was computed with DALEX (v2.4.3) for comparative ranking of features.


### ROC analysis
  ROC curves were generated to evaluate the predictive performance of machine-learning classifiers trained on the 11 shared hub genes. For each disease cohort (PD and MASLD), data were partitioned into training and test sets, and models were benchmarked using caret under fivefold cross-validation. Probabilistic predictions on the test sets were assessed with pROC (v1.18.5), and the AUC was calculated to quantify classification performance.


### Random forest (RF)
  Random forest classifiers were implemented with the expression profiles of the 11 shared genes as predictors. Models were initialized with 500 trees, and the number of trees yielding the lowest OOB classification error was selected as optimal. Variable importance was assessed using Gini-based measures, and genes were ranked according to their contribution to classification accuracy. The top-ranked genes were retained as candidate features for further validation.


### Support vector machine recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE)
  SVM-RFE was performed using a custom R script based on the e1071 package. Five-fold cross-validation was applied to iteratively remove the least informative features. Classification error curves were plotted against the number of retained features, and the subset size corresponding to the minimum cross-validated error was identified. Genes within this subset were defined as SVM-RFE-selected core features.


### LASSO logistic regression
  LASSO regression models were fitted using glmnet with five-fold cross-validation to determine the optimal penalty parameter (lambda). Coefficients across the regularization path were visualized, and genes with non-zero coefficients at the optimal lambda (lambda.min) were extracted. These retained predictors were regarded as LASSO-selected markers.


### SHAP-based model interpretation
  XGBoost classifiers were trained on the 11 shared genes, and SHAP values were computed to interpret model predictions. Global importance was assessed by the mean absolute SHAP value, yielding a ranked list of predictive genes. Beeswarm plots were used to depict the distribution of SHAP contributions across samples, while a directional heatmap summarized the normalized influence of each gene toward different groups. Genes that consistently demonstrated high SHAP importance and strong directional contributions were prioritized as core candidate markers.


### Spatial transcriptomics and cell-type deconvolution
  Spatial transcriptomic datasets of periodontitis tissues were downloaded from the CROST database (accession IDs: VISDS000661, VISDS000662, VISDS000663). MASLD spatial transcriptomic data were obtained from the GEO database (GSE192741). Raw 10x Genomics Visium data were processed in Seurat v5.0.1. For each sample, spots within tissue boundaries were retained, and low-quality spots (bottom 1% sequencing depth) were removed. SCTransform normalization was applied while regressing out mitochondrial percentage, followed by PCA. Batch effects across samples were corrected using Harmony v1.2.0, and shared variable features (n = 3000) were selected for integration. Graph-based clustering and UMAP were performed on Harmony embeddings to identify spatial clusters. Cell-type deconvolution was conducted using RCTD (spacexr v2.1.0) with a scRNA-seq reference annotated at the cell-type level. A sparse counts matrix was extracted from Seurat objects for compatibility, and a Reference object was constructed from the reference dataset after filtering unannotated or low-quality cells. RCTD was run in doublet mode, and both dominant (first-type) and secondary (second-type) predictions were assigned to each spot. Deconvolution results were written back into the Seurat metadata, and spatial distributions of inferred cell types were visualized with Seurat’s SpatialDimPlot function.


### Expression of ANXA6-associated gene signature in spatial datasets
  The 11 shared hub genes identified from WGCNA-DEG integration were evaluated within the deconvolved spatial transcriptomic datasets. Average expression of each gene was computed across annotated cell types, and cell-type-level heatmaps were generated using pheatmap (v1.0.12). In addition, a composite module score was calculated with Seurat’s AddModuleScore function to assess the activity of the 11-gene signature in individual spots. Module scores were aggregated by cell type, and visualization included heatmaps of Z-scored expression as well as bar plots summarizing average signature activity per cell type.


### γδ T cells stratification and differential expression analysis
  γδ T cells were subsetted from the annotated scRNA-seq datasets in Seurat v5.0.1. Cells were stratified into ANXA6-high and ANXA6-low groups according to the median expression level of ANXA6. Group labels were stored in the Seurat metadata. Gene expression distributions were visualized using violin and box plots. Differential expression analysis was performed with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test implemented in Seurat’s FindMarkers function, controlling for sequencing depth and mitochondrial content as latent variables.


### Cell-cell communication analysis
  Intercellular communication between ANXA6-high and ANXA6-low γδ T cells and other cell populations was analyzed using CellChat (v1.6.1). A CellChat object was constructed from normalized expression matrices with cell-type annotations. Overexpressed genes and interactions were identified, and interaction probabilities were computed using the inbuilt ligand-receptor database (CellChatDB.human). Communication networks were summarized at both the interaction and pathway levels. Outgoing and incoming signaling roles were quantified by centrality analysis, and visualizations included circle plots, bubble plots, and signaling-role heatmaps.


### Transcription factor regulatory inference
  Regulatory factors associated with ANXA6 expression were inferred using decoupleR (v2.7.0) with the VIPER algorithm. Transcription factor activity scores were computed, and partial correlation coefficients between transcription factor activity and ANXA6 expression were calculated. Results were filtered by absolute correlation magnitude, and transcription factors consistently correlated across disease contexts were retained. Overlaps between top-ranked regulators were determined to prioritize reproducible candidates.


### Drug perturbation analysis
  DEGs between ANXA6-high and ANXA6-low γδ T cells were analyzed using a Connectivity Map–style perturbation framework, by querying the Enrichr API against the LINCS L1000 Chem Pert library. Upregulated and downregulated gene sets were separately queried, and enrichment results were integrated by computing the geometric mean of adjusted q-values. Candidate small molecules were prioritized by lowest combined scores. Top candidates were visualized with lollipop charts to represent enrichment strength across contexts.
