# Machine learning derived ORC6 as a hub biomarker for predicting tumor microenvironment, prognoses, and therapeutic responses in lung adenocarcinoma

## Introduction
  Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), which accounts for 60 % of all lung cancers, is a common subtype of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and has become one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths worldwide [1,2]. Despite significant advances in chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted therapy for LUAD, some patients still fail to benefit from these treatments [3,4]. Therefore, identifying with reliable biomarkers to predict the prognosis and treatment response for LUAD is urgently needed.

  Recent studies have demonstrated that ORC6, a crucial component of the origin recognition complex (ORC), plays a significant role in various cancers [5]. Beyond its well-established functions in DNA replication and cell cycle regulation, ORC6 has been implicated in processes such as chromatin remodeling, genomic instability, and tumor progression. For instance, ORC6 overexpression has been associated with poor prognosis and enhanced tumor growth in cancers like breast, ovarian, and colorectal cancer [6,7]. Additionally, emerging studies suggest that ORC6 may contribute to immune evasion and therapy resistance, key factors in cancer progression [8]. Studies have shown that ORC6 may influence tumor cells' evasion of immune surveillance and resistance to chemotherapy and immunotherapy by regulating the cell cycle and DNA repair mechanisms [9]. Overexpression of ORC6 is closely associated with the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment, enabling tumor cells to survive and expand under immune system attack [10]. These findings highlight ORC6 as a promising therapeutic target, particularly in NSCLC, where its role remains underexplored [11]. This study aims to expand on the understanding of ORC6 in LUAD, providing a theoretical basis for targeting ORC6 in this malignancy.

  With the establishment of public databases and the development of visual websites, the possibility of systematic analysis of ORC6 in different tumor types based on bioinformatics analysis has become important in the field of cancer research. In this study, we employed multiple databases to elucidate the prognostic value of ORC6 in LUAD and delineate its immunological significance in the context of LUAD.


## Materials and methods

### Data collection
  The transcriptional data for LUAD was sourced from TCGA (https://www.cancer.gov/) [12], while the transcriptional information for healthy lung tissue was obtained from the GTEx database (https://www.gtexportal.org/home/) [13]. The LUAD dataset from the GEO database was downloaded for validation of ORC6, including the following datasets: GSE19188, GSE41271, GSE11969, GSE72094, GSE26939, GSE29016, GSE42127 [14]. Additionally, the diagnostic and prognostic capabilities of ORC6 were validated in the Cho cohort immunotherapy dataset [15].


### Screening of hub gene
  The RNA-seq data for TCGA-LUAD cohort was downloaded from the TCGA database in TPM format. Standardization was performed using the log(x+1) method. Subsequently, gene annotation information was obtained from the GENECODE database to annotate the expression profiles of mRNAs in the cohort. Clinical data for TCGA-LUAD cohort, including prognostic outcomes (overall survival, OS; progression-free interval, PFI; disease-specific survival, DSS), age, gender, pathological staging, tumor grading, etc., was downloaded from UCSC Xena database and matched with sample IDs [16]. Cox regression was employed to screen for prognostic biomarkers (Prognostic outcomes: OS, PFI, DSS) with a threshold of HR > 1 and p-value < 0.05. The “limma” package was used to identify upregulated mRNAs in tumor samples compared to normal samples, with a threshold of |LogFC| > 1 and adjusted p-value (adj. p-value) < 0.05 to control the false discovery rate (FDR).

  Finally, overlapping mRNA were obtained from the four groups. To eliminate redundant genes and identify those with the strongest predictive power, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) Cox regression was applied to the intersecting mRNA list. A 10-fold cross-validation was performed to determine the optimal penalty parameter (λ), and the model with the λ value that yielded the minimum mean cross-validated error (lambda.min) was selected, resulting in 16 mRNA molecules with non-zero coefficients. Subsequently, a Random Forest (RF) model was constructed using the R package 'randomForest' (version 4.7-1.1) with 10,000 trees (ntree = 10000). The importance of each gene was calculated based on the "%IncMSE" (Percentage Increase in Mean Squared Error) metric. The model's performance was internally validated using the out-of-bag (OOB) error estimation. The mRNA with the highest importance score was identified as the final core gene [17].


### Analysis of prognostic relevance
  We employ Kaplan–Meier (K-M) curves to analyze OS, DSS, and PFI in the cohort. For survival analysis, patients were stratified into high-expression and low-expression groups based on ORC6 expression. The optimal cut-off value for ORC6 expression, used to dichotomize the patients, was determined using the “surv_cutpoint” function from the R package ‘survminer’ (version 0.4.9). This method calculates the cut-point that corresponds to the most significant (log-rank test) separation between the resulting survival curves.


### Analysis of tumor immune based on ORC6 expression
  In the immune cell analysis, we employed multiple algorithms including TIMER, CIBERSORT, QUANTISEQ, MCP-counter, and EPIC to estimate the abundance of immune cells in different samples. In addition, the ESTIMATE algorithm was utilized to calculate immune score and stromal score to reflect the microenvironmental status. Heatmap was used for visualizing the infiltration levels of 33 immune cell types in TCGA cancers.


### Nomogram and predictive performance evaluation
  We conducted univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses to determine if ORC6 can serve as an independent prognostic factor. Additionally, we utilized the “rms” package to construct a nomogram for predicting 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year OS. Furthermore, calibration curves were employed to assess the predictive accuracy of survival and diagnostic predictions.


### Enrichment analysis
  The gene ontology (GO) was employed for annotating genes based on their biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components. The kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) was utilized for annotating gene pathways [18]. Differential analysis of high-score and low-score samples from different cohorts was conducted using the “limma” package, and overlapping genes from the differential analysis were subjected to enrichment analysis. Enrichment pathways with a P-value<0.05 and q-value<0.05 were considered statistically significant.


### Database validation
  The expression intensity, impact on cell cycle, and protein expression of the ORC6 gene were retrieved from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database [19].


### In vitro assays
  All human LUAD cell lines (H1299, H1975, SPC-A1, and A549) and human bronchial epithelial cells (BEAS-2B) were purchased from the Type Culture Collection of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). All cell lines were cultured in 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS; Hyclone, Logan, UT) at 37°C in a 5 % CO2 incubator. Total RNA was isolated from cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). H1299 cells were transfected with Lipofectamine 2000 (Solarbio) at 60–70 % confluency. H1299 and A549 cells were seeded in 96-well plates and at different time points after transfection, 10 µL of Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Tokyo, Japan) was added to each well. After further incubation, the optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek, Beijing, China). Primer sequences for ORC6, GAPDH, as well as ORC6 interference sequence and detailed qRT-PCR and transfection methods were described in previous literature.

  GAPDH, 5′-AAAAGCATCACCCGGAGGAGAA-3′ (forward) and 5′-AAGGAAATGAATGGGCAGCCG-3′ (reverse);

  ORC6, 5′-ACCCCAAAGCACTGAGTTGA-3′ (forward) and 5′-CGAACTCACCTCAGCATGTC-3′ (reverse).


### Western blot
  Total protein was extracted from cell samples by lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The isolated proteins were separated through sodium dodecyl SDS-PAGE and transferred to a PVDF membrane. Afterwards, the membranes were blocked with 5 % bovine serum albumin at room temperature for 1h and incubated with the specific primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. TBST was used to wash the membranes before and after the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies (the target proteins were produced by immunoblot with specific antibodies after the PVDF membrane cutted by protein ladder). The relative expression levels of the proteins were detected using an ECL detection system. GAPDH was used as an internal control.


### Immunofluorescence staining of ORC6 in H1299 and A549 cells
  To investigate the subcellular localization of ORC6 protein, immunofluorescence staining was performed in H1299 and A549 cell lines. Cells were seeded on glass coverslips in 24-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. After reaching 70 % confluence, cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature and then permeabilized with 0.1 % Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min. Following permeabilization, cells were blocked with 5 % bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 1 hour to prevent non-specific binding.

  The cells were then incubated with a rabbit anti-ORC6 primary antibody (dilution 1:200 in 1 % BSA) overnight at 4°C. After primary antibody incubation, cells were washed three times with PBS and incubated with a goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody conjugated to Alexa Fluor 568 (dilution 1:500 in 1 % BSA) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole, dilution 1:1000 in PBS) for 5 min. Following several washes in PBS, coverslips were mounted on slides using an antifade mounting medium.

  Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope equipped with appropriate filters for DAPI and Alexa Fluor 568. The localization and expression levels of ORC6 were analyzed by observing the colocalization of the red fluorescence signal from ORC6 with the blue fluorescence of the nuclei stained with DAPI.


### Statistical analysis
  The statistical analyses were executed using R software (version 4.0.1), as described earlier in this study. The significance level was set at a p-value below 0.05, indicating statistical significance.


## Results

### Machine learning derived ORC6 as a hub biomarker in LUAD
  In the TCGA-LUAD cohort, we employed Cox regression analysis to identify key mRNAs associated with different prognostic outcomes, and used the “limma” package to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) significantly upregulated compared to normal samples. The overlapping mRNA molecules obtained from the above analysis totaled 380, exhibiting diverse prognostic implications (Fig. 1A). To further narrow down the candidate genes, we employed LASSO regression to identify 16 mRNA molecules with the highest predictive power (Fig. 1B). Subsequently, we constructed a RF model and calculated gene importance scores (Fig. 1C) to assign weights to the 16 mRNA molecules, ultimately identifying ORC6 as the core gene. ORC6 exhibited significant upregulation in tumor samples from both the GSE19188 cohort (Fig. 1D) and the TCGA-LUAD cohort (Fig. 1E). Furthermore, K-M curves for OS, PFS, and DSS demonstrated that expression of ORC6 effectively stratified patients into different risk groups (Figs. 1F-H).Fig. 1Screening and prognosis of ORC6.(A) Venn diagrams of the overlapping DEGs between different datasets. (B) LASSO regression analysis. (C) Gene importance ranking in random forest tree model. ORC6 mRNA expression in LUAD compared to normal samples in the GSE19188 (D) and the TCGA datasets (E). (F-H) K–M analysis of OS, PFS, and DSS.Fig 1 dummy alt text

  Furthermore, we investigated the expression and mutation profile of ORC6 at a pan-cancer level. Our results revealed that ORC6 exhibited significantly higher expression in most tumor samples, with the exception of testicular germ cell tumors \(TGCT) where no differential expression was observed (Supplementary Figure 1A). In terms of somatic mutations, ORC6 had the highest mutation frequency of 1.4 % in cervical squamous cell carcinoma and endocervical adenocarcinoma (CESC), while the mutation frequency was 0.4 % in LUAD (Supplementary Figure 1B). In addition, ORC6 expression also showed significant differences in different copy number variation (CNV) groups. In LUAD, higher expression of ORC6 was observed in cases with copy number amplification (Supplementary Figure 1C).

In the baseline data, ORC6 was found to be expressed at higher levels in male patients (Fig. 2A), asian populations (Fig. 2B), and older patients (Fig. 2C). Interestingly, ORC6 expression was higher in the necrotic regions compared to non-necrotic regions (Fig. 2D). Higher clinical stages (Fig. 2E), T stages (Fig. 2F), and M stages (Fig. 2G) were associated with increased ORC6 expression, suggesting that ORC6 may be a potential oncogenic gene. ### ORC6 represents different immune cell infiltration
  To investigate the role of ORC6 in the immune microenvironment of LUAD, we employed various algorithms and multiple cohorts to ensure robust results. Correlation analyses revealed that although the relationship between ORC6 and different immune cells varied across different cohorts, a negative correlation was observed with cytotoxic cells such as activated CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells (Fig. 5A). Further correlation analysis with various immune regulators reaffirmed the immunosuppressive role of ORC6 (Fig. 5B). Lastly, we highlighted the correlation of ORC6 with commonly used immune therapy targets in LUAD, such as LAG3 (Fig. 5C) and PDCD1 (Fig. 5D). ### ORC6 reflects changes in biological pathways in LUAD
  In order to investigate the biological pathways that may be influenced by ORC6, we conducted KEGG analysis. The results revealed that ORC6 positively regulates signaling pathways such as p53 and mTOR, while negatively regulates pathways such as cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction (Fig. 6A). These KEGG findings further support the potential mechanism of ORC6 as an oncogenic factor and its role as an immunosuppressive factor. Finally, GSEA analysis based on KEGG gene sets reaffirmed the robustness of the above results (Fig. 6B).Fig. 6Pathway and process enrichment analysis.(A) Results of KEGG analysis.   Considering that different small molecule drugs may have therapeutic effects on patients activated by different pathways, we conducted drug sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Figure 2). The results showed that patients with high ORC6 expression may have weaker sensitivity to most small molecule drugs, such as AGK-2, AM-580, AZD7762, etc. In addition, patients with high ORC6 expression may exhibit resistance to many small molecule drugs, such as CAY10576, BMC-536924, AM-580, etc.

## Discussion
  Prior researches have indicated the involvement of ORC6 in various biological processes throughout cancer progression [20,21]. However, the prognostic significance of ORC6 expression levels and its potential influence on tumor-associated mechanisms, including modulation of the tumor microenvironment and immune suppression, in several human cancers are not yet understood and warrant further investigation. To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive study on the expression and functional roles of ORC6 in LUAD.

  In the present study, we have observed a significant increase in ORC6 mRNA expression in LUAD tissue samples when compared to normal lung tissue samples in the GSE19188 and TCGA-LUAD cohorts. Additionally, K-M survival analysis for OS, PFS, and DSS showed that differential expression of ORC6 effectively stratified patients into distinct risk groups, suggesting a potential role for ORC6 as an oncogene in the development and progression of LUAD. The present study also observed the mRNA expression level of ORC6 was closely correlated with various cliniocopathological parameters. ORC6 may be considered a diagnostic biomarker and its expression levels tended to increase with increasing tumor stage and grade. Furthermore, the expression of ORC6 was further validated in multiple GEO datasets. These results indicated that ORC6 serve as an oncogene, playing an important role in LUAD.

  The pan-cancer analysis revealed that ORC6 expression was significantly elevated in 33 types of cancer, with the exception of TGCT, indicating its wide participation in tumorigenesis across various cancer types. Previous studies have suggested that ORC6 plays an essential role in coordinating chromosome replication and segregation with cytokinesis, thereby promoting cell proliferation [22,23]. Intriguingly, our study uncovered that ORC6 exerts diverse roles in tumorigenesis, acting as either a tumor promoter or suppressor in different cancer types. In terms of somatic mutations, ORC6 had the highest mutation frequency of 1.4 % in CESC, while the mutation frequency was 0.4 % in LUAD. In addition, ORC6 expression also showed significant differences in different CNV groups. In LUAD, higher expression of ORC6 was observed in cases with copy number amplification. Altogether, these results imply that ORC6 expression level may predict the prognosis of LUAD patients. Nonetheless, the precise molecular mechanism of action of ORC6 in LUAD remains to be elucidated.

  In recent years, the advancement of immunotherapy has significantly improved the prognosis of cancer patients in advanced stages [[24], [25], [26], [27]]. However, the efficacy of immunotherapy is frequently impacted and undermined by the complex interplay between the tumor and the host immune system [28]. Our results demonstrate that the expression level of ORC6 is associated with response to ICIs, with patients exhibiting lower ORC6 expression showing better response to treatment, whereas those with higher expression have a worse prognosis. Furthermore, our analysis revealed that ORC6 expression is a promising biomarker for predicting immune response, as evidenced by a high AUC value of 0.818.

  Accumulating evidence have shown that the immune microenvironment is closely associated with tumor prognosis [29,30]. Immune cell infiltration is widely recognized as an indicator of the immune microenvironment within tumors [[31], [32], [33]]. Our study is the first to report a significant statistical association between the expression level of ORC6 and immune cell infiltration. A significant negative correlation was observed between the expression of ORC6 and the infiltration level of cytotoxic CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells, while a positive correlation was found between ORC6 expression and the infiltration level of cancer-associated fibroblasts and tumor endothelial cells, as determined by various algorithms and multiple cohorts. Previous studies have demonstrated that immune cell infiltration may contribute to tumorigenesis, development, and metastasis [34,35]. Cancer-associated fibroblasts are the most abundant cancer stromal cells that induce tumor cell proliferation, therapeutic resistance and immune exclusion [36,37]. Tumor endothelial cells play a crucial role in tumor angiogenesis and the suppression of T cells in the tumor environment [38,39].

  Finally, CD4+ and CD8+ T cells function as killer cells that dominate antitumor immune responses and greatly influence the outcome of cancer immunotherapy [40]. We investigated the correlation between ORC6 expression and the expression levels of immune-related genes. Our data revealed significant correlations between ORC6 expression and the expression of both immunosuppressive and immunostimulatory markers. Interestingly, a positive correlation was observed between ORC6 expression and immunosuppression-related genes, particularly LAG3 and PDCD1 (PD-1). Additionally, our findings revealed a negative correlation between ORC6 expression and immune score and stromal score, collectively suggesting a potential role of ORC6 in promoting an immunosuppressive microenvironment in LUAD. However, it is important to acknowledge a key limitation of this study: the evidence presented here for ORC6′s role in immune modulation is primarily correlative. While our bioinformatic analyses robustly link high ORC6 expression to a suppressive immune contexture, the direct mechanistic role of ORC6 in shaping the tumor immune landscape remains to be experimentally validated. Based on our findings that ORC6 is a core regulator of DNA replication and cell cycle, we postulate a plausible mechanism. The ORC6-driven proliferative advantage and potential genomic instability may lead to the remodeling of the tumor microenvironment, possibly through the upregulation of specific cytokines, chemokines, or other soluble factors that recruit immunosuppressive cells (e.g., cancer-associated fibroblasts) and impair the infiltration and function of cytotoxic T lymphocytes [36,37]. Directly testing this hypothesis—for instance, through co-culture assays of ORC6-modulated LUAD cells with immune cells, or via in vivo studies using syngeneic mouse models—represents a crucial and logical next step to establish causality. These future experiments will be essential to confirm ORC6 as a bona fide immunomodulatory target and to unravel the precise molecular pathways involved.

  Our functional enrichment and pathway analyses revealed that ORC6 plays a significant role in regulating nuclear cell cycle DNA replication, which is consistent with previous studies. In addition, we found that ORC6 is involved in other important molecular biological processes, including cell signal transduction and metabolic pathways. Our GSEA results also demonstrated that the P53 signaling pathway was significantly enriched in response to high ORC6 expression in LUAD patients. Additionally, it should be noted that ORC6 functions not only as a tumor suppressor but also an independent prognostic factor in LUAD. We validated the role of ORC6 as an important regulator in the early stages of DNA replication. Our results indicate that ORC6 localizes in the nucleus during the G1 phase of the cell cycle and subsequently translocates to the cytoplasm at the onset of the S phase.

  Supriya G. Prasanth and colleagues demonstrated that the inhibition of ORC6 led to a notable reduction in cellular proliferation and an elevated incidence of cellular apoptosis. Their findings underscore the indispensability of ORC6 as a pivotal gene orchestrating the synchronization of chromosome replication and segregation alongside cytokinesis [41]. The observed DNA damage response resulting from a reduction in ORC6 expression appears to be facilitated via the p53-dependent pathway governing cell cycle regulation. Our conjecture postulates that an elevated expression of ORC6 within LUAD could potentially be a driving factor behind genome instability. This phenomenon might arise due to the cumulative occurrence of erroneous DNA replication events, coupled with p53 deletions/mutations, which are prevalent in over 50 % of cases of LUAD. Depletion of the p53 protein in LUAD additionally contributes to the perturbation of genomic stability and the accrual of genetic mutations. Subsequent investigations are imperative to attain a comprehensive comprehension of the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying ORC6 in the context of LUAD.


### Limitations of the Study
  This study has several limitations. Firstly, as noted above, the immunomodulatory role of ORC6 is inferred from comprehensive bioinformatic analyses rather than direct functional experiments. Secondly, while our in vitro models confirmed the oncogenic functions of ORC6 in proliferation, they do not fully recapitulate the complexity of the tumor microenvironment in vivo. Future work employing co-culture systems and animal models is warranted to validate these findings and elucidate the underlying mechanisms.


## Conclusions
  In conclusion, our study provides evidence that ORC6 is an oncogenic driver and its overexpression is associated with poor prognosis in LUAD. Thus, ORC6 may represent a promising therapeutic target for LUAD in the future. Further studies are required to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the role of ORC6 in LUAD and to evaluate its therapeutic potential.
