# Acacetin Alleviates Loperamide‐Induced Functional Constipation by Inhibiting P53‐Mediated Apoptosis in Colonic Epithelial Cells


## Background
  Functional constipation (FC) is a prevalent gastrointestinal disorder with limited therapeutic options. Yunweiling (YWL), a traditional Chinese herbal medicine from Radix Inulae extract, has been used for treating constipation, but its underlying mechanisms remain unclear. This study aimed to investigate the protective effects and mechanisms of acacetin, a bioactive compound of YWL, in alleviating FC.


## Methods
  Molecular docking analysis was performed to identify the targets of bioactive compounds in YWL formula. A loperamide‐induced FC mouse model was established, and the effects of acacetin on gastrointestinal motility, colon histology, and signaling pathways were evaluated. Human colonic mucosal epithelial cells were used to investigate the protective effects of YWL extracts and acacetin against loperamide‐induced apoptosis and the involvement of the tumor protein P53 (P53) signaling pathway.


## Results
  Molecular docking analysis revealed acacetin as a bioactive chemical targeting P53 protein. In the FC mouse model, acacetin administration improved gastrointestinal motility, alleviated colon mucosal damage, and suppressed the upregulation of P53 and its downstream apoptotic signaling. In colonic epithelial cells, loperamide induced apoptosis by activating P53 and inhibiting Akt1 signaling, which was attenuated by YWL extract and acacetin treatment. The protective effects of YWL and acacetin were abrogated by the P53 activator Nutlin‐3a, suggesting that the inhibition of P53 signaling is crucial for their anti‐apoptotic properties.


## Conclusion
  Acacetin alleviates loperamide‐induced FC by inhibiting P53‐mediated apoptosis in colonic epithelial cells, providing new insights into the therapeutic mechanisms of traditional Chinese herbal medicine YWL in treating constipation.


## Introduction
  Constipation is a highly prevalent gastrointestinal disorder characterized by difficult or infrequent bowel movements, often accompanied by symptoms such as abdominal discomfort, bloating, and straining during defecation [1, 2]. It is estimated that approximately 14% of the global population suffers from chronic constipation, with a higher prevalence among women and elderly individuals [3]. Several risk factors contribute to the development of constipation, including a low‐fiber diet, inadequate fluid intake, lack of physical activity, certain medications, and underlying medical conditions such as irritable bowel syndrome, diabetes, and neurological disorders [4, 5]. Current treatment options for constipation include dietary modifications, increased fluid intake, exercise, laxatives, and in severe cases, surgical interventions [6]. However, many of these treatments have limited efficacy and potential side effects, highlighting the need for more effective and safer therapeutic approaches [7].

  Colonic epithelial cells play a crucial role in maintaining intestinal homeostasis, and their dysfunction has been implicated in the pathogenesis of constipation [8]. Several studies have reported alterations in the colonic epithelial barrier function, impaired mucus production, and dysregulated ion transport in patients with constipation [9, 10]. For instance, Guarino et al. found that females with slow transit constipation exhibit altered progesterone receptor expression and serotonin levels in their colon epithelial cells, suggesting these factors may play a role in the pathophysiology of the condition [11]. Cell death in the gut epithelium has a great implication in inflammatory bowel diseases [12, 13]. In patients with intractable slow transit constipation, there is significant involvement of glial cells and an increased apoptosis of enteric neurons, which may contribute to the neuropathological mechanisms underlying the condition [14]. However, whether colonic epithelial cell death contributes to constipation has not been well‐established.

  Traditional Chinese medicine has a long history of treating various gastrointestinal disorders, including constipation [15, 16]. Numerous herbal formulations and compounds derived from these medicines have been investigated for their potential therapeutic effects in alleviating constipation [17, 18]. For instance, the Chinese herbal formula Yunweiling (YWL), which is derived from Radix Inulae (Inula nervosa Wall., also known as Inula racemosa Hook.f.), has been widely used for treating gastric disorders and irritable bowel syndrome [19, 20]. Radix Inulae is rich in diverse bioactive compounds, including sesquiterpene lactones (such as alantolactone and isoalantolactone), flavonoids (including acacetin and luteolin), polysaccharides, and inulin, which have been reported to exhibit a wide range of pharmacological activities, including anti‐inflammatory, antioxidant, antimicrobial, and gastroprotective properties [19, 20]. Despite the traditional use and known phytochemical profile of YWL, the underlying mechanisms by which this traditional remedy exerts beneficial effects in constipation remain to be fully elucidated, warranting further investigation.

  Our previous work has shown the protective effects of YWL in an animal model of functional constipation, and identified several bioactive compounds and their potential targets through integration of network pharmacology, including acacetin which potentially targets P53 [21]. The current study aimed to investigate the protective effects and underlying mechanisms of acacetin in alleviating functional constipation. Through a loperamide‐induced mouse model of functional constipation and in vitro studies in human colonic epithelial cells, it was demonstrated that YWL active component acacetin inhibits P53‐mediated apoptosis in colonic epithelial cells, thereby improving gastrointestinal motility and alleviating constipation symptoms. The inhibition of the P53 signaling pathway appears to be a crucial mechanism by which acacetin exerts its therapeutic effects against functional constipation, providing new insights into the mode of action of this traditional herbal remedy in treating gastrointestinal motility disorders.


## Materials and Methods

### Bioinformatics Analysis
  The active components of Yunweiling (YWL) were obtained from literature reports and screened in the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology Database (TCMSP, http://lsp.nwu.edu.cn/tcmsp.php) based on oral bioavailability (OB ≥ 30%) and drug‐likeness (DL ≥ 0.18) criteria. Potential drug targets of the active YWL components were retrieved from the TCMSP database. Functional constipation (FC)‐related genes were obtained from the GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/) and Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM, https://www.omim.org/) databases. The protein–protein interaction network was constructed and visualized using Cytoscape software (version 3.8.2, https://cytoscape.org/). Molecular docking between the YWL compound acacetin and P53 protein was conducted using AutoDock Vina (http://vina.scripps.edu/).


### Yunweiling (YWL) Extract Preparation
  YWL (Inulae Nervosae Radix Et Rhizoma) was purchased from Nebula Bioscience (NBC‐234138, Beijing, China). The powdered substances were extracted using a mixture of 75% ethanol and water. Following this, the ethanol was eliminated through reduced pressure at temperatures ranging between 45°C and 55°C. The concentrated extract went through a separation process involving dichloromethane and water to segregate the dichloromethane layer. Subsequent to this, the dichloromethane was evaporated under reduced pressure in the temperature range of 45°C–55°C. The resulting pellet was weighed, dissolved in DMSO, and stored at −80°C until further use.


### Mouse Model of Functional Constipation (FC) and Intervention
  Eight‐week‐old male C57BL/6 mice were obtained from Cyagen (Guangzhou, China). The mice were housed in a specific pathogen‐free facility with a 12 h light/dark cycle and had ad libitum access to food and water. A loperamide‐induced functional constipation mouse model was established using oral gavaging of loperamide hydrochloride (HY‐B0418A, MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) suspension [22, 23]. The loperamide hydrochloride suspension was prepared by dissolving loperamide hydrochloride in 0.9% saline at a concentration of 1 mg/mL. The mice in the model group received daily oral gavaging of the loperamide suspension at a dose of 10 mg/kg/day for 7 consecutive days. The control group mice were gavaged with 0.9% saline at the same volume and duration as the model group for 7 days. After the induction of functional constipation, different doses of acacetin (HY‐N0451, MedChemExpress) were administered to the mice by gavaging for 2 weeks as intervention (YWL‐high: 50 mg/kg/day; YWL‐medium: 25 mg/kg/day; YWL‐low: 10 mg/kg/day). The in vivo doses of acacetin were calculated based on the effective in vitro concentration (10 μM, equivalent to 2.84 mg/L). Using standard pharmacokinetic conversion methods that account for volume of distribution (estimated 3 L/kg–5 L/kg for flavonoid compounds) and oral bioavailability (estimated 20%–30%), the calculated target dose was approximately 45 mg/kg/day. Accordingly, we selected three doses (10, 25, and 50 mg/kg/day) to establish a dose–response relationship in the mouse model.

  To validate the gastrointestinal motility function in the mice, an activated charcoal propulsion test was performed. After the last treatment, the mice were fasted for 12 h but allowed free access to water. A 10% activated charcoal suspension in 0.9% saline (0.4 mL) was administered by oral gavaging to mimic intestinal content. The time from gavaging to the first excretion of black feces was recorded. Additionally, the number of fecal pellets and the total fecal weight within 8 h were measured. If a statistically significant difference (p < 0.05) was observed in these parameters compared to the control group, the functional constipation model was considered successfully established.


### Hematoxylin & Eosin (H&E)
  Distal colon tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) using the H&E Staining Kit (ab245880, Abcam) following the manufacturer's protocol. The sections were deparaffinized through a graded series of ethanol solutions (100%, 95%, 70%) to distilled water, and sequentially stained with Hematoxylin Solution for 5 min to label nuclei. The sections were differentiated in Acid Ethanol Solution for 3 min, blued in Ammonia Water Solution for 1 min, and counterstained with Eosin Y Solution for 3 min for cytoplasm and extracellular matrix. After dehydration through graded ethanol solutions (70%, 95%, 100%) and clearing in xylene, the stained sections were mounted for microscopic examination to evaluate histological changes in the colonic mucosa and muscle fibers between different experimental groups.


### Cell Culture and Treatment
  Human colonic mucosal epithelial cells were obtained from Procell (CP‐H040, Wuhan, China). The cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM, Gibco, 11,965,092, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, 10,099,141, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin solution (Gibco, 15,140,122, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. For loperamide treatment, loperamide hydrochloride (HY‐B0418A, MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Sigma‐Aldrich, D8418, St. Louis, MO, USA) to prepare a stock solution of 20 mM. The cells were treated with increasing concentrations of loperamide (5, 10, and 20 μM) for 24 h. The Yunweiling (YWL) extract was prepared as described previously and dissolved in DMSO to make a stock solution of 10 mg/mL. Acacetin (HY‐N0451, MedChemExpress) was dissolved in DMSO to prepare a 20 mM stock solution. The cells were pretreated with 40 μg/mL YWL extract or 10 μM acacetin for 2 h before the addition of 20 μM loperamide for 24 h stimulation. In some experiments, the cells were treated with 10 μM Nutlin‐3a (HY‐10029, MedChemExpress), a P53 activator, along with loperamide and YWL extract.


### RT‐qPCR
  Total RNA was extracted from colon tissue samples or cultured cells using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, 74,104, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The RNA concentration and purity were determined by a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). Reverse transcription was performed using the High‐Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, 4,368,814, Waltham, MA, USA) to synthesize cDNA from 1 μg of total RNA. Quantitative real‐time PCR (qPCR) was carried out using the PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, A25742) on a QuantStudio 3 Real‐Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). The reaction mixture consisted of 10 μL SYBR Green Master Mix, 1 μL of each forward and reverse primer (10 μM), 2 μL of cDNA template, and nuclease‐free water to a final volume of 20 μL. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: 50°C for 2 min, 95°C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 1 min. The relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2‐ΔΔCt method with GAPDH as the internal reference gene. All reactions were performed in triplicate.


### Immunoblotting
  Protein was extracted from colon tissue samples or cultured cells using RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Scientific, 89,901) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Thermo Scientific, 78,440 and 78,420). The protein concentrations were determined by the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, 23,227). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS‐PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, IPVH00010). After blocking in 5% non‐fat milk, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: anti‐Akt1 (Cell Signaling Technology, 2967, Danvers, MA, USA), anti‐phospho‐Akt1 (Ser473) (Cell Signaling, 4060), anti‐P53 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc‐126, Dallas, TX, USA), anti‐phospho‐P53 (Ser15) (Cell Signaling, 9284), anti‐cleaved caspase‐3 (Cell Signaling, 9661), anti‐Bax (Cell Signaling, 2772), and anti‐β‐actin (Sigma‐Aldrich, A5441). Membranes were washed and incubated with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling, 7074 and 7076). Signals were developed using the SuperSignal West Femto Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Scientific, 34,095) and detected by a ChemiDoc Imaging System (Bio‐Rad).


### Cell Viability Assay
  Cell viability was determined by the Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8, Dojindo, CK04, Kumamoto, Japan). Briefly, cells were seeded at a density of 8000 cells/well in 96‐well plates and treated as indicated for 24 h. At the end of treatment, 10 μL of CCK‐8 solution was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for 2 h at 37°C. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA).


### TUNEL Analysis of Apoptosis
  Apoptotic cells were detected by the Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT) dUTP Nick‐End Labeling (TUNEL) assay using the In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit (Roche Applied Science, 11,684,795,910, Penzberg, Germany). Briefly, cells grown on coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X‐100. The cells were then incubated with the TUNEL reaction mixture containing terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase and fluorescein‐labeled dUTP for 1 h at 37°C in the dark. After washing, the cells were counterstained with DAPI (Invitrogen, D1306, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and mounted on glass slides. Apoptotic cells with fragmented DNA were visualized as green fluorescent signals under a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). The percentage of TUNEL‐positive cells was quantified by counting at least 500 cells per sample.


### P53 Transcription Activity Assay
  The transcriptional activity of P53 was measured using the P53 Transcription Factor Assay Kit (Colorimetric) (ab207225, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. This assay detects the amount of active P53 bound to its consensus binding site immobilized on a 96‐well plate. Nuclear extracts were prepared from cultured cells using the Nuclear Extraction Kit (Abcam, ab113474). The protein concentrations were determined by the BCA assay. Nuclear extracts containing 5 μg of protein were added to the wells of the P53 consensus binding site‐coated plate and incubated for 1 h at room temperature with mild agitation. After washing, a P53 primary antibody was added and incubated for 1 h, followed by incubation with an HRP‐conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h. The colorimetric readout was obtained by adding the developing solution and stopping the reaction with the stop solution provided in the kit. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Instruments). The absorbance value is proportional to the amount of active P53 bound to the consensus binding site.


### Statistics
  Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Differences between two groups were analyzed by unpaired two‐tailed Student's t‐test. For multiple group comparisons, one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used, followed by Tukey's post hoc test to identify significant differences between groups. A p‐value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.


## Results

### Identification of Acacetin as a P53‐Targeting Bioactive Compound From YWL
  Our recent work identified 7 active components of YWL through TCMSP database screening (OB≥ 30%, DL ≥ 0.18), which yielded 78 potential therapeutic targets through intersection with FC‐related genes [21]. Subsequently, a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed to explore the interactions between targets, and the top 5 core proteins were identified, including Akt1, tumor necrosis factor (TNF), tumor protein P53 gene (P53), prostaglandin‐endoperoxide synthase 2 (PTGS‐2), and caspase‐3 (Caspase‐3) (Figure 1A). Furthermore, molecular docking was performed on the core targets, revealing that P53 protein could potentially interact with the compound acacetin from YWL (Figure 1B). In the current work, we investigated the protective effects and mechanisms of acacetin as a potential P53‐targeting agent in alleviating FC.


### Acacetin Administration Improves Gastrointestinal Motility in Loperamide‐Induced Constipation Model
  Based on a loperamide‐induced constipation model in mice, different doses of YWL decoction were administered to the mice by gavaging (YWL‐high: 50 mg/kg/day; YWL‐medium: 25 mg/kg/day; YWL‐low: 10 mg/kg/day). The following parameters were monitored by an activated charcoal propulsion test after fasting: defecation initiation time, fecal pellet counts at 8 h, fecal water content. Additionally, gastric emptying and intestinal propulsion rates were examined to demonstrate the effect of acacetin on gastric and intestinal motility functions in mice. The results showed that compared with the control group, the model group had a significantly delayed defecation time after gavaging activated charcoal, a lower 8 h fecal pellet count, a reduced distinguishable water content and a compromised small intestine propulsion rate, indicating the successful establishment of the FC model. After treatment with different doses of acacetin, the FC in mice showed significant improvement (with the highest dose showing the strongest effects), indicating that acacetin has a potential therapeutic effect on FC (Figure 2A–2D). The comparison of small intestine length and gastrointestinal transit ability demonstrated that the small intestine length of FC mice was significantly reduced, and the propulsion rate of activated charcoal was decreased, indicating that FC affects intestinal transit in mice. After treatment with different doses of acacetin, gastrointestinal transit ability was significantly improved, as evidenced by the enhanced propulsion rate of activated charcoal, with the highest dose showing the strongest effect (Figure 2E–2F). These data demonstrated the therapeutic effect of acacetin on loperamide‐induced constipation model.


### Acacetin Treatment Improves the Histological Condition of Colonic Mucosa and Dampens P53 Signaling in the Mouse Model of FC
  Histological analysis of the distal colon tissue by H&E staining showed that FC induction caused atrophy of colonic villi and muscle fibers in the muscular layer, as well as fibrous deposition. After acacetin treatment, this condition was improved in a dose‐dependent manner (Figure 3A). We further measured the expression of five candidate target genes of YWL (Akt1, TNF‐α, P53, PTGS‐2, and caspase‐3) in the colon tissues of different groups. The results showed that Akt1 and caspase‐3 did not show significant changes, while there was a notable increase in TNF‐α, P53, and PTGS‐2 expression in the FC model group compared to the control samples. Acacetin treatment suppressed their upregulation in the FC model group (Figure 3B). The analysis of apoptosis‐related proteins unveiled the enhanced cleavage of caspase‐3 and increased Bax expression in the FC model group, an effect suppressed by acacetin administration (Figure 3C). Meanwhile, we found that the increased phosphorylation level of P53 protein in the FC model group was associated with a reduced AKT1 phosphorylation (Figure 3D), indicating the potential interplay of these two signaling proteins. Acacetin treatment largely reversed their phosphorylation changes in a dose‐dependent manner (Figure 3D). These findings suggest that acacetin improves the histological condition of colonic mucosa and dampens P53 signaling in the mouse model of FC.


### Loperamide Activates P53 Signaling and Reduces AKT1 Activation, Inducing Apoptosis in Colonic Mucosal Epithelial Cells
  Next, we sought to investigate whether YWL extract has a beneficial effect against loperamide‐induced apoptosis in colonic mucosal epithelial cells. Human colonic mucosal epithelial cells were treated with increasing concentrations of loperamide (5–20 μM) for 24 h. CCK‐8 assay demonstrated that loperamide reduced cell viability in a dose‐dependent manner (Figure 4A), which was associated with an increase of apoptotic events detected by TUNEL staining (Figure 4B). Consistently, loperamide above 10 μM caused the cleavage of caspase‐3 and increased expression of Bax (Figure 4C). Meanwhile, loperamide above 10 μM suppressed the phosphorylation of AKT1 and promoted P53 activation (Figure 4D). These data indicate that loperamide activates P53 signaling and reduces AKT1 activation, inducing apoptosis in colonic mucosal epithelial cells.


### YWL Extract and Acacetin Protects Against Loperamide‐Induced Apoptosis in Colonic Mucosal Epithelial Cells
  To demonstrate the protective effect of YWL extract and the active compound acacetin on loperamide‐induced apoptosis, we first conducted a dose‐responsive analysis. For YWL extract, no significant loss of cell viability was observed until 40 μg/mL (Figure 5A), and acacetin below 20 μM did not cause notable toxicity in colonic mucosal epithelial cells (Figure 5B). We therefore selected 40 μg/mL YWL extract or 10 μM acacetin as an intervention in cells treated with 20 μM loperamide. Cell viability analysis revealed the protective effect of YWL extract and acacetin against loperamide‐induced cytotoxicity (Figure 5C). Loperamide‐induced apoptosis was also suppressed by YWL extract and acacetin (Figure 5D), as well as loperamide‐induced cleavage of caspase‐3 and Bax upregulation (Figure 5E). YWL extract and acacetin treatment suppressed P53 upregulation and phosphorylation, but restored the phosphorylation of AKT1 after loperamide stimulation (Figure 5F). To further demonstrate the activation of P53, we examined the transcription activity of P53 by detecting the amount of P53 proteins bound to specific DNA sequence of the P53 consensus binding site that had been immobilized onto a 96‐well plate. This assay clearly showed that YWL extract and acacetin treatment suppressed Loperamide‐induced P53 activation (Figure 5G).


### Activating P53 Signaling Abrogates the Protection of Acacetin Against Loperamide‐Induced Apoptosis
  To show that the protective effect of acacetin is dependent on P53 inactivation, we treated colonic mucosal epithelial cells with loperamide, loperamide and acacetin, or loperamide and acacetin plus Nutlin‐3a (a well‐established P53 activator). P53 transcription activity analysis confirmed that Nutlin‐3a restored P53 activation in cells treated with loperamide and acacetin (Figure 6A). This was concomitant with the increased P53 phosphorylation upon Nutlin‐3a treatment, as revealed by immunoblotting (Figure 6B). In contrast, the activation of P53 suppressed AKT1 phosphorylation (Figure 6B), indicating that AKT1 activity is negatively regulated by P53 activation. In the meanwhile, the protective effect of acacetin on loperamide‐induced cytotoxicity was abrogated by Nutlin‐3a (Figure 6C). The suppression of loperamide‐induced apoptosis was also abolished after P53 activation by Nutlin‐3a (Figure 6D). Thus, these data suggest that the protective effect of acacetin is dependent on its ability to inactivate P53 signaling.


## Discussion
  This study demonstrated that the bioactive compound acacetin, one of the key active components identified from YWL through network pharmacology analysis, can alleviate loperamide‐induced functional constipation by inhibiting P53‐mediated apoptosis in colonic epithelial cells. In a loperamide‐induced mouse model of functional constipation, acacetin improved gastrointestinal motility, alleviated colon mucosal damage, and suppressed the upregulation of P53 and its downstream apoptotic signaling. Mechanistic studies in human colonic epithelial cells revealed that loperamide induces apoptosis by activating P53 and inhibiting Akt1 signaling, which was attenuated by the treatment with YWL extract and its active component acacetin. Notably, the protective effects of acacetin against loperamide‐induced apoptosis were abrogated by the P53 activator Nutlin‐3a, suggesting the inhibition of P53 signaling is crucial for its anti‐apoptotic properties. These findings uncover a novel mechanism by which acacetin alleviates functional constipation, providing a rationale for its therapeutic application in gastrointestinal motility disorders.

  Intestinal and colonic epithelial cells play a pivotal role in maintaining normal bowel function and motility. These cells are responsible for regulating fluid and electrolyte balance, producing mucus to facilitate stool passage, and mediating neurotransmission involved in peristaltic movements [24]. In constipation, dysregulation of epithelial cell functions has been implicated, including altered barrier integrity, impaired mucus secretion, and disturbances in epithelial ion transport [25, 26, 27]. For instance, single‐cell RNA sequencing analysis of human colonic epithelial cells in healthy individuals and patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) unveiled novel epithelial cell subpopulations with disease‐associated expression signatures, providing insights into epithelial cell plasticity and dysregulation underlying intestinal inflammation [25]. Our findings suggest that apoptosis of colonic epithelial cells induced by loperamide treatment may contribute to the pathogenesis of constipation. Notably, YWL extract and its bioactive component acacetin protected against loperamide‐induced colonic epithelial cell death, which may preserve the structural and functional integrity of the colonic epithelium, thereby improving gastrointestinal motility and alleviating constipation symptoms.

  Accumulating evidence highlights the therapeutic potential of traditional Chinese medicine in treating constipation. A recent study demonstrated the efficacy of luteolin, a flavonoid compound abundantly present in various traditional Chinese medicines, in alleviating loperamide‐induced functional constipation in mice by improving gastrointestinal motility and modulating proteins involved in smooth muscle contraction [28]. In a mouse model of constipation, another study explored the beneficial effects of MaZiRenWan, the most frequently used Traditional Chinese Medicine formula ( Cannabis sativa  L.), where it alleviated loperamide‐induced constipation by modulating the composition of gut microbiota [29]. The traditional Chinese medicine Radix Inulae, from which Yunweiling is derived, contains bioactive compounds such as sesquiterpenes, polysaccharides, and inulin, which exhibit anti‐inflammatory, antioxidant, and antimicrobial properties, and has been traditionally used for treating gastrointestinal disorders [30]. Inulin, a polysaccharide from Radix Inulae, has been shown to alleviate constipation and regulate gut microbiota in a mouse model by increasing beneficial bacteria and improving intestinal motility [31]. Clinical evidence also suggests that dietary inulin supplementation improves irritable bowel syndrome with constipation [32, 33]. Our data align well with the beneficial effects of Radix Inulae on functional constipation, and offer novel angles about how Radix Inulae extract (YWL) may preserve integrity and homeostasis of colonic epithelial cells in the model of loperamide‐induced constipation. However, the potential therapeutic effects of other active compounds in YWL and their synergistic mechanisms warrant further investigation in future studies.

  Loperamide, an opioid receptor agonist used as an anti‐diarrheal agent, has been shown to induce apoptosis in a variety of cancer cells [34, 35, 36]. Our study corroborates these findings, as loperamide induced apoptosis in human colonic epithelial cells, evidenced by increased expression of apoptotic markers. The loperamide‐induced constipation model is a well‐established and widely used experimental approach for studying functional constipation in rodents [22, 23]. Loperamide, a μ‐opioid receptor agonist, induces constipation by inhibiting intestinal peristalsis, reducing gastrointestinal motility, and delaying intestinal transit time, which closely mimics the pathophysiological features observed in patients with opioid‐induced constipation and slow‐transit constipation. Furthermore, therapeutic interventions that show efficacy in this model have demonstrated translational potential in clinical settings, supporting its relevance for evaluating novel treatments for functional constipation [22, 23]. Nevertheless, it should be acknowledged that animal models cannot fully recapitulate the complexity of human functional constipation, which involves multifactorial etiologies including dietary, psychological, and neurological factors; therefore, clinical studies are warranted to validate the therapeutic efficacy of acacetin in patients with functional constipation.

  The tumor suppressor P53 and Akt1 signaling pathway play crucial roles in regulating apoptosis and cell survival [37, 38]. P53 is required for inflammation‐induced epithelial cell death in inflammatory bowel disease [39, 40], while Akt kinase activation blocks apoptosis in intestinal epithelial cells [41]. Our data showed that loperamide activated P53 and inhibited Akt1 signaling, promoting apoptosis in colonic epithelial cells. YWL extracts and its active component acacetin attenuated loperamide‐induced apoptosis by inhibiting P53 activation and maintaining Akt1 signaling. Notably, the protective effects were abrogated by the P53 activator Nutlin‐3a, suggesting inhibition of P53 signaling as a key mechanism underlying the anti‐apoptotic properties. These findings demonstrated that acacetin exhibited protective effects against loperamide‐induced apoptosis, suggesting that the P53‐mediated anti‐apoptotic mechanism represents a shared pathway contributing to their therapeutic efficacy. However, YWL extract contains multiple bioactive compounds that may exert additional therapeutic effects through distinct mechanisms. Further studies are needed to comprehensively characterize the mechanisms of action of other bioactive compounds in YWL and their potential synergistic interaction.


## Conclusion
  This study reveals that acacetin alleviates loperamide‐induced functional constipation by inhibiting P53‐mediated apoptosis in colonic epithelial cells, both in a mouse model and human cell line experiments, highlighting P53 inhibition as a crucial mechanism underlying its therapeutic effects. Targeting P53‐mediated apoptosis emerges as a critical mechanism by which acacetin exerts its therapeutic effects in alleviating functional constipation, providing new insights into the mode of action of traditional herbal remedy YWL. Our findings also highlight the importance of maintaining colonic epithelial integrity in treating gastrointestinal motility disorders. Future studies could further explore the synergistic effects of acacetin and other therapeutic agents, evaluate its efficacy in clinical trials, investigate the potential broader applications of P53 inhibition in other gastrointestinal diseases associated with epithelial cell death or dysfunction, and thoroughly assess the long‐term safety and potential side effects of acacetin.
