# Development of VP30-targeted nanoparticles using DPS4 fusion peptides for the inhibition of Ebola virus

## Introduction
  Ebola virus (EBOV) is a highly infectious filovirus characterized by an exceptionally high mortality rate [1]. Ebola Virus Disease (EVD), a zoonotic and virulent infectious disease characterized by hemorrhagic fever [2], induces immunosuppressive responses, aberrant inflammatory reactions, and coagulation dysfunction [3], culminating in significant fluid loss, electrolyte imbalance, multi-organ failure [4], and case fatality rates potentially reaching 90% [5]. Since its first recorded outbreak in 1976 [6], Ebola has continued to emerge periodically in various African countries. Notably, the epidemic in West Africa from 2014 to 2016 resulted in tens of thousands of illnesses and deaths [7, 8]. Even as recently as 2022, outbreaks have been reported in the Republic of Congo, Uganda, and other regions in Africa [9], posing a serious threat to global public health and necessitating widespread attention.

  The current clinical management of Ebola virus disease (EVD) predominantly entails symptomatic treatment and supportive care, including fluid and electrolyte supplementation to address symptomology. Antibiotics and anti-inflammatory agents may also be employed as adjunctive measures [10]. Nevertheless, the availability of effective, specifically targeted antiviral therapeutics for EVD remains circumscribed. The World Health Organization (WHO) currently recommends monoclonal antibody (mAb)-based interventions such as Ebanga™ (Ansuvimab) and REGN-EB3 (Inmazeb™), which target the EBOV glycoprotein (GP), for patients infected with the Zaire ebolavirus species. These therapeutics, however, have demonstrated efficacy exclusively against this specific species, with no substantive data supporting their utility against other Ebolavirus species [11]. Another GP-targeting mAb, ZMapp®, failed to meet its primary endpoint in a Phase II clinical trial [12]. Furthermore, several evaluated small-molecule compounds, including favipiravir and brincidofovir, failed to demonstrate significant clinical benefits in trials [13–15]. Remdesivir, targeting the viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, demonstrated promising preclinical efficacy and was utilized emergently during the 2018–2019 Kivu Ebola epidemic, yet demonstrated inferior efficacy relative to mAb treatments in clinical evaluations [16]. Additional investigational-stage inhibitors targeting diverse viral proteins remain in preclinical development. These encompass VP35 inhibitors (e.g., VP35 PMO, P-PMO, myricetin), VP40 inhibitors (e.g., sorbitol, mannitol, galactitol), GP inhibitors (e.g., clomiphene, toremifene, bepridil), VP30 inhibitors (e.g., ginkgolic acid, embelin, kobe2602), VP24 inhibitors (e.g., VP24 PMOs, quercetin), and polymerase inhibitors (e.g., lamivudine, zidovudine) [17–19]. Given the limitations inherent in existing treatments, there exists an imperative necessity to develop more efficacious anti-Ebola therapeutics with clearly elucidated mechanisms of action.

  EBOV represents a non-segmented, negative-sense RNA virus featuring a genome of approximately 19 kilobases. This viral genome encompasses seven genes that encode a minimum of seven proteins: nucleoprotein (NP), VP35, VP40, glycoprotein (GP), VP30, VP24, and the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase L protein. Transcriptional editing of the GP gene yields secretory glycoprotein (sGP), small soluble glycoprotein (ssGP), and the structural glycoprotein GP1,2 [20].

  The EBOV life cycle commences with attachment to host cell receptors, followed by cellular entry primarily through macropinocytosis. Fusion with endosomal membranes subsequently releases the viral genome into the cytosol. The viral RNA polymerase then transcribes and replicates the genomic RNA to synthesize viral proteins. Ultimately, newly formed viral components assemble into progeny virions, which bud from the host cell membrane to propagate infection [21]. The VP30 protein functions as a critical transcription factor that binds to viral RNA, regulates mRNA synthesis, and facilitates both transcription and replication of Ebola viral genome within host cells [22]. Previous investigations by our group have established that VP30 directly interacts with NP to potentiate viral transcription and replication [23], a mechanism conserved across diverse filoviruses [24]. A specific molecular pocket within VP30 binds to an NP-derived peptide, thereby regulating Ebola virus RNA synthesis [25]. Furthermore, host ubiquitin ligase retinoblastoma-binding protein 6 (RBBP6) has been shown to interact with the VP30 protein of Ebola virus at the identical binding interface as that between NP and VP30 [25]. This interaction inhibits the binding between NP and VP30, consequently reducing Ebola virus RNA synthesis. Collectively, these findings indicate that the binding interface between VP30 and NP represents a promising target for developing anti-Ebola drugs.

  Ferritin-like proteins are widely used in the biotherapeutics field for applications such as drug delivery, imaging, and diagnosis [26]. The DNA-binding protein from starved cells (DPS) is a highly conserved prokaryotic ferritin that also binds DNA in numerous species. Homologs of DPS are present in most bacterial species and some archaea. This protein may contribute to global gene expression regulation, potentially through chromatin reorganization. DPS employs both its ferritin and DNA-binding capabilities to mitigate environmental stresses, such as oxidative stress. Due to its compact size, hollow spherical structure, and exceptional stability, DPS is being investigated for biotechnological applications, including protein delivery [27, 28]. Specifically, DNA-binding protein from starved cells 4 (DPS4) has been identified as a scaffold candidate owing to its relative rigidity, ease of production in Escherichia coli (E. coli), and non-membranous nature [29–31].

  In this study, we targeted the binding interface between VP30 and its interaction partners NP and RBBP6. Six mutant peptide variants derived from RBBP6 and NP were generated. Concurrently, fusion constructs incorporating these peptides with the DPS4 protein were engineered. All mutant peptides were evaluated using established biochemical and cell-based assays to identify potential inhibitory variants. Furthermore, these peptides exhibited strong apparent affinities with the VP30 protein and conferred potent antiviral activity against both the Ebola virus minigenome system and EBOV transcription and replication-competent virus-like particles (trVLPs). Finally, structural insights into the interactions of potent variants peptides with VP30 were elucidated by X-ray crystallography and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations.


## Methods

### Cell lines and culture
  The EBOV trVLPs system including Huh7.5.1-VP30 cells and EBOVΔVP30-EGFP virus were kindly provided by Professor Qiang Ding from Tsinghua University School of Medicine. Additionally, Huh7-4P cells were obtained from Professor Jin Zhong at the Shanghai Institute of Immunity and Infection, Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS). These cell lines were cultured in complete growth medium comprising Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (P/S). The cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.


### Design of peptide inhibitors for VP30 protein
  To optimize the interaction between VP30 and the wild-type (WT) NP peptide, the PARE algorithm [32] was utilized to predict beneficial mutations at the binding interface. This computational approach, implemented through the HyPare server (https://hypare.weizmann.ac.il/hypareb/main), identifies potential mutation sites by evaluating the size, charge state, hydrophilicity, and secondary structure propensity of amino acid residues. Specifically, residues at the VP30-NP interface were targeted for modification to enhance binding affinity and structural stability. This methodology allowed for a systematic optimization of the peptide sequence based on established protein-protein interaction data. Molecular cloning techniques were used to design and construct a total of eight plasmids: four encoding RBBP6 (WT and three mutants) and four encoding NP (WT and three mutants). The DPS4 gene sequence (UniProt ID: A0A0F8JT29) was ligated to the WT or mutant sequences encoding RBBP6 or NP peptides via multi-step PCR to generate fusion genes. These fusion genes were subsequently cloned into the pET28 vector, yielding the following plasmids: pET28-DPS4-RBBP6, pET28-DPS4-RPL1, pET28-DPS4-RPL2, pET28-DPS4-RPL3, pET28-DPS4-NP, pET28-DPS4-NPL1, pET28-DPS4-NPL2, and pET28-DPS4-NPL3.


### Expression and purification of WT and mutant fusion peptides
  WT and mutant peptides fused to the DPS4 protein were expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3) cultured in lysogeny broth (LB) medium. Following incubation with shaking at 37 °C for 4 h, the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) reached approximately 0.6. Protein expression was then induced by adding 0.5 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), and cultures were incubated for an additional 12 h at 25 °C. Bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation and resuspended in lysis buffer (50 mM Na2HPO4, pH 8.0; 300 mM NaCl; 20 mM imidazole; 0.5% Triton X-100; 0.5 mM Tris (2-carboxyethyl) phosphine (TCEP). Cell disruption was achieved by sonication, followed by centrifugation at 25,000 × g for 25 min at 4 °C to collect the supernatant.

  Purification protocols for both wild-type (WT) and mutant fusion peptides were identical. Initial purification employed a Ni-NTA affinity chromatography column (Senhui Microsphere Technology Co., Ltd). Purification was performed sequentially using the following buffers: Buffer 1 (50 mM Na2HPO4, pH 8.0; 300 mM NaCl; 20 mM imidazole; 0.5 mM TCEP; 0.5% Triton X-100); Buffer 2 (50 mM Na2HPO4, pH 8.0; 300 mM NaCl; 40 mM imidazole; 0.5 mM TCEP; 0.5% Triton X-100); Buffer 3 (50 mM Na2HPO4, pH 8.0; 300 mM NaCl; 80 mM imidazole; 0.5 mM TCEP; 0.5% Triton X-100); Buffer 4 (50 mM Na2HPO4, pH 8.0; 300 mM NaCl; 80 mM imidazole; 0.5 mM TCEP); Buffer 5 (50 mM Na2HPO4, pH 8.0; 300 mM NaCl; 300 mM imidazole; 0.5 mM TCEP). Subsequently, an anion-exchange chromatography step was implemented to remove excess impurities, utilizing Buffer A (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0; 50 mM NaCl) and Buffer B (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0; 1 M NaCl). Finally, the peptide was subjected to size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) on a Superose™ 6 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva, United States) equilibrated with 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, and 150 mM NaCl. Ferritin, conalbumin from chicken egg white, and Blue Dextran were sequentially separated by SEC under identical experimental conditions. SEC calibration standards were obtained using a Gel Filtration Calibration Kit - High Molecular Weight (catalog no. 28403842, Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA), which includes protein standards such as conalbumin (~ 75 kDa), ferritin (~ 440 kDa), and Blue Dextran 2000 (~ 2,000 kDa) for void volume determination and calibration curve construction.

  Final product purity was verified by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (10% SDS-PAGE), and concentrated peptide was obtained using a 100 kDa molecular weight cut-off (MWCO) centrifugal concentrator (Millipore, United States).


### Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) assay
  SPR technology was used to evaluate the apparent affinity of wild-type (WT) and mutant peptides fused to the DPS4 for the VP30 protein. We immobilized VP30 on a protein metal chip as the stationary phase, diluted the WT and mutant peptides with PBS buffer as the mobile phase, and measured their binding ability using the Plexera PlexArray™ HT system. Using PBS as the binding buffer and 0.01 M glycine pH 2.0 as the regeneration buffer, these peptides were loaded successively from low concentration to high concentration, allowing them to combine and dissociate with VP30 protein. After completing the program, the Plexera analysis program was used to analyze the continuous binding and dissociation process of the sample with VP30 protein. The apparent dissociation constant (Kd, app) was calculated using BIAcore evaluation software. Given that the analytes utilized in these experiments are multivalent nanoparticles rather than monovalent ligands, the recorded binding responses reflect avidity-driven interactions. Therefore, the derived Kd, app values should be interpreted as apparent affinities rather than intrinsic binding constants. These metrics are employed herein primarily for the relative comparison of different constructs under identical experimental parameters, acknowledging that factors such as rebinding and mass transport may influence the observed kinetics.


### Minigenome antiviral assay
  The Ebola minigenome system is a well-established model for simulating Ebola virus genome transcription and replication in Huh7-4P cells, commonly employed for antiviral activity screening [17, 33]. Huh7-4P cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 0.1 mg/mL hygromycin. On Day 1, cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well in 100 µL of medium and incubated overnight at 37 °C under 5% CO2. On Day 2, WT and mutant peptides underwent twofold serial dilution in DMEM to generate ten concentration gradients ranging from 12.5 µM to 0.024 µM. Thereafter, 100 µL of each peptide dilution was added per well. The DPS4 protein lacking fusion peptides, served as negative controls. Plasmid construction, protein purification, and cellular processing protocols for this protein were identical to those used for the peptide fusion proteins. A medium-only control group was included. Following a 48-hr incubation at 37 °C and 5% CO₂, the cell supernatant was aspirated.

  Cell lysis and luminescence detection were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions for the Renilla-Lumi™ Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Kit (Beyotime, China). The resultant luminescence values were subsequently analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9 software to derive EC₅₀ values.


### Anti-EBOV trVLPs assay
  The EBOV trVLPs system enables efficient evaluation of Ebola virus therapeutics under Biosafety Level 2 (BSL-2) conditions. This methodology entails substituting the VP30 gene within the cDNA clone of the Mayinga strain of Zaire Ebola virus with the enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) gene, specifically via the plasmid pUC57-EBOV-ΔVP30-EGFP. The resultant defective virus is subsequently propagated and amplified in Huh7.5.1 cells genetically engineered to overexpress VP30. Huh7.5.1-VP30 cells were plated at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well in 6-well plates (Corning, United States), cultured overnight, and infected with EBOVΔVP30-EGFP at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1. Following 6 hr of incubation, the viral medium was aspirated, and the cells were maintained for an additional 72 h in serum-supplemented medium. The viral supernatant was harvested, and cellular debris was removed by filtration through a 0.45-µm membrane (BIOFIL, China). The viral solution was aliquoted into EP tubes and stored at -80 °C.

  Subsequently, Huh7.5.1-VP30 cells were seeded into 48-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well. Proteins, including RBBP6, RPL1, NP, NPL3 containing DPS4, and DPS4 (serving as a negative control), were serially diluted in 5-fold increments starting from 20 µM and administered to the Huh7.5.1-VP30 cells. After incubating for 1 h in a 37 °C incubator, 5% CO₂ incubator, EBOVΔVP30-EGFP virus particles were introduced at an MOI of 0.1. Following an additional 48 h of culture, nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33,258 reagent (Beyotime, China), and imaging was conducted using the Cell Imaging Microplate Detection System-Cytation5 (Biotek, United States). Remdesivir, an inhibitor of Ebola virus RNA-dependent RNA polymerase [34], was employed as a positive control and serially diluted from 2 µM in 5-fold steps, with all other procedures consistent with the aforementioned protocol.


### 3-(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay
  To evaluate the cytotoxicity of peptide inhibitors, Vero and Huh7 cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well in 96-well plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C under 5% CO2. Peptide inhibitors (solvent: PBS) were serially diluted in serum-free DMEM medium, commencing at 100 µM. A five-fold dilution was employed for each concentration, tested in triplicate, generating a total of 10 concentration gradients. Following incubation, MTT reagent was added to each well at a final concentration of 0.5 mg/mL, followed by an additional 4-hour incubation. The medium was subsequently discarded, and 150 µL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added to each well. Absorbance at 570 nm (OD570) was measured using a Tecan Spark microplate reader.


### Negative staining transmission electron microscopy (NS-TEM) and data collection
  The negative staining method was employed for the initial structural evaluation of the DPS4-NPL3 protein, which was expressed through the fusion of DPS4 and NPL3 using the plasmid pET28-DPS4-NPL3, following the aforementioned expression and purification protocols. The DPS4-NPL3 sample was diluted to a concentration of 0.03 mg/mL in a buffer containing 20 mM Tris (pH 8.0) and 150 mM NaCl. A 5 µL aliquot of the sample was applied to a carbon-coated copper grid (300 mesh) that had been previously hydrophilized via glow discharge. After allowing the particles to adsorb for a specific period, excess sample was blotted with filter paper. The grid was immediately washed with deionized water to remove residual buffer salts and prevent salt crystallization artifacts. Subsequently, the grid was stained with 3% (w/v) uranyl acetate solution at ambient temperature for staining over 40 s, blotted to remove excess stain, and air-dried.

  Micrographs were collected using a Thermo Scientific Talos L120C transmission electron microscope operating at an acceleration voltage of 120 kV. Images were recorded on a Ceta CMOS detector at a nominal magnification of 92,000×, corresponding to a calibrated pixel size of 1.527 Å. Data were acquired with a defocus range of -1.0 to -2.0 μm. All image processing steps were performed using CryoSPARC v4.5.3. A total of 152 micrographs were imported, and contrast transfer function (CTF) parameters were estimated using CTFFIND4. After curating exposures to remove poor-quality images, particles were manually picked to train the picker. The extracted particles were subjected to multiple rounds of 2D classification to remove false positives and damaged particles. A subset of selected particles was used for ab initio reconstruction, followed by homogeneous refinement and final non-uniform refinement.


### Dynamic light scattering (DLS) assay
  DLS quantifies the hydrodynamic diameter, encompassing the Z-average and particle size distribution of solvated molecules. A 1 mg/mL DPS4-NPL3 protein sample, purified via a Superose 6 gel filtration column, was analyzed using a DynaPro Titan instrument (Wyatt Technology Corp.). Samples were centrifuged at 4 °C and 12,000 rpm to remove precipitates prior to analysis. Subsequently, 20 µL of ddH2O was injected into the cuvette using a syringe, and a test performed to confirm instrument readiness. Following water removal, the sample was introduced for analysis, ensuring bubble-free handling. Instrument configuration parameters comprised: temperature maintained at 10 °C, material refractive index set at 1.45, dispersant refractive index at 1.330, measurement intervals of 20 s, and six replicate acquisitions.


### Crystallization
  Protein crystallization was performed to illustrate the antiviral mechanism of RPL1 and NPL3 peptides. Peptides RPL1 and NPL3 were synthesized by GenScript (Nanjing). The peptides were incubated with VP30 protein for 1 h at a molar concentration ratio of 1:1.5. The mixture was then seeded in a sitting drop crystal plate, and the crystallization screening kit developed by Hampton Research was used to screen initial hit conditions. The sitting drop crystal plate was placed in a crystal incubator at 18 °C. Six days later, typical crystal complexes of NPL3 or RPL1 peptide with VP30 protein were observed. For the final complex of VP30 and NPL3, growth occurred in a buffer containing 0.15 M cesium chloride and 15% polyethylene glycol 3350 (PEG3350), seeded with apo VP30 crystals. The complex crystal of VP30 and RPL1 grew in a buffer containing 0.1 M sodium malonate pH 5.0 and 12% w/v polyethylene glycol 3,350, also seeded with apo VP30 crystals. The crystals were sent to the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility for X-ray diffraction and data collection [35]. Initial processing of the diffraction images was done using the XDS Program Package to determine lattice type, diffraction intensity, and cell parameters [36]. Phaser was used to identify the solution, followed by manual checking with Coot and refinement with refmac5 or phenix. refine. Finally, PyMOL was utilized to prepare the figures [37].


### Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations
  The three-dimensional structures of VP30, corresponding to PDB IDs 9UP3 and 9UP4 for the peptide-VP30 complexes, were generated using PyMol. MD simulations were conducted using OpenMMDL, a framework developed by Gerhard Wolber and colleagues, derived from OpenMM and optimized for protein-ligand interactions [38]. The MD setup was performed by openmmdl_setup, which applied the Amber ff14SB force field to proteins and GAFF to ligands. Proteins were solvated in an orthorhombic box containing TIP3P water, 0.15 M NaCl, and a 10 Å padding. A Monte Carlo barostat was integrated into the system at 1 bar pressure and 300 K temperature. Simulations were executed via Langevin dynamics at 300 K for 200 ns, incorporating a 0.5 ns equilibration phase. MD computations were performed on local workstation with NVIDIA RTX A5000 GPUs (NVIDIA Corporation, Santa Clara). Analysis of the molecular dynamics data was conducted using openmmdl_analysis.


## Results

### Design and characterization of peptide inhibitors targeting VP30 protein
  To identify potential binding sites for the VP30 protein, we aligned the complex structures of RBBP6 (PDB ID: 6E5X) and NP (PDB ID: 5VAP) with VP30. The root mean square deviation (RMSD) values between these complexes were only 0.34 Å, indicating high structural similarity, and their interaction interfaces overlapped significantly (Fig. 1A). To optimize peptide inhibitors that effectively cover the VP30 binding interface, we planned to simultaneously modify peptides from RBBP6 and NP protein involved in VP30 interactions.

  Fig. 1Design and characterization of peptides. A. Structural overlay of RBBP6 peptides (PDB: 6E5X) and NP peptides (PDB: 5VAP) at their respective interfaces with the EBOV VP30 protein. The left panel illustrates a comparative structural analysis of the RBBP6-VP30 and NP-VP30 complexes, while the right panel displays the surface representation of the binding interfaces. Peptides are shown in cartoon representation (RBBP6 in green; NP in blue), with the corresponding VP30 proteins colored in salmon and sand, respectively. B. Amino acid sequences and schematic of the expression plasmids for WT and mutant peptides. WT residues are labeled in black, while mutations are highlighted in blue (NP) and purple (RBBP6). C. SDS-PAGE (10%) analysis of the eight purified DPS4-peptide fusion proteins, confirming high purity (> 95%) and correct molecular weight. D. SEC elution profiles obtained using a Superose 6 column. The chromatogram of DPS4-NPL3 is shown as a representative example, compared against molecular weight standards (ferritin and conalbumin) processed under identical buffer and flow rate conditions to evaluate the oligomeric state. E. Hydrodynamic size distribution determined by DLS. The bar graph represents the Z-average particle size, with error bars indicating the standard deviation from triplicate measurements. The estimated molecular weight of DPS4-NPL3 (259 kDa) was calculated based on the DLS-derived diffusion coefficient. F. NS-TEM of the DPS4-NPL3 nanoparticles.   Electrostatic attraction plays a key role in Brownian motion between molecules [39] and the PARE algorithm evaluates the relationship between protein complex formation rates and Coulomb energy of intermolecular interactions [40]. Previous studies indicated that residues 554–567 of RBBP6 and residues 601–612 of NP are critical for binding to VP30 [23]. Using the PARE algorithm, we designed potential higher affinity peptide inhibitory variants by mutating these amino acid sites in WT RBBP6 and NP peptides. The results showed that positively charged lysine (K) and arginine (R), as well as negatively charged glutamic acid (E) and aspartic acid (D), were introduced. Ultimately, six mutants, three for RBBP6 (RPL1, RPL2, RPL3) and three for NP (NPL1, NPL2 and NPL3), were designed (Fig. 1B). Previous studies have established that NP and RBBP6 peptides lack cellular permeability [23]. To enhance membrane permeability, we utilized DPS4, a protein that self-assembles into 12-mer polymers and presents peptides on a spherical surface [29].

  Fusion plasmids were constructed to encode engineered RBBP6, WT NP, and various mutant peptides (Fig. 1B). Utilizing standard molecular cloning, protein expression, and purification protocols, eight distinct DPS4-fused peptide constructs were isolated. All purified proteins exhibited high homogeneity and achieved a purity level exceeding 95%, as confirmed by SDS-PAGE (Fig. 1C and Fig. S1).

  SEC using a Superose 6 column showed that the DPS4-NPL3 protein eluted at approximately 15 mL. Comparison with molecular weight standards—specifically ferritin (440 kDa) and conalbumin (75 kDa)—placed the elution volume of DPS4-NPL3 between these two markers, suggesting that the protein exists predominantly as a monodisperse dodecameric assembly in solution. This oligomeric state was further validated by comparing the elution profile directly against standard molecular weight markers (Fig. 1D). These data corroborate subsequent DLS analysis, which demonstrated that these constructs, exemplified by DPS4-NPL3, exhibit high monodispersity with a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.12 ± 0.001. DLS measurements revealed nanoparticles with an average hydrodynamic radius (Rh) of 6.4 nm and an estimated molecular weight of approximately 259 kDa. Although this reflects a ~ 13% deviation from the theoretical mass of 297.6 kDa (Fig. 1E), such a discrepancy is within the anticipated experimental error range for DLS-derived estimations. These variations are typically attributed to the influence of the hydration shell and potential deviations from perfectly spherical particle geometry [41].

  To assess the morphology and structural homogeneity of the DPS4-NPL3 nanoparticles, NS-TEM was performed. From a dataset of 152 micrographs, 10,526 particles were selected for 2D classification and subsequent 3D reconstruction. The resulting 3D model yielded a resolution of 9.12 Å. Although the resolution is inherently constrained by the negative-staining method, the reconstruction reveals a distinct globular architecture with discernible surface protrusions, thereby confirming the structural integrity and successful assembly of the nanoparticle (Fig. 1F).


### Identification of peptide mutants with enhanced VP30 binding affinity
  To identify variants with improved binding characteristics, SPR assays were conducted to quantify the interactions between the VP30 protein and both WT and mutant peptides fused to the DPS4 scaffold (Fig. 2A). Both the WT and mutant peptides derived from RBBP6 and NP demonstrated measurable binding responses to VP30. Given the multivalent nature of the DPS4-peptide nanoparticles, the binding data were analyzed to determine Kd, app, which reflect the functional avidity of the constructs.

  Fig. 2SPR analysis of the interactions between DPS4-fused proteins and VP30. A. Schematic representation of the SPR assay configuration. The VP30110−272 protein was immobilized on the sensor chip surface, followed by the sequential injection of DPS4-fused WT or mutant peptides to monitor apparent binding and dissociation kinetics. B–I. Representative sensorgrams illustrating the binding of DPS4-RBBP6, DPS4-NP, and their respective mutant variants to immobilized VP30110−272 across a range of concentrations. The binding response (RU) is plotted as a function of time, with solvent-only injections utilized as a baseline control. Apparent dissociation constants Kd, app were determined using BIAevaluation software. As these values reflect functional avidity derived from multivalent interactions rather than intrinsic monovalent affinity, they are intended for the relative comparison of binding potencies under standardized experimental conditions

  In the RBBP6-derived group, the Kd, app values were determined to be 33.5 nM for WT RBBP6, 21.5 nM for the RPL1 mutant, 44.9 nM for RPL2, and 32.6 nM for RPL3. Within the NP-derived group, the Kd, app values were 33.6 nM for WT NP, 32.4 nM for NPL1, 36.4 nM for NPL2, and 19.0 nM for NPL3 (Fig. 2B–I).

  Comparative analysis of these Kd, app values revealed that variants RPL2 and RPL3 exhibited reduced apparent affinity relative to WT RBBP6. In contrast, the RPL1 mutant demonstrated a moderate enhancement in binding strength under identical experimental conditions (Fig. 2B–E). Within the NP series, NPL3 exhibited the most significant improvement in apparent affinity (Kd, app = 19.0 nM) compared to both the WT NP (33.6 nM) and the other mutants in the group (Fig. 2F–I).

  In summary, based on these SPR-derived apparent affinity measurements, the mutant fusion peptides RPL1 and NPL3 were identified as having superior binding profiles compared to their respective WT counterparts. Consequently, these two variants were selected for further functional evaluation, including cell-based antiviral activity assays.


### Identifying variants with antiviral activity in the EBOV minigenome system
  To further investigate the inhibitory effects of WT and mutant peptides fused with DPS4 on Ebola virus, we employed the EBOV minigenome system. This system was developed by Jin Zhong et al. [42]. Briefly, minigenome RNA was constructed, comprising the EBOV leader/trailer, untranslated regions (UTRs), and the Gaussia luciferase (GLuc) reporter gene. Subsequently, a Huh7 cell line stably expressing the four EBOV replication complex proteins (NP, VP35, VP30, L) was established. Minigenome RNA transcribed by T7 RNA polymerase was then transfected into these cells via electroporation. Cells stably supporting EBOV viral genome transcription and replication were selected using antibiotics. These engineered cells express luciferase, which emits fluorescence upon substrate addition (Fig. 3A). Results demonstrated that Renilla luciferase (Rluc) fluorescence values in Huh7-4P cells exhibited concentration-dependent inhibition with increasing concentrations of RBBP6, NP WT peptide, and NP mutant peptides. This indicates that these peptides variably inhibit Ebola viral genome transcription and replication (Fig. 3B-I).

  Fig. 3Antiviral activity of WT and mutant peptides in the minigenome system. A. Schematic representation of the antiviral mechanism exerted by WT and mutant peptides in Huh7-4P cells. B-I. Representative dose-response curves demonstrating the inhibitory effects of RBBP6, NP WT, and mutant peptides on viral genome transcription in Huh7-4P cells utilizing the EBOV minigenome system. The X-axis denotes peptide concentration (RBBP6, NP WT, and mutants), while the Y-axis indicates the luminescence intensity derived from the minigenome-encoded luciferase reporter gene. J. Dose-dependent modulation of Rluc luminescence intensity by the negative control protein DPS4, demonstrating its lack of antiviral activity in this assay system

  In the RBBP6 group, the half-maximal effective concentration (EC50) values for mutants RPL1 and RPL2 were lower than that of the WT RBBP6, yielding EC50 values of 3.5 µM for RBBP6, 1.5 µM for RPL1, 1.7 µM for RPL2, and 8.0 µM for RPL3 (Fig. 3B–E). Within the NP group, all mutant peptides exhibited reduced EC50 values relative to the WT NP, with NPL3 demonstrating the lowest value of 1.8 µM, while the EC50 values for NP, NPL1, and NPL2 were 3.7 µM, 2.6 µM, and 2.4 µM, respectively (Fig. 3F–I). To verify that the inhibitory effects were specifically attributable to these peptides, we evaluated the DPS4 scaffold alone as negative controls. The DPS4 scaffold did not exhibit significant antiviral activity in the EBOV minigenome (Fig. 3J), confirming that DPS4 serves as a neutral scaffold and does not independently influence the experimental outcomes.

  Integrating these antiviral assay results with the SPR affinity data identified two DPS4-fusion mutant peptides (RPL1 and NPL3), demonstrating both high binding affinity for VP30 and superior antiviral activity.


### Assessment of multiple peptides targeting EBOVΔVP30-EGFP infection in vitro
  The trVLPs system for Ebola virus constitutes a valuable tool for evaluating anti-EBOV compounds under BSL-2 conditions [34]. This system replicates the structural and functional characteristics of complete virions, including transcriptional and replicative capabilities. Initially, EGFP was substituted for VP30 within the minigenome, generating the pUC57-EBOV-VP30-EGFP plasmid. Co-transfection of this plasmid with vectors expressing the five other EBOV proteins into 293T cells yielded fully assembled virus-like particles. Supernatants containing these particles were used to infect VP30-overexpressing cells, thereby enabling viral replication and serial passage. Successful infection manifested as detectable green EGFP fluorescence signals (Fig. 4A). This trVLPs methodology was subsequently employed to assess the antiviral potential of peptides NPL3 and RPL1, previously identified through in vitro screenings.

### Characterization of NPL3 and RPL1 peptide binding to VP30 protein
  The binding interfaces identified between NP and VP30 in our previous study overlap significantly with those of RPPB6 and VP30. To further elucidate the differences between NPL3 and RPL1, we compared the structures of these complexes. Our structural comparison revealed that all peptides are partially overlapped (Fig. 7A). Notably, the structure of VP30 is highly consistent across all complexes, with an RMSD value of only 0.32 Å. This suggests that the binding interface between NP and VP30 is a stable and conserved region. In contrast, a further structure alignment between RPL1 and NPL3 revealed large structural differences, with an RMSD value of 2.30 Å. This indicates that although both peptides bind to the same epitope of VP30, there are significant differences in their interaction residues (Fig. 7B). These structural differences suggest that NPL3 and RPL1 have distinct binding modes to VP30, which may be exploited for therapeutic purposes.

### MD simulations of NPL3 and RPL1 interactions with VP30
  To investigate the binding dynamics of peptides to VP30, molecular dynamics simulations employing the OpenMMDL approach were conducted for NPL3 and RPL1 engaging with VP30. Simulations focused on the VP30 binding site utilized complexes resolved in this study. Following 200 ns of simulation, the binding dynamics between VP30 and NPL3 or RPL1 were assessed through barcode and interaction mode analyses.

  Within the VP30-NPL3 complex, hydrogen bonding involving nitrogen atom of Q203 (N2087) was consistently observed across all ten binding modes, indicating a stable interaction with VP30 residue Q203 (Fig. 8A, upper left; Fig. S2). Beyond hydrogen bonds, prominent hydrophobic interactions were frequently detected, achieving maximal frequencies of 86.67% and 80.66% (Fig. 8A, middle left; Fig. S2). Binding mode 2 emerged as the predominant conformation, exhibiting all essential interactions for NPL3-VP30 binding throughout the trajectory (Fig. 8A, lower left). For the VP30-RPL1 complex, the interaction pattern closely resembled that of VP30-NPL3, primarily mediated by hydrogen and hydrophobic bonds. The frequency of hydrogen bonding with VP30 residue H193 was lower compared to Q203 in NPL3; however, in binding mode 14, RPL1 demonstrated enhanced hydrogen and hydrophobic bonding configurations (Fig. 8B).

  Analysis of interaction frequencies demonstrated that thirteen residues exhibited frequencies exceeding 0.9 in MD simulations of the NPL3-VP30 complex (Fig. 8C). These residues—R213, Q229, W230, L226, F222, A225, E209, V210, P206, and Q203—corresponded to those identified within the interaction networks of previously determined crystal complex structures (Fig. 5F). In MD simulations of the RPL1-VP30 complex, nine residues surpassed the 0.9 frequency threshold (Fig. 8D). Among these, E197 and W230 participated in hydrogen bonding interactions, while L199, S234, M237, and V207 were involved in hydrophobic interactions within the corresponding crystal complexes (Fig. 6F). Several residues displaying high interaction frequencies in simulations were absent from the crystal structures of VP30-NPL3 or VP30-RPL1 complexes, indicating that the crystal structures captured only the most stable conformations during VP30-peptide binding.

  Collectively, the MD simulations elucidated the dynamic binding processes of RPL1 and NPL3 with VP30, uncovering additional residues implicated in the interaction mechanisms between VP30 and peptide inhibitory variants.


## Discussion
  In early 2025, a resurgence of Ebola virus disease (EVD) in Uganda underscored the persistent threat of the virus and the limitations of current countermeasures [43]. While the rVSV-ZEBOV vaccine is FDA and EMA approved, concerns regarding its long-term efficacy and side-effect profile remain [44, 45]. Given the unpredictable nature of EVD outbreaks, there is a critical need for stable, efficacious therapeutics with well-defined mechanisms of action.

  The phosphorylation status of the Ebola virus VP30 protein is a key regulator of viral genome transcription and replication [46]. Consequently, the VP30–NP interface has emerged as a promising target for therapeutic intervention [25, 47]. Building on our previous identification of small-molecule inhibitors [17, 18, 23], this study engineered peptides (NPL3 and RPL1) derived from NP and RBBP6 binding motifs. When displayed on the DPS4 nanoparticle scaffold, these constructs exhibited significantly enhanced antiviral activity in cell-based models compared to WT peptide fusions.

  The observed cellular efficacy of these constructs is closely linked to the internalization and metabolic fate of the DPS4 scaffold. Based on previous studies of ferritin-like protein nanocages, DPS4 nanoparticles are expected to be internalized primarily via endocytic pathways, including macropinocytosis and receptor-mediated endocytosis, followed by trafficking through the endo-lysosomal system [41, 48, 49]. Once localized within acidic endosomal and lysosomal compartments, peptide release may occur through two plausible mechanisms. First, the proteinaceous DPS4 scaffold is susceptible to lysosomal proteases, which facilitate the degradation of the nanocage and subsequent release of the conjugated peptides. Second, the acidic microenvironment may induce conformational destabilization or transient “breathing” of the DPS4 nanocage, promoting peptide exposure or dissociation. While the precise intracellular release mechanism remains to be elucidated experimentally, these pathways provide a reasonable framework for understanding DPS4-mediated peptide delivery and its subsequent inhibition of viral replication.

  However, interpreting the biophysical basis of this inhibition requires a careful distinction between peptide-specific molecular recognition and nanoparticle-driven phenomena. The DPS4 scaffold presents peptides in a multivalent assembly, which significantly enhances functional effects through increased local peptide concentration and avidity-driven binding. Consequently, the SPR data should be interpreted as apparent affinities rather than intrinsic binding constants. Because the analytes are multivalent nanoparticles interacting with immobilized VP30, the reported values reflect a complex combination of sequence-specific binding, rebinding contributions, and potential mass transport limitations [50–52]. While these values demonstrate the overall efficiency of the nanoparticle constructs, the numerical differences in apparent affinities should not be misinterpreted as reflecting direct changes in intrinsic peptide–protein interaction kinetics alone.

  This distinction is further supported by our structural results. Crystallographic analysis and MD simulations provide high-resolution insights into how isolated, monovalent peptides bind to VP30, rationalizing the improvements achieved through sequence engineering. Nevertheless, these monovalent structures do not capture the spatial orientation, flexibility, or simultaneous engagement geometry of multiple peptides on the dodecameric DPS4 scaffold. Therefore, while the structural data validate the molecular mechanism at the binding site, they do not fully explain the nanoparticle-level avidity or the resulting cellular antiviral effects.


## Conclusions
  In summary, this study provides a robust proof-of-concept for the use of multivalent nanoparticle-displayed peptides to target the Ebola virus VP30–NP interaction. We demonstrated that engineered peptides NPL3 and RPL1, when presented on a DPS4 scaffold, achieve potent inhibition in vitro, likely through endo-lysosomal uptake and subsequent intracellular release. However, framing these results as a direct step toward translational drug development would be premature. The therapeutic potential of these constructs remains to be validated in vivo, with particular focus on the immunogenicity of the bacterial ferritin-derived scaffold and its systemic pharmacokinetic profile. Our findings establish a promising biophysical framework for designing high-avidity inhibitors, yet further research into the translational readiness of such proteinaceous nanocages is essential.
