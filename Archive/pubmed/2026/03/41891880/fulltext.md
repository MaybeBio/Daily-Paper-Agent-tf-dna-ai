# Accurate prediction of cohesin and RNA Polymerase II-associated chromatin interactions using convolutional neural networks


## Abstract
  The three-dimensional (3D) genome organization specifies how the distal regulatory elements in the linear genome interact with target genes to regulate transcription. Several experimental methods have been developed to study the 3D genome organization. However, these methods are, in general, expensive, technically challenging, and time-consuming. We present Convolutional Neural Networks-Chromatin Interaction Predictor (CNN-ChIPr), a machine learning method for predicting the relative strength of cohesin- and RNA Polymerase II (RNA Pol II)-associated chromatin interactions/loops using experimental ChIP-seq data and other public inputs that can be easily obtained without additional new experiments. To leverage the pattern-recognition capability of CNN, we formatted the multiple ChIP-seq data, defining the features of interaction anchor regions into two-dimensional (2D) grids. The results showed that CNN-ChIPr performs well in predicting cohesin- and RNA Pol II-associated chromatin interactions at the peak-level resolution. The predictions can also be used to reconstruct contact maps with high similarity to the maps constructed by the original data. In addition to cohesin loops and RNA Pol II loops, CNN-ChIPr can accurately predict Hi-C interactions as well. We demonstrate the utility of this approach by identifying chromatin loops, target genes, and downstream pathways associated with enhancers regulated by the binding of tissue-specific master transcription factors, androgen receptor (AR) and estrogen receptor (ER), in prostate cancer and breast cancer cells, respectively. Collectively, CNN-ChIPr complements experimental 3D genome mapping technologies and provides a powerful alternative in contexts where such assays are impractical or infeasible, such as clinical specimens or time-course studies.


## Introduction
  The three-dimensional (3D) genome organization is one of the main factors that specify how the genes are regulated by their regulatory elements. This, in turn, tunes the response of cells to environmental changes. Many experimental methods are available to study the 3D genome organization, such as Hi-C [1], ChIA-PET [2], Micro-C [3], HiChIP [4], and PLAC-Seq [5], among others. The ENCODE portal [6] has generated gold-standard atlases of cohesin-mediated chromatin loops in 24 cell lines using the chromatin interaction analysis by paired-end tag (ChIA-PET) technology [7]. Prior studies by our group and others reported RNA Polymerase II-associated chromatin interactions using the same methods [8, 9]. However, these methods are, in general, expensive, technically challenging, and time-consuming. Therefore, there is a tremendous need to develop new computational methods to accurately predict cohesin- and RNA Pol II-associated chromatin interactions using simple experimental inputs. The current study makes advances in this arena.

  Machine learning methods have been proposed to predict 3D chromatin interactions (see review articles [10, 11]). A recent method, Orca, uses a model that encodes DNA sequences into bins of different sizes and outputs the contact maps for a region of size 1 Mbp – 256 Mbp at nine different resolutions ranging from 4 Kbp to 1 Mbp [12]. This output format provided by Orca is most suitable for predicting Hi-C and Micro-C contact maps. Other methods, like Akita [13] and DeepC [14], are limited to only interactions within 1 Mbp. The three methods above focus more on predicting the effect of mutations and genetic variations on 3D genome folding. ChINN can predict whether Hi-C interactions, in addition to RNA Pol II and CTCF ChIA–PET interactions, exist or not using DNA sequence as input [15] without estimating the interaction strength. In prior studies, we demonstrated that RNA Pol II chromatin interaction strength at gene promoters strongly correlates with transcript abundance [8]. Therefore, unlike classifier-based models, regression models can provide additional information on target gene transcription. DeepTACT uses DNA sequence and chromatin accessibility data to predict promoter capture Hi-C interactions [16]. However, DeepTACT is also a classifier and is limited to predicting enhancer-promoter and promoter-promoter interactions. We developed Chromatin Interaction Predictor (ChIPr) [17] earlier to predict the interaction strength of cohesin-mediated chromatin loops. We recently applied ChIPr to propose the “pearl necklace” model of PAX2 transcriptional silencing—a phenomenon observed in over 80% of human endometrial tumors [18]. Mechanistically, we demonstrated that the PAX2 gene resides within an insulated neighborhood formed by cohesin loops, which act as barriers to prevent the spread of repressive H3K27me3 signals to adjacent genes. To our knowledge, this is the first report of a highly recurrent loss of tumor suppressor function driven by alterations in the histone code rather than the DNA code. However, ChIPr cannot be used to predict RNA Pol II-associated chromatin interactions. In summary, there is a critical need to develop novel methods to accurately predict cohesin- and RNA Pol II-associated chromatin interactions at a high-resolution, in a genome-scale, and with the ability to identify cell-type-specific loops.

  Here, we present a convolutional neural network (CNN)-based model [19, 20], CNN-ChIPr, to predict the strength of cohesin- and RNA Pol II-associated loops. We have also shown that CNN-ChIPr can accurately predict high-resolution Hi-C interactions. Importantly, we have shown that CNN-ChIPr can identify chromatin loops, target genes, and pathways associated with tissue-specific master transcription factors, such as AR and ER, in androgen-responsive prostate cancer cells and estrogen-responsive breast cancer cells, respectively. CNN-ChIPr can also be a valuable tool for the interpretation of the non-coding genome, especially for identifying functional disease-associated SNPs and their gene targets from GWAS studies.


## Materials and methods

### Data used
  We used several ChIP-seq datasets of transcription factors (TFs), architectural proteins, and active and inactive histone marks. We also used ATAC-seq data as a measure of chromatin accessibility. These different data were used to make the model inputs valid for predicting different protein-mediated interactions. For instance, among the inputs are the ChIP-seq data for RNA Pol II, RAD21, and CTCF. In addition, we used the two histone marks ChIP-seq data of H3K27ac and H3K27me3, which are marks for active and inactive chromatin, respectively. Furthermore, we used several more inputs that can be easily obtained without any new experimental efforts. For instance, we used the genomic distance between the two anchor peaks. Also, we used a flag indicating whether the two anchor peaks belong to the same TAD. Since TADs are ∼70% conserved among cell lines and species [21], we used the TADs coordinates of the IMR90 cell line, although our experiments were on other cell lines (GM12878, K562, H1, and HepG2). Moreover, we used another flag to indicate whether or not the two anchor peaks contain CTCF motifs in a convergent orientation.


### ChIP-seq data maps
  Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) were originally designed to process data with a grid-like topology (like 2D images). We aim to shape the ChIP-seq inputs in a 2D grid suitable for input to the 2D CNN. Each ChIP-seq input sample will have one row in the 2D grid, and the row of a specific input will be centered at the peak of interest. The elements before the central element will represent normalized read counts of the upstream 5 Kbp bins of the peak of interest. Similarly, the elements following the central one will hold the normalized read counts of the downstream 5 Kbp bins. Thus, a row corresponding to an input sample for one of the two anchor peaks will consist of nine elements (the central element is the peak of interest, and the other elements correspond to 20 Kbp up and downstream of the central peak). Six input samples will build a small 2D grid of 6 × 9 entries.

  As described above, we construct a 2D grid of the input data for each of the two anchor peaks of interaction. The two maps are then concatenated together. However, to avoid treating elements of the second map as neighboring elements to the first anchor peak, we insert a small map of zeros between the two maps of the two anchor peaks. This map of zeros will have a width equal to the width of the CNN filter used (‘3’ in our case).


### CNN-ChIPr structure
  The schematic of CNN-ChIPr is shown in Fig. 1. The first module in CNN-ChIPr is the ChIP-seq data map encoder. It takes the ChIP-seq data map (described above) for the two potential candidate anchor peaks of the interaction as its input and outputs the reduced (latent) dimension features of that map. We use the ChIP-seq data of RAD21, RNA Pol II, CTCF, H3K27ac, and H3K27me3. Also, we use ATAC-seq data as the sixth row of the ChIP-seq map (Fig. 1). This ChIP-seq map passes through a convolutional layer to extract the features necessary for predicting hypothetical chromatin interaction strength. The outputs of the convolutional layer pass through a flattening layer whose outputs are fully connected to a hidden layer with a relatively small number of artificial neurons (∼20). This layer can be considered as the reduced (latent) dimension features of the input ChIP-seq data map.

  Similarly, the second module of CNN-ChIPr is the DNA sequence map encoder. It takes the DNA sequence map of the two anchor peaks as its input and outputs the reduced (latent) dimension features of that DNA sequence map. For the DNA sequence information, we use one-hot encoding to create a sequence map for each anchor peak using 512 bps centered at the center of the anchor peak. We concatenate the two DNA sequence maps of the two peaks and insert a 2D grid of width 40 (the width of the CNN filter used for processing the sequence map [16]) with all elements set to zero in the middle (Fig. 1). The DNA sequence map passes through a convolutional layer and a flattening layer. The output of the flattening layer is also fully connected to a hidden layer with a relatively small number of neurons that can also be considered as the reduced (latent) dimension features of the DNA sequence map.

  We added other inputs to the model as well. For instance, we added the genomic distance between the two anchor peaks. Since TADs are ∼70% conserved across cell types [21], we added a flag indicating whether or not the two anchor peaks belong to the same TAD. We used the high-quality TADs information of a common cell line (IMR90), other than those, we tried to predict their interactions. In addition, we added a flag to indicate whether or not the CTCF motif exists in the two anchor peaks and in a convergent orientation. Each of these three inputs passes through a small neural network with one hidden neuron (linear activation function), and the output of this neuron (which relates linearly to the original values of the three inputs) is connected to the next layer. The next layer is a concatenation layer that concatenates the outputs of the ChIP-seq data and DNA sequence map encoders, and the genomic distance, TADs flag, and CTCF flag. The concatenation layer is connected to two fully connected layers. The output of the model is the predicted interaction strength between the two potential candidate anchor peaks. Since the output neuron has a linear activation function, the output prediction may be negative in 1–2% of the cases. In this case, we consider the prediction strength very small (∼0).


### Processing of ChIA-PET data
  Processing of ChIA-PET data was performed using the ChIA-PET2 tool [22]. It first trims the bridge linker sequence. Then, the filtered reads are mapped to the human reference genome hg19 using bwa [23]. Duplicates are then removed, keeping only paired-end tags (PETs) with high mapping quality (MAPQ > 30). ChIP-seq-like peaks are then called using MACS2 [24], and loops are detected according to PETs that link the anchor peaks.


### ChIP-seq and ATAC-seq data normalization
  We calculated the read counts for each peak and its upstream and downstream bins. Then, we used RPKM for ChIP-seq and ATAC-seq read count normalization. More specifically, we divide the read count of the peak or the bin by the “per million scaling factor,” defined as the total read count of the chromosome divided by 1 000 000. This step accounts for the effect of different sequencing depths. We then divide by the peak or bin size in Kbps, which accounts for the peak or bin size.


### Hyperparameters selection
  To decide the structure of CNN-ChIPr, we used grid search to find the combination of hyperparameters that yields the best performance. The hyperparameters we searched for were the number of CNN filters for the CNN module (layer) processing the ChIP-seq maps. We also searched for the number of CNN filters of the CNN module processing the DNA sequence maps. In addition, we looked for the best number of neurons in the hidden layers connected to the flattening layers of both CNN modules. Moreover, we looked for the number of neurons in the final two fully connected layers (see Fig. 1 for illustration). We looked for the best hyperparameters for the full model used to predict cohesin interactions' strengths. We got all loops from all chromosomes of the GM12878 RAD21 ChIA-PET sample. Then, a random 25% of these loops are held out as test data (using the function train_test_split()). The remaining loops are partitioned into training and validation data (by setting validation_split = 0.2 in the model.fit() function). We used the mean squared error (MSE) loss function for our regression task and the Adam optimizer [25]. According to the grid search results, several combinations of hyperparameter values resulted in very closely performing models according to the validation loss (Supplementary Table S2). For our experiments, we used 16 CNN filters of size 3 × 3 for the module processing the ChIP-seq maps. We also used 32 filters for the module processing the DNA sequence maps. We used 25 and 10 neurons as the size of the hidden layers connected to the flattening layers of the two CNN modules processing ChIP-seq and DNA sequence maps, respectively. We used 256 and 64 neurons as the sizes of the two final fully connected layers. The validation loss we obtained for the models tested in the grid search and their performance on the held-out test data from the same cell line can be found in Supplementary Table S2.


### Other hyperparameters used
  We used other hyperparameters directly without looking for them in the grid search. For instance, we used 3 × 3 as the filter size for the CNN layer processing the ChIP-seq map for the three models of CNN-ChIPr (full model, minimal model1, and minimal model2). When we tried to explore using fewer ChIP-seq samples, the filter size was 3 × 3 for three ChIP-seq samples (Three_Ips), 2 × 3 for two ChIP-seq samples (Two_Ips), and 1 × 3 for one ChIP-seq sample (One_Ip). In these three particular cases, the CNN filter slides only in the horizontal direction because the height of the ChIP-seq features map is equal to the height of the CNN filter. We also used 4 × 40 [16] as the CNN filter size for the CNN layer processing the DNA sequence map. Also, we used the ReLU activation function for all the layers in the model except for the output layer, for which we used the linear activation function. We used a learning rate of 10−4, a batch size of 256, and a maximum number of epochs of 200 with early stopping. To prevent overfitting, we also used L2 regularization and added a dropout layer after the flattening layer of both CNN modules, with a dropout rate of 0.2.


### Regression models for predicting chromatin interaction strength
  The relation between the input features of our model and the chromatin interaction strength is non-linear. For instance, chromatin interaction strength is inversely correlated to the genomic distance, and the relation is approximately linear in the log-log scale [1, 26]. Thus, we trained two generalized linear regression models on a log-log scale to predict the chromatin interaction strength. In the first model, GLR, the response variable representing the interaction strength is log-transformed. We also log-transformed the flattened ChIP-seq map and the genomic distance. We used other untransformed variables: CTCF flag, TADs flag, and linear representation of the DNA sequence with numbers ‘1’, ‘2’, ‘3’, and ‘4’ representing the nucleotides A, C, G, and T, respectively [27]. In the second model, GLR_dist, we just used the log-transformed genomic distance as input, and the log-transformed interaction strength is the output. We trained the two models on the RAD21 and RNA Pol II ChIA-PET data of the GM12878 and K562 cell lines. We used sklearn LinearRegression to build the generalized linear regression models [28]. We compared the performance of CNN-ChIPr to the performance of these two models in predicting the chromatin interaction strength.


### Comparison with the state-of-the-art Hi-C methods
  We compared predictions of CNN-ChIPr of Hi-C contact maps to the predictions of the three variants of ChIPr [17], C.Origami [29], Akita [13], and DeepC [14]. CNN-ChIPr and the three variants of ChIPr can predict the interactions of the whole chromosome, and accordingly, they can predict contact maps of any size. However, C.Origami can predict contact maps of “2” Mbp size with “8” Kbp bins resolution. On the other hand, Akita and DeepC can predict interactions up to “1” Mbp genomic distance. Thus, we chose to predict contact maps of “2” Mbp size with “8” Kbp bin resolution for CNN-ChIPr, the three variants of ChIPr, and C.Origami. For Akita and DeepC, we predicted contact maps of “1” Mbp size and “8” Kbp bins resolution. We compared the similarity between the contact maps of each method and its corresponding contact map obtained from experimental Hi-C interactions with the same size and resolution used for the method. For comparison, we calculated the logged Hi-C intensity with iterative correction and eigenvector decomposition (ICE): (log(ICE normalized counts + 1)) for the contact maps predicted using different methods and their corresponding original Hi-C contact maps for the selected regions as suggested by C.Origami [29]. Although the contact maps of Akita and DeepC are smaller than those of other methods, they have enough points to get reliable statistical results for distance-stratified correlation, insulation score correlation, and correlation between entire contact maps. Also, since Akita and DeepC are sequence-based approaches, we used their models trained on GM12878 cell line data to predict the ∼40 regions used for the comparisons from the GM12878 cell line.


### Insulation score
  Topologically associating domains (TADs) are genomic regions with intra-domain interactions more frequent than inter-domain interactions. TADs are demarcated with TAD boundaries, which are associated with insulators that hamper chromatin interactions between adjacent TADs. The “insulation score” quantifies the insulation strength at different genomic positions [30]. We measured the insulation score for the genomic positions at the boundaries of two adjacent TADs, sliding over the diagonals of predicted contact maps of CNN-ChIPr and the other methods we compared it with. We also measured the insulation scores in the corresponding original contact maps. We then measured the Pearson correlation between scores obtained by predictions versus those obtained by original data for different methods.


### Stratified distance correlation
  Distance-stratified Pearson correlation is the correlation between the shifted diagonal in the predicted contact matrix and the corresponding diagonal in the original contact matrix. In our experiments, we measured the correlation until the genomic distance of 500 Kbp with a step size of “8” Kbp (the bin size of the contact maps used).


### Constructing contact maps from peak-level interactions
  Contact maps at a specific resolution are square symmetric matrices with bin sizes equal to the resolution of the contact map. Each entry in the contact map represents the strength of interaction between the two bins forming this entry. To get the interaction strength between two bins, we calculate the total interaction strength of all the peak-level interactions that overlap with these two bins. For instance, to create a 25 Kbp resolution contact map for a specific chromosome, we divide the chromosome into 25 Kbp bins. Then, for each pairwise interaction between two bins, we calculate the total interaction strength of all loops that overlap with this pair of bins. This total interaction strength will be the value of the entry representing this pair of bins in the contact map.


### Control interactions for ChIA-PET samples
  We created new random contacts based on the “mirror image” idea. That is, if there is an experimental contact between the two peaks, P1 and P2, we create a random contact between P2 and P3 where the genomic distance between P2 and P3 is equal to that between P1 and P2, and P3 is on the opposite side. Suppose we find that the contact between P2 and P3 already exists among the experimental contacts. In that case, we create a contact between two random loci with the same genomic distance between loci as the experimental contact (this case rarely happened in the RAD21 ChIA-PET samples, which have a relatively smaller number of interactions, but happened in ∼12% of the cases in the K562 RNA Pol II ChIA-PET sample, which has more than 8 000 000 interactions). Thus, the majority of the random contacts share all the epigenetic features of one of the two peaks and the genomic distance (the most crucial feature for prediction, as shown in the Results section).


### ROC and precision-recall curves
  For the ROC and precision-recall curves, we considered the true positive interactions to be those that have an interaction strength ≥3 in the original ChIA-PET sample. The “false” interactions are those that have interaction strength < 3 in the original ChIA-PET sample. In addition, we generated more “false” interactions (around 40 times the number of “true” interactions in a specific ChIA-PET sample). These additional “false” interactions do not exist in the original ChIA-PET sample. However, their anchor peaks are enriched with RAD21 or RNA Pol II for RAD21 and RNA Pol II ChIA-PET samples, respectively. In addition, the distance between the anchor peaks of these “false” interactions is between 0.5 and 5 Mbp. The purpose of adding these additional “false” interactions is to test the ability of CNN-ChIPr to predict them as “false” (i.e. assign weak interaction strength to them).


### Pathway enrichment analysis
  We performed pathway enrichment analysis using the online tool Enrichr [https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/] [31–33]. We used the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) [34, 35] in our analysis.


## Results
  The schematic of CNN-ChIPr is shown in Fig. 1. CNN-ChIPr uses the ChIP-seq data for RNA Pol II, RAD21, and CTCF. In addition, we use the ChIP-seq for the histone marks H3K27ac and H3K27me3, which are indicators of chromatin activity and repression, respectively. Also, we use ATAC-seq data, which can indicate chromatin accessibility (Fig. 1). In addition, we use other inputs that do not need new experimental data, such as DNA sequence, genomic distance, a flag that indicates whether the two interacting peaks have CTCF motifs in convergent orientation, and a flag indicating whether the two peaks reside in the same topologically associating domain (TAD) (details about the structure of the CNN-ChIPr model are in the Materials and methods section). To predict cohesin-mediated chromatin interaction strengths, we tested CNN-ChIPr three times. First, we used all the ChIP-seq inputs of the model described above; hereafter, we call this the “full model.” Second, we used only inputs from four ChIP-seq experiments (RAD21, RNA Pol II, CTCF, and H3K27ac); hereafter, this model is called “minimal model1.” Lastly, we also used the inputs of three ChIP-seq experiments (RAD21, RNA Pol II, and CTCF) in addition to the ATAC-seq data; hereafter, this model is called “minimal model2.” We trained the models using GM12878 cell line data and tested them on the data of K562, HepG2, and H1 cell lines.


### CNN-ChIPr accurately predicts cohesin-mediated interactions at the peak-level resolution
  We trained the full model, minimal model1, and minimal model2 described above to predict cohesin-mediated chromatin interaction strengths. We trained the models on GM12878 data and tested their performance on three other cell lines: K562, H1, and HepG2. We predicted the interaction strengths of the same loops in the original ChIA-PET samples of the three cell lines. To test the accuracy of the predictions obtained by the three models, we measured the Pearson correlation coefficients between the predicted interaction strengths and the original ones. As a negative control for comparison, we generated random contacts with the same genomic distance as the original loops (for the description of the stringent design of the Control interactions, please see Materials and methods section) and predicted their interaction strengths using CNN-ChIPr. We found that the correlation coefficients obtained by the predictions of the three CNN-ChIPr models (full model, minimal model1, and minimal model2) were comparable to each other (Fig. 2A-C). Since CTCF and cohesin colocalize to form chromatin loops [36], we also used CTCF ChIA-PET data to validate the performance of CNN-ChIPr. We found that predicted interaction strengths for RAD21-mediated loops that overlap with CTCF ones in both anchors highly correlate with the corresponding CTCF original ChIA-PET loops (Supplementary Fig. S1A–C). In addition, we found that the predictions obtained by the three models correlate significantly better with the original interactions than the CNN-ChIPr predictions obtained from the random contacts (Fig. 2A–C). We also plotted the precision-recall curves (PRCs) and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for the predictions we obtained (see Materials and methods). We found that the areas under PRC (AUPRC) and ROC curves (AUROC) for the predictions of the three CNN-ChIPr models were bigger than those for the random contacts (Fig. 2D–I).

  We also used the GM12878 data to train generalized linear regression models in the log-log scale using all the inputs of CNN-ChIPr (GLR model) and using the genomic distance only as input (GLR_dist) (see Materials and methods). The GLR and GLR_dist models were also used to predict the interaction strengths of the other three cell lines, K562, HepG2, and H1. The CNN-ChIPr predictions correlated significantly higher with experimental interactions than the predictions obtained by the two generalized linear regression models GLR and GLR_dist (Supplementary Fig. S1D-I). Given that minimal model1 performs very close to the full model, we used it to predict cohesin-mediated interaction strengths for the two other cell lines, HepG2 and H1. We also found that the predictions obtained for these two cell lines correlate significantly better with the original interactions and have bigger AUPRC and AUROC than the predictions obtained from the random contacts (Supplementary Fig. S2A-F). In addition, they highly correlate with the CTCF original loops that overlap with RAD21-mediated loops in both anchors (Supplementary Fig. S2G and H). They also correlate significantly higher with the experimental interactions than the predictions obtained by GLR and GLR_dist models (Supplementary Fig. S3). All these results indicate the robustness of the CNN-ChIPr models and their ability to predict the interactions of different cell lines with similar performance.


### CNN-ChIPr accurately predicts cohesin-mediated contact maps
  High-resolution contact maps can also extract important 3D structural information, such as the TAD boundaries [21]. Thus, we constructed contact maps (see Materials and methods section) using the predictions we obtained via the three models, i.e. full model, minimal model1, and minimal model2, at 5 Kbp resolution for the cell line K562. We also constructed the 5 Kbp resolution maps for the two cell lines H1 and HepG2 using the predictions we obtained from minimal model1. We measured the similarity between the contact maps constructed by CNN-ChIPr predictions and those constructed using the original ChIA–PET interactions using Pearson correlation coefficients. We obtained higher correlation values using the predictions obtained by CNN-ChIPr than when comparing with contact maps constructed by the predictions of the GLR_dist model (Fig. 3A-C). These results further validate the good performance of CNN-ChIPr. They also further support that the performance of minimal model1 is comparable to that of the full model used in the case of the K562 cell line.


### CNN-ChIPr can predict ChIA-PET identified cell-type-specific loops
  To further test the accuracy of CNN-ChIPr, we measured the percentage of the cell-type-specific loops identified by experimental RAD21 ChIA-PET data [7], which overlap with strong predicted interactions (PETs ≥ 3) obtained by CNN-ChIPr. For K562, we found that ∼80% of the experimentally identified ChIA-PET loops were captured as strong interactions by the full model (Fig. 3D). A slightly lower percentage was obtained when using minimal model1 and minimal model2. As for the two cell lines, H1 and HepG2, the percentage of cell-type-specific loops captured by predictions was ∼90%. We also plotted the precision-recall curves for this analysis. Specifically, we considered the cell-type-specific loops for each cell line as the ‘True’ loops. For the ‘False’ class, we used the corresponding control loops with the same genomic distance between the two anchor peaks (see Materials and methods). The CNN–ChIPr interaction prediction strength is the predicted score, and the ground truth is ‘1’ for the cell-type-specific loops and ‘0’ for the control ones. We calculated the AUPRC and found very high AUPRCs (above 0.95) for all the cases (Supplementary Fig. S4A-E). This illustrates that cell-type-specific loops are predicted as stronger loops compared to the control ones, showing the reliability of CNN-ChIPr. In addition, we sorted the CNN-ChIPr predictions for cell-type-specific loops and the corresponding control loops in descending order of predicted interaction strength. We kept only the top N predicted interactions for each cell type, where N is the number of cell-type-specific loops of that cell type. The false positives are the control interactions found within these strongest N interactions. We found the false-positive rates are <10% in all cases (Supplementary Fig. S4F). These cell-type-specific loops can be considered positive controls, and accurately predicting them as strong interactions is another validation of the good performance of CNN-ChIPr.

  One of the main functions of RAD21-mediated interactions is that they act as boundaries for TADs. In [21], the authors performed Hi-C experiments for nine cell lines; two of them are among the cell lines we used in our study, GM12878 and K562. The contact domain boundaries of these two cell lines are available on GEO GSE63525. To get the bins forming the domain boundaries, we extended the boundary locations given by 20 Kbp upstream and 20 Kbp downstream. Thus, the bins we used as domain boundaries are 40 Kbp in size (which is the Hi-C contact maps resolution used to detect TADs in [37]). We grouped the ChIA–PET interactions that overlapped with these 40 Kbp bins, TAD boundaries, and calculated the ChIA-PET experimental and predicted interaction strength between these boundaries. We checked the percentage of these grouped interactions, linking Hi-C-calculated TAD boundaries, that were detected as strong by the original RAD21 ChIA-PET and the corresponding CNN-ChIPr predictions for GM12878 and K562 cell lines. We found that 80% and 63% of the TAD boundaries (obtained using Hi-C data) overlapped with experimental RAD21 ChIA-PET loops in GM12878 and K562 cell lines, respectively. Among these TAD boundaries detected by ChIA-PET experiments, 82.5% and 74.7% of them were detected as strong interactions (total interaction strength linking the two bins ≥ 3) by CNN-ChIPr predictions vs. 87.4% and 71.4% detected as strong interactions in the original (experimental) ChIA-PET of GM12878 and K562 cell lines, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S4G). We did the same experiment when extending the TAD boundary locations by 10 Kbp upstream and 10 Kbp downstream (making the domain boundary 20 Kbp in size). Using these smaller TAD boundaries, we found that 58% and 33% of them overlapped with experimental RAD21 ChIA-PET loops in GM12878 and K562 cell lines, respectively. Among these overlapping loops, 66% and 56% were detected as strong interactions by CNN-ChIPr predictions versus 76% and 56% detected as strong interactions in the original ChIA-PET of GM12878 and K562 cell lines, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S4H). These results show the high performance of CNN-ChIPr in detecting important interactions like TAD boundaries and the reasonable agreement between CNN-ChIPr predictions and the original data.


### Relative importance of input features
  We used the leave-one-out strategy to measure the relative importance of the input features that do not need new experimental data, i.e. genomic distance, DNA sequence, CTCF flag, and TADs flag. Specifically, we trained the minimal model1 to predict RAD21 interactions several times, eliminating one of the above input features at each time. We trained the models on the GM12878 cell line data and tested them on the data of the K562 cell line with all the chromosomes combined. We observed that removing the genomic distance resulted in a clear drop in performance, indicating that it is the most important of the input features tested (Fig. 3E). The removal of the other three inputs (DNA sequence, CTCF flag, and TADs flag) resulted in a much smaller decrease in performance, indicating that they are relatively less important (Fig. 3E). One other feature expected to be important could be the CTCF binding sites. Since we also had another input that represents the convergence of CTCF motifs in the two interacting peaks, this may be the reason why removing the CTCF flag or genomic sequence alone had a minor effect on the performance. When we removed both the CTCF flag and genomic sequence inputs together, this resulted in a relatively larger drop in performance (Fig. 3E). We also tested the effect of removing the TADs flag with the genomic distance. We found a minor decrease in performance compared to when the genomic distance only was removed (Fig. 3E). This shows that the TADs flag has some contribution to the performance of CNN-ChIPr, which encouraged us to keep using this input feature.

  We also explored the effect of using less experimental data on the performance of CNN-ChIPr. Specifically, we trained three new models using the GM12878 data to predict the strength of RAD21-mediated interactions. The models use RAD21 ChIP-seq data alone (One_ip), RAD21 and RNA Pol II ChIP-seq data (Two_ips), and RAD21, RNA Pol II, and H3K27ac ChIP-seq data (Three_ips), respectively. All three models use DNA sequence, genomic distance, CTCF flag, and TADs flag as well. We trained these three models, in addition to the minimal model1, 50 times on the GM12878 data. We predicted RAD21 interaction strength for the K562, H1, and HepG2 cell lines using the three models and compared their performance with the minimal model1. We measured the Pearson correlation between predictions of all interactions of all 23 chromosomes and the corresponding original interaction strengths using the 50 trained models, using each specific set of inputs (One_ip, Two_ips, Three_ips, and minimal model1). We found that the minimal model1 outperforms the three other models (Fig. 3F and Supplementary Fig. S5). Although the improvement achieved by minimal model1 over the other models is relatively small, we recommend using minimal model1 if all its required ChIP-seq samples are available. Nevertheless, the other models can still provide useful information, and the code required to train them is available at [https://git.biohpc.swmed.edu/s206442/cnn_chipr].


### CNN-ChIPr is robust to changing the training data
  To check the robustness of CNN-ChIPr to changing the training data, we trained CNN-ChIPr models on the data of another well-established ENCODE cell line, K562, and tested them on the other cell lines for the RAD21-mediated interactions. We found that CNN-ChIPr performed well both at the peak-level resolution and at the 5 Kbp resolution when tested on the three cell lines GM12878, H1, and HepG2 for the RAD21-mediated interactions (Fig. 4A–C, Supplementary Figs S6, S7, and S8).


### Predicting RNA Pol II-associated interaction strengths using CNN-ChIPr
  Accurate prediction results of CNN-ChIPr on the RAD21 ChIA-PET data encouraged us to test it on RNA Pol II ChIA-PET data. We used the same set of inputs to CNN-ChIPr that we used to predict the RAD21 interactions. We trained the three models (full model, minimal model1, and minimal model2) on GM12878 cell line data. Since the number of interactions available in the experimental GM12878 RNA Pol II ChIA-PET data is more than 1 000 000 interactions, we used odd chromosomes only for training and tested the performance on the K562 cell line data. We found that the predictions of the CNN-ChIPr models correlate significantly higher with experimental interactions and yield bigger AUPRC and AUROC than the predictions of random contacts with the same genomic distance (see Materials and methods, Fig. 4D–I and Supplementary Fig. S9A–C). Also, the predictions of CNN-ChIPr models yielded significantly higher Pearson correlation coefficients with original interactions than those obtained by the predictions of GLR and GLR_dist models trained on GM12878 RNA Pol II ChIA-PET data (Supplementary Fig. S9D-I). We also tested the performance of minimal model1 on the HepG2 cell line and observed similar conclusions to those we observed for the K562 cell line (Supplementary Fig. S10A-E).

  In addition, we constructed contact maps at 5 Kbp resolution using the CNN-ChIPr predictions of the K562 and HepG2 cell lines. We calculated the Pearson correlation coefficients between these contact maps and those obtained using experimental interactions. We compared these correlation coefficient values with those measured between contact maps obtained from GLR_dist predictions and experimental (original) interactions. In general, the correlation coefficient values obtained for CNN-ChIPr contact maps were higher than those obtained for the GLR_dist ones (Supplementary Fig. S10F and G). All these results show the capability of CNN-ChIPr in predicting RNA Pol II-associated interaction strengths.

  We noticed the poor performance of CNN-ChIPr in predicting Chr22 in the K562 cell line (Supplementary Fig. S10F). Upon inspection of the ChIP-seq data of Chr22 in the K562 cell line, we found that a significant region of Chr22 seems to have abnormal enrichment in the four ChIP-seq samples (RAD21, RNA Pol II, CTCF, and H3K27ac), while the rest of the chromosome is relatively depleted in the four samples (see Supplementary Fig. S11). We checked Chr22 in the GM12878 cell line; we did not find this abnormality (Supplementary Fig. S11). This abnormality in Chr22 in the K562 cell line is likely due to the reciprocal translocation between Chr9 (ABL1 gene) and Chr22 (BCR gene) [38]. With closer inspection of Chr22 of the K562 cell line, we found that the regions [chr22:16 888 900–17 285 557, hg19] and [chr22:18 970 562–22 040 245, hg19] have a 7–9 fold copy number amplification [39]. Also, the region [chr22:22 053 831–23 635 638, hg19] has a 15–20 fold copy number amplification [39]. The segment [chr22:17 286 146–18 970 561, hg19] has a 3-fold copy number amplification [39]. As this translocation and copy number amplification affected the ChIP-seq samples, which we use as inputs to CNN-ChIPr, the predictions of Chr22 of the K562 cell line will be subsequently affected.

  We also trained CNN-ChIPr models on the K562 cell line data. It also showed good performance when tested on the GM12878 and HepG2 cell lines for the RNA Pol II-associated interactions (Fig. 5A–C and Supplementary Figs S12 and S13). These results show that CNN-ChIPr is robust enough to change the data used for training the model for predicting the RNA Pol II-associated interactions as well.


### CNN-ChIPr accurately predicts cell-type-specific RNA Pol II interactions
  Super enhancers (SEs) are a group of enhancers found close to each other and span a range of thousands of kilobase pairs [40]. These SEs are usually located close to cell-type-specific genes to regulate them. We obtained the list of SEs and their associated genes of the GM12878, K562, and HepG2 cell lines from the dbSuper database [41]. We focused on the experimentally detected RNA Pol II-associated interactions that overlap with the SEs and their associated genes across the three cell lines. We measured the Pearson and Spearman correlations between those experimental interaction strengths and their predicted values using CNN-ChIPr. We observed high correlation values for the three cell lines (Fig. 5D). We also classified the interactions as weak (PETs < 3) and strong (PETs ≥ 3) and measured the agreement between predicted and experimental interactions in terms of the interaction strength. We found high levels of agreement between predicted and experimental interactions in the range of 75–80% for the cell lines tested (Fig. 5E). All these results show that CNN-ChIPr accurately predicts cell-type-specific RNA Pol II interactions.


### CNN-ChIPr outperforms state-of-the-art methods of chromatin interaction prediction
  We compared the performance of CNN-ChIPr in predicting RAD21 interactions to 3Dpredictor [42]. 3Dpredictor is a method for predicting Hi-C interactions between bins of fixed sizes. Thus, we used the K562 RAD21 ChIA–PET interactions to build a 25 Kbp bin interactions list and used it to train 3Dpredictor. We trained both CNN-ChIPr and 3Dpredictor with the odd chromosomes of the K562 cell line and predicted the interactions of the even chromosomes. We compared the stratum-adjusted correlation coefficient (SCC) [43] values between the contact maps of both methods with the contact maps constructed from the original ChIA-PET data of the even chromosomes. We found that CNN-ChIPr predictions correlate significantly better with the original data than 3Dpredictor predictions (Supplementary Fig. S14A).

  Since Hi-C is a very popular experimental method for detecting the 3D organization of chromosomes, we also used CNN-ChIPr to predict Hi-C interactions at 5 Kbp resolution. We used a subset of interactions of the odd chromosomes of the K562 cell line for training and predicted the interactions of the even chromosomes. We constructed 25 Kbp resolution contact maps from the 5 Kbp resolution prediction of interactions. We also trained 3Dpredictor with 25 Kbp resolution Hi-C interactions of the odd chromosomes of the K562 cell line. We compared the contact maps of even chromosomes reconstructed at 25 Kbp resolution using the predictions of both methods to those reconstructed using the original Hi-C data. We found the SCC values obtained by CNN-ChIPr are significantly higher than those obtained by 3Dpredictor (Supplementary Fig. S14B). These results show that CNN-ChIPr can outperform this state-of-the-art method for Hi-C interactions prediction.

  We also trained CNN-ChIPr with the Hi-C data of the IMR90 cell line (the odd chromosomes). We used the model to predict the interactions of all chromosomes of the GM12878 cell line. Contact maps of predicted vs. original regions of Chr7 at different resolutions (500, 100, 50, and 25 Kbp) visually show the similarity of CNN-ChIPr predictions with the original data (Supplementary Figs S15 and S16). In addition, we constructed ∼40 random 2Mb regions (Supplementary Table S1) from the GM12878 cell line and compared the performance of predicting these regions to the performance of predicting the same regions by Akita [13], DeepC [14], the three variants of ChIPr [17], and C.Origami [29] (see Materials and methods). Since Akita and DeepC are sequence-based approaches, we used the GM12878 output channel for Akita and the model trained on GM12878 cell line data for DeepC to predict the ∼40 regions used for the comparisons from the GM12878 cell line. For ChIPr (which uses ChIP-seq inputs) and C.Origami (which uses ChIP-seq and ATAC-seq inputs), we used their models trained on the IMR90 cell line (as we did with CNN-ChIPr) and the input ChIP-seq or ATAC-seq data of the GM12878 cell line to predict the ∼40 regions of the GM12878 cell line. Our initial comparison focused on the distance-stratified Pearson correlation (see Materials and methods). We measured the correlation until the genomic distance of 500 Kbp with a step size of “8” Kbp (the bin size of the contact maps used). We observed that CNN-ChIPr is consistently better than all other methods at all genomic distances compared (Fig. 5F).

  In addition, we measured the insulation score (see Materials and methods) for the genomic positions at the boundaries of two adjacent TADs sliding over the diagonals of predicted contact maps of CNN-ChIPr and the other methods we compared it with. We also measured the corresponding insulation scores in the corresponding original contact maps. We chose 200 Kbp to be the size of the sliding TADs because it is close to the median size of contact domains observed in [21] (∼185 Kbp). We observed that CNN-ChIPr-calculated insulation scores correlate significantly higher with the insulation scores calculated using the corresponding original contact maps than all other methods we compared with (Fig. 5G). These results show how the predictions of CNN-ChIPr are reliable for detecting TAD boundaries.

  Also, we measured the Pearson correlation between the contact maps predicted by CNN-ChIPr and the other methods with the corresponding contact maps obtained from original interactions. We observed that CNN-ChIPr correlates significantly higher with the original maps than C.Origami, DeepC, Akita, and RF-ChIPr (Supplementary Fig. S17). The performance of CNN-ChIPr is comparable to DNN-ChIPr and GB-ChIPr according to this metric (no significant difference, Supplementary Fig. S17). This is a further indication of the quality of CNN-ChIPr predictions with respect to the whole contact maps. All these results show the good performance of CNN-ChIPr in predicting both ChIA-PET and Hi-C interactions.


### Applying CNN-ChIPr on breast and prostate cancer cell lines
  We first identified the regions enriched with the pioneer transcription factor FOXA1, estrogen receptor (ER), and the enhancer mark H3K27ac based on the corresponding ChIP-seq data for MCF7 cells. We identified the MCF7 RNA Pol II ChIA–PET interactions where one of the two anchors of the interaction overlaps with one of the regions enriched with the above three factors, and the other anchor overlaps with a gene body. These interactions demarcate the genes that are connected to (FOXA1 + ER) enriched enhancers by at least one strong interaction (interaction strength ≥ 3) (hereafter denoted by strongly associated genes). They also determine the genes that are weakly connected to those enhancers (interaction strength < 3) (hereafter denoted by weakly associated genes). We identified strongly and weakly associated genes according to interaction strengths determined by the original MCF7 RNA Pol II ChIA-PET data, and also according to the interaction strengths predicted by CNN-ChIPr. We found that the strongly associated genes identified either by CNN-ChIPr predictions or the original interactions have significantly higher FPKM expression values (Fig. 6A and B). We also checked the gene expression levels of randomly selected genes, and the nearest genes to enhancers enriched with (FOXA1 + ER). We found that strongly associated genes (identified by CNN-ChIPr predictions) have significantly higher FPKM gene expression values than the other gene sets in the MCF7 cell line (Supplementary Fig. S18A). We then performed pathway enrichment analysis of these strongly associated genes (see Materials and methods section for more details). We found that the most enriched pathways are the Estrogen-related ones (Fig. 6C and D). This shows that the genes strongly associated with (FOXA1 + ER) enriched enhancers according to CNN-ChIPr prediction yield the same most enriched pathways as using the original ChIA-PET data.

  Also, we analyzed RNA-seq samples of DMSO- and estrogen-exposed MCF7 cell lines [44]. We calculated the upregulated differentially expressed genes in the estrogen-exposed samples. We then measured the overlap between those upregulated genes and the strongly associated genes identified above, according to the predicted interaction strengths, and also the original ones. We found that strongly associated genes identified by either predictions or original data significantly overlap with the upregulated genes (Fig. 6E). We also provide a visual example to show the consistency between the predictions of RAD21 and RNA Pol II interactions of the MCF7 cell line and their corresponding original ChIA–PET interactions in the MYC region (Fig. 6F-I). We got the list of enhancers of the MCF7 cell line from EnhancerAtlas 2.0 [45].

  Similarly, we identified the genes strongly and weakly associated with (FOXA1 + AR) enhancers in the LNCaP prostate cancer cell line. We also found that strongly associated genes have significantly higher expression values than weakly associated ones (Fig. 7A and B). In addition, we found that strongly associated genes (according to CNN-ChIPr predictions) have significantly higher FPKM gene expression values than the randomly selected genes and also the nearest genes to the AR and FOXA1-enriched enhancers in the LNCaP cell line (Supplementary Fig. S18B). The pathway analysis of the strongly associated genes showed that the Androgen response pathway is the most enriched one (Fig. 7C and D). We also analyzed RNA-seq samples of DMSO- and Androgen-exposed LNCaP cell lines [GEO GSE202755] and calculated the upregulated differentially expressed genes in the Androgen-exposed samples. We also found a significant overlap between this set of genes and the strongly associated genes either identified by original data or predictions of CNN-ChIPr (Fig. 7E). The consistency of the predicted RAD21 and RNA Pol II-associated interactions with their corresponding original ChIA–PET interactions is illustrated in the KRT8 and KLK3 regions of the LNCaP cell line (Fig. 7F–I and Supplementary Fig. S19). We got the list of enhancers of the LNCaP cell line from EnhancerAtlas 2.0 [45]. This analysis shows that CNN-ChIPr can be reliably used for enhancer-target gene prediction and leads to the same conclusions with or without experimental 3D genome data. It also shows the importance of looping information and the value in knowing the loop strength, as it shows that enhancers can regulate genes beyond their nearest neighbors [8]. The looping information is essential for correctly linking enhancers to their target genes. Once these enhancer–gene connections are established using 3D genome information, enhancer activity correlates strongly with target gene expression. However, in the absence of 3D genome context, enhancer–target gene assignments cannot be accurately predicted. All these results show that the application of CNN-ChIPr can be extended to cases where 3D genome experiments are not practical or feasible, such as rare clinical specimens or time-course measurements.


## Discussion
  Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have shown great performance in computer vision applications, and they have also been used a lot in computational genomics applications when taking DNA sequences as inputs. Here, we formatted the conventional one-dimensional (1D) ChIP-seq data inputs as a 2D matrix to tap into the power of CNN models. This approach enabled us to consider the peaks of interest and their close neighborhood, likely affecting the interaction strength. In addition, using the input as a 2D matrix with CNN needs fewer trainable parameters than using a fully connected artificial neural network. Thus, it requires relatively fewer training examples. Also, it will capture the essential ChIP-seq enrichment patterns even if they are slightly shifted from the central peak.

  We mainly used four cell lines in our study: GM12878, K562, H1, and HepG2. GM12878 is a lymphoblastoid cell line produced from the blood of a female donor [46]. H1 is a karyotypically normal human embryonic stem cell [47]. On the other hand, the K562 cell line is an immortalized chronic myelogenous leukemia (CML) cell line whose cells contain pervasive aneuploidy and multiple gross structural abnormalities [48]. Lastly, HepG2 is a cell line derived from a male patient with liver carcinoma [49]. Thus, the four cell lines we focused on have different characteristics. Nevertheless, training the model using the GM12878 cell line’s high-quality data helped to predict the interaction strength of other cell lines with high accuracy in both cases of the RAD21 and RNA Pol II ChIA-PET interactions.

  We tested the relative importance of the input features that do not require new experimental data, i.e. genomic distance, genomic sequence, and CTCF and TADs flags. We found that the most important feature among these was the genomic distance. Removing the genomic sequence, CTCF flag, or TADs flag alone had a minor effect on the performance. However, removing the genomic sequence and CTCF flag together resulted in a relatively bigger drop in performance. In addition, we observed that GM12878 is better suited as training data than K562. This is likely due to the relatively normal karyotype and far fewer copy number variations (CNVs) and translocations of the GM12878 cell line [50].

  Moreover, we tested the possibility of training CNN-ChIPr models on Promoter Capture Hi-C (PCHi-C) [51] and Hi-C data and tested these models to predict ChIA-PET interactions. We used the model trained on PCHi-C data to predict RNA Pol II ChIA–PET interactions and used the one trained on Hi-C data to predict both RAD21 and RNA Pol II ChIA–PET interactions. However, we found that models trained on RNA Pol II or RAD21 ChIA-PET data better predict the corresponding ChIA–PET interaction strength (Supplementary Figs S20 and S21). We suggest that this difference in performance is due to the different nature of both data types. ChIA-PET is robust in analyzing interactions mediated by specific transcription factors [52], including ERα (estrogen receptor alpha) [2], RNA Pol II [40, 53], and CTCF [9]; and components of the cohesin complex [54]. On the other hand, Hi-C and its derivative methods can capture genome-wide chromatin interactions without restrictions on mediating proteins [55]. Despite the high quality, a limitation of our models is that some experimental technique-specific biases may be retained.

  Given the prevalence of the Hi-C method, we also tested the ability of CNN-ChIPr to predict Hi-C interactions. We found that it can accurately predict high-resolution Hi-C interactions and outperforms several state-of-the-art Hi-C interaction prediction methods.

  The performance of CNN-ChIPr depends on the quality of the data used in training the model and the quality of the data of the new cell line for which we want to predict its loop strengths. For the ChIP-seq data to use with CNN-ChIPr, we suggest following the quality control (QC) metrics suggested by ENCODE [6]. According to the datasets we used in our study, we recommend a read depth of ∼12 million uniquely mapped reads [56], with a high signal-to-noise ratio, showing a clear difference between the ChIP sample and the control. We also recommend that the number of peaks be more than 10 000 and that the fraction of reads in peaks (FIP) be more than 20%. According to our analysis, we found that replicate experiments with comparable QC metrics will give comparable predicted results using CNN-ChIPr (Supplementary Fig. S22).

  One of the main advantages of CNN-ChIPr is that we can easily add more ChIP-seq inputs just by adding more rows to the ChIP-seq 2D matrix. In addition, we can extend the model by adding more inputs of different types if available and necessary, either by adding more modules or by concatenating more inputs to the concatenation layer.
