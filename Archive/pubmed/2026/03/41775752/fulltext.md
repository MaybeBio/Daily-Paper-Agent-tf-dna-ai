# A high density of T-cell lymphocytes and Tregs subset correlate to a worse survival in major salivary gland carcinomas


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-39357-y.


## Introduction
  Salivary gland carcinomas (SGC) are rare, accounting for less than 1% of human neoplasms. They have highly variable 5-year survival rates, ranging from 90% of the most indolent histotypes or low-grade tumors, to the 55% of the more aggressive high-grade tumors and advanced stages1. Treating these carcinomas is challenge due to their rarity and heterogeneity. According to the 2024 guidelines from the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), the recommended first-line treatment is surgical removal followed by radiotherapy (RT) for advanced stages (https://www.nccn.org/guidelines/). However, treatment options (RT or chemotherapy) are limited for recurrent or metastatic cases, especially high-grade carcinomas, typically requiring traditional cisplatin-based chemotherapy protocols. Several targetable molecular alterations have been identified in salivary gland carcinomas, including HER2 upregulation, androgen receptor overexpression, Notch receptor activation, Neurotrophic Tyrosine Receptor Kinase (NTRK) gene fusions, and RET gene alterations2. Based on these findings, we aim to investigate if lymphocytic activity differs between histologically high-grade SGC and low-grade SGC. In detail, we will focus on identifying and categorising T lymphocytes, which play a key role in the body’s immune response to tumor evaluating the presence of T-helper lymphocytes, cytotoxic T cells, T-regulatory lymphocytes, and T-exhausted lymphocytes in the tumor microenvironment (TME), and lastly the PD-L1 expression in both immune and tumor cells. Furthermore, we will assess the population of tumor-associated macrophages to understand their potential role in tumor growth and progression as well as B-lymphocytes and natural killer (NK) cells.

  Identification and quantification of lymphoid and myeloid populations was, also, carried out by applying artificial intelligences (AI) algorithm.

  Finally, our aim was to assess how immune response to cancer might impact on patients’ prognosis, defining the correlation with cancer histotype and progression.


## Results

### Clinical data and histopathology
  Among the 103 neoplastic cases, 97 specimens were obtained via excisional or surgical procedures (94%), and 6 specimens (6%) were obtained through incisional biopsies. Patients’ age distribution ranged from 13 to 87 years (mean 61 years). The case cohort included 47 females (46%) and 56 males (54%). Parotid gland was the primary tumor site in 76.8% of cases, while 23.2% arose from the submandibular gland, none arose from the sublingual gland; 51.9% cases were on the right side, 46,8% were on the left side, and 1.3% were bilateral. The site side was not specified in 3 cases. The median size (major dimension) was 22.9 mm (range 7–50 mm). Tumor stage was pT1 in 42.9% of cases, pT2 in 11.2%, pT3 in 36.4%, and pT4 in 9.1% (specifically, 5.2% pT4a and 3.9% pT4b); stage was not assessable in the 5 cases receiving incisional biopsy only. The pN status was undetermined in 47 cases; 51.4% were pN0, 17.1% were pN3b, 17.1% were pN2, and 14.4% were pN1. According to the AJCC 8th edition staging, 40.3% of cases were stage I, 10.4% were stage II, 29.9% were stage III, and 19.5% were stage IV. Specifically, stage IV includes 6.5% of stage IVa, 11.7% stage IVb, and 1.3% of stage IVc.

  After the histopathological revision, according to the last WHO classification1, the case series was composed as follows: 32 mucoepidermoid carcinomas, 9 adenoid-cystic carcinomas, 21 salivary duct carcinoma, 14 carcinoma ex-pleomorphic adenoma, 6 secretory carcinomas, 3 myoepithelial carcinomas, 7 epithelial-myoepithelial carcinomas, 4 basal cell adenocarcinoma, 3 salivary gland carcinoma NOS, 1 acinic cell carcinoma and 3 polymorphous adenocarcinoma.

  The histological grade, applied according to the updated guidelines for reporting was low grade in 35.8%, intermediate grade in 16%, and high-grade in 48.2%. The extraparenchymal invasion was observed in 48.4% cases, perineural invasion in 65.4%, and vascular invasion in 73.8%. Necrosis was present in 40.2% of the cases, and the mean mitotic count was 3.3 per 10 high-power fields (HPF). The histopathological data are summarised in Table 1. The lymphocytic infiltrates were moderate in 32.9%, mild in 26.8%, intense in 15.9%, absent in 6.1%. In 18% of cases an intense lymphoid infiltrate, associated to secondary follicular formation with germinal centre, was detected.

### Correlations between immunohistochemistry and SGC characteristics
  In the normal salivary gland specimens, 7 cases exhibited mild lymphocytic infiltrates, while 3 cases showed moderate infiltrates. All non-neoplastic specimens contained polytypic lymphocytic infiltrates comprising both T and B lymphocytes, with a predominant T-CD4 subset (44% of the total lymphocytic infiltrates) compared to the T-CD8 subset, which represented 6% of the lymphocytic infiltrates.

  In the study group, some interesting differences, summarized in Table 2, were observed between lymphoid populations and histotypes. In particular, a higher number of intratumoral CD20-positive lymphocytes was observed in mucoepidermoid carcinoma, salivary duct carcinoma, carcinoma ex pleomorphic adenoma, myoepithelial carcinoma, epithelial-myoepithelial carcinoma, basal cell adenocarcinoma, and salivary gland carcinoma not otherwise specified (NOS), as compared to other histotypes. Higher expression of intratumoral CD4-positive lymphocytes was found in mucoepidermoid carcinoma, adenoid-cystic carcinomas, salivary duct carcinoma, secretory carcinomas, and polymorphous adenocarcinomas. Among the peritumoral lymphoid populations, a higher number of CD8-positive lymphocytes was observed in mucoepidermoid carcinomas, salivary duct carcinomas, epithelial-myoepithelial carcinomas, and basal cell adenocarcinomas. Finally, the higher CD68 macrophage expression was observed in salivary duct carcinoma and carcinomas ex-pleomorphic adenoma. Due to the high number of potential histotypes, and the scarce number of cases in some groups, no statistical analysis was made, since it would not be reliable.

  We then compared the intratumoral and peritumoral lymphoid populations with histological grade, stage, endovascular invasion, and perineural invasion. We observed that a higher number of peritumoral FOXP3 lymphocytes was associated with tumor necrosis; specifically, 50% of cases with a score of 3 and 57.6% of specimens with a score of 2 exhibited necrosis, whereas 57.1% of score 0 and 80.8% of score 1 specimens were without necrosis (χ2 = 8.41; p = 0.038).

  Furthermore, intratumoral M2 (CD163-positive) macrophages were associated with higher-grade tumors; notably, 55.1% of M2-polarised specimens were high-grade carcinomas, in contrast to 38.8% in low-grade carcinomas (χ²= 9.17; p = 0.01). Conversely, an opposite trend was observed in peritumoral infiltrates, where 36.1% of specimens with M2-polarised macrophages were high-grade carcinomas, whereas 52.8% were from low-grade carcinomas (χ²= 7.06; p = 0.029).

  Regarding PD-L1 expression on background cells within the peritumoral tissue, higher PD-L1 levels were detected in T1 tumors, with 66.7% of score 4 specimens being pT1, compared to 33.3% of pT3 and 0% of pT4 cases (χ²= 32.33; p = 0.009).


### Survival analysis
  After 29.3 ± 25.9 months of mean follow-up (median 23; range 1-108 months), 20 (19.4%) cases of tumor recurrence were registered (median PFS 20 months) and 16 (15.5%) patients died.

  In our series, the overall survival (OS) was not significantly associated with lymphoid populations. Noteworthy, the presence of lymphoid populations within or around the tumor showed a statistically significant association with progression-free survival (PFS), and in detail, the amount of peritumoral CD3-positive lymphocytes and the intratumoral T regulatory cells (Tregs) co-expressing CD4 and FOXP3 (Fig. 1). Notably, the increasing in M2 macrophages (co-expressing CD68 and CD163) showed a trend with worse PFS, but without statistical significance (Fig. 1). A correlation between PFS and histotypes was also observed, confirming the already-known data (data not shown). Moreover, we observed a lower amount of CD4-positive intratumoral TILs in stage 3 and 4 SGC (mean 1.78 and 1.91) compared to stage 1 and 2 (2.14 and 2.27; p = 0.006): albeit this could represent a bias, no stage-dependent trends were reported in FOXP3 immunoreactivity (p = 0.338).

  The multivariate Cox regression analysis identified tumor stage as a prognostic factor for OS and PFS (p = 0.014) and confirmed CD3-positive lymphocytes (p = 0.002), and CD4 + FOXP3+ Tregs (p < 0.001) as prognostic factors for PFS.

### QuPath analysis
  QuPath was able to treat each slide as a separate unit in a hierarchical manner for output data and measurements. One of the main functions of QuPath, and any surgical pathology analyses software for that matter is to identify cells and define individual features. More notably, for each cell, the program can provide all measurements and export them to a spreadsheet. If cell delineation is acceptable, Cell Detection can be run for all tissue slides.

  To test the QuPath quantification, we analysed the agreement between peri/tumoral TILs quantification (by CD3 IHC) assessed by the pathologist and quantified by the QuPath software: we documented a strong agreement between the two (Cohen’s kappa coefficient 0.71), with 14 discordant cases (all between 2 + and 3+, see Table 3).

## Discussion
  To the best of our knowledge, there are only a few studies focusing on Tumor-Infiltrating Lymphocytes (TILs) and Tumor-Associated Macrophages (TAMs) in salivary gland carcinomas (SGC)3–5. More recently, Zuljan et al. (2025) demonstrated that advanced SGCs frequently exhibit an immunosuppressive microenvironment dominated by macrophage infiltration and limited T-cell activity, with therapeutic vulnerabilities emerging only in tumors showing relatively higher T-cell to macrophage ratios6. Similarly, De Virgilio et al. (2023) reported that a high density of specific TIL and TAM subpopulations is correlated with an increased probability of lymph node metastasis7.

  Our study confirms that tumor infiltrates, specifically intratumoral CD68-positive and marginal CD163-positive TAMs, are associated with lymph node spreading. Moreover, Kaplan–Meier analysis suggested a trend in which higher intratumoral M2 macrophage density is associated with poorer prognosis, although this observation did not reach statistical significance in the log-rank test. Specifically, among the 15 progressed cases, 93.3% exhibited TAM-M2 polarization in the intratumoral infiltrate, whereas only 51.5% of non-progressing cases showed TAM-M2 polarization. These findings suggest a potential prognostic role for intratumoral M2-polarized TAMs, consistent with previous studies, but surely further investigation in larger cohorts is needed to confirm statistical significance. The previous data regarding the prognostic role of CD3-positive cells are contrasting: a high density of CD3-positive cells has been associated with better survival in ovarian, endometrial, and colon carcinomas8–10. However, some other studies, such as De Virgilio’s, have suggested a correlation between CD3 expression and a poorer prognosis, although these findings were based on a smaller number of cases6. In our study, we observed that a higher number of CD3 + lymphocytes in the peritumoral background was associated with a shorter prognosis. As CD3 is a marker of pan-T cells, it is understandable that the co-expression of additional markers determined by the abundance of some molecules, could favour an immunostimulatory response or alternatively an immunosuppressive TME.

  In detail, a TME enriched in Th-1(CD3+-CD4+) polarized cells shows an immunostimulatory TME. Conversely, a high number of Tregs (CD3-CD4-FOXP3-positive), defines an immunosuppressive TME3. In our study, we found an enrichment of Tregs in TME that negatively affected the PFS. Similarly, a higher number of Th lymphocytes was found to be associated with a worse PFS. Although this data seems in contrast to what has documented from previous studies linking higher Th cells to antineoplastic response, it is well established that in response to the cytokines’ milieu in the TME, naïve CD4 T-cells can differentiate into different Th subsets with divergent and contrasting effects in antitumoral immune responses. Notably, these Th subsets are plastic and, following the appropriate environmental cues, can switch their phenotype from one subset to another. Since the knowledge of the direct effect of Th cells on the tumor cells is limited and well defined in some specific cancers such as colorectal, gastric or pancreatic11,12, further studies are needed to better characterize the phenotype and the right function of the different Th cell subtypes. This may help to unravel potential therapeutic targets.

  PD-L1 expression has been associated with poor clinical outcomes, since the binding of PD-L1 to its receptor (PD-1) can help tumor cells escape the immune surveillance by enhancing Tregs’ function and promoting the exhaustion of cytotoxic T-cells13. In our series, we did not find any prognostic significance of PD-L1 expression in correlation to OS and PFS. This result could be justified firstly under a technical point of view: indeed, we used the PD-L1 clone SP263 -routinely applied in our lab setting- although some recent studies in the literature have demonstrated the application of other more sensitive clones with spatial transcriptomic techniques to detection low-level PD-L1 expression, frequently missed by conventional IHC14. Further studies with the application of these high-sensitivity methodologies are needed to better define the prognostic role of PD-L1 expressing cells in SGC. Regarding a biological explanation, it is worthwhile noting how immune evasion in SGC may not be driven only by the PD-1/PD-L1 axis, since recent studies have found other immune-checkpoint molecules, like the B7 homolog 4 (B7-H4), member of the same B7 family as PD-L1 and associated with immunosuppressive microenvironments in SGC in an alternative fashion15.

  In agreement with our current data, investigating the prognostic effect of intratumoral and peritumoral CD8-positive cells’ density and their citoxicity seem to have the greatest impact on tumor survival among all TILs subpopulations. However, conflicting results are reported by the different studies focused on SGC. Mosconi et al. found that a high CD8 + T cell density in the invasive front displayed significantly poor OS16, while Kesar et al. recently documented that the quantity of CD8 + T cells in the intratumoral compartment displayed an indifferent impact on patient survival17.

  In line with other studies that found no significant evidence of CD8 + TIL density with patient prognosis18–20, our data showed no correlation between CD8-positive T cells and OS or PFS. The specific role of CD4-positive T-cell subsets has been rarely explored in SGC. A higher CD4 + expression compared with CD8 + TIL (CD4/CD8 ratio up to 10:2) was found to be associated with a better prognosis in the salivary duct carcinoma21, but not in other histotypes22,23. Immunosuppression and T-cell exhaustion by CD4-positive cells are mainly encouraged by FOXP3 upregulation on SGC specimens and peripheral blood samples18,24. However, to the best of our knowledge, we observed for the first time a correlation between FOXP3-and CD4-positive cells and survival in SGCs. In detail, usually the intratumoral Tregs are associated with a worse prognosis, supporting the hypothesis of their immunosuppressive role and dysfunction in the SGC. Due to the limited sample size in our study, a definitive correlation between nodal status, metastasis, OS and TME cannot be established. For the same reason, the results cannot be stratified according to other known variables, such as histology and grading. However, our study yields encouraging results, suggesting that the tumor microenvironment may be a valuable additional prognostic factor in salivary gland carcinomas. In contrast to previous studies, which sometimes depicts mucoepidermoid carcinoma (MEC) as an indifferent subtype in terms of immune infiltrate17, we documented that the majority of MECs in our cohort exhibited high levels of immune infiltrates, particularly CD3⁺, CD4⁺, CD8⁺, and CD20⁺ cells in both tumoral and peritumoral regions. These findings align with recent evidence indicating that immune composition in SGCs varies significantly by histological subtype; for example, excretory duct–derived tumors, including MEC and salivary duct carcinoma, have been shown to display higher immunogenicity compared to intercalated duct–derived subtypes such as adenoid cystic carcinoma (AdCC) and myoepithelial carcinoma25. Additionally, anatomic subsite may influence immune cell distribution and phenotype, as demonstrated by Tasoulas et al. (2024), who reported differences in CD4⁺ T cell and NK cell populations across AdCC intraglandular locations26. Taken together, these data underscore the heterogeneity of TIL patterns in SGC according to both histotype and tumor site, supporting our observation of subtype-specific immune landscapes. In addition, although the retrospective analysis of tissue samples may represent a source of bias, we used an objective counting tool (QuPath) to obtain a clearer and defined methodology and standardisation of TIL/TAM quantification. Indeed, digital analyses have overcome many problems of conventional quantitative scoring by pathologists: capacity to evaluate thousands or millions of cells, higher reproducibility in the slide scanned images, and speed in evaluating multiple slides or cases in the research setting27. While other studies have attempted to validate the use of software and compare it with real-life pathological diagnoses, especially using TILs and PD-L1 expression28, we intended to verify something more subtle -but practical- that is the ability of pathologist with no previous training to use a platform in order to quantify and compare morphometric data. We were able to assess TILs and TAM in a moderate-sized cohort of SGC with good agreement between the pathologist assessment and QuPath quantification. Evaluating the immune TME is puzzling, since tissue samples are usually limited, and only a static representation of one dynamic and ongoing process. To better understand the interplay between different cells, one approach is represented by the use of multiplex IHC with the benefits of using several epitopes to study cellular compositions and relations, while quantifying and locating TME29; another emerging approach is represented by spatial transcriptomics, able to localize immune checkpoint molecules -such as PD-L1 and B7-H4- in relation to immune cell localization15. QuPath represents a useful tool for immune quantification, and we found a good correlation between multiplex and single-plex IHC and between automatic and manual scoring. Further studies should focus on standardising the definition of low and high density of TIL and TAM, using larger samples and TAM markers with the primary aim to define their potential role in SGC progression and outcome.

  The main limitations of our study are represented by the small sample size and the retrospective nature of the analysis. As stated above, various SGC histology harbour a different rate of metastatic spread and different follow-up duration, and we could not perform multivariate analysis to adjust this bias. Given the rarity and great heterogeneity of SGC, other multicentric studies are mandatory to finely investigate the role of TIL and TAM in SGC progression. Additional limitation is, as stated above, the application of antibodies with limited diagnostic accuracy (e.g. the PD-L1 clone SP263) that would have required validation with a wider IHC panel.

  In conclusion, TILs are potential predictors of progression and prognosis in SGC. A high density of T lymphocytes and Treg subset correlated to a worse PFS. However, further multicentric and prospective analyses are needed to better define the prognostic role of TIL and especially TAM, as well as the interplay between PD-L1 and complementary pathways. Here we demonstrated the utility of QuPath in identifying and scoring tumor cells and IHC quantification. We hope that digital images and quantification will be widely used in clinical diagnosis and research. Artificial intelligence-based methods could fully quantify a deep-immune score mirroring the immune profile of neoplastic patients, their prognosis and finally open a way to novel immunotherapeutic targets.


## Methods
  The potential ambiguity between relative abundance and cell density has now been clarified. Cell quantification was primarily performed as cell density (number of positive cells per mm²) using automated counts in QuPath. Percentage-based scores were subsequently derived from these density values solely for standardized semi-quantitative reporting. Both the Immunohistochemistry and Statistical Analysis sections have been revised to clearly reflect this workflow.A total of 103 patients with diagnosed SGC, observed and treated at the SOD Otorhinolaryngology, Azienda Ospedaliero-Universitaria (A.O.U) Careggi, Florence, Italy, and the UOC Otorhinolaryngology, Azienda USL Toscana Nord-Ovest, between 2015 and 2023, were retrospectively included. Clinical data were collected for age, sex, and tumor site. Haematoxylin & eosin (H&E) stained slides, and formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue specimens were collected from the archive of the Pathology Unit of Azienda Toscana Nord-Ovest and Careggi University Hospital (Florence, Italy). Surgical specimens and excisional or incisional biopsies were collected. Eligibility criteria for bioptic specimens enrolled were tumor cell enrichment ≥ 50% per slide, presence of peritumoral salivary gland tissue, absence or scarce fibrosis and necrosis.

  In addition, we collected 10 negative (non-neoplastic) controls, from 10 normal salivary gland tissues, consecutively selected from the archives of the Pathology Unit of Azienda Toscana Nord-Ovest, starting in 2015: these non-neoplastic tissues consisted of salivary gland parenchyma obtained from surgical samples of patients with a mean age of 59.9 years (range 20–87), including 6 males and 4 females, excised for chronic sialoadenitis. The site of origin was the parotid gland in 9 cases and the submandibular gland in 1 case. The laterality was evenly distributed, with 5 cases on the left and 5 on the right. These 10 control cases were used to assess the regular composition and distribution of lymphocytic population in non-neoplastic salivary glands, as well as to set up the image analysis.

  Two expert pathologists reviewed the slides and formulated the diagnosis according to the World Health Organization (WHO) classification of the Head and Neck Tumors 5th edition (WHO) and staged the tumors according to the latest American Joint Committee for Cancer (AJCC). The histological grade was assigned by default for those histological entities with an intrinsic low, intermediate, or high grade. For entities requiring histological grading, the following scoring systems were used: the Brandwein scoring system for mucoepidermoid carcinomas30; the Van Weert, Zhang, and Xu score systems respectively for adenoid-cystic carcinomas31–33, and the WHO 5th edition for the salivary gland carcinoma NOS1. This study was conducted according to the STROBE Guidelines and under the permission of the Ethic Committee of Azienda Sanitaria Toscana Nord Ovest and AOU Careggi, Florence. The sample size calculation was performed for progression-free survival analysis by the log-rank test. Based on previous exploratory data, a two-sided α error of 0.05, and a statistical power of 80%, a hazard ratio of 0.40 between comparison groups was considered clinically relevant. Considering an expected event rate of 20%, a minimum sample size of 50–70 patients was estimated to achieve 10 progression events, achieving a 95% confidence level with a 5% margin of error.


### Immunohistochemistry
  Immunohistochemical (IHC) analyses were performed on representative FFPE sections. Standard procedures were applied following the manufacturer’s instructions. Ventana Benchmark ULTRA by Roche Diagnostic in Monza, Italy and Leica Biosystem BOND III autostainer were the automated staining systems used. The tissue samples were processed using Ventana, Italy’s UltraView Universal Detection Kit. The following antibodies in single and/or double staining were evaluated: CD20 (L26) for B-lymphocytes, CD3 (2GV6) for T-lymphocytes, CD8 (SP57) for cytotoxic T-lymphocytes (CTL), CD4 (SP35) for helper T-cells (TH), CD56 (123C3) for natural killer cells (NK), PD-1 (NAT105) for T-exhausted (T-exh) lymphocytes (co-expression of CD8-PD-1), PD-L1 (SP263), FOXP3 (SP97) for T-regulatory (T-reg) cells (co-expression of CD4-FOXP3), CD163 (10D6) and CD68 (KP-1) for M1 and M2 tumor-associated macrophages (TAM).   For each case, 6 hot-spot regions − 3 intratumoral and 3 peritumoral- were analysed. Each hot-spot region covered an area of ~ 0.2 mm² and was identified using QuPath’s Density Map tools to highlight areas of highest immune cell infiltration. Therefore, cell densities were automatically counted in each hot spot using QuPath, as the number of positive cells per area (cells/mm²) for each marker. For reporting in a standardized semi-quantitative format, the primary quantitative measure (cell density, expressed as cells/mm²) was subsequently converted into percentage-based scores.

  The IHC scoring system for CD3, CD20, CD4, CD8, CD56, PD-1, CD8/PD-1, CD4/FOXP3, CD163, and CD68 was based on the percentage of positive cells:

  0 = no positive cells.1 = 1–5% positive cells.2 = 6–50% positive cells.3 = > 50% positive cells.

  PD-L1 expression on tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes was scored as:

  0 = < 1% positive cells.1 = 1–5%.2 = 6–10%.3 = 11–50%.4 = > 50%.

  Macrophage polarization was quantified by calculating the M2 ratio, defined as the number of CD68⁺/CD163⁺ macrophages divided by the total number of CD68⁺ macrophages. A ratio > 0.5 indicated predominance of M2-polarized macrophages, whereas a ratio < 0.5 indicated M1 predominance.

  Mean values for densities and the M2 ratio were calculated from multiple hot-spot fields to provide a single representative value per case. This approach ensures a fully quantitative, reproducible, and standardized assessment of immune infiltration while maintaining consistency with previously established scoring systems34–36.


### Digital slides and QuPath scoring method
  Both negative and neoplastic H&E and IHC slides were digitized using the Aperio GT 450 DX scanner (Leica Biosystems) in BigTIFF format and analysed with QuPath v0.5. The peritumoral region (tumor periphery) was defined as the area extending for a distance equivalent to one high-power field (HPF) at 400× magnification from the tumor margin, corresponding to the tumor–host interface, while the intratumoral region (tumor center) was defined as the area extending inward from the inner border of this peritumoral zone toward the tumor center. Slides were viewed at 20x magnification, and density map tools identified hot-spot areas with the highest stained cell density. For both intratumoral and peritumoral tissues, the average antibody expression was measured in three high-power (40x) fields37,38.

  Cell detection was performed using QuPath’s built-in segmentation algorithm, with settings adjusted according to tissue type (Table 5). Smoothed features (within a 25 μm radius) were calculated to homogenize image segmentation. This smoothing step refers to post-detection feature averaging and is distinct from pixel-level image filtering parameters reported in the Table 5, which shows a pre-detection parameter.

  An expert pathologist manually annotated cell groups (tumor, immune, and other) using the Polygon tool. A detection classifier was trained by annotating numerous cells and refining the model through trial and error. Once finalized, it was used across all samples to automatically classify cells (Fig. 2). The number of various cell types in hot-spot regions was then automatically counted. All data were extracted from QuPath, and further calculations (e.g., proportion of positive lymphocytes) were done in Excel. The proportion of positive lymphocytes was defined as the ratio of positively stained lymphocytes to the total number of infiltrating lymphocytes counted in that region after digital segmentation.

  Double-stain IHC images require the discrimination of 4-pixel classes: two stain chromogens, the counterstain (Haematoxylin), and a residual/background component. The standard dual-colour deconvolution approach is limited to three components and was therefore insufficient for this application39. To overcome this limitation, a supervised pixel classifier was trained using manually annotated regions from 20 representative slides. Pixel classification was performed in QuPath using colour-based features derived from RGB values, rather than object-level measurements or intensity thresholds. An ensemble-based classifier implemented in QuPath as “Random Trees” (conceptually equivalent to a Random Forest classifier) was used. The trained classifier assigned each pixel to one of the four predefined classes, enabling accurate separation of the two chromogens, counterstain, and residual signal.


### Statistical analysis
  Patients’ demographics, tumor characteristics, and data from IHC analyses were collected and organized using Microsoft Excel®. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages, while continuous variables were reported as means ± standard deviations (SD) or as medians with range (min-max). Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TIL) and TAM densities, as well as their respective ratios, were quantitatively assessed as continuous variables and, when appropriate, categorized according to the above-mentioned scoring systems34–36. As far as PD-L1 is concerned, tumor cell positivity was assessed as the percentage of tumor cells exhibiting membrane staining of any intensity. Immune cell positivity was evaluated as the percentage of PD-L1–positive immune cells relative to the total number of immune cells within the tumor area, including intratumoral and contiguous peritumoral stroma. Total cell positivity was determined for each high-power field (40×) as the percentage of positive cells, including both tumor and immune/stromal cells, and subsequently averaged across all analysed hot-spot regions to obtain a single representative value per case.

  These immune parameters, along with Immunoscore values, were analysed in relation to histological type and grade, pathological stage (pT, pN, pM), perineural invasion, necrosis, perivascular invasion, by appropriate statistical tests (i.e., chi square test) and clinical outcomes including progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS), the latter defined as the time from surgery to death from any cause, while the PFS was defined as the time from surgery to the relapse occurrence. PFS and OS were estimated using Kaplan–Meier method, and the log-rank test was employed in univariable analyses to identify possible prognostic factors. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals, and Cox proportional hazards regression was applied in univariable models, with hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs reported. A multivariable model was not used due to the small sample size and low number of events. Statistical analyses were conducted using the R software (version 4.0.1) and GraphPad Prism v6.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant to exclude the null hypothesis. Inter-rater reliability between pathologists’ assessments and QuPath-based evaluations was measured using Cohen’s Kappa.
