# Betulin Protects Against Cardiac Hypertrophy by Improving AMPK/Nrf2‐Dependent Mitochondrial Function


## ABSTRACT
  Pathological cardiac hypertrophy (PCH) drives heart failure and affects global mortality, without specific pharmacotherapy. Betulin is a natural pentacyclic triterpene exhibiting diverse pharmacological properties. We investigated the therapeutic potential and underlying mechanisms of betulin in PCH. PCH was induced in C57BL/6J mice via angiotensin II (Ang II) infusion and transverse aortic constriction, with betulin administered in the last 2 weeks. Cardiac function was evaluated using echocardiography, and myocardial injury and remodeling were assessed using histological staining and molecular analyses. RNA sequencing and biochemical analyses elucidated molecular mechanisms. Molecular docking (MD) and molecular dynamics simulations (MDS) analyses predicted potential binding modes of betulin with AMP‐activated protein kinase (AMPK) and nuclear factor erythroid 2‐related factor 2 (Nrf2). To confirm AMPK/Nrf2 pathway‐dependent mitoprotective and cardioprotective effects of betulin, the AMPKα2−/− mice, AMPK inhibitor, siNrf2, and AMPK agonist intervention were used. Betulin treatment significantly ameliorated cardiac hypertrophy, fibrosis (reducing fibrotic area by 65%), and dysfunction (increasing EF by 13.8% and FS by 11.6%) in PCH mice. Transcriptomic and biochemical analyses revealed that betulin activated AMPK phosphorylation, promoted Nrf2 nuclear translocation, and upregulated antioxidant genes, restoring mitochondrial function in hypertrophied hearts and Ang II‐stimulated cardiomyocytes. MD and MDS analyses indicated that betulin enhanced interactions between AMPK and Nrf2. AMPK inhibition significantly reversed betulin‐mediated alleviation of mitochondrial dysfunction by the AMPK/Nrf2 signaling axis. Cardioprotective effects of betulin were abolished in AMPKα2−/− mice and Nrf2‐deficient cardiomyocytes. Betulin ameliorates pressure overload‐induced PCH and mitochondrial dysfunction by activating AMPK/Nrf2 signaling pathway, highlighting it as a therapeutic agent for PCH.


## Introduction
  Pathological cardiac hypertrophy (PCH) is a vital driver of heart failure progression and an independent risk factor contributing to elevated morbidity and mortality associated with cardiovascular diseases (Messerli et al. 2007; Oldfield et al. 2020). This condition is characterized by cardiomyocyte enlargement, impaired cardiac contractility, myocyte fibrosis, chronic heart dysfunction, and sudden death (Ye, Huang, et al. 2023a; Ye, Zhou, et al. 2023b). Although several anti‐hypertrophy treatments targeting angiotensin II (Ang II) and β‐adrenergic receptors are clinically available (McKinsey and Kass 2007), their efficacy in mitigating cardiac hypertrophy remains suboptimal, highlighting the urgent need for novel and effective pharmacological interventions.

  Mitochondrial dysfunction is vital for PCH progression. As the heart is a high‐energy‐demanding organ, mitochondria serve as the primary source of Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP) via oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), which is essential for sustaining cardiac excitation‐contraction coupling (Da Dalt et al. 2023; Qi et al. 2020). Impaired mitochondrial function disrupts ATP production and cellular energy metabolism, directly contributing to cardiomyocyte enlargement (Teixeira et al. 2023). Concomitantly, mitochondrial damage leads to excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), exacerbating oxidative stress and accelerating the deterioration of cardiac structure and function (Teixeira et al. 2023). AMP‐activated protein kinase (AMPK) is a central energy sensor that regulates key metabolic pathways in response to energy fluctuations (Wang et al. 2018). AMPK activation ameliorates cardiac hypertrophy by modulating mitochondrial energy metabolism, mitochondrial biogenesis, fatty acid oxidation (FAO), autophagy, and glucose metabolism (Wang et al. 2018; Han et al. 2025), with AMPKα2 specifically enhancing mitochondrial function to protect against hypertrophy and contractile dysfunction (Wang et al. 2018). Nuclear factor erythroid 2‐related factor 2 (Nrf2) is a master transcription factor of antioxidant defenses that translocates to the nucleus upon activation and binds to the antioxidant response element, inducing detoxifying and antioxidative genes, including heme oxygenase‐1 (HO‐1) and NADPH dehydrogenase quinone 1 (NQO‐1), to bolster antioxidant capacity and redox homeostasis (Yue et al. 2024; Ci et al. 2017; Cheng et al. 2024). Nrf2 activation reduces oxidative stress and mitigates hypertrophy‐associated cellular injury (Zoccarato et al. 2025). Recent findings have revealed a functional coupling between AMPK and Nrf2. AMPK activation promotes Nrf2 nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity, establishing a protective AMPK/Nrf2 axis (Zhang et al. 2026). While the protective role of AMPK/Nrf2 signaling against inflammation and oxidative stress has been well‐documented in other organs, such as kidney (Lu et al. 2023) and liver (Cheng et al. 2024; Shen et al. 2023), its precise contribution to maintaining mitochondrial functional integrity during the development of PCH has been underexplored.

  Natural compounds exhibiting therapeutic efficacy with minimal toxicity represent a growing focus in cardiovascular disease drug discovery. Betulin (lup‐20(29)‐ene‐3β,28‐diol) is a pentacyclic triterpene abundantly present in birch bark, possessing diverse pharmacological activities, including anti‐inflammatory, antibacterial, and antiviral properties (Bai et al. 2016; Ma and Long 2016). Notably, betulin alleviates myocardial inflammation in streptozocin‐induced diabetic mice via regulating the Sirt1/Nucleotide‐binding Leucine‐rich Repeat Pyrin Domain‐containing Protein 3 (NLRP3)/Nuclear Factor‐kappa B (NF‐κB) pathway (Wen et al. 2020). However, most prior studies have restricted their focus to the anti‐inflammatory properties of betulin in metabolic diseases. Despite these promising findings, the potential of betulin to target the AMPK/Nrf2 axis to preserve mitochondrial functional integrity in PCH remains largely unknown.

  In this study, we explored the therapeutic potential of betulin against pressure overload‐induced cardiac hypertrophy using Ang II‐stimulated primary cardiomyocytes in vitro. This finding was further validated in vivo using Ang II injection and transverse aortic constriction (TAC) mouse models. Mechanistic studies were performed in vitro and in vivo using AMPK inhibitors and AMPKα2 knockout (AMPKα2−/−) mice. During in vitro studies, experiments using AMPK agonists combined with Nrf2 knockdown (siNrf2) were performed to further verify that Nrf2 serves as a downstream effector. Molecular Docking (MD) and molecular dynamics simulation (MDS) were used to investigate the binding modes of betulin with AMPKα2 and Nrf2 proteins, and to evaluate the interaction between betulin and these two proteins. Mechanistically, betulin restored AMPK phosphorylation and activated the downstream AMPK/Nrf2 signaling axis, thereby attenuating mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress in cardiomyocytes.


## Materials and Methods

### Animals
  Male AMPKα2 global knockout mice (AMPKα2−/−) in a C57BL/6J genetic background were acquired from GemPharmatech (Nanjing, China). Age‐matched wild‐type (WT) littermates were used as controls. AMPKα2−/− mice were genotyped via Sanger sequencing (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China), and AMPKα2 deficiency in cardiac tissue was confirmed by Western bloting. Male C57BL/6J mice (20 ± 2 g) were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of the Hangzhou Medical College (Hangzhou, China). The animals were housed under standardized conditions, including a controlled temperature of 22 ± 2°C, a relative humidity of 40%–60%, and a 12‐h light–dark cycle. The animals had ad libitum access to food and water. All animal experiments were conducted in strict accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Laboratory Animal Care and Welfare at Hangzhou Medical College (Approval No. ZJCLA‐IACUC‐20020204; Hangzhou, China). Additionally, the animals used in these studies were acclimated to laboratory conditions for 2 weeks.


### Establishment of Animal Models
  For the Ang II mouse model (Han et al. 2025), mice were randomly divided into two groups: Sham and Ang II (n = 6 for the sham group and n = 18 for the Ang II group). Ang II was obtained from MedChemExpress (purity ≥ 99.98%; HY‐13948, New Jersey, USA). Either Ang II (1 μg/kg/min) or normal saline was infused through osmotic minipumps (Alzet model 1004, DURECT Corporation, Cupertino, USA) for 4 weeks. For minipump surgery, the mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane combined with oxygen using an air anesthesia machine. An osmotic pump was then subcutaneously implanted into the back of each mouse. Systolic blood pressure was monitored and recorded using a noninvasive tail‐cuff pressure analysis system (BP‐2010A, Softron, Tokyo, Japan) at indicated intervals.

  For the TAC mouse model (Han et al. 2025), mice were randomly assigned to either the sham or TAC surgery groups (n = 6 for the sham group and n = 18 surviving mice for the TAC group). Briefly, the mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane. A left thoracotomy was performed, and the transverse aorta was constricted using a 6–0 silk suture tied around a 27‐G needle. The needle was subsequently removed, and the chest was closed. Throughout the procedure, the respiratory rate, body temperature, and heart rate (HR) were continuously monitored. In the sham group, the procedure was identical except for the suture ligation. There was a 33.33% mortality rate during TAC surgery and no mice died after TAC surgery. Two mice that died during TAC surgery were excluded.

  From the third week, the Ang II or TAC‐challenged mice were randomly divided into three experimental groups respectively (n = 6/group): Vehicle (0.5% carboxymethylcellulose sodium solution, CMC‐Na), low‐dose betulin (15 mg/kg; purity ≥ 98.0%; HY‐N0083, MedChemExpress), and high‐dose betulin (30 mg/kg), which were based on previously published studies (Ci et al. 2017; Bai et al. 2016; Tang et al. 2011; Ayyappan et al. 2020). We adopted these specific doses to study the dose‐dependent therapeutic effects while avoiding potential cytotoxicity or off‐target effects observed at higher concentrations. Betulin was diluted in 0.5% CMC‐Na solution and administered to the mice via oral gavage every day for 2 weeks. Mice in the vehicle group received an equivalent volume of CMC‐Na solution over the same period.

  After 4 weeks, the cardiac function of the mice was evaluated using an ultrasound imaging system. Following this assessment, the mice were euthanized with an intraperitoneal injection of phenobarbital sodium (40 mg/kg), and all hearts were harvested. Heart weight and tibia length were measured. Heart tissues were either snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen for subsequent gene and protein studies or fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histological evaluation. Blood samples were also collected, and serum was separated via centrifugation at 4°C and 3000 rpm for 15 min, which was later used for atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP, JL20612, Jianglai, Shanghai, China) and creatine kinase‐myocardial band (CK‐MB, JL10961, Jianglai) determination.


### Cardiac Function Evaluation
  Systolic function in the mouse heart was evaluated using a multi‐mode small animal ultrasound imaging system (VINNO Technology, Suzhou, China). During echocardiographic assessment, anesthesia was induced with 2% isoflurane, and the mice were positioned on a plate without mechanical ventilation. Two‐dimensional M‐mode echocardiography was performed from a long‐axis view at the mitral valve level. Fractional shortening (FS) was determined by calculating the percentage difference between the left ventricular end‐systolic and end‐diastolic diameters (LVESD and LVEDD, respectively) using the formula FS% = (LVEDD − LVESD)/LVEDD × 100%. Ejection fraction (EF) was calculated using the formula: EF (%) = [(left ventricular end–diastolic volume, LVEDV) − (left ventricular end‐systolic volume, LVESV)]/LVEDV × 100%. For accuracy, all echocardiographic parameters were assessed from images captured of five consecutive cardiac cycles in each mouse, and the average of the three measurements was used for calculations.


### RNA Sequencing Analysis
  The RNA integrity from heart tissues was mainly evaluated using an Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). From 1 mg of total RNA, mRNA was extracted using magnetic beads linked with oligo(dT). The isolated mRNA was fragmented into smaller segments and reverse transcribed into cDNA using random hexamer primers. cDNA library construction and sequencing were conducted using the BGISEQ‐500 platform (Beijing Genomics Institute, Shenzhen, China). High‐quality reads were mapped to the mouse reference genome using Bowtie2 (version 2.2.5) and HISAT2 (version 2.0.4). Gene expression was normalized as fragments per kilobase of exon model per million mapped reads (FPKM) using RSEM (version 1.2.12). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified and filtered using a threshold of p < 0.05 and a log2 fold change > 1 or < −1 using the DESeq2 R package. Gene set enrichment analysis was conducted, considering significance criteria as a p < 0.05.


### Cell Culture
  Neonatal rat cardiomyocytes (NRCMs) were isolated using a standardized protocol as previously described (Han et al. 2025). Briefly, heart tissues from neonatal rats were excised, rinsed in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), and digested using a mixture of pancreatin and collagenase. Fibroblasts were separated using a differential adherence culture. Both fibroblasts and cardiomyocytes were maintained in Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 4.5 g/L glucose and 10% fetal bovine serum and incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2. After 48 h of culture, NRCMs displayed regular contractions and were subsequently used for experimental procedures.


### JC‐1 Staining
  NRCMs were stained with JC‐1 dye (10 μM; T3168; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) at 37°C for 30 min, washed thrice with PBS, and observed directly under a microscope (200× amplification; Leica Microsystems). The ratio of red to green fluorescence intensity was calculated using the ImageJ software.


### Dihydroethidium (DHE) Staining
  To assess the level of total superoxide, heart tissues and NRCMs were incubated with a 5 μM DHE (D11347, Thermo Fisher Scientific) working solution at 37°C for 30 min. The samples were then washed thrice with PBS and observed directly under a microscope (200× magnification; Leica Microsystems).


### Molecular Docking (MD)
  Due to the absence of experimentally resolved crystal structures for the full‐length AMPKα2 and Nrf2 proteins, structural modeling of these proteins and their complex was performed using AlphaFold3 webserver (https://alphafoldserver.com/). The amino acid sequences of mouse AMPKα2 (UniProt ID: Q8BRK8) and Nrf2 (UniProt ID: Q60795) were obtained from the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/). MD was conducted using AutoDockFR (AutoDock for Flexible Receptors) software (version 1.0), with the docking grid centered on the ATP‐binding pocket, and 200 docking poses were generated using the default parameters. The resulting complexes were visualized and analyzed using the PyMOL software.


### Molecular Dynamics Simulations (MDS)
  MDS were performed using Gromacs (version 2024.3) software. Protein preprocessing was conducted with AmberTools (version 22). The General Amber Force Field was assigned to the protein, while hydrogenation and restrained electrostatic potential (RESP) charge calculation were performed using ORCA (version 5.0.4). Subsequently, the preprocessed data were incorporated into the topological file for MDS. All simulations were conducted at 300 K and 1 bar. The Amber99sb‐ildn force field was used the solvent was water (Tip3p water model), and the total charge of the system was neutralized by adding sodium ions. Before the production simulation, energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent algorithm. This was followed by 100,000 steps of isothermal‐isovolumetric and isothermal‐isobaric equilibration, with a coupling constant of 0.1 ps and a total duration of 100 ps. MDS production was run for 5,000,000 steps with a time step of 2 fs, corresponding to a total simulation duration of 100 ns. Trajectory analysis was performed using the built‐in tools of GROMACS. Key structural parameters, including the root‐mean‐square deviation (RMSD), root‐mean‐square fluctuation (RMSF), and radius of gyration (Gyrate), were calculated and analyzed. MDS was performed by Hefei Kejing Biotechnology Co. Ltd.


### Cellular Thermal Shift Assay (CETSA)
  NRCMs' lysates were treated with betulin (20 μM) or dimethylsulfoxide and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. Subsequently, the samples were divided into aliquots and heated at graded temperatures (42°C, 52°C, 57°C, 62°C, 67°C, and 72°C) for 3 min. The soluble fractions were isolated by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 15 min, and the supernatants were analyzed for AMPKα2 expression via Western blotting.


### Experimental Design and Compliance
  This study was conducted following recent guidelines for rigorous pharmacological research on natural products, including standardization of extract preparation, reproducibility of bioassays, dose correlation, clinical relevance, safety and toxicity, and validation of mechanistic hypotheses through multiomics integration (Wang et al. 2024; Heinrich et al. 2020).


### Statistical Analysis
  Data are expressed as mean with standard error of the mean (SEM). The exact group size (n) in figure legends denotes independent biological replicates, not technical replicates. In in vivo experiments, our sample sizes were equal to 6, and the normal distribution of each data was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. In in vitro experiments, as each experimental dataset is an average of many cultured cells, we assumed that the data were normally distributed relying on the central limit theorem. For normally distributed data with equal variance, an unpaired two‐tailed Student's t‐test was applied to compare two groups, and one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test was applied to compare multiple groups of data. For normally distributed data with unequal variance, Welch's t‐test was applied for comparison between two groups, and Welch ANOVA tests followed by Dunnett's T3 post hoc tests were performed to analyze multiple groups of data. For non‐normally distributed data, the Mann–Whitney U test was used for two groups, and the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn's post hoc test was used for multiple groups. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0.0; San Diego, CA, USA), with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Representative images were selected to reflect the average results of the respective groups.


## Results

### Betulin Mitigated Ang II‐Induced Cardiomyocyte Hypertrophy in NRCMs
  To study the potential effects of betulin on cardiomyocytes, NRCMs were used as an in vitro model. We first determined the NRCMs viability following treatment with several betulin (0.1–312.5 μM). A cell counting kit‐8 assay demonstrated that betulin concentrations from 0.1 to 62.5 μM did not induce cytotoxicity in NRCMs (Figure 1B), justifying the selection of 10 and 20 μM for subsequent experiments. Moreover, the mRNA levels of hypertrophic (Anp and Bnp) and fibrotic (Col1a1 and Tgfb) genes were significantly upregulated in Ang II‐challenged NRCMs, while betulin treatment markedly attenuated these changes (Figure 1C–F). Consistently, Ang II induced cardiomyocyte hypertrophy as evidenced by an increased cell area, which was substantially reversed by betulin treatment (Figure 1G). These findings suggest that betulin mitigates Ang II‐induced cardiomyocyte hypertrophy in NRCMs.


### Betulin Alleviated Ang II‐Induced Cardiac Dysfunction, Hypertrophy, and Fibrosis In Vivo
  Based on our in vitro studies, we assessed the effects of betulin on Ang II‐induced cardiac hypertrophy in vivo. C57BL/6 mice were infused with Ang II at a rate of 0.11 μL/h for 4 weeks, and betulin (15 or 30 mg/kg) was administered during the last 2 weeks (Figure 2A). After Ang II infusion, the systolic blood pressure, serum level of Ang II, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine were comparably elevated. Betulin treatment did not influence these changes (Figure 2B,C and Table S1). Conversely, betulin significantly diminished the Ang II‐induced elevation of serum ANP and CK‐MB levels (Figure 2D,E) and similarly reduced cardiac mRNA expression of Anp and Bnp (Figure 2F,G). A notable amelioration in cardiac performance was recorded in betulin‐treated mice based on echocardiographic parameters, as evidenced by the recovery of EF and FS, and decreased diastole left ventricle internal dimension (LVIDd) and diastole interventricular septal thickness (IVSD) (Figure 2H–M). Consistent with these findings, betulin‐treated mice exhibited smaller hearts and reduced heart weight‐to‐tibia length and heart weight‐to‐body weight ratios (Figure 2N–P). Histopathological evaluations, including hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) staining, confirmed that betulin significantly reduced Ang II‐induced cardiac hypertrophy (Figure 2Q–S). We further evaluated the antifibrotic effects of betulin. Betulin significantly suppressed Ang II‐induced upregulation of fibrotic genes (Tgfb and Col1a1; Figure S1A,B). Consistently, Masson and Sirius red staining revealed a reduction in collagen deposition and fibrotic areas in the heart tissue (Figure S1C–F). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that betulin effectively preserves cardiac function and alleviates cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis in Ang II‐infused mice.


### Betulin Attenuated Cardiac Hypertrophy and Fibrosis in TAC‐Induced Mice
  To validate our findings from the Ang II model, we used the TAC model, a well‐established method for inducing pressure‐overload hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (Figure 3A). Betulin treatment did not exacerbate the hepatic or renal function markers in mice (Table S2). Compared to the sham group, TAC‐challenged mice exhibited significant elevations in serum ANP and CK‐MB levels, and in mRNA expression of Anp and Bnp. These alterations were completely reversed by betulin treatment (Figure 3B–E). Moreover, echocardiography revealed that betulin treatment recovered cardiac systolic dysfunction in TAC‐operated mice (Figure 3F–K). Additionally, betulin attenuated TAC‐induced increases in gross heart size (Figure 3L–N) and cardiomyocyte size (Figure 3O–Q). The antifibrotic effects of betulin were also confirmed in TAC‐induced mice. As revealed in Figure S2A,B, Tgfb and Col1a1 mRNA levels in TAC‐induced heart tissues were significantly downregulated by betulin treatment. Moreover, betulin treatment significantly reduced the degree of cardiac fibrosis and collagen deposition (Figure S2C–F). These findings confirm that betulin effectively attenuates TAC‐induced cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis.


### Betulin Exerted Cardioprotective Effects via AMPK and Nrf2 Pathway Activation
  To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the anti‐hypertrophic effects of betulin in the heart, RNA sequencing analysis was performed. A total of 461 downregulated genes and 1109 upregulated genes were identified between the Ang II and sham groups (Figure 4A). By comparison, 119 genes were downregulated and 683 genes were upregulated following betulin treatment in Ang II‐induced heart tissues (Figure 4B). Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) analysis highlighted the potential involvement of the AMPK pathway in the cardioprotective effects of betulin (Figure 4D). As expected, betulin significantly reversed Ang II‐induced reduction of AMPKT172 in heart tissues (Figure 4E,F). Similar results were observed in NRCMs (Figure 4G,H). Intersection analysis identified 47 DEGs upregulated by Ang II and downregulated upon betulin treatment (Figure 4I). These DEGs were selected for the predictive analysis of transcription factors using the Enrich website. Nrf2 emerged as the top candidate (Figure 4J). Nrf2, a well‐established downstream effector of AMPK, is widely accepted as a key transcription factor responsible for regulating antioxidant defense systems (Ci et al. 2017; Wang et al. 2019). Therefore, we studied whether betulin affected the Nrf2 pathway. Western blotting validated that betulin remarkably rescued the Ang II‐induced suppression of Nrf2 pathway components, including HO‐1, NQO‐1, total‐Nrf2, and nuclear‐Nrf2 heart tissues and NRCMs (Figure 4E–H).

  AMPKα2 is a critical subunit of AMPK, which protects against cardiac hypertrophy and contractile dysfunction by enhancing mitochondrial OXPHOS (Han et al. 2025). Based on the direct binding of AMPKα2 to Nrf2 observed in H9c2 cells (Figure S3A), we hypothesized that betulin might stabilize this interaction. MD placed betulin within the ATP‐binding pocket of AMPKα2, adjacent to the activation loop (A‐loop) (Figure S3B). Structural analysis revealed extensive hydrogen bonding at the AMPKα2‐Nrf2 interface (Figure S3C,D). Notably, the modeled structure indicated that AMPKα2T172 (located within the flexible A‐loop) is spatially proximal to Nrf2T80 (Figure S3E). This proximity suggests that betulin binding may induce conformational changes in the A‐loop, facilitating crosstalk between AMPKα2T172 and Nrf2T80.

  To elucidate the dynamic stability of the betulin‐AMPKα2‐Nrf2 complex, we performed 100 ns MDS. RMSD and RMSF analyses collectively demonstrated that the complex achieved a stable equilibrium while maintaining appropriate structural flexibility (Figure S3F,G). Furthermore, the consistent decrease in radius of gyration (Rg) and solvent accessible surface area (SASA) suggests that betulin binding induces structural compaction (Figure S3H–J), reflecting a tightened intermolecular interface between AMPKα2 and Nrf2. MM/PBSA calculations confirmed a strong binding affinity (ΔG total = −158.715 kcal/mol), with significant contributions from key residues, including LEU146, VAL30, ILE77, and LEU22 (Figure S3K–M). Finally, CETSA in NRCMs demonstrated that betulin treatment significantly enhanced the thermal stability of AMPKα2 (Figure S3N). Taken together, these results indicate that betulin directly binds to AMPKα2, thereby stabilizing the AMPKα2‐Nrf2 complex and facilitating their interaction.


### Betulin Alleviated Ang II–Induced Myocardial Hypertrophy and Mitochondrial Dysfunction Via AMPK/Nrf2 Signaling Axis Activation
  Previous studies have demonstrated that AMPK regulates mitochondrial homeostasis (Han et al. 2025; Ci et al. 2017), whereas Nrf2 is widely recognized as a key regulator of mitochondrial membrane potential and oxidative stress homeostasis (Yang et al. 2020). Next, we evaluated the effect of betulin on mitochondrial injury and oxidative stress. As revealed in Figure 5A,B, Ang II‐induced mice exhibited elevated intracellular ROS production, which was attenuated by betulin treatment. In vitro results in NRCMs followed a similar trend (Figure 5C,D). Moreover, electron microscopy revealed mitochondrial swelling, mitochondrial ridge disappearance, and increased mitochondrial size in NRCMs with Ang II stimulation, while betulin mitigated these changes. A modified five‐grade scoring system was used to assess mitochondrial damage (Hsieh et al. 2019), and the results indicated that betulin effectively improved Ang II‐induced mitochondrial damage in NRCMs (Figure 5E). To evaluate the mitochondrial function integrity, mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed using the JC‐1 probe. Notably, exposure to Ang II resulted in a considerable decline in the mitochondrial membrane potential in NRCMs, whereas betulin‐treated NRCMs exhibited resistance to this Ang II‐induced change (Figure 5F). Moreover, betulin significantly increased ATP production (Figure 5G) and complex I/II‐mediated mitochondrial respiration (Figure 5H,I).

  To study the hierarchical order of the AMPK/Nrf2 pathway in response to betulin, we used compound C (2 μM), an AMPK inhibitor, to perturb the pathway in Ang II‐stimulated NRCMs. As presented in Figure 6A, betulin attenuated Ang II‐induced cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, while AMPK inhibition significantly reversed these effects. Additionally, AMPK inhibition prevented the betulin‐induced recovery of the protein expression levels of the downstream Nrf2 pathway (Figure 6B). Moreover, DHE and JC‐1 staining confirmed that AMPK blockade significantly diminished the protective effects of betulin against mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress (Figure 6C–F). To further validate Nrf2 as a downstream effector, we knocked down Nrf2 using a specific siRNA (siNrf2). Nrf2 silencing abrogated the protective effects of betulin, as evidenced by cardiomyocyte hypertrophy (Figure 6G,H) and elevated ROS levels (Figure 6I,J) in Ang II‐induced NRCMs. Furthermore, pharmacological activation of AMPK with AICAR failed to rescue these pathological phenotypes in Nrf2‐deficient cardiomyocytes (Figure 6G–J). Collectively, these findings suggest that betulin attenuates Ang II‐induced cardiomyocyte hypertrophy and mitochondrial dysfunction by activating the AMPK/Nrf2 signaling pathway.


### AMPKα2 Knockout Diminished the Cardioprotective Effects of Betulin in Ang II‐Challenged Mice
  To further verify that betulin exerts this cardioprotective effect by activating AMPK, we used WT and AMPKα2 knockout mice and administered betulin (30 mg/kg) over the final 2 weeks (Figure 7A). Neither AMPKα2 knockout nor betulin affected the Ang II‐induced blood pressure profile in mice (Figure 7B). ANP and CK‐MB serum levels (Figure 7C,D), along with hypertrophy markers (Anp and Bnp) (Figure 6E,F), revealed that betulin significantly blunted Ang II‐induced myocardial hypertrophy in WT mice, However, no such effect was observed in AMPKα2−/− mice. Echocardiography analysis further demonstrated that betulin ameliorated Ang II‐induced cardiac dysfunction in WT mice but failed in AMPKα2−/− mice (Figure 7G–K). Morphological analyses and ratio of heart weight to tibia length (HW/TL) evidenced that AMPKα2 deficiency completely reversed the reduction in heart size caused by betulin (Figure 7L,M). Additionally, analysis of pathological changes and fibrotic gene expression revealed that betulin remarkably reduced Ang II‐induced cardiac fibrosis in WT mice but not in AMPKα2−/− mice (Figure 7N,O and Figure S4A–D). Collectively, these results demonstrate that AMPKα2 deficiency diminishes the cardioprotective effects of betulin in Ang II‐challenged mice.


## Discussion
  This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the cardioprotective effects of betulin in PCH, focusing on its regulation of mitochondrial function. Our principal findings are as follows: (1) Betulin administration markedly ameliorated myocardial hypertrophy and interstitial fibrosis induced both by Ang II and TAC in mice; (2) betulin mitigated Ang II‐induced mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress, effects that were mediated through AMPK/Nrf2 signaling axis activation; (3) betulin's cardioprotective effects were abolished in AMPKα2−/− mice and Nrf2‐deficient cardiomyocytes. Our findings highlight betulin as a potential therapeutic agent to impede the progression of PCH and offer valuable theoretical insights for the development of betulin‐based treatment strategies. Moreove, betulin administration at the effective therapeutic doses does not induce hepatotoxicity or nephrotoxicity.

  Betulin is a natural triterpenoid compound that exhibits antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, antifibrotic, and metabolic regulatory effects (Bai et al. 2016; Ma and Long 2016). Previous studies have demonstrated its protective role in septic rats, where betulin attenuated lung and liver injury through NF‐κB and MAPK pathways suppression (Lee et al. 2024). Similarly, in pulmonary fibrosis models, betulin reduces collagen deposition and exerts potent antifibrotic effects via Transforming Growth Factor‐beta (TGF‐β) signaling pathway inhibition (Ofori et al. 2023). These findings highlight the multi‐target and organ‐protective potential of betulin across various pathological contexts. Recently, research on betulin has increasingly focused on cardiovascular diseases, with studies highlighting its ability to inhibit pro‐inflammatory cytokine expression in human cardiac AC16 cells (Zhang et al. 2015). Based on this evidence, we investigated whether betulin could confer protective benefits in PCH. Our results first demonstrate that betulin effectively ameliorates myocardial hypertrophy, dysfunction, and fibrosis triggered by Ang II and TAC in mice. Nevertheless, the precise mechanism through which betulin exerts these cardioprotective effects remains largely unclear and warrants further study.

  Our data from RNA sequencing analysis revealed a unique involvement of the AMPK signaling pathway in the betulin treatment of pressure overload‐induced mice. AMPK is a key sensor of cellular energy status, playing a central role in maintaining metabolic homeostasis and supporting mitochondrial function. Previous research has demonstrated the AMPK role in regulating cellular energy homeostasis, particularly under stress conditions, including hypertension and heart failure. For instance, Han et al. revealed that phosphatase of regenerative liver 2 (PRL2)‐induced AMPK dephosphorylation promoted metabolic imbalance and exacerbated cardiac hypertrophy, whereas AMPK activation confers protection against myocardial stress (Han et al. 2025). Similarly, Yang et al. highlighted that metformin, through its activation of AMPK, alleviates oxidative stress, reinforcing the notion that AMPK activation is a protective response to cellular injury (Yang et al. 2020). In our study, betulin significantly restored the pressure overload‐decreased AMPK phosphorylation, alleviating mitochondrial dysfunction and reducing PCH. These effects were abolished by inhibiting AMPK with compound C in primary cardiomyocytes or in the hearts of AMPKα2‐KO mice, even after betulin treatment. These results highlight the vital role of cardiac AMPK in PCH progression. Next, we explore the relationship between AMPK and its downstream Nrf2 in the context of betulin treatment. Previous research has suggested that AMPK phosphorylation promotes Nrf2 activation and nuclear translocation, enabling its antioxidant activity, particularly in diabetic kidney disease and fatty liver disease. However, the role of the AMPK/Nrf2 pathway in betulin‐induced mitochondrial protection in PCH remains unclear. Our findings emphasize the cardioprotective potential of this pathway under betulin treatment. Betulin significantly restored AMPK phosphorylation, enhanced Nrf2 accumulation and nuclear translocation, and subsequently upregulated key antioxidant genes, including HO‐1 and NQO‐1. Previous studies have reported that pThr172‐AMPKα1 directly phosphorylates Ser40 of Nrf2, enhancing the nuclear accumulation of Nrf2 in the kidneys of diabetic mice (Lu et al. 2023). Our docking results demonstrated that betulin binds to a region near the beginning of the activation loop (A‐loop) in the ATP‐binding pocket of AMPKα2. This binding induces conformational changes in AMPKα2, thereby facilitating an interaction between the Thr172 site of AMPKα2 and Nrf2Thr80. This indicates that betulin enhances the interaction between AMPK and Nrf2. However, how the AMPK/Nrf2 axis translates into broader metabolic benefits and protection against cardiac hypertrophy is still unspecified.

  The heart requires substantial energy in the form of ATP to sustain contractions that meet systemic demands. Mitochondria, occupying 30%–40% of the cardiomyocyte volume, are central to energy production, generating nearly 90% of cellular ATP via OXPHOS to support excitation‐contraction coupling (Da Dalt et al. 2023; Qi et al. 2020; Hinton Jr et al. 2024). Accumulating evidence has revealed that AMPK‐mediated mitochondrial abnormality reduces cardiac ATP level and suppresses OXPHOS, which impairs systolic and diastolic function and induces pathological hypertrophy in cardiomyocytes (Peoples et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2025; Shen et al. 2020; Zaha and Young 2012). Additionally, mitochondrial damage leads to excessive intracellular ROS production, which exacerbates oxidative stress and accelerates the decline of cardiac structure and function (Teixeira et al. 2023; Hinton Jr et al. 2024). Consistent with the findings of Han et al., inactivated AMPK and subsequent dysfunctions of mitochondria have been observed in Ang II and TAC‐induced cardiac hypertrophy mice (Han et al. 2025). Notably, betulin treatment effectively mitigated these alterations, including a reduction in mitochondrial structural damage, restoration of mitochondrial membrane potential, and attenuation of oxidative stress, thereby reversing cardiac hypertrophy. These findings are consistent with studies that exhibit betulin improves mitochondrial function and reduces mitochondrial stress in models of Chagas cardiomyopathy and alcoholic steatohepatitis (Ayyappan et al. 2020; Buko et al. 2019; Amiri et al. 2020). Consequently, we consider that betulin‐mediated AMPK/Nrf2 axis activation prevents cardiomyocyte hypertrophy through the maintenance of mitochondrial function.

  In this study, we evaluated both low and high concentrations of betulin to determine its dose–response relationship in PCH. Our results demonstrated that both concentrations significantly ameliorated pathological phenotypes. Notably, the high‐concentration group exhibited only a marginal, non‐significant improvement over the low‐concentration group, suggesting a therapeutic plateau within the experimental range. This finding has important clinical implications. First, the efficacy of the low‐dose regimen suggests that betulin can exert robust cardioprotection at minimal dosages, potentially reducing the risk of off‐target toxicity and improving cost‐effectiveness for long‐term management. Second, the observed efficacy plateau provides a reference for establishing an upper dosage limit, avoiding unnecessary dose escalation. Furthermore, toxicological evaluation confirmed that betulin within this therapeutic range induced no observable hepatorenal toxicity. In conclusion, this study clarifies the potential of betulin in treating PCH and provides key experimental support for subsequent clinical translation research from dosage optimization and safety verification.

  In summary, our study demonstrates that betulin attenuates cardiac hypertrophy via an AMPK/Nrf2‐dependent pathway, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic candidate. However, this study has several limitations to guide future investigations. First, while we used global AMPKα2 knockout (AMPKα2−/−) mice based on the high abundance of AMPKα2 in cardiac tissue, we cannot fully rule out potential confounding effects arising from extra‐cardiac tissues. Future studies using cardiac‐specific AMPKα2 deletion models are warranted to precisely dissect the cardiomyocyte‐intrinsic mechanisms of betulin. Second, the primary focus of this study was to elucidate the molecular mechanism rather than to perform a comparative efficacy analysis; therefore, a positive control was not included. Subsequent research will incorporate clinically established agents to benchmark the therapeutic potency of betulin. Third, despite the advantages of betulin as a natural compound, its clinical application is challenged by low bioavailability and hydrophobicity. To bridge this translational gap, future formulation strategies, such as the development of betulin‐loaded nanoparticles or the synthesis of hydrophilic derivatives, are essential to enhance its pharmacokinetic properties and clinical utility. Finally, our insights rely exclusively on murine models. Although these models recapitulate key features of hypertrophy, they cannot fully replicate the complex heterogeneity and metabolic nuances of human cardiac pathology. The translational relevance, including optimal dosage and safety profiles in humans, remains to be validated through preclinical large‐animal models and clinical trials.


## Conclusion
  In summary, our study demonstrates that betulin effectively mitigates pressure overload‐induced cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis in primary cardiomyocytes and murine models. The cardioprotective effects of betulin are mediated through AMPK/Nrf2 signaling axis activation, which ameliorates mitochondrial dysfunction. This study elucidates the pharmacological effects and underlying molecular mechanism of betulin in mitigating hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, providing a potential therapeutic strategy for PCH.
