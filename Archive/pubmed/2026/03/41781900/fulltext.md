# Identification of metabolism and inflammation-related biomarkers and potential drugs for schizophrenia based on comprehensive bioinformatics and machine learning


## Background
  Schizophrenia (SCZ) is a significant and widespread mental illness, where metabolism and inflammation-related genes (MIRGs) are crucial to understanding its underlying biology. This study aimed to identify peripheral biomarkers linked to metabolism and inflammation, validate these through the creation of a diagnostic model, investigate their connections with miRNA and transcription factors (TFs), and find potential drugs for targeting.


## Methods
  The datasets GES38484 and GSE54913 were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, while the MIRGs were sourced from both the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways and GeneCards. Key signature genes were identified using differential expression analysis, alongside techniques such as least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, support vector machine (SVM), Random Forest (RF), and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) methodologies. A logistic regression model was constructed for diagnostics, and its performance was assessed using a separate testing database. Furthermore, the signature genes were verified through RT-qPCR, and levels of immune infiltration were assessed using single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA). Finally, regulatory networks involving gene-miRNA and gene-TF interactions were created utilizing Cytoscape, while candidate drugs were identified through the Drug-Gene Interaction database.


## Results
  Six hub MIRGs were identified (HDC, NCKAP1L, FUCA1, CD40LG, CX3CR1, and SNAP25), leading to the development of a diagnostic signature based on these genes which served as potential biomarkers for SCZ (AUC = 0.884), further confirmed in the validation cohort (AUC = 0.933). The diagnostic model illustrated distinctions between low- and high-risk groups, highlighting differences in immune cell infiltration patterns. In-depth examination of these six genes indicated connections to 47 targeted miRNAs, 11 TFs within regulatory networks, and 40 potential therapeutic agents.


## Conclusion
  Findings from this research offer significant understanding regarding the function of MIRGs in SCZ, which could aid in the creation of viable candidate biomarkers and possible therapies that modify the disease for SCZ.

## Introduction
  Schizophrenia (SCZ) is a severe and persistent neurodevelopmental disorder marked by cognitive impairments, sensory anomalies, emotional detachment, and difficulties in social functioning, impacting roughly 1% of the worldwide population [1, 2]. Despite the clinical diagnosis being established for over a hundred years and the advancements in antipsychotic medications leading to notable improvements in acute symptoms, challenges remain in public health due to the elevated recurrence rates, disability levels, and a life expectancy reduced by 10 to 20 years compared to the general populace [3–5]. Prompt identification and diagnosis could yield significant advantages for both individuals diagnosed with SCZ and society as a whole [6]. Presently, the diagnostic standards for SCZ continue to rely on clinical symptoms and depend heavily on the psychiatrist’s assessment, which includes standard clinical interviews and potentially inaccurate accounts from patients or caregivers [7]. These diagnostic practices are inadequate, as subjective factors and varied clinical manifestations contribute to high misdiagnosis rates [8, 9]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to explore more effective strategies for diagnosing SCZ.

  For an extended period, the focus of SCZ research has been on neurotransmitter imbalances (like the dopamine hypothesis) and abnormalities in brain structure (including hippocampal atrophy and dysfunction of the prefrontal cortex). However, these factors do not adequately account for the disease’s heterogeneity and comorbidity. Recently, a growing body of evidence indicates that SCZ is not merely a “brain disorder,” but rather a systemic condition characterized by metabolic dysfunctions and imbalances in the immune system. These metabolic issues, such as obesity, insulin resistance, and dyslipidemia, alongside chronic low-grade inflammation, have been shown to be significantly linked to the risk, pathological progression, and unfavorable outcomes of SCZ [10, 11]. The intricate network of interactions among these components is emerging as a crucial breakthrough for understanding the disease’s etiology and for formulating new intervention strategies [12, 13].

  Metabolic disorders exhibit a characteristic “dual high incidence” among individuals diagnosed with SCZ. Firstly, patients experiencing their initial episode without prior medication have demonstrated a propensity for abnormal metabolic indicators. This observation implies that metabolic disorders could represent an intrinsic pathological aspect of the illness itself, rather than merely side effects stemming from the use of antipsychotic medications, particularly atypical antipsychotics [14–16]. Secondly, the occurrence of metabolic syndrome within the long-term SCZ population ranges from 30% to 60%, which is 2–3 times greater than that observed in the general population [17, 18]. This metabolic irregularity further elevates the risk for cardiovascular conditions [19], diabetes, and other physical ailments in these patients, serving as a major contributor to reduced life expectancy [20, 21]. Clinical evidence indicates that the rate of obesity among individuals with SCZ is 50% higher compared to the general populace, with the likelihood of increased fasting blood glucose levels being twice as high, and rates of abnormal lipid profiles exceeding those of the general population by over threefold [22, 23]. This metabolic phenotype does not arise at random; instead, it shows positive correlations with the intensity of the disease’s core symptoms and the level of cognitive deficits [24, 25]. This suggests that metabolic disorders may share potential pathophysiological processes with SCZ, including atypical neural development, imbalances in gut microbiota, and disturbances in hormonal secretions.

  Chronic low-grade inflammation may act as a potential link between SCZ and metabolic disorders, and its potential roles and related processes are gradually being investigated. In patients with SCZ, levels of inflammatory markers (including C-reactive protein, interleukin-6, and tumor necrosis factor-α) in peripheral blood are found to be significantly elevated [26]. Additionally, brain inflammatory responses, such as activated microglia and altered astrocyte functions, have been validated through autopsy investigations and imaging studies [27]. Inflammatory processes can directly impair neuronal synaptic activity and disrupt neurotransmitter equilibrium, while also provoking psychiatric symptoms. Furthermore, these inflammatory reactions can stimulate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, inhibit insulin signaling pathways, contribute to fat accumulation and glucose metabolism issues, ultimately resulting in metabolic disorders [28, 29]. Conversely, metabolic conditions like obesity can worsen systemic inflammation by releasing pro-inflammatory substances (such as leptin and adiponectin) from adipose tissue, thereby creating a detrimental cycle of “inflammation-metabolic disorder-psychiatric symptoms” [30]. Moreover, genetic epidemiological research has indicated that there is an overlap of genes related to SCZ, metabolic syndrome, and inflammation (such as TLR4, IL6R, and PPARG), further substantiating the molecular connections between these conditions [31–33]. Nevertheless, there is still a paucity of research specifically targeting the influence of metabolism and inflammation-related genes (MIRGs) on the onset, progression, diagnosis, and prognosis of SCZ.

  The Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) hosts extensive gene expression datasets provided by numerous research organizations globally [34]. The integration of machine learning and bioinformatics facilitates a deeper insight into the complex pathophysiological processes of various diseases by extracting and interpreting biological information sourced from these public repositories. Traditional methods typically focus on individual genes or a small set of proteins; in contrast, machine learning enables the examination of complex biological systems as integrated units. This technique has demonstrated its utility across a range of diseases, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD), SCZ, and Parkinson’s disease [35–37]. Blood samples are more accessible and preferable for routine clinical diagnostics compared to brain tissue [9, 38]. Recently, identifying biomarkers for SCZ has emerged as a key area of research, with numerous strategies deployed to uncover possible diagnostic and therapeutic markers. Reviews of existing literature and empirical investigations have pointed to a wide range of candidate biomarkers, encompassing blood-based substances, metabolic profiles, genetic indicators, and structural characteristics. Xuan et al. employed gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) to examine serum metabolites in unmedicated patients with SCZ both prior to and following risperidone treatment. Their results indicate that metabolic alterations could function as potential biomarkers linked to both the illness and the response to treatment [39]. Liu et al. further utilized GC-MS on peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) to identify metabolites with differential expression that can differentiate SCZ patients from healthy controls, thus reinforcing the significance of metabolomics in discovering biomarkers [8]. Similarly, Harris et al. introduced a model illustrating the connection between the brain and the periphery via hormones and regulatory molecules, presenting evidence of a molecular signature that includes inflammatory, hormonal, and growth factors in individuals experiencing their first episode of SCZ [40]. However, most previous studies have focused separately on either immune or metabolic abnormalities, with few establishing an integrated pipeline that combines machine learning, clinical validation, diagnostic modeling, risk stratification, and drug prediction. This study aims to identify peripheral blood diagnostic biomarkers that link metabolic and inflammatory dysregulation in schizophrenia through a comprehensive and clinically translatable approach. We examined the GSE38484 and GSE54913 datasets sourced from GEO, which included peripheral blood samples from both SCZ patients and healthy subjects. By implementing a thorough bioinformatics approach, we identified essential MIRGs and developed a novel diagnostic model, which was subsequently validated to evaluate the importance of MIRGS in the diagnosis of SCZ. Furthermore, this study identified two distinct risk subtypes, each defined by different immune infiltration patterns, established two regulatory networks for the diagnostic signature genes (gene-miRNA and gene-TFs) that may provide novel clues for understanding the RNA regulatory processes associated with SCZ, and screened for potential drugs correlated with these signature genes. In summary, our groundbreaking findings present a highly effective MIRGS for the identification and risk stratification of SCZ patients, potentially enhancing precision and personalized treatment strategies.


## Materials and methods

### Data acquisition
  An extensive review of the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) was performed utilizing the search keywords “schizophrenia” or “SCZ,” along with “array” type, and “Homo sapiens.” Criteria for dataset inclusion mandated that each group comprises a minimum of fifteen patients and ten controls, gene symbols and Entrez IDs were to be present in the annotated platforms (GPL), samples utilized for analysis originated from peripheral blood, and each dataset had at least 200 differentially expressed genes (DEGs)- this threshold was established to ensure sufficient transcriptomic signals for subsequent pathway enrichment analysis, biomarker identification, diagnostic model construction, and risk stratification, given the subtle gene expression changes observed in the peripheral blood of schizophrenia patients. Consequently, GSE38484 and GSE54913 were chosen and merged to establish a metadata file, which included 124 SCZ samples and 108 control samples. The batch effects of the expression data were mitigated using the ComBat function available in the SVA package of R software [41]. Data reliability was verified through principal component analysis (PCA) conducted with the ‘FactoMineR’ (Version 2.8) package, and PCA visualizations were generated using the ‘ggplot2’ (Version 3.4.2) package. We designated merged dataset as the training cohort and our cohort (SCZ = 15, healthy control = 17) as the external validation dataset. A total of seventeen metabolism-related gene sets from the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways database were analyzed to compile metabolism-related genes (MRGs) (Table S1) [42, 43]. From the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/), we extracted 1092 inflammation-related genes (IRGs) by applying the term “inflammation” with a relevance score of ≥ 4 as the screening threshold (Table S2). The set of metabolism and inflammation-related genes (MIRGs) is formed by the union of MRGs and IRGs, followed by the removal of duplicate genes.


### Identification and function enrichment analysis of differentially expressed metabolism and inflammation-related genes (DE-MIRGs)
  The R package ‘limma’ was employed for background correction, inter-array data normalization, and the analysis of differential expression between samples from subjects with SCZ and control participants. In this study, taking into account the inherent features of peripheral blood transcriptome data and our primary emphasis on genes associated with inflammation and metabolism, applying the conventional stringent criteria (|log2FC| > 0.5 or 1, FDR < 0.05) would inadequate in detecting a sufficient number of target genes. This inadequacy would hinder subsequent functional enrichment analysis and core gene identification, prompting the establishment of criteria to discern differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with |log2FoldChange|> (mean(abs(log2FoldChange)) + 2*sd(abs(log2FoldChange))) and a false discovery rate adjusted p-value of < 0.05. A volcano plot for the DEGs was created using the ‘ggplot2’ R package. The DE-MIRGs were identified by intersecting the DEGs with metabolism and inflammation-related genes (MIRGs) through Venn diagrams. A protein-protein interaction (PPI) network for the DE-MIRGs was constructed using the STRING database (https://string-db.org/). Additionally, the ‘clusterProfiler’ R package was employed for conducting Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses on the node genes within the PPI network.


### Machine learning screening candidate diagnostic genes based on DE-MIRGs for SCZ
  The machine learning algorithms were executed using R. To identify candidate genes, four different machine learning techniques were employed: least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) [44], Random Forest (RF) [45], support vector machines with recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) [46, 47], and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) [48]. These methods utilized the R packages glmnet (Version 4.1-7), randomForest (Version 4.7–1.1), e1071 (Version 1.7–13), and xgboost (Version 1.7–11). All models underwent hyperparameter optimization and feature robustness verification through cross-validation. For the Random Forest (RF) model, a 5-fold nested cross-validation framework was employed, wherein the outer fold was utilized to assess model generalization and the inner fold aimed to optimize the hyperparameter ‘mtry’. Genes exhibiting a MeanDecreaseGini greater than 2 were initially screened in each fold, and stable features with a selection frequency of 60% or higher across the five folds were subsequently identified. LASSO regression was applied to determine the optimal penalty parameter (λ) via 10-fold cross-validation, retaining only those features with non-zero regression coefficients. For Support Vector Machine Recursive Feature Elimination (SVM-RFE), a model was developed using a linear kernel, optimizing the hyperparameter ‘C’ through 5-fold cross-validation, followed by the screening of optimal features via recursive feature elimination. XGBoost underwent hyperparameter optimization through 5-fold cross-validation, with high-contribution features selected based on feature gain. Rather than relying on heuristic selection, the intersection of stable features identified by the four models was ultimately designated as the core feature set, thereby ensuring the statistical rigor of feature selection and enhancing the robustness and reproducibility of the core features. Ultimately, the genes that intersected among the four groups of candidate genes were designated as the final diagnostic signature genes (DSGs).

In this formula, β0 represents the Y-intercept, Coefi indicates the logistic regression coefficient for the i-th gene, xi is the expression level of the i-th gene, and n denotes the total number of genes included in the regression model. The diagnostic model was developed using the training set, while the testing set was employed to assess the model’s reliability. For internal robustness assessment, 20-fold stratified cross-validation was applied to the merged dataset, while 5-fold stratified cross-validation was used for our specific cohort. Additionally, unbiased cross-validation area under the curve (AUC) was performed. The expression levels and diagnostic efficacy of the DSGs in SCZ were initially confirmed through differential expression analysis and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve evaluations. Subsequently, a nomogram was created via logistic regression analysis to represent the diagnostic model and project the likelihood of SCZ based on the identified DSGs, utilizing the “rms” package. Each gene in the signature possesses a distinct score, and the “total points” represent the cumulative scores of all signature genes. The accuracy of the nomogram was evaluated using the AUC from the ROC curve as well as the calibration curve. Additionally, the clinical benefits of the nomogram were demonstrated via decision curve analysis (DCA) and the clinical impact curve (CIC).


### Risk stratification and correlation between immune cells and DSGs
  Based on the highest quartile of risk scores, the samples within the combined dataset were categorized into two groups: high-risk and low-risk. In order to investigate the characteristics of the immune microenvironment linked to risk stratification in SCZ, we executed an immune infiltration analysis through two complementary computational approaches: single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) and CIBERSORT deconvolution analysis. Acknowledging the fundamental differences in the classification granularity and terminology of immune cells between these two methodologies, we performed cross-method comparisons based on immune cell lineage and functional similarities (instead of direct matching of specific cell subtypes) to confirm the reliability of immune infiltration patterns and mitigate the risk of false positives. Next, to assess the variations in immune cell types among the different risk categories, the Wilcoxon rank sum test was employed. Additionally, Spearman’s correlation analysis was performed to investigate the relationships between the potential marker genes and immune cells. A p-value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.


### Clinical samples collection and quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)
  All protocols involving the use of human blood samples adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics Committee at the Medical School of Henan University, China (HUSOM-2018-282). Blood samples were obtained from fifteen patients diagnosed with SCZ and seventeen age-matched healthy individuals who had no reported history of psychiatric disorders or significant health issues, recruited from the Kaifeng Fifth People’s Hospital and the Huaihe Hospital affiliated with Henan University in China. All patients included in this study were recent-onset patients who satisfied the criteria set by the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition. No significant differences were found in terms of age, education, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, or sex between the SCZ patients and the healthy controls. Detailed patient characteristics can be found in Table S3. Informed consent was acquired from the legal guardians of the patients and the healthy participants before sample collection. To isolate RNA from peripheral blood, we modified the protocol of the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Cat No:74104). The RT-qPCR analysis was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo, California, USA) on the cDNA, in accordance with established guidelines [49, 50]. Gene expression quantification was carried out using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH serving as the internal control. Details of the primers utilized in the RT-qPCR assays are provided in Table S4.


### Construction of regulatory network and drug prediction
  The prediction of miRNA-mRNA interactions involving the DSGs was carried out utilizing the databases miRDB (http://www.mirdb.org/), miRanda (http://www.microrna.org/), and TargetScan (https://www.targetscan.org/vert_80/). A regulatory network of gene transcription factors (TFs) was developed with the help of the miRNet database (https://www.mirnet.ca/). Furthermore, to identify potential drugs aimed at the DSGs, we accessed the Drug-Gene Interaction Database (DGidb) (https://dgidb.genome.wustl.edu/), which consolidates drug-gene/protein interaction data from various sources including The Druggable Genome and the Therapeutic Targets Database [51]. All findings were visualized using Cytoscape software (version 3.7.2).


### Statistical analysis
  Analyses were performed utilizing the R programming language, and comparisons of data from various groups were made using the Wilcoxon test. Unless stated otherwise, a P-value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.


## Results

### Data processing and identification of DE-MIRGs
  The boxplot and PCA plot of GSE38484 and GSE54913 data sets (Fig. 1A, C) revealed significant differences in the sample distributions across the two datasets, suggesting the presence of a batch effect. Following the application of the empirical Bayesian method COMBAT to correct for this effect, the distribution of data among the datasets became more aligned. In particular, the median values for each dataset were predominantly aligned along a line (Fig. 1B), and the samples exhibited a clustered and interwoven arrangement (Fig. 1D), collectively indicating a successful correction for the batch effect.

  In comparison to the control cohort, the expression levels of 502 genes within the SCZ patient group exhibited significant alterations, with 275 genes being up-regulated and 227 down-regulated (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Table S5). Additionally, we employed a Venn diagram to identify and depict the differentially expressed DE-MIRGs by intersecting differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with MIRGs, resulting in the identification of 83 DE-MIRGs (Fig. 1F and Supplementary Table S6). To investigate the role of DE-MIRGs in both the onset and progression of SCZ, we analyzed the regulatory interactions among these DE-MIRGs and established a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network involving the 83 identified genes using the STRING database. This network visualization consisted of 257 edges and 72 nodes, emphasizing CD8A, CCL5, PTGS2, TNFRSF1A, CD40LG, CD27, PRF1, TFRC, ITGAL, and CSF1R as the ten key core genes (Fig. 1G). Subsequently, we performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) to investigate the functional implications of the 72 DE-MIRGs that contribute to the PPI network nodes in SCZ. The results indicated that the Gene Ontology (GO) biological processes (BP) were mainly enriched in leukocyte-mediated immunity, leukocyte cell-cell adhesion, and the regulation of immune effector processes. Significant cellular components (CC) associated with these processes included the external side of the plasma membrane, cytoplasmic vesicle lumen, and vesicle lumen, among others. The molecular function (MF) terms primarily encompassed cytokine binding, electron transfer activity, and oxidoreductase activity (Fig. 2A). KEGG pathway analysis indicated that the target genes were considerably involved in pathways associated with neurodegeneration, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, Alzheimer’s disease, and Parkinson’s disease (Fig. 2B). Notably, such enrichment in neurodegeneration-related pathways may reflect common underlying processes, such as inflammatory activation and mitochondrial dysfunction, rather than mechanisms that are specific to the diseases shared between schizophrenia and neurodegenerative disorders.

### Screening and integrative analysis of key peripheral diagnostic biomarkers
  Utilizing the combined dataset, 72 DE-MIRGs mentioned above were found to be significantly associated with the presence of SCZ through univariate regression analysis (Table S7). Following this, we employed four distinct machine learning algorithms to ascertain diagnostic biomarkers from the 72 DE-MIRGs. Through LASSO analysis, we identified 24 feature genes: NCKAP1L, CX3CR1, HDC, PTGER2, FUCA1, VCAN, HLA-A, CD8A, CD40LG, SNAP25, BLK, DGAT2, TFRC, IRAK3, CAMP, TMEM126A, ARG1, SRM, F5, CHUK, LYST, CTSG, TNFRSF1A, and S100A12 (Fig. 3A). A subsequent assessment utilizing the SVM-RFE algorithm revealed a collection of 26 feature genes from the DE-MIRGs (Fig. 3B). Moreover, XGboost and Random Forest (RF) analyses highlighted 25 genes (Fig. 3C) and 17 genes (Figs. 3D and E) that were considered diagnostically relevant. Ultimately, the integration of results from these four machine learning approaches resulted in the identification of six vital genes: HDC, NCKAP1L, FUCA1, CD40LG, CX3CR1, and SNAP25 (Fig. 3F). Figure 3G illustrates the distribution of these genes among the differentially expressed ones. Importantly, out of the six proposed diagnostic genes, FUCA1, SNAP25, and HDC have been linked to metabolic processes, while NCKAP1L, CX3CR1, and CD40LG are related to inflammatory pathways (Fig. 3H).

  Subsequently, we assessed the expression levels of diagnostic genes and their efficacy in differentiating between subjects with SCZ and the control group. The combined dataset served as the training set, while our cohort was utilized as the testing set. In both the training and testing datasets, we observed consistent and significant variances in the expression of HDC, NCKAP1L, FUCA1, CD40LG, CX3CR1, and SNAP25 between the SCZ cohort and controls (Figure S1A and S1B). Additionally, we plotted receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for the diagnostic genes, with the unbiased cross-validation area under the curve (AUC) for each gene illustrated in Figures S1C and S1D. Based on the data from both the training and testing sets, all feature genes exhibited AUC values exceeding 0.65, indicating that HDC, NCKAP1L, FUCA1, CD40LG, CX3CR1, and SNAP25 have the potential to serve as diagnostic markers for SCZ, as their AUCs ranged from 0.6 to 1.0.


### Development and evaluation of diagnostic model for SCZ
  We utilized potential diagnostic genes to develop a model aimed at gene-disease connections through the logistic regression approach. To exemplify this model, a nomogram was constructed based on the training cohort (merged dataset), which incorporated six DE-MIRGs (Fig. 4A). In this nomogram, the fractions of each gene corresponded with those depicted in the nomogram. The predictive elements for SCZ identified included HDC, NCKAP1L, FUCA1, CD40LG, CX3CR1, and SNAP25. Aside from FUCA1, lower concentrations of HDC, NCKAP1L, CD40LG, CX3CR1, and SNAP25 showed a positive association with the SCZ diagnosis. Subsequently, the AUC was employed to assess the discriminative strength of the nomogram model, revealing the highest predictive precision (AUC = 0.884, 95% CI:0.801–0.968) compared to the previously mentioned six DE-MIRGs (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Figure S1C). The calibration curve of the nomogram regarding SCZ diagnosis in the training cohort exhibited minimal discrepancy between the actual SCZ occurrence and the anticipated occurrence, signifying that the nomogram model offered a highly accurate prediction for SCZ (Fig. 4C). To evaluate the clinical applicability and significance of our model, we performed decision curve analysis (DCA) and clinical impact curve (CIC) evaluations. The DCA highlighted the prospective clinical importance of the nomogram model, indicating that patients categorized at high-risk thresholds, between 0.1 and 1.0, could significantly benefit from this model (Fig. 4D). Moreover, the CIC of the model within the training cohort indicated that the anticipated number of high-risk patients consistently exceeded the outcomes of SCZ when the risk threshold fell within the 0-0.4 range, with cost-benefit ratios remaining acceptable in that range (Fig. 4E). Simultaneously, the nomogram’s accuracy and real-world applicability reached optimal effectiveness, supported by the ROC curve (Fig. 4F), calibration curve (Fig. 4G), DCA curve (Fig. 4H), and CIC (Fig. 4I) in the testing cohort. Finally, to further assess the predictive accuracy of the MIRGs model, we conducted thorough comparisons with seven previously established diagnostic models for SCZ, which included gene signatures obtained from both peripheral blood and brain tissue samples [52–57]. Utilizing the corresponding genes from these seven models, we calculated the risk score for each patient by applying the same methodology used in the training cohort. All comparisons were executed using the DeLong test, recognized as the gold-standard statistical method for evaluating C-index across different diagnostic models. Our findings revealed that the MIRGs signature attained a considerably higher C-index compared to all seven established models (P < 0.001) (Fig. 4J). These results suggest that our machine learning-based gene signature related to metabolism and inflammation possesses strong diagnostic capabilities for SCZ. Nonetheless, the identified model signature might serve as promising indicators for peripheral diagnosis, and further investigation is needed to explore their potential links to brain pathology in future studies that involve paired samples from both brain tissue and blood.

### Risk stratification with differential immune characteristics in SCZ based on diagnostic model
  The samples within the combined dataset were categorized into high-risk and low-risk groups according to the upper quartile threshold of 4.399. This threshold was selected to define a clinically meaningful high-risk subgroup while ensuring adequate sample size for comparative analysis. Following this, an analysis of immune infiltration was conducted to assess immune regulation across the different risk categories. The ssGSEA results revealed distinct immune infiltration patterns between high-risk and low-risk SCZ patients (Fig. 5A). Specifically, patients in the high-risk category exhibited significantly elevated enrichment levels of T cell subsets characterized by effector and activated phenotypes, including activated CD4 T cells, activated CD8 T cells, gamma delta (γδ) T cells, Th17 helper cells, effector memory CD4 T cells, and effector memory CD8 T cells. In contrast, the low-risk SCZ group demonstrated higher enrichment levels of innate immune cells and immune suppressor cells, such as activated dendritic cells (DCs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), monocytes, and neutrophils. A heatmap illustrating the differential expression of immune cells was generated using the ‘pheatmap’ package in R (Fig. 5B). To confirm the reliability of the ssGSEA findings, we further performed immune cell deconvolution using the CIBERSORT algorithm with the LM22 signature gene set, which focuses on 22 peripheral blood immune cell subtypes. Consistent with the ssGSEA results, the CIBERSORT analysis revealed significant differences in immune cell infiltration between the two risk groups (Figure S2A). High-risk SCZ patients exhibited significantly elevated levels of resting memory CD4 T cells and activated natural killer (NK) cells, while showing reduced levels of regulatory T cells (Tregs), resting NK cells, and neutrophils. No significant differences in MDSCs and monocytes were detected by CIBERSORT, which may be attributed to the limited representation of these cell types within the LM22 gene set.

  To address the inconsistencies in cell classification between the two methods, we consolidated immune cells from both analyses into five primary functional lineages: T cell lineage, dendritic cells (DCs), innate immune cells, natural killer (NK) cells, and immune regulatory cells, facilitating cross-method trend comparisons (Table S8). Across the two approaches, three main consistent trends emerged that indicate the essential immune characteristics associated with SCZ risk stratification. (1) T cell lineage: Both analytical methods indicated that the high-risk group exhibits abnormal activation of the adaptive immune response mediated by T cells. The ssGSEA analysis revealed an enrichment of effector/activated T cell subsets in the high-risk population, whereas CIBERSORT identified an increase in resting memory CD4 T cells (which are precursors to effector T cells) and a decrease in regulatory T cells (Tregs) within the high-risk cohort. Collectively, these findings support the notion that patients at high risk for SCZ experience excessive activation of T cell-mediated adaptive immunity, alongside a compromised immune regulatory function characterized by Treg depletion. (2) Neutrophils: This represented the most consistent observation across both methodologies. The ssGSEA approach indicated elevated neutrophil levels in the low-risk group (and consequently a decrease in the high-risk demographic), while CIBERSORT directly identified a reduction in neutrophil presence in those considered high-risk. This alignment suggests that diminished neutrophil infiltration is associated with increased disease risk in SCZ, which may reflect potential alterations in innate immune defense mechanisms among high-risk individuals. (3) Immune regulatory cells (Tregs): Although Tregs were not directly identified through ssGSEA, the substantial presence of effector/activated T cells (e.g., Th17 cells) in the high-risk group indirectly implies a reduction in Treg-mediated immune regulatory functions. This interpretation was confirmed through CIBERSORT, which indicated a lower presence of Treg cells in the high-risk population. Collectively, these findings support the idea that the reduction of Tregs is a significant characteristic of immune dysregulation in patients with high-risk schizophrenia and may be a pivotal factor contributing to the heightened activation of effector T cells. Nevertheless, differing patterns were noted for certain cell types, including activated dendritic cells, natural killer (NK) cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and monocytes, which can be explained by the fundamental differences in the algorithmic approaches and gene set compositions employed by the two techniques, rather than conflicting outcomes. Further experimental validation of this inference is necessary in future studies.

  Ultimately, following the findings from the ssGSEA analysis, a Pearson correlation analysis was performed to explore the relationships among six diagnostic biomarkers and various immune cell types (Fig. 5C). Importantly, HDC showed a strong positive correlation with neutrophils, MDSC, and activated dendritic cells, whereas it exhibited a negative correlation with activated CD8 T cells. Furthermore, NCKAP1L revealed a significant positive correlation with monocytes and MDSC, and CD40LG indicated a notable negative correlation with gamma delta T cells (Fig. 5D-J). In relation to the CIBORSERT analysis results, it was noted that HDC, NCKAP1L, and CX3CR1 displayed a significant negative correlation with resting memory CD4 T cells; NCKAP1L, CD40LG, and CX3CR1 exhibited a significant positive correlation with regulatory T cells; additionally, CX3CR1 was positively correlated with resting NK cells (Figure S2B-I).


### Gene-miRNA, gene-TF, and gene-potential therapeutic drug network
  The networks illustrating interactions among genes, miRNAs, transcription factors (TFs), and drugs were developed through network analysis. Six candidate gene-miRNA networks were established, revealing that hsa-miR-515-5p has the capability to regulate the expression levels of both CD40LG and SNAP25; hsa-miR-363-5p influences the expression of CD40LG and FUCA1; hsa-miR-214-5p affects the expression of NCKAP1L and SNAP25; and hsa-miR-194-3p can control the expression of CD40LG as well as NCKAP1L concurrently (Fig. 6A). In the gene-TF interaction network, a total of 11 transcription factors were identified (Fig. 6B). Notably, CD40LG was found to be targeted by JUND, RELA, STAT1, NEKB1, GATA3, FOS, and REL, while the expression of HDC was influenced by HDAC7, KLF4, and KAT5, with SP1 identified as a regulator of SNAP25. Next, Utilizing the DGIdb database (https://dgidb.org/), a platform for mining association-based drug-gene interactions, we constructed a gene-drug interaction network and identified 39 potential agents linked to our SCZ marker genes (Fig. 6C and Table S9). Specifically, 29 drugs (e.g., dapirolizumab pegol, ruplizumab) were associated with CD40LG, 6 agents (e.g., trenibotulinumtoxin, prabotulinumtoxin A) with SNAP25, 4 drugs (e.g., FMH, AMA) with HDC, and 1 drug (CHEMBL5206259) with CX3CR1. It is important to note that botulinum toxins were included only as gene-drug association hits and are not biologically plausible for systemic treatment of SCZ. Based on interaction scores, we retrieved the molecular structures of the top 10 candidate compounds via the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), obtaining valid structures for 5 compounds (Supplementary Figure S3A). Molecular docking, a structure-based approach for preliminary drug screening [58, 59], was performed to predict the binding affinities between the 5 candidate inhibitors (AMA, FMH, CHEMBL5206259, lixivaptan, therapeutic steroid) and their corresponding target proteins (HDC, CX3CR1, SNAP25). High-resolution crystal structures of these candidate genes (resolution < 3 Å) were acquired from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/). The calculated binding energies were as follows: AMA-FMH (HDC) at -6.0 and − 4.6 kcal/mol, respectively (Figs. 6D and E); lixivaptan (HDC) at -8.9 kcal/mol (Supplementary Figure S3B); CHEMBL5206259 (CX3CR1) at -9.7 kcal/mol (Supplementary Figure S3C); and therapeutic steroid (SNAP25) at -10.5 kcal/mol (Supplementary Figure S3D). According to the standard criteria (binding energy < -5.0 kcal/mol for considerable binding; < -7.0 kcal/mol for strong binding) [53], AMA, lixivaptan, CHEMBL5206259, and the therapeutic steroid exhibited favorable in silico binding affinities with their respective predicted targets.

## Discussion
  Schizophrenia (SCZ) comprises a group of profoundly severe neurodevelopmental disorders that create significant economic and social challenges globally [60]. It is widely acknowledged that timely and precise recognition and diagnosis are crucial for enhancing the clinical management of this condition [61]. Nevertheless, no serum biomarkers for SCZ have been discovered thus far, and the molecular foundations of SCZ are still inadequately understood, underscoring the necessity of investigating and validating blood-based peripheral indicators to enable accurate diagnosis and guide targeted treatment approaches. This study identifies diagnostic biomarkers associated with metabolism and inflammation in SCZ through public data mining, machine learning, and clinical sample validation. Compared to existing transcriptomic studies, our work presents an integrated framework that not only reveals co-regulated metabolic-inflammation signatures but also facilitates diagnostic risk stratification and preliminary drug screening. This approach provides a more systematic and translational strategy for SCZ biomarker research.

  Metabolism and inflammation serve as fundamental regulators of immune homeostasis, with dysregulation of this interconnected network increasingly acknowledged as a crucial factor in the pathophysiology of SCZ [33, 62]. In the current research, we acquired two distinct gene expression datasets from peripheral blood (GSE38484 and GSE54913). Utilizing differential expression gene (DEG) analysis alongside KEGG and GO enrichment evaluations, we pinpointed 83 differentially expressed metabolism and inflammation-related genes (DE-MIRGs), which were largely concentrated in immune regulatory functions (including leukocyte-mediated immunity, leukocyte cell−cell adhesion, immune effector process regulation, leukocyte proliferation, and T cell activation regulation) as well as pathways associated with neurodegeneration. These results correspond with the increasing evidence that a dysregulated metabolism-inflammation-immunity axis plays a role in the pathogenesis of SCZ [63]. Following this, we applied four complementary machine learning techniques—Random Forest, XGBoost, LASSO, and SVM-RFE—to identify reliable diagnostic biomarkers, successfully discovering six signature genes (HDC, NCKAP1L, FUCA1, CD40LG, CX3CR1, and SNAP25). A diagnostic nomogram model, developed through logistic regression analysis based on these genes, demonstrated promising diagnostic performance for SCZ, with AUC values between 0.754 and 1.00. Importantly, risk stratification analysis using this nomogram highlighted that high- and low-risk SCZ subgroups exhibited significant differences in the infiltration levels of various immune cell types, including activated CD4 T cells, activated CD8 T cells, γδ T cells, Th17 helper cells, effector memory CD4 T cells, MDSCs, monocytes, and neutrophils. Our findings support that dysfunction in the immune system, closely linked to both metabolic and inflammatory issues, shows a significant correlation with SCZ, and additionally reinforces the clinical importance of the signature genes we have identified. As far as we know, this study is one of the initial efforts to thoroughly analyze the relationships between genes related to metabolism and inflammation and SCZ, considering both transcriptional and immune cellular viewpoints. This approach offers new perspectives on the peripheral molecular characteristics of this intricate neuropsychiatric condition.

  In this study, the SCZ diagnostic model was developed utilizing six diagnostic signature genes linked to metabolism and inflammation, which could prove beneficial in clinical settings. Histidine decarboxylase (HDC) serves as the sole enzyme that facilitates the transformation of L-histidine into histamine [64], and it plays a significant role in regulating histamine synthesis, thereby connecting it to inflammation and neurological disorders. In the context of inflammation, HDC influences immune responses by aiding in the differentiation of myeloid cells, as shown by its elevated expression in CD11b+Ly6G+ immature myeloid cells (IMCs) that exhibit characteristics similar to MDSCs. Mice lacking HDC (HDC-/-) demonstrate heightened inflammatory responses in colitis models, which is attributed to the dysfunctional maturation of CD11b+Ly6G+ immature myeloid cells [65]. Regarding neurological conditions, the impairment of HDC is linked to neuropsychiatric symptoms such as Tourette syndrome, due to its impact on histaminergic neurotransmission, which is essential for maintaining homeostasis in the central nervous system [66]. Moreover, histamine produced by HDC can influence the phenotypes of microglia and astrocytes, helping to alleviate chronic neuroinflammation associated with neurodegenerative disorders [67, 68]. NCKAP1L, alternatively called hematopoietic protein-1 (HEM-1), has been implicated in various biological processes and disease contexts. Castro et al. provided foundational insights by identifying that defects in NCKAP1L lead to a novel syndrome characterized by immunodeficiency, lymphoproliferation, and hyperinflammation, highlighting its critical role in immune regulation [69]. This underscores the importance of NCKAP1L in maintaining immune homeostasis and preventing hyperinflammatory states. Further investigations into the functional role of NCKAP1L in immune cells revealed its significance in macrophage development. Suwankitwat et al. demonstrated that Hem-1 is essential for alveolar macrophage development. The study showed that deficiency in Hem-1 resulted in impaired macrophage differentiation, leading to pulmonary alveolar proteinosis due to the accumulation of surfactant and cell debris in the airways [70]. This suggests that NCKAP1L-related pathways are vital for proper macrophage function and lung immunity. The FUCA1 gene is responsible for producing the enzyme α-L-fucosidase, which is crucial for the metabolism of glycoconjugates that contain fucose. Its significance is emphasized by its involvement in numerous biological processes and disease conditions. As reported by Willems et al., a thorough analysis of the mutational spectrum of FUCA1 has been conducted through detailed mutation assessments on an exon-by-exon basis, identifying mutations across nearly all cases studied concerning fucosidosis, a type of lysosomal storage disorder [71]. This underscores the vital role of FUCA1 in maintaining normal cellular metabolism and the potential pathological outcomes resulting from its impairment. Although some research has investigated gene expression patterns in diverse contexts, such as cancer and immune responses, the specific function of FUCA1 within these scenarios has not been extensively addressed in the discussed literature. Ongoing investigations into the roles and regulatory mechanisms of FUCA1 are crucial for a deeper comprehension of its impact on health and disease.

  The CD40LG gene is situated within the q26.3 segment of the X chromosome and is responsible for encoding the CD40 ligand (CD40L, also known as CD154) (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gene/959). CD40L predominantly appears on the surface of activated CD4 T cells and interacts with its receptor, CD40, which is primarily found on B cells, dendritic cells, macrophages, and endothelial cells. This interaction is vital for initiating immune responses [72, 73]. Specifically, the CD40-CD40L interaction is essential for the activation of B cells, class switching of antibodies, the formation of germinal centers, and T cell-dependent immune responses [73, 74]. Recently, the significance of the CD40LG gene in the context of autoimmune and inflammatory diseases has garnered considerable interest. Irregular expression levels of CD40L have been closely associated with the onset and progression of several autoimmune conditions, including systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, and multiple sclerosis [75–77]. Notably, these conditions exhibit shared immunopathological traits with SCZ, suggesting that CD40LG may contribute to immune-related processes associated with SCZ. In recent years, the chemokine receptor CX3CR1 has been the focus of considerable research due to its varied functions in the central nervous system as well as in peripheral immune responses. In their early studies, Harrison et al. examined the relationship between fractalkine (FKN) produced by neurons and CX3CR1, which emphasized the pathways of cellular communication that occur between neurons and microglia in both healthy and disease states [78]. In models of neurodegenerative diseases, the function of CX3CR1 seems to be intricate and influenced by the surrounding context. Cardona et al. found that the absence of CX3CR1 leads to disrupted microglial activity, contributing to heightened neurotoxicity in models of Parkinson’s disease and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, where a more significant loss of neurons was noted in knockout mice [79]. In contrast, research by Lee et al. indicated that the lack of CX3CR1 resulted in diminished microglial activation and reduced β-amyloid accumulation in models of Alzheimer’s disease, implying a potential protective role against amyloid-related pathology [80]. Synaptosome-associated protein 25 (SNAP25) is a key soluble element of the N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive factor attachment protein receptor complex, crucially influencing the regulation of various vital cellular functions. These functions encompass neurotransmitter release, synaptic communication, extravasation of secretory vesicles, intercellular signaling, and the opening of ion channels [81–83]. Recent studies have increasingly highlighted a link between modifications in SNAP25 and SCZ. Interestingly, Ramos-Miguel et al. noted diminished fragmentation of SNAP25 protein in the postmortem brains of SCZ patients, which could lead to dysregulation in the SNARE complex and heightened SNARE activity, possibly impacting synaptic transmission [84]. Moreover, Ramos-Miguel et al. documented an increase in SNARE protein interactions, including those with SNAP25, in certain brain regions of individuals with SCZ, implying changes in synaptic machinery that may contribute to neurochemical abnormalities [85]. Nonetheless, we acknowledge that analyzing the six-gene signature from a biological standpoint requires a meticulously balanced consideration of both disease-specific and non-specific mechanisms. Although HDC, NCKAP1L, FUCA1, CD40LG, CX3CR1, and SNAP25 have been individually associated with neuroimmune regulation, synaptic activity, and inflammatory processes relevant to the onset of schizophrenia, we cannot overlook the possibility that variations in expression could signify downstream immune activation, medication effects, or non-specific stress-related or inflammatory states, rather than mechanisms that are distinctively linked to schizophrenia. It remains plausible that the signature identified represents a wide-ranging immune or stress-related phenotype that is commonly observed across numerous psychiatric and neuroinflammatory disorders. However, the robust diagnostic potential of the six-gene model points to its possible significance in clinical settings, even if it does not exclusively embody disease-specific causal pathways. Additional research involving groups of unmedicated patients and longitudinal approaches will be crucial to separate disease-specific influences from the effects of medication, generalized immune activation, and non-specific stress responses.

  Additionally, investigations concentrating on immune infiltration have suggested a link between immunological dysfunction and SCZ [86]. This research comprehensively examined the immune cell lineage in the peripheral blood of individuals with SCZ, uncovering notable variations in immune cell subsets across patients classified into different risk categories. This provides novel clues toward understanding immune-related processes associated with SCZ. The findings demonstrated that patients at high risk displayed significant characteristics of adaptive immune cell activation, such as markedly increased levels of activated CD4 T cells, activated CD8 T cells, gamma delta T cells, Th17 helper cells, and effector memory T cells. This finding aligns closely with recent investigations into the immune activation hypothesis in SCZ [87], thus reinforcing the essential role of immune system dysfunction in the disease’s pathogenesis. Activated CD4 T cells act as primary regulators of immune responses, and their over-activation could result in autoimmune reactions that subsequently attack normal structures within the nervous system. Prior research has indicated that individuals with SCZ possess autoantibodies targeting neuroantigens, which may recognize proteins of the nervous system via molecular mimicry mechanisms and instigate autoimmune neuroinflammation [88, 89]. The rise in activated CD8 T cells points to the involvement of cytotoxic immune responses in the pathological development of SCZ, potentially linked to neuronal injury and synaptic impairment. CD8 T cells can trigger apoptosis in target cells through either the perforin-granzyme pathway or the Fas-FasL pathway, and may exert detrimental effects on neurons and oligodendrocytes in neuroinflammatory contexts. Notably, there is a considerable rise in Th17 cells, which holds significant pathophysiological implications. Th17 cells, a type of pro-inflammatory T lymphocyte, primarily produce pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-17, IL-21, and IL-22. Numerous investigations have indicated that cytokines, particularly IL-17, can traverse the blood-brain barrier, interacting directly with the central nervous system and fostering neuroinflammatory responses, thereby impacting neuronal functions and synaptic plasticity [90, 91]. In stark contrast to the high-risk cohort, individuals in the low-risk cohort exhibited markedly heightened levels of activated dendritic cells, MDSCs, monocytes, and neutrophils. This finding uncovers an alternative pattern of immune response in individuals with SCZ, which is marked by the activation of innate immune cells. Dendritic cells, regarded as the most potent antigen-presenting cells, indicate an improved capacity of the body to identify and process both exogenous and endogenous antigens when activated. MDSCs represent a diverse group of cells with significant immunosuppressive capabilities, fulfilling crucial regulatory functions in various autoimmune conditions and chronic inflammatory circumstances [92]. In patients with SCZ, an increase in MDSCs may signify the body’s effort to counterbalance excessive immune activation via immunosuppressive pathways, albeit this regulatory system may be compromised or insufficient in its efficacy. The rise in monocytes and neutrophils further substantiates the notion of a systemic inflammatory condition in individuals with SCZ. Monocytes function as circulating precursors that can transform into tissue macrophages and dendritic cells, playing essential roles in the inflammatory process. As a critical first line of defense in innate immunity, an increase in both the quantity and activity of neutrophils is generally correlated with acute inflammatory reactions. Nonetheless, in chronic conditions like SCZ, a persistent rise in neutrophils may indicate ongoing low-grade inflammation. Such systemic inflammatory changes may be associated with altered central nervous system function through several potential pathways: inflammatory cytokines may affect blood-brain barrier permeability; inflammatory mediators may modulate the synthesis, release, and reuptake of neurotransmitters; and chronic inflammation has been proposed to contribute to neuronal injury and synaptic dysfunction via oxidative stress [93–95].

  Next, we further observed a significant positive association between the expression of the signature gene HDC and the presence of neutrophils, MDSCs, and activated dendritic cells. Conversely, there was a negative association with activated CD8 T cells. This finding offers valuable insights into the function of specific genes in the immune regulation of SCZ. The HDC gene encodes for histidine decarboxylase, a crucial enzyme involved in the synthesis of histamine. Histamine, which acts as a vital inflammatory mediator and neurotransmitter, is involved in various functions within immune regulation and the nervous system [96]. The observed positive association of HDC with neutrophils and MDSCs may indicate the influence of the histamine system on innate immune responses, whereas the negative correlation with activated CD8 T cells implies that histamine might exert an inhibitory effect on cytotoxic T cell activities. The NCKAP1L gene encodes a protein that is involved in the reorganization of the actin cytoskeleton and facilitates cell migration, thus playing a crucial role in the chemotaxis and activation of immune cells [69, 97]. The observed positive association between NCKAP1L and monocytes, as well as MDSCs, may indicate the gene’s essential function in modulating the behavior and movement of myeloid cells. In individuals suffering from SCZ, the expression levels of NCKAP1L could influence the migration and differentiation of monocytes towards the central nervous system, contributing to the establishment and persistence of neuroinflammatory responses. CD40LG is responsible for encoding CD40 ligand, a significant co-stimulatory molecule present on T cell membranes, which plays a role in T cell activation, differentiation, and regulation of their functions. The identified negative relationship between CD40LG and gamma delta T cells might signify the inhibitory impact of the CD40-CD40L signaling pathway on gamma delta T cell activity. This finding is highly relevant, as various studies have confirmed an abnormal elevation of gamma delta T cells in SCZ; thus, the expression dynamics of CD40LG may provide clues for understanding the molecular processes associated with this alteration. Of course, we also acknowledge that while the correlations between particular immune cell subsets and the risk score indicate the involvement of immune-related mechanisms in the pathophysiology of schizophrenia, these results should be viewed as correlative instead of indicative of causation. The existing evidence does not suffice to confirm definitive causal relationships between specific immune cell populations and the mechanisms of the disease. It is impossible to draw solid conclusions about directional causality, as the noted immune variations may be secondary outcomes of disease processes, effects of medications, or general states related to inflammation or stress, rather than primary factors that lead to pathogenicity. Additional experimental and longitudinal research will be required to elucidate the exact functions of these immune subsets and their causal links to the development of schizophrenia.

  From a clinical standpoint, our results hold notable practical implications. First, variations in immune cell lineages could function as biomarkers for evaluating risk and prognostic assessment in individuals diagnosed with SCZ. The characteristics of adaptive immune cell activation seen in high-risk individuals might indicate more aggressive disease processes and reduced treatment efficacy, whereas the innate immune response profiles observed in low-risk individuals may signify a comparatively better prognosis. Second, these immune markers could inform the choice of personalized treatment approaches. For instance, therapies aimed at the IL-17 signaling pathway might be beneficial for patients with significantly increased Th17 cell levels; similarly, strategies that modulate myeloid cell function may be more suitable for individuals with heightened MDSC levels. Lastly, these results establish a conceptual foundation for the creation of new immunomodulatory treatments. By concentrating on specific subsets of immune cells or associated signaling pathways, it may become feasible to more effectively manage SCZ symptoms and enhance patient outcomes. However, it is essential to recognize the fundamental limitations associated with the present study. The dependence on publicly available datasets inherently imposes certain constraints: (1) these datasets may not entirely encapsulate the significant heterogeneity present among SCZ patients globally, encompassing differences in clinical subtypes, treatment strategies, and genetic backgrounds, which might restrict the widespread applicability of our findings; (2) The utilized public GEO transcriptome datasets (GSE38484 and GSE54913), in conjunction with our in-house RT-qPCR validation cohort comprising 15 schizophrenia (SCZ) cases and 17 controls, lack adequate clinical data necessary for stratifying SCZ samples based on medication status, disease stage, and symptom severity. There is a lack of thorough experimental validation in this study to accurately assess the specificity and sensitivity of the identified MIRG-based biomarkers for SCZ diagnosis; such validation is vital for converting these bioinformatic observations into tools relevant in clinical practice; No comparisons with existing clinical or molecular biomarkers were conducted, leaving the clinical utility of the identified biomarkers ambiguous. (3) The requirement for datasets to include at least 200 differentially expressed genes was implemented to ensure an adequate number of genes for subsequent pathway analysis, model construction, and risk stratification. This criterion was essential due to the subtle expression changes observed in the peripheral blood of schizophrenia patients; however, it may also introduce selection bias toward datasets exhibiting more pronounced case–control differences, potentially limiting the generalizability of the identified biomarkers. Additionally, clinical heterogeneity, such as variations in illness stage, medication status, and comorbidities across datasets, may have impacted peripheral gene expression profiles, which should be taken into account when interpreting the results.

  This study is exploratory in nature, focusing on the identification of potential diagnostic biomarkers for schizophrenia (SCZ) and candidate targeting compounds. The RT-qPCR validation was designed as a preliminary verification to confirm expression patterns rather than to assess clinical utility. The suggested diagnostic model based on MIRGs relies solely on in silico analyses, which cannot replace the need for empirical data obtained from laboratory experiments. In the future, subsequent investigations should tackle these issues by: (1) increasing the sample size and integrating multi-center, ethnically diverse groups to confirm the reliability of the model across independent cohorts; (2) conducting functional studies both in vitro (e.g., utilizing cell lines, primary immune cells) and in vivo (e.g., employing animal models of SCZ) to clarify the specific molecular pathways through which these specific MIRGs influence immune-inflammatory processes in SCZ; and (3) incorporating clinical metadata to investigate the links between MIRG expression patterns and patient outcomes (e.g., response to treatment, progression of disease), as well as performing stratification analyses and comparisons with existing biomarkers to comprehensively evaluate clinical utility.


## Conclusion
  This study presented a new signature of metabolism and inflammation-related genes (MIRGS), comprising HDC, NCKAP1L, FUCA1, CD40LG, CX3CR1, and SNAP25. The objective was to enhance early diagnosis and risk evaluation of SCZ through an extensive bioinformatics analysis. The MIRGS demonstrated considerable diagnostic capabilities for SCZ and could function as a crucial biomarker for its detection.
