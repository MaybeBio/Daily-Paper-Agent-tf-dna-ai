# Sororin locks the DNA-exit gate of cohesin to preserve sister-chromatid cohesion


## Abstract
  The cohesin complex mediates sister-chromatid cohesion by topologically entrapping DNA within an SMC1-SMC3-RAD21 ring, yet how Sororin preserves cohesion beyond its known role of antagonizing Pds5 binding to the release factor Wapl has remained unclear. Here, we show that the extreme C-terminal region (CTR) of Sororin functions as a direct structural lock for cohesin’s DNA-exit gate by engaging the RAD21-SMC3 interface. Centromere-tethered Sororin-CTR fully restores cohesion after Sororin depletion, whereas constitutive chromatin tethering prevents cohesin removal, recapitulating Wapl-loss phenotypes, including impaired mitotic chromosome condensation, decatenation and segregation. Through biochemical reconstitution, AlphaFold3-guided modeling, and targeted mutagenesis, we define conserved hydrophobic and electrostatic contacts between Sororin-CTR and the RAD21-SMC3 gate, the disruption of which abolishes cohesion in a Wapl-dependent manner. Furthermore, mitotic phosphorylation of Sororin selectively disrupts Pds5 binding while leaving gate engagement intact, providing a regulated molecular switch for cohesin release. Together, these findings redefine Sororin as a dual-function regulator that both antagonizes Wapl-Pds5 and directly locks the RAD21-SMC3 exit gate to stabilize sister-chromatid cohesion while permitting its timely dissolution.


## Introduction
  Accurate chromosome segregation is fundamental to genomic stability and cellular viability1. This process relies on the cohesin complex, a conserved ring-shaped molecular machine that topologically entraps DNA to perform two essential functions: mediating sister-chromatid cohesion in trans and organizing chromatin architecture through DNA loop extrusion in cis2. Cohesive cohesin maintains the physical connection between sister chromatids from S‑phase until anaphase to ensure their faithful separation3–6, whereas extrusive cohesin shapes the three‑dimensional genome organization7–12. Given these central roles, dysregulation of cohesin contributes to developmental disorders and tumorigenesis, underscoring the need for a detailed mechanistic understanding of cohesin regulation13–17.

  Structurally, the cohesin ring is formed by two SMC proteins, SMC1 and SMC3, bridged by the kleisin subunit RAD21 (also called Scc1/Mcd1); RAD21 further associates with Pds5 and SA1/SA2 to recruit regulatory factors (Fig. 1A)18. SMC1 and SMC3 dimerize at their hinge domains, while their ATPase head domains are connected by RAD21 at the opposite ends19. DNA is thought to enter the ring through the SMC1‑SMC3 hinge and exit via a transiently opened interface between RAD21 and SMC320–23.   During S‑phase, acetylation of SMC3 by Esco1 and Esco2 promotes recruitment of Sororin, which stabilizes cohesion primarily by competing with Wapl for binding to Pds526,27,35–45. In early mitosis, phosphorylation of Sororin disrupts its association with Pds5, allowing Wapl to rebind and trigger cohesin release from chromosome arms24–27,46–50. In contrast, centromeric cohesin is protected by Sgo1, which associates with PP2A to counteract Sororin phosphorylation, until Separase-mediated cleavage of RAD21 initiates anaphase51–56.

  Despite its essential role in cohesion maintenance27,36,44,57,58, Sororin’s mechanistic contribution beyond Wapl displacement remains unresolved26,27,37,49,54,59–63. A central unanswered question is whether Sororin stabilizes cohesion solely through indirect competition with Wapl for Pds5 binding or whether it also directly engages cohesin to reinforce closure of the DNA-exit gate58,64. Although the C-terminal region of Sororin has been implicated in cohesion and proposed to interact with the SA2 subunit65,66, the molecular basis and functional relevance of this interaction remain unclear.

  Here, we show that the extreme C-terminal region (CTR) of Sororin directly binds the RAD21-SMC3 interface, functioning as a molecular lock for cohesin’s DNA-exit gate. Through integrated cell-biological, biochemical, and structural analyses, we demonstrate that this gate-locking activity is both necessary and sufficient for cohesion maintenance, operates independently of Pds5 competition, and is tightly regulated during the cell cycle. Our findings redefine Sororin as a dual-function regulator that both antagonizes Wapl binding to Pds5 and directly stabilizes cohesin ring closure to ensure faithful chromosome segregation.


## Results

### The extreme C‑terminus of Sororin is necessary and sufficient to promote cohesion
  To define the minimal Sororin domain required for sister-chromatid cohesion, we generated GFP‑tagged Sororin truncation mutants (Fig. 1B), and assessed their ability to promote cohesion in mitotic HeLa cells arrested with the spindle destabilizer nocodazole (Fig. 1C–E). Chromosome spreads from control cells displayed the characteristic X‑shaped morphology, reflecting normal chromosome-arm resolution. As expected, expression of full‑length Sororin‑GFP, but not H2B‑GFP, markedly impaired arm resolution, consistent with Sororin’s established role. Strikingly, the C‑terminal fragments Sororin (150-252)‑GFP and Sororin (223–252)‑GFP also strongly inhibited arm resolution, whereas the N‑terminal fragments Sororin (1-149)‑GFP and Sororin (1-222)‑GFP did not.

  Consistent with previous reports27,44,67, all Sororin‑GFP variants localized to interphase nuclei but were largely excluded from mitotic chromosomes (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Fig. 1). To exclude the possibility that the inactivity of the N-terminal fragments resulted from defective chromatin association, we forcibly tethered them to chromatin by fusion to histone H2B (Fig. 1F–H). While H2B‑Sororin‑GFP potently blocked arm resolution, H2B‑Sororin (1-149)‑GFP and H2B‑Sororin (1-222)‑GFP remained ineffective.

  Together, these results demonstrate that the extreme C‑terminal region (residues 223–252), hereafter termed the CTR, is both necessary and sufficient for Sororin to promote sister-chromatid cohesion.


### Centromere-tethered Sororin-CTR bypasses the requirement for Sororin but not Esco1/2 in cohesion protection
  Given that Sororin accumulates at mitotic centromeres27,54,59,62, we asked whether targeted localization of the CTR to centromeres could functionally replace full-length Sororin. To this end, we generated HeLa cell lines stably expressing either Sororin-CTR-GFP fused to the centromere-targeting domain of CENP-B (CB-Sororin-CTR-GFP) or a CB-GFP control (Fig. 2A), then depleted endogenous Sororin by siRNA and examined its effect on mitotic index (Fig. 2B–D). As expected, Sororin knockdown in parental HeLa cells induced profound mitotic arrest, increasing the mitotic index from 4.5% to 39.8%, consistent with spindle-assembly-checkpoint activation following cohesion loss44. Comparable mitotic arrest was observed upon Sororin depletion in CB-GFP-expressing cells. In contrast, Sororin depletion had little effect on the mitotic index of CB-Sororin-CTR-GFP cells, indicating that centromeric Sororin-CTR effectively rescued this defect. Consistent with this, chromosome-spread analysis revealed severe cohesion loss in ~85% of Sororin-depleted parental or CB-GFP cells, compared with only 5.9% of CB-Sororin-CTR-GFP cells (Fig. 2E, F).   We next examined whether centromeric Sororin-CTR could compensate for the loss of Sgo1, which promotes Sororin-dependent cohesion protection at mitotic centromeres54,62. Depletion of Sgo1 caused cohesion loss in 84% of CB-GFP cells, a phenotype comparable to Sororin knockdown (Supplementary Fig. 2A–C). This defect was partially but significantly rescued in CB-Sororin-CTR-GFP cells, where only 32.9% showed cohesion loss. This partial rescue suggests that while centromeric Sororin-CTR can mitigate Sgo1 depletion, it cannot fully substitute for Sgo1, which also protects cohesin through PP2A-mediated dephosphorylation54.

  During S phase, Esco1 and Esco2 acetylate two conserved lysine residues (K105 and K106) in the SMC3 ATPase head, thereby promoting Sororin recruitment27,43,68–70. We therefore asked if these two acetyltransferases remain essential when Sororin-CTR is constitutively localized to centromeres. Co-depletion of Esco1/2 by siRNA induced similar levels of cohesion loss in CB-GFP and CB-Sororin-CTR-GFP cells (~23.3% and ~20.5%, respectively) (Fig. 2G–J). Thus, enforced centromeric localization of Sororin-CTR cannot bypass the requirement for Esco1/2.

  In summary, constitutive centromeric targeting of Sororin-CTR is sufficient to replace full-length Sororin, but not Sgo1 or Esco1/2, in protecting sister-chromatid cohesion


### Chromatin-tethered Sororin‑CTR impairs mitotic chromosome condensation and decatenation
  The cohesin release factor Wapl regulates cohesin’s dynamic association with chromatin25,47. Depletion of Wapl prevents cohesin release from mitotic chromosomes, leading to defective sister-chromatid resolution, impaired chromosome condensation, and segregation errors48,50. Because Sororin antagonizes Wapl, we asked whether constitutive retention of Sororin-protected cohesin on chromatin could phenocopy Wapl depletion.

  To address this, we generated HeLa cell lines stably expressing H2B-Sororin-CTR-GFP, which tethers the CTR constitutively to chromatin, or an H2B-GFP control (Fig. 3A). In these cells, we first examined the release of cohesin from chromosome arms in nocodazole-arrested mitotic cells. Following endogenous Sororin depletion, the cohesin subunit SMC1 remained largely chromosome-associated in H2B-Sororin‑CTR-GFP cells, but was efficiently removed in H2B-GFP cells (Fig. 3B), confirming that chromatin-tethered Sororin-CTR blocks cohesin release. Consistently, expression of H2B-Sororin-CTR-GFP but not H2B-GFP potently inhibited chromosome arm resolution (Fig. 3C).   We next assessed the requirement for Sgo1 and Esco1/2 under these conditions. Sgo1 knockdown caused severe cohesion loss in H2B-GFP cells, but this defect was largely suppressed in H2B-Sororin-CTR-GFP cells (Supplementary Fig. 3A, B). This indicates that chromatin-tethered Sororin-CTR can largely bypass the requirement for Sgo1, supporting the model that Sgo1 protects cohesin both at centromeres and on chromosome arms71–75. In contrast, co-depletion of Esco1/2 resulted in comparable cohesion defects in both H2B-GFP and H2B-Sororin-CTR-GFP cells (Supplementary Fig. 3C–F), demonstrating that Esco1/2 perform non-redundant functions that cannot be bypassed by Sororin.

  To determine the consequences of impaired cohesin removal, we analyzed mitosis by live-cell and fixed-cell imaging. Live-cell imaging revealed comparable mitotic durations in cells expressing H2B-GFP and H2B-Sororin‑CTR-GFP (38.5 min vs. 36.7 min; Fig. 3E, F and Supplementary Movie 1, 2). However, anaphase chromosome-segregation errors increased dramatically, from 4.4% in control cells to 38.3% in H2B-Sororin-CTR-GFP cells (Fig. 3G). Analysis of paraformaldehyde (PFA)-fixed cells showed a specific increase in anaphase chromosome bridges from 6.7% to 32.9%, whereas lagging‑chromosome rates remained similar (3.8% vs. 4.5%) (Fig. 3H). Consistently, centromeric accumulation of Aurora B, which is important for correcting erroneous kinetochore-microtubule attachments76–81, was comparable in cells expressing H2B-GFP and H2B-Sororin-CTR-GFP (Supplementary Fig. 3G, H), indicating that the increased chromosome bridges do not result from Aurora B mislocalization. Furthermore, H2B-Sororin-CTR-GFP cells exhibited a ~ 3-fold increase in ultrafine DNA bridges marked by PICH (37.9% vs. 12.4%; Fig. 3I), indicative of defective decatenation. A similar increase in anaphase bridges was observed upon overexpression of untethered Sororin-CTR-GFP (Supplementary Fig. 3I–K), linking these defects directly to the CTR’s cohesion-stabilizing activity.

  In summary, constitutive chromatin tethering of Sororin-CTR prevents mitotic cohesin removal and recapitulates key features of Wapl loss, including defective chromosome condensation, impaired decatenation, and erroneous chromosome segregation.

  A previous study suggested that the C‑terminus of Sororin interacts with the SA2 subunit65. To test whether SA2 is required for cohesin’s interaction with Sororin‑CTR, we performed pull-down assays in SA2-knockout HeLa cells generated by CRISPR/Cas9. GST-Sororin-CTR retained binding to SMC3, SMC1, RAD21, and SA1 in the absence of SA2 (Fig. 4C, D and Supplementary Fig. 4A, B). In contrast, siRNA-mediated depletion of RAD21 abolished the interaction between GST-Sororin-CTR and the SMC subunits (Fig. 4E), identifying RAD21 as the critical anchor. Consistent with the previous observation that the D793K mutation prevented SA2 from binding RAD2182, this mutation eliminated the co-immunoprecipitation of Myc-tagged SA2 with GFP-fused RAD21 in HEK-293T cells (Supplementary Fig. 4C). Notably, this mutation also prevented Myc-SA2 from binding GST-Sororin-CTR (Supplementary Fig. 4D), indicating that SA2 binding to Sororin-CTR depends on its interaction with RAD21.

  Within the cohesin ring, RAD21 connects to the head domains of SMC3 and SMC1 via its N-terminal helical domain (NHD) and C-terminal winged-helix domain (WHD), respectively (Fig. 4F)34,83. To determine which protein is recognized by Sororin-CTR, we co-expressed Myc-tagged RAD21 with GFP-tagged SMC3 or SMC1 in HEK-293T cells. GST‑Sororin‑CTR selectively pulled down RAD21‑Myc together with SMC3‑GFP, but not with SMC1‑GFP (Fig. 4G), demonstrating preferential binding to the RAD21-SMC3 interface.

  Because the RAD21-SMC3 interaction involves RAD21’s NHD and the head-proximal coiled-coil of SMC3 (referred to as SMC3‑HD)34,83, we tested whether these regions were sufficient for Sororin-CTR binding. Pulldown assays showed that GST-Sororin-CTR bound Flag-tagged SMC3-HD only when co-expressed with Myc-tagged RAD21 fragments comprising residues 1-281 (referred to as N281) or residues 1-103 (referred to as N103), and that deletion of RAD21’s Pds5‑interaction region (residues 102-122) did not affect this interaction (Fig. 4H and Supplementary Fig. 4E).

  We further generated a recombinant protein complex formed between 2xStrep-tagged RAD21-N103 and SMC3-HD-Flag, which were co-expressed in E. coli and purified with the streptavidin beads. Pull-down assays showed that this minimal RAD21-N103/SMC3-HD complex directly bound GST-Sororin-CTR and GST-Sororin (Fig. 4I, J), establishing a direct, specific interaction.

  We next examined the cell-cycle regulation of this interaction. We synchronized HeLa cells in early-S phase and G2 phase by a double-thymidine block-and-release protocol and performed pull-down assays. GST-Sororin-CTR bound endogenous SMC3, SMC1, and RAD21 more efficiently in G2-phase cell lysates than in early S-phase lysates (Fig. 4K). Furthermore, this interaction persisted in late-G2 phases (arrested by the Cdk1 inhibitor RO-3306) but was markedly reduced in mitotic cells (arrested by nocodazole) (Fig. 4L). Thus, Sororin-CTR binding to cohesin peaks during the cohesion-maintenance phase of the cell cycle.

  Finally, to distinguish chromatin-bound from soluble cohesin pools, we performed chromatin fractionation followed by co-immunoprecipitation. Sororin-GFP stably expressed in HeLa cells co-precipitated RAD21, SMC3, and Pds5B specifically from the chromatin-bound fraction of mitotic cells, but not from the soluble fraction (Fig. 4M, N), confirming that Sororin interacts with functional, chromatin-associated cohesin.

  Collectively, these results demonstrate that Sororin’s CTR directly and specifically binds the RAD21-SMC3 interface in a cell-cycle-regulated manner on chromatin, consistent with a model in which Sororin stabilizes cohesion by locking the closed DNA exit gate of cohesin.


### Mitotic phosphorylation of Sororin selectively disrupts Pds5B binding while preserving interaction with the RAD21-SMC3 interface

In G2-arrested cells, Sororin-GFP efficiently co-immunoprecipitated RAD21-N103-Myc, SMC3-HD-Flag, and endogenous Pds5B (Fig. 5A). In mitotic cells, however, the Sororin-Pds5B interaction was abolished, whereas Sororin’s binding to RAD21-N103 and SMC3-HD was retained. Moreover, λ-phosphatase treatment of lysates from nocodazole-treated HEK-293T cells markedly enhanced the Sororin-Pds5B co-immunoprecipitation, without affecting interactions with RAD21-N103 or SMC3-HD (Fig. 5B).   Sororin contains a conserved YSR motif that is required for Pds5 binding26. Consistently, co-immunoprecipitation assays showed that endogenous Pds5B associated with Sororin-GFP, but not with the Sororin-ASE mutant in which the YSR motif (residues 146-148) was replaced by ASE (Fig. 5D). GST pull-down assays further demonstrated that GST-Sororin, but not GST-Sororin-ASE, bound endogenous Pds5B (but not its paralog Pds5A) from HeLa cell lysates (Fig. 5E). Notably, the Sororin-ASE mutant exhibited reduced binding to cohesin core subunits, indicating that Pds5B contributes to the overall association of Sororin with cohesin.

  To identify the phosphorylation site responsible for disrupting Pds5B binding, Sororin-GFP immunoprecipitated from HeLa cells was analyzed by mass spectrometry, revealing 18 phosphorylation sites (Supplementary Fig. 5A and Supplementary Data 1). Among these, serine 145 (S145), which lies adjacent to the YSR motif and is conserved across vertebrates, emerged as a strong candidate (Fig. 5F). Using a phospho-specific antibody raised against Sororin phosphorylated at S145 (Sororin-pS145), we found that S145 phosphorylation was detected exclusively in mitotic cells (Fig. 5G). Antibody specificity was confirmed using Flag-tagged Sororin purified from mitotic cells, as the signal was absent in the S145A mutant and abolished by λ-phosphatase treatment (Supplementary Fig. 5B, C).

  S145 resides within a consensus Aurora kinase phosphorylation motif (R/K-x-S/T-Φ). Accordingly, treatment of nocodazole-arrested mitotic cells with Hesperadin eliminated the pS145 signal on Flag-Sororin (Fig. 5H), indicating that Sororin-S145 phosphorylation in cells requires Aurora B kinase activity. To determine whether Aurora B can phosphorylate Sororin at S145, we performed in vitro kinase reactions using bacterially produced recombinant GST-Aurora B in complex with the IN-box of INCENP (GST-Aurora B/IN-box)84. In the presence of ATP, this complex phosphorylated Flag-Sororin at S145 (Fig. 5I). These results indicate that Aurora B is the kinase responsible for Sororin-S145 phosphorylation.

  We next assessed the effect of S145 phosphorylation on the Sororin-Pds5B interaction. Pull-down assays using a synthesized Sororin peptide (residues 136-158) and a recombinant MBP-tagged Pds5B fragment (residues 1-300) showed that the non-phosphorylated peptide bound Pds5B, whereas the S145-phosphorylated peptide did not (Fig. 5J). Moreover, in vitro phosphorylation of the Sororin peptide by GST-Aurora B/IN-box similarly abolished Pds5B binding (Fig. 5K), demonstrating that S145 phosphorylation directly disrupts this interaction.

  Consistent with these in vitro findings, co-immunoprecipitation assays in G2-synchronized HeLa cells revealed that Myc-tagged Pds5B associated with wild-type Sororin-GFP, but not with the phospho-mimetic Sororin-S145E-GFP mutant (Supplementary Fig. 5D). Moreover, Aurora B inhibition in mitotic cells enhanced Pds5B binding to wild-type Sororin-GFP, but not to Sororin-S145E-GFP (Supplementary Fig. 5E), confirming that Aurora B-dependent phosphorylation at S145 disrupts the Sororin-Pds5B interaction in vivo.

  We further tested the effect of a phospho-deficient mutation, S145N, on Sororin interactions. In mitotic cells, Sororin-S145N exhibited enhanced association with Pds5B, SMC3, and RAD21 compared with wild-type Sororin, whereas no difference was observed in G2-phase cells (Supplementary Fig. 5F, G).

  Finally, because Sgo1-associated PP2A protects centromeric cohesion by dephosphorylating Sororin51–54, we examined whether the S145N mutant could bypass Sgo1 depletion. Although the S145N mutant partially rescued the cohesion defect caused by Sgo1 knockdown, it did not fully restore cohesion (Supplementary Fig. 5H–J). This indicates that preservation of the Sororin-Pds5 interaction via S145 dephosphorylation represents a major, but not exclusive, function of Sgo1-PP2A in cohesion protection.

  In summary, Aurora B-mediated phosphorylation of Sororin at S145 acts as a mitotic switch that selectively disrupts its interaction with Pds5B, thereby promoting cohesin release from chromosome arms, while leaving its gate-locking interaction with the RAD21-SMC3 interface intact.

  To experimentally test these predictions, we performed binding assays using bacterially expressed recombinant proteins. A complex consisting of RAD21-N103-2xStrep and SMC3-HD-Flag was efficiently pulled down by GST-Sororin-CTR, but not by the double mutant GST-Sororin-CTR-W233A/E252K (hereafter WA/EK), in which W233 and E252 were mutated to alanine and lysine, respectively (Fig. 6C). A comparable loss of binding was observed for the GST-Sororin-CTR-F241A/F247A mutant (hereafter FA/FA), where both F241 and F247 were replaced by alanine. Consistently, pull-down assays in HeLa cells showed that endogenous SMC3, SMC1, RAD21, and SA2 interacted with GST-Sororin-CTR, but not with the WA/EK or FA/FA mutants (Fig. 6D). Moreover, a cancer-associated Sororin-E252Q mutation reduced co-immunoprecipitation of SMC3 and RAD21 with Sororin-Flag in HEK-293T cells (Supplementary Fig. 6C), suggesting that this mutation partially compromises Sororin’s gate-locking function and may contribute to genomic instability.

  We next examined the contribution of residues within RAD21-NHD and SMC3-HD to Sororin binding. In HEK-293T cells, simultaneous mutation of RAD21 Y3 to alanine and K50 to glutamic acid (hereafter YA/KE) abolished the ability of co-expressed RAD21-N103-Myc and SMC3-HD-Flag to bind GST-Sororin-CTR (Fig. 6E). Co-immunoprecipitation assays indicated that the YA/KE mutations did not affect the interaction between RAD21-N103-Myc and SMC3-HD-Flag (Supplementary Fig. 6D). Similarly, mutation of SMC3 K1034 and K1038 to glutamic acid (hereafter KE/KE) strongly reduced pull-down of co-expressed SMC3-HD-Flag and RAD21-N103-Myc by GST-Sororin-CTR (Fig. 6F), without affecting RAD21-SMC3 association (Supplementary Fig. 6E). Additional binding assays performed in HEK-293T cells showed that GST-Sororin-CTR failed to pull down the co-expressed mutants RAD21-N103-Y3A and SMC3-HD-R1099E-Flag, in which residues RAD21 Y3 and SMC3 R1099 were mutated to alanine and glutamic acid, respectively (Fig. 6G). We confirmed that these two mutations did not perturb the binding between RAD21 and SMC3 (Supplementary Fig. 6F).

  To further validate these interactions using a fully recombinant system, we co-expressed RAD21-N103-2xStrep and SMC3-HD-Flag in E. coli and immobilized this complex on streptavidin beads. The wild-type RAD21-N103-2xStrep/SMC3-HD-Flag complex efficiently pulled down GST-Sororin-CTR, whereas complexes containing either RAD21-N103-YA/KE or SMC3-HD-KE/KE failed to do so (Fig. 6H). Consistent results were obtained when these complexes were used to pull down Myc-Sororin expressed in HEK-293T cells (Fig. 6I).

  Esco1/2-mediated acetylation of SMC3 at K105/K106 promotes Sororin recruitment to chromatin in S phase26,27,35,39–45. Notably, neither acetylation-deficient (K105A/K106A) nor acetylation-mimetic (K105Q/K106Q) mutations in SMC3 affected co-immunoprecipitation of Sororin-GFP with the SMC3-HD-Flag/RAD21-N103-Myc complex (Supplementary Fig. 6G). Consistently, AlphaFold3 modeling indicates that K105 and K106 are spatially distant from the Sororin-binding residues (K1034, K1038, R1099) on SMC3 (Supplementary Fig. 6H; AlphaFoldDB: Q9UQE7). Thus, SMC3 acetylation licenses Sororin recruitment without directly regulating its binding to the RAD21-SMC3 interface.

  Collectively, these data identify critical residues in Sororin-CTR, RAD21-NHD, and SMC3-HD that together mediate Sororin binding to the RAD21-SMC3 interface.

  We next tested whether the WA/EK and FA/FA mutants could support Sororin’s function in protecting sister-chromatid cohesion.   We next determined the contribution of SMC3 residues involved in Sororin binding. ## Discussion
  The establishment and maintenance of sister-chromatid cohesion are fundamental for genome stability and depend on precise regulation of the cohesin complex21. Sororin has long been recognized as a key cohesion factor, with its function primarily attributed to antagonizing the release factor Wapl through competitive binding to Pds526,27,37,54,60–62,64. In this study, we redefine Sororin’s role by demonstrating that its extreme C-terminal region (CTR) acts as a direct structural lock for cohesin’s DNA-exit gate (Fig. 9J). This gate-locking activity operates synergistically with, yet independently of, Pds5 competition, providing a unified mechanism that stabilizes sister-chromatid cohesion while permitting its timely dissolution during mitosis.

  We show that the 30-amino acid CTR of Sororin is both necessary and sufficient to promote cohesion. Remarkably, constitutive tethering of this minimal domain to centromeres fully restores cohesion in Sororin-depleted cells, demonstrating that centromere-localized gate engagement can bypass the requirement for full-length Sororin. By contrast, genome-wide tethering of Sororin-CTR phenocopies the effects of Wapl loss48,50,87, causing defective chromosome condensation, impaired decatenation, and segregation errors. These findings underscore the struct need for spatial and temporal control of Sororin activity: persistent gate locking is required at centromeres to protect cohesion until anaphase, whereas timely removal of Sororin and cohesin from chromosome arms is essential for proper mitotic progression.

  At the molecular level, we identify a conserved network of hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions through which Sororin-CTR directly engages the RAD21-SMC3 interface. AlphaFold3-guided structural modeling, supported by mutagenesis and binding assays, pinpoints key Sororin residues (W233, F241, F247, and E252) that contact RAD21 and SMC3. Disruption of these interactions abolishes Sororin binding to cohesin and results in cohesion loss. Notably, these defects are fully suppressed by Wapl co-depletion, demonstrating that Sororin’s essential function is to protect cohesin’s the exit gate from Wapl-mediated opening rather than to recruit accessory factors or alter cohesin ring conformation. This genetic epistasis firmly establishes Sororin as a direct antagonist of Wapl at the cohesin gate.

  Our data further elucidate how Sororin activity is regulated across the cell cycle. Sororin-CTR binding to cohesin peaks during G2 phase, when cohesion is maintained, and is markedly reduced during mitosis, consistent with cohesion establishment following DNA replication and its dissolution before chromosome segregation. We additionally identify a phosphorylation-dependent regulatory switch that differentially modulates Sororin interactions. Aurora B-mediated phosphorylation at S145 selectively disrupts Sororin binding to Pds5 while preserving its engagement with the RAD21-SMC3 gate. This distinction provides a mechanism for cohesin release in early mitosis: on chromosome arms, phosphorylation permits Wapl rebinding and cohesin removal, whereas at centromeres, PP2A-mediated dephosphorylation primarily preserves Sororin-Pds5 interaction and sustained gate locking, thereby ensuring continued cohesion protection.

  Our findings also clarify the relationship between Sororin and cohesin subunits SA2 and SMC3. Although previous work proposed a direct Sororin-SA2 interaction65, we show that SA2 is dispensable for Sororin-CTR binding to the cohesin core and instead associates indirectly via RAD21. Furthermore, we demonstrate that Esco1/2-mediated acetylation of SMC3, a critical licensing step for Sororin recruitment27,35–38, does not directly influence Sororin affinity for the RAD21-SMC3 interface. Additionally, we find that chromatin-tethered Sororin-CTR cannot bypass the requirement for Esco1/2 in safeguarding cohesion. These results support a sequential model in which SMC3 acetylation enables Sororin recruitment during S phase, followed by CTR-mediated gate locking to stabilize the cohesin ring. Separation of recruitment and locking functions likely provides a robust, multilayered mechanism for cohesion establishment.

  The implications of these findings extend beyond sister-chromatid cohesion. Cohesin also plays a central role in organizing interphase genome architecture through DNA loop extrusion19. Persistent gate-locked cohesin on chromatin, as observed upon ectopic Sororin-CTR tethering, interferes with mitotic chromosome compaction and decatenation. This raises important questions regarding the functional state of gate-locked cohesin: does it retain loop extrusion activity, and how does its unscheduled persistence during mitosis disrupt higher-order chromatin organization? Future studies integrating live-cell imaging of cohesin dynamics with genome-wide chromatin conformation analyses will be essential to link cohesion maintenance to broader principles of genome organization88,89.

  Clinically, mutations in cohesin components and their regulators underlie cohesinopathies and are frequently observed in cancer15. The Sororin-CTR interaction surface on RAD21 and SMC3 identified here represents a potential hotspot for pathogenic variants that compromise gate locking and promote genomic instability. Consistent with this idea, we show that a cancer-associated Sororin-E252Q mutation weakens its interaction with cohesin. Systematic interrogation of mutations affecting this interface may reveal novel disease mechanisms and inform therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating cohesin dynamics, potentially sensitizing cancer cells to replication or mitotic stress.

  In summary, we propose a revised dual-function model for Sororin in cohesion maintenance (Fig. 9J). Sororin acts not only as a competitive inhibitor of Wapl via Pds5 but also as a structural lock that directly occludes the RAD21-SMC3 exit gate. This gate-locking function is spatially restricted to centromeres and temporally regulated by phosphorylation, ensuring that cohesion is maintained precisely where and when it is required. By defining this direct molecular mechanism, our work resolves long-standing questions in cohesin biology and provides a refined framework for understanding, and potentially targeting, cohesin regulation in development and disease.


## Methods

### Cell lines, plasmids, siRNA, transfection, and drug treatments
  HEK-293T cells, HeLa cells, and their derivatives were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), while RPE-1 cells and their derivatives were cultured in DMEM/F12. Both media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. All cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. HeLa cells stably expressing Sororin-GFP (WT, WA/EK, or FA/FA), CENP-B-GFP, CENP-B-Sororin-CTR-GFP, H2B-GFP, H2B-Sororin-CTR-GFP, and SMC3-GFP (WT or KE/KE) were cultured in medium supplemented with 1.5 µg/ml blasticidin (Sigma). HeLa cells stably expressing Tet-on inducible RAD21-SFB (WT or YA/KE) were maintained in medium containing 0.5 mg/ml G418 (Calbiochem). Pools of RPE-1 cells expressing Tet-on inducible Sororin-SFB (WT, WA/EK, or FA/FA) were generated by lentiviral infection and selected in medium containing 0.5 mg/ml G418.

  The construct for pGEX-6P-Aurora B (60-361)-rbs-INCENP (790-856) was a gift from Dr. Andrea Musacchio (Max Planck Institute of Molecular Physiology). The Myc-Pds5B construct was kindly provided by Dr. Hongtao Yu (Westlake University). To generate the Flag-Sororin, Lenti-Sororin-SFB, and Lenti-RAD21-SFB constructs, the corresponding cDNAs in the entry vectors were transferred into Gateway-compatible destination vectors harboring an N-terminal Flag-tag and a C-terminal SFB-tag, respectively. Myc-SA2 was constructed similarly using a vector harboring an N-terminal Myc-tag. To generate Myc-Sororin, the cDNA encoding Sororin was amplified by PCR and subcloned into an expression vector containing a Myc epitope tag. The pBos-H2B-Sororin-GFP plasmid series was constructed by subcloning PCR-amplified fragments encoding full-length Sororin or truncated variants (1-149, 1-222, 223-252) into pBos-H2B-GFP (BD PharMingen) using the BamHI restriction site. The pBos-CENP-B-GFP plasmid was constructed by replacing the H2B coding sequence in pBos-H2B-GFP with KpnI/BamHI-digested PCR fragments encoding the centromere-targeting domain of CENP-B (residues 1-163). The pBos-CB-Sororin (223-252)-GFP construct was generated by subcloning the corresponding Sororin fragment into pBos-CENP-B-GFP. The pBos-Sororin-GFP plasmid series was constructed by replacing the H2B coding sequence in pBos-H2B-GFP with PCR-amplified sequences encoding full-length Sororin or its truncated variants (1-149, 150-252, 1-222, 223-252). The pBos-SMC1-GFP, pBos-SMC3-GFP, and pBos-RAD21-GFP constructs were generated by replacing the H2B coding sequence with PCR-amplified coding sequences of SMC1, SMC3, and RAD21, respectively. Plasmids encoding GST-fused Sororin (Full-length or truncations) were generated by subcloning the corresponding PCR fragments into pGEX-4T1 (GE Healthcare). The MBP-Pds5B (1-300) construct was generated by cloning the PCR products into pMAL-C2E (New England Biolabs). For the pEF6-RAD21-Myc construct series, PCR-amplified fragments encoding full-length RAD21 or its variants (1-281, 1-101 + 123-281, 1-103) were cloned into the pEF6-Myc vector. The pEF-Sororin-Flag and pEF-SMC3-HD-Flag constructs were generated by cloning PCR-amplified Sororin and SMC3-HD fragments (residues 1-253 and 959-1217 connected by a GGGGS linker) into pEF-Flag, respectively. To generate the pET45-RAD21-N103-2xStrep-rbs-SMC3-HD-Flag construct, the PCR-amplified RAD21-N103-2xStrep fragment, rbs fragment, and SMC3-HD-Flag fragment were subcloned into pET45 using the MultiF Seamless Assembly Mix Kit (ABclonal Biotechnology).

  All point mutations were introduced using either the QuikChange II XL Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Agilent Technologies) or the MultiF Seamless Assembly Mix Kit (ABclonal Biotechnology). All constructs were verified by DNA sequencing to confirm the presence of the desired mutations and the absence of unwanted sequence alterations.

  siRNA duplexes targeting Sororin, Esco1, Esco2, Sgo1, Wapl, RAD21, and SMC3 were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT) or RiboBio: siSororin targeting the 3’UTR (5’-CUCGGAAAGUUUCCUCGCGUAdTdT-3’), siEsco1 (5’-AGAAGGUCGUUACGCAGUAdTdT-3’), siEsco2 (5’-GAAAGAACGUGUAGUAGCAdTdT-3’), siSgo1 (5’-GAGGGGACCCUUUUACAGAdTdT-3’), siWapl (5’-CGGACUACCCUUAGCACAAdTdT-3’), siRAD21 (5’-AUACCUUCUUGCAGACUGUdTdT-3’), siRAD21#2 (5’-GCACUACUACUUCUAACCUdTdT-3’), siSMC3 (5’-GGAGGGCAGUCAGUCUCAAGAUGAAdTdT-3’). siRNA transfections were carried out twice at a 24-h intervals using Oligofectamine (Invitrogen) or Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For SMC3 siRNA transfection, cells at approximately 40% confluence were transfected once using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX. Plasmid transfections were performed using FuGENE 6 (Promega) or Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

  Cell-cycle synchronization was performed as follows: cells were synchronized at the G1/S boundary using a double-thymidine (2 mM, Sigma) block; in G2 phase using either a single-thymidine block or a double-thymidine block followed by an 8-h release; in late G2 phase using RO-3306 (9 μM; Selleckchem) for 18 h; and in prometaphase using nocodazole (100 ~ 300 ng/ml; Selleckchem) for 15–18 h or STLC (5 μM, Tocris Bioscience) for 3 h. Mitotic cells were collected by mechanical detachment (“shake-off”). The kinase inhibitors used in this study were as follows: CDK1 (Roscovitine, Selleckchem; 100 μM), Aurora B kinase (Hesperadin, Selleckchem; 25–100 nM), and Plk1 (BI 2536, Selleckchem; 100 nM). The proteasome inhibitor MG132 (10 μM; Selleckchem) was also used.

### Lentivirus packaging and infection
  The Tet-On inducible SFB-tagged lentiviral vector and packaging plasmids (pMD2G and pSPAX2) were provided by Dr. Jun Huang (Life Sciences Institute, Zhejiang University). Virus-containing supernatant was collected 48 h after the co-transfection of the lentiviral vector and packaging plasmids (pMD2G and pSPAX2) into HEK-293T cells. HeLa cells were infected with the viral supernatants in the presence of 8 μg/ml polybrene (Sigma), and stable pools were selected using medium containing 0.5 mg/ml G418. For the experiments described in this study, expression of the indicated genes in the stable pools was induced by the addition of 1 μg/ml doxycycline (Selleckchem) for 24 h.


### CRISPR/Cas9-mediated editing of the SA2 gene STAG2 in human cells
  Single-guide RNA (sgRNA) for human STAG2 (encoding SA2) was designed, ordered as complementary oligonucleotides, annealed, and cloned into the dual Cas9 and sgRNA expression vector pX330 (Addgene, #42230) using BbsI restriction sites. The resulting plasmid was transfected into HeLa cells using FuGENE 6 according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After 48 h incubation, cells were seeded at low density to establish clonal cell lines following brief selection with 1 μg/ml puromycin for 2–3 days. To obtain SA2-KO cells, clones showing undetectable SA2, as confirmed by immunostaining and immunoblotting, were isolated. SA2 KO cells were generated using an sgRNA targeting the sequence 5’-GGCCCAGCAGAAAAGGGCAA-3’.


### Fluorescence microscopy, chromosome spreads, and chromosome length measurement
  HeLa cells were typically fixed in 2% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 10 min at room temperature, followed by permeabilization with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 5 min. Cells were subsequently blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS and incubated with primary antibodies for 1–2 h, followed by secondary antibody incubation for 1 h, all incubations were performed at room temperature in 3% BSA/PBS. Nuclear DNA was counterstained with DAPI for 5 min.

  For chromosome spread preparation, HeLa cells were treated with 100-300 ng/ml nocodazole for 3 h. Mitotic cells were collected by selective detachment and incubated in hypotonic buffer (75 mM KCl or 0.25×PBS) for 10–15 min at room temperature. Cells were then cytocentrifuged onto glass coverslips using a Cytospin at 260 × g for 5 min. Following attachment, chromosome spreads were fixed in 2% PFA in PBS for 15–20 min, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min, blocked with 3% BSA in PBS, and incubated with primary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. For SMC1 immunofluorescence staining, cells were pre-extracted with 0.5% Triton in PHEM buffer (60 mM Pipes, 25 mM HEPES, 10 mM EGTA, and 2 mM MgCl2, pH 6.9) for 5 min, then fixed with 4% PFA in PHEM for 20 minutes.

  Fluorescence microscopy was performed at room temperature using a Nikon ECLIPSE Ni microscope equipped with a Plan Apo Fluor 60×oil immersion objective (NA 1.4) and a Clara CCD camera (Andor Technology). To measure the length of mitotic chromosomes, images were visualized using NIS-Elements BR software (Nikon). Chromosome lengths were measured along the outline of the center of the chromosomes using a point-by-point tracing tool. Cells were randomly selected for each condition, and all chromosomes within the selected cells were measured.


### Time-lapse live cell imaging
  Time-lapse live cell imaging was carried out using the GE DV Elite Applied Precision DeltaVision system (GE Healthcare) equipped with Olympus oil objectives of 60×(NA 1.42) Plan Apo N or 40×(NA 1.35) UApo/340 oil objective, an API Custom Scientific complementary metal-oxide semiconductor camera, and Resolve3D softWoRx imaging software. Cells were plated in four-chamber glass-bottomed 35-mm dishes (Cellvis) coated with poly-D-lysine, and imaged in a climate-controlled and humidified environment (37 °C and 5% CO2). Chromosomes were labeled with H2B-GFP. Images were captured every 3 minutes and processed using Adobe Photoshop and Adobe Illustrator.


### Protein expression and purification
  Plasmids encoding GST-, Strep-, or MBP-fused proteins were transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) competent cells. Transformed cells were cultured in LB medium supplemented with the appropriate antibiotic at 37 °C until the optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) reached 0.6–0.8. Protein expression was induced by adding 0.4 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) and incubating at 16 °C for 18 h.

  For GST fusion proteins, cells were lysed by sonication in Buffer A (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5% Triton X-100, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride/PMSF)). For MBP fusion proteins, lysis was performed in Buffer B (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5% Triton X-100). Lysates were clarified by centrifugation and incubated with Glutathione Sepharose 4B beads (GE Healthcare) for GST fusion proteins or Amylose Resin (New England Biolabs) for MBP fusion proteins. After washing with the respective lysis buffer, proteins were eluted using 100 mM reduced glutathione for GST fusions or 10 mM maltose for MBP fusions.

  For Strep-fused protein purification, cells were resuspended in lysis buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 1% Triton X-100, 1 mM EDTA, and 1 mM PMSF). Following sonication and a freeze-thaw cycle, the lysate was clarified by centrifugation, and the supernatant was applied to a Strep-Tactin Superflow affinity column (IBA Lifesciences). The resin was washed four times with lysis buffer, and bound proteins were eluted with lysis buffer supplemented with 10 mM desthiobiotin.


### In vitro kinase reaction
  For the phosphorylation of Flag-Sororin (Fig. 5I), asynchronous HEK-293T cells transiently expressing Flag-Sororin (WT or S145A) were lysed in buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, 0.1% NP-40, and 10 mM MgCl2, supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors as in P150 buffer. The anti-Flag immunoprecipitates were then subjected to phosphorylation by 0.2 μg GST-Aurora B/IN-box. For the phosphorylation of Sororin peptide (Fig. 5K), 0.5 μg of substrates was incubated with 0.2 μg of recombinant GST-Aurora B/IN-box. The kinase reactions were conducted in 50 μl kinase reaction buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2) with 0.2 mM ATP for 20 min at 30 °C. The reactions were terminated by adding SDS sample buffer, boiled, and analyzed by immunoblotting.


### Chromatin fractionation
  Mitotic cells collected after 18 h of nocodazole treatment were lysed in fractionation buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 10% glycerol, 0.2% NP-40) supplemented with 1 mM DTT, protease inhibitor cocktail (P8340, Sigma), 10 mM NaF, and 20 mM β-glycerophosphate. After centrifugation at 2000 g for 5 min, the supernatant was collected and used for immunoblotting and immunoprecipitation. The chromatin pellets were washed three times with the same buffer and subsequently analyzed by immunoblotting and immunoprecipitation.


### Pull-down, immunoprecipitation, and immunoblotting
  For GST- or Strep-fused protein pull-down assays with cell lysates, HeLa cells were lysed in P150 buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 10 mM MgCl₂, 5 mM EDTA) supplemented with 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), protease inhibitor cocktail, 1 mM PMSF, 0.1 μM okadaic acid (Calbiochem), 10 mM sodium fluoride, 20 mM β-glycerophosphate, and Benzonase (GenScript). Insoluble material was removed by high-speed centrifugation, and the lysates were precleared with either glutathione Sepharose 4B beads (GE Healthcare) or StrepTactin 4FF beads (Smart Life Sciences). The precleared lysates were then incubated with bead-immobilized GST- or Strep-fused proteins for 4 h at 4 °C. Beads were washed 4 times with lysis buffer, boiled in SDS sample buffer, and subjected to immunoblotting.

  For Sororin (136-158) peptide pull-down of recombinant MBP-Pds5B-N300, 1 μg of peptides were incubated with 4 μg of MBP-Pds5B-N300 in 1 ml of binding buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 0.5% Triton X-100) for 3 h, followed by 1-h incubation with NeutrAvidin Plus Ultralink Resin (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The resin was then washed 4 times with binding buffer. The Sororin peptide of residues, corresponding to residues 136-158 (either wild-type or the pS145 form) and followed by a Lys-biotin moiety, were synthesized by GL Biochem (Shanghai).

  For co-immunoprecipitation, cells were lysed in P150 buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 10 mM MgCl₂, 5 mM EDTA) supplemented with the same additives as above. Insoluble material was removed by high-speed centrifugation, and the lysates were pre-cleared with rProtein A/G beads (Smart Life Sciences, Cat. No. SA032100). The precleared lysates were incubated for 4 h at 4 °C with Anti-GFP Affinity beads (Smart Life Sciences, SA070005), Anti-Flag M2 Affinity Gel (Sigma, A2220), Anti-Myc Affinity Gel (GNI, GNI4510-MC), or control IgG. After washing with P150 buffer, bound proteins were eluted by boiling in SDS sample buffer for immunoblotting.


### Mass spectrometry
  For the data presented in Supplementary Fig. 5A and Supplementary Data 1, immunoprecipitated Sororin-GFP were separated by SDS-PAGE (n = 1). The gels were minimally stained with Coomassie brilliant blue. Protein bands were excised according to the molecular weight marker range. The excised protein bands were cut into small pieces, then reduced and alkylated in 10 mM DTT and 55 mM IAA, respectively. For digestion, 0.5 µg of sequencing-grade modified trypsin (Promega, USA) was added, followed by incubation overnight at 37 °C. Prior to mass spectrometry analysis, the peptide preparations were desalted using StageTip and reconstituted with 0.1% formic acid (FA).

  Mass spectrometry analysis was performed using a Thermo Scientific Q Exactive HF-X Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer instrument coupled with an Easy-nLC 1200 system. Mobile phase A and B were water and 80% acetonitrile, respectively, each containing 0.1% formic acid. Protein digests were loaded directly onto an analytical column (75 µm×15 cm, 1.9 µm C18, 1 µm tip) at a flow rate of 450 nl/min. Samples were separated using a linear gradient of 6-8% B for 2 min, 8-27% B for 40 min, 27–40% B for 12 min, 40-90% B for 3 min, 90% B for 3 min.MS/MS data were collected in a data-dependent manner using a top-20 method with a full MS mass range from 400 to 1400 m/z, 60,000 resolution, and an AGC target of 3 × 106. MS2 scans were triggered when an ion intensity threshold of 4.5 × 105 was reached. A dynamic exclusion time of 30 s was used. Ions with charge state 1, 6, 8, and > 8 were excluded. The resulting raw data were uploaded into MaxQuant (version 2.0.3.0) and searched against the UniProtKB Sororin (Q96FF9) sequence database. Trypsin/P was specified as the cleavage enzyme, allowing up to two missing cleavages. The mass tolerance for precursor ions was set to 20 parts per million in first search and 5 parts per million in main search, and the mass tolerance for fragment ions was set to 0.02 Da. Carbamidomethylation (C, +57.0215 Da) was used as the fixed modification. Phospho (STY, +79.9663 Da) was included as variable modifications. The false discovery rate (FDR) was adjusted to <1%, with a minimum score for modified peptides set to >20.


### Quantitative RT-PCR analysis
  Total cellular RNA was extracted with TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The homogenate was incubated on ice for 10 min and centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was mixed with chloroform, vigorously shake, and incubated at room temperature. After centrifugation, the upper aqueous phase was mixed with an equal volume of isopropanol, followed by centrifuged. The RNA pellet was washed twice with cold 75% ethanol and dissolved in DEPC-treated water. Next, 1000 ng of RNA was reverse-transcribed using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara, RR047A). Quantitative PCR was carried out using SYBR qPCR SuperMix (Vazyme, Q711-03) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The Esco2 primer sequences were (5’- ATCCAATCTTCAGTGCATCTTCA-3’ and 5’- TGTTTCTGACCAGCGTCGAT-3’).


### Structure prediction
  The structure of a complex formed between the Sororin C-terminal fragment (residues 191-252), the SMC3-HD (residues 1-253 and 959-1217 connected by a GGGGS linker), and the RAD21 N-terminal fragment (residues 1-110) was predicted using AlphaFold3. All predictions were performed via the public AlphaFold3 web server (https://www.alphafoldserver.com) using the default settings and without applying any constraints. A representative complex was visualization in PyMOL v3.1.3.


### Sequence alignment
  Multiple sequence alignments of Sororin, RAD21, and SMC3 were performed using UniProt Align (https://www.uniprot.org/), and the results were visualized using ESPript 3.0 (https://espript.ibcp.fr/ESPript/ESPript/).


### Statistics
  Statistical analyses were conducted using a two-tailed, unpaired Student’s t-test in GraphPad Prism 6. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All representative images, including Western blots and micrographs, were obtained from independent biological replicates that yielded consistent results.
