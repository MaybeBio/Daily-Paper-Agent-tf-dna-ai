# Functional Data Strengthen Clinical Validation of PhenoScore Phenotype‐Guided AI for 
ANKRD11
 Missense Variants


## ABSTRACT
  KBG syndrome (KBGS, OMIM #148050) is a rare genetic disorder caused by heterozygous truncating or missense variants in the ANKRD11 gene or a deletion of 16q24.3 involving ANKRD11. While truncating variants clearly disrupt protein function, the interpretation of missense variants is more challenging, as many remain variants of uncertain significance (VUS). To address this, we evaluated PhenoScore, an open‐source AI‐based phenomics framework integrating facial recognition and medical data analysis, for predicting the pathogenicity of ANKRD11 missense variants and providing supporting evidence for variant interpretation within the ACMG framework, specifically the PP4 criterion. PhenoScore was trained on 79 individuals with truncating variants in ANKRD11 and age‐, sex‐, and ethnicity‐matched controls with other neurodevelopmental disorders, and its performance was compared to AlphaMissense, REVEL, and the evaluation of a clinical geneticist. Six individuals with functionally confirmed pathogenic missense variants were used for testing. PhenoScore achieved high predictive accuracy with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.95 and a Brier score of 0.089, and pathogenic missense variants in the test set received a mean prediction score of 0.94. PhenoScore significantly outperformed REVEL (p < 0.01), especially in cases supported by functional and clinical evidence, while no significant difference was observed compared to AlphaMissense (p = 0.63); importantly, the two tools showed complementary strengths. These findings suggest that PhenoScore represents a promising tool for clinical variant interpretation, as it quantifies phenotypic concordance with KBGS and provides objective evidence that can strengthen the PP4 criterion within the ACMG framework. Combined with molecular prediction tools like AlphaMissense, PhenoScore may help reduce uncertainty surrounding VUS in ANKRD11 by complementing these scores.


## Introduction
  KBG syndrome (KBGS, OMIM #148050) is a rare genetic disorder characterized by a range of physical, neurodevelopmental, and intellectual challenges, which manifest through various clinical features.

  Key clinical features include macrodontia of the permanent upper central incisors, craniofacial dysmorphic features such as prominent eyebrows and ears, a broad nasal tip, anteverted nares and a long philtrum. Other characteristics include short stature, congenital anomalies (heart defects, palatal and urogenital abnormalities), delayed bone age, and skeletal anomalies such as scoliosis, spina bifida occulta, and delayed closure of the anterior fontanel [1, 2, 3, 4]. Intellectual and developmental variability is common, with many individuals experiencing persistent developmental delays and varying degrees of intellectual deficits [5]. Behavioral problems, including hyperactivity, difficulty with change, obsessive‐compulsive behavior, and autism spectrum disorder (ASD), are also common [6]. Other features may include hearing and speech problems, epilepsy, vision problems, feeding difficulties and early puberty [7].

  KBGS is caused by heterozygous truncating or missense variants in the ankyrin repeat domain containing 11 (ANKRD11) gene or a deletion of 16q24.3 involving ANKRD11 [1, 3, 4, 5]. ANKRD11 encodes a protein that regulates gene expression and influences various cellular processes, playing an essential role in normal development [8]. The majority of variants causative of KBGS are protein truncating variants (PTVs), with missense variants accounting for small proportion of those (3%) [9]. For both PTVs and missense variants, a loss‐of‐function effect has been demonstrated. Nevertheless, only a small minority of the missense variants have been subjected to functional tests like in silico analyses and cell based experiments. A previous study performed fluorescence imaging in HEK293T/17 cells transfected with EGFP‐tagged ANKRD11 constructs to assess subcellular localization, along with protein stability and degradation assays using translation and proteasome inhibitors to measure fluorescence intensity over 24 h. Additionally, luciferase reporter assays with a CDKN1A/P21 promoter were conducted to evaluate the impact of ANKRD11 variants on transcriptional repression. Extensive testing like this is usually unavailable and therefore, the pathogenicity of almost all of these missense variants remains elusive and these are often classified as variants of unknown significance (VUS) [9]. The classification of a variant as a VUS follows the guidelines of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) [10], which uses a combination of population data, computational predictions, functional studies, and segregation analysis to assess pathogenicity.

  Within this framework, the PP4 criterion allows phenotypic evidence to support pathogenicity when a patient's phenotype is highly specific for a disease with a single genetic etiology. Currently, application of PP4 relies largely on subjective clinical assessment. The interpretation of VUS could benefit from standardized, objective tools that quantify phenotypic concordance to more rigorously apply PP4. While a clinical diagnosis of KBGS can be established based on phenotype, this does not resolve the variant classification itself; the variant may remain a VUS even when a clinical diagnosis is established, as phenotype matching does not prove that a specific variant is causal.

  In general, the presence of VUSs complicates clinical management and counseling due to their unclear clinical implications, leading to uncertainty and distress for patients and their families. Therefore, reducing the number of VUSs is essential to improving clinical outcomes [11].

  Several methods have already been investigated and developed to predict the pathogenicity of missense variants, including molecular pathogenicity scores such as Sorting Intolerant from Tolerant (SIFT) [12], MutationTaster [13], Combined Annotation‐Dependent Depletion (CADD) score [14] and others, with REVEL (Rare Exome Variant Ensemble Learner) [15] and the relatively novel AlphaMissense model [16], the latter appearing to be among the most effective pathogenicity prediction scores.

  Beyond molecular predictions, clinical information is essential for variant interpretation as well. To facilitate and standardize VUS interpretation, PhenoScore was developed [17]. It is an open‐source, AI‐based phenomics framework that uses facial recognition technology and Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) data analysis to quantify phenotypic similarity between individuals [17].

  A previous study demonstrated the potential of PhenoScore with respect to ANKRD11 cases with a loss‐of‐function effect (n = 22) [17]. However, only individuals with truncating variants were investigated in this study. Therefore, the objective of this study is to evaluate the ability of PhenoScore to identify phenotypic concordance with KBGS in individuals carrying missense variants, using facial photographs and other phenotypic data, thereby providing evidence to support the PP4 criterion in variant interpretation. We compare these results with the outcomes of functional studies and predictions of AlphaMissense and REVEL. This study will thus be the next step in clinically validating PhenoScore and therefore applying artificial intelligence in clinical practice in clinical genetics.


## Materials and Methods

### Data Collection and Processing
  We collected clinical data of individuals with truncating and missense variants in ANKRD11 from multiple sources. This included data from individuals seen at our outpatient clinic in the Radboud university medical center, records available on the Human Disease Genes website (HDGW) ([18], https://humandiseasegenes.nl/ankrd11), and additional cases identified by reaching out to a network of collaborators. We excluded related individuals, as similarities between family members can lead to bias. In addition, at least one frontal facial photograph had to be available per individual. Clinical and molecular information, including phenotypic information and genetic test results, was collected for each individual and all data were converted into HPO terms and added to the HDGW ([18], https://humandiseasegenes.nl/ankrd11) Informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians prior to their inclusion in the study through the Genetics and Rare diseases Biobank of the Radboud university medical center (Medical Ethical Research Committee Oost Nederland approval #2018‐4985) [19].


### Training Phase
  In order to construct the PhenoScore prediction model for KBGS, it was necessary to first identify a matched control group based on age, sex, and ethnicity from our internal control database at Radboud university medical center. For each individual, a control with a neurodevelopmental disorder (NDD) who had been evaluated in our outpatient clinic was selected and this procedure was repeated five times, as in the original PhenoScore paper [17]. The control group was selected from a database comprising over 1600 individuals, all of whom had both facial images and phenotypic data available for analysis. In the event that a suitable match could not be identified for a specific case, that individual was excluded from further participation in the study [17]. Subsequently, all phenotypic data, including facial photographs of all individuals in the data set, were provided to PhenoScore for the purpose of determining whether PhenoScore could distinguish between individuals with KGBS and control individuals with other NDDs.

  Extracting facial features from the collected photographs is the first step in training, using a convolutional neural network, QMagFace in this instance [20]. Simultaneously, PhenoScore uses HPO terms to categorize observed clinical features and calculate phenotypic similarities between individuals with KBG syndrome caused by truncating variants and controls. The extracted facial features and computed phenotypic similarities are used as input to train the PhenoScore model. A Support Vector Machine (SVM), a widely used classification algorithm in machine learning, is used as the classifier to train the model on the training dataset [17]. During training, the model learns to discriminate between individuals with KBG syndrome and those without by finding the optimal hyperplane that separates individuals with KBGS from the controls using the extracted facial features and phenotypic similarities as input data [21].

  To evaluate the performance of the classifier, a cross‐validation technique was used. The cross‐validation process consisted of five iterations, each corresponding to one fold that was kept as a test, while the remaining four folds were used to train the framework. During each iteration, the SVM classifier is trained on the training subset, using the extracted facial features and phenotypic similarities to learn the patterns associated with KGBS. After completing all five iterations, the Brier score (for which lower is better) and AUC (for which higher is better) obtained from each fold are aggregated to derive the overall performance of the PhenoScore framework after training.


### Testing Phase
  Validation of the PhenoScore predictions was conducted using an independent dataset of individuals with missense variants in the ANKRD11 gene. This dataset is distinct from the training dataset that consists of individuals with truncating variants in ANKRD11 (or a deletion of the gene), ensuring unbiased evaluation of PhenoScore's performance. Using this set as input, the actual ability of PhenoScore to identify phenotypic concordance with KBGS in individuals carrying missense variants can be tested. PhenoScore generates a prediction score as output: the value of this score is between 0 and 1, where a score below 0.3 is associated with phenotypic similarity with the control, a score above 0.7 with KBGS, and a score between 0.3 and 0.7 is defined as inconclusive [17].


### Comparison With Molecular Prediction Scores
  Previous functional research in cell‐based assays, including fluorescence microscopy and luciferase reporter assays, has demonstrated that certain missense ANKRD11 variants disrupt protein function without affecting its nuclear localization. These findings suggest that ANKRD11 missense variants can result in a loss‐of‐function, either through decreased protein levels or diminished transcriptional activity, which is consistent with the loss‐of‐function mechanism described for protein truncating variants [9]. To ascertain whether PhenoScore enhances the capabilities of existing validated prediction tools, the prediction scores of PhenoScore were contrasted with the outcomes of these functional tests and the predictions of established molecular prediction tools, REVEL and AlphaMissense. REVEL employs a pathogenicity prediction score based on existing pathogenicity scores from a range of predictive tools in order to distinguish likely pathogenic mutations from benign variants [15]. It incorporates factors such as amino acid conservation and protein stability. AlphaMissense [16] is a deep learning model that builds upon the capabilities of AlphaFold2 [22] in predicting protein structures. It predicts how single amino acid changes can alter the three‐dimensional conformation of proteins and affect their function, thereby providing insights into the potential loss or gain of function that could result from a certain mutation. By employing the prediction scores derived from these two tools, the clinical significance of genetic variants can be elucidated. Furthermore, the diagnosis of KBGS for each individual was assessed by evaluating the alignment of their phenotype with the clinical features of KBGS. This assessment was guided by the clinical geneticist's judgment, considering the extent to which the patient's presentation matched known characteristics of KBGS. Additionally, the classification of missense variants as VUS also considered whether the variant occurred de novo or was inherited. De novo variants are widely regarded as more likely to be pathogenic, providing further evidence for distinguishing missense variants. The clinical geneticist's interpretation, combined with previous functional test results, contributed to the final assessment of the patient's overall phenotype. The scores of PhenoScore, REVEL and AlphaMissense were compared using a two‐sided Mann‐Whitney‐U test to assess statistical significance and a p‐value lower than 0.05 was considered significant.


### Further Validation of the Framework
  To gain further insight into the optimal number of individuals needed for a framework such as PhenoScore to function, additional analysis was conducted with a varying number of individuals. The objective was to substantiate the initial hypothesis regarding the model's predictive capabilities, namely that they improve with an increasing number of entries [17]. To this end, five datasets were created, each containing between 10 and 50 individuals. The datasets were constructed incrementally by adding 10 new individuals at a time to the existing dataset, ensuring that the larger datasets included all individuals from the smaller ones.


## Results

### Demographic Composition
  The study cohort comprised 93 individuals (n = 93) with variants in ANKRD11. For three individuals, an estimate of the person's age in the photograph had to be made in advance since their age at the moment the photograph was taken was unknown. For one adult participant, only a photograph at pediatric age was available, so this patient had to be excluded. The training of PhenoScore involved a total of 79 individuals (n = 79) with a diagnosis of KGBS based on a truncating variant in ANKRD11 or a deletion of (part of) the gene, that met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. To subsequently test the ability of PhenoScore to identify phenotypic concordance with KBGS in individuals carrying missense variants, a dataset consisting of 14 individuals (n = 14) with missense variants in ANKRD11 was used.

  The median age of the total number of individuals was 9.0 years. The sex distribution was 63% male and 37% female, indicative of a slight male predominance in the cohort. From the in‐house database, the model was unable to find a suitable matched control for three individuals with truncating variants, so the final analysis was performed with 76 individuals (n = 76).


### Clinical Features
  The majority of individuals included in our dataset (n = 93) exhibited abnormalities of the ears, nose, and mouth, including dental abnormalities such as macrodontia. Moreover, developmental delays, intellectual disability, and behavioral issues, including autism, were prevalent. The primary somatic abnormalities involve congenital heart defects and digestive system abnormalities, as well as hearing and vision problems. ## Results

### Training Phase
  The trained PhenoScore model performed with high accuracy, achieving a prediction score of 0.95 for KBG syndrome. This score represents the AUC of the model when both facial features and phenotypic data are included in the analysis. In addition to the combined score, the model also displays separate scores when the analyses were performed using only facial data or phenotypic data.

  Moreover, the Brier score, which measures the accuracy of quantitative predictions, was calculated to be 0.089. This low Brier score indicates that the model's predictions closely correspond to the actual outcomes, with a smaller score representing more accurate predictions. Statistical analysis within the framework yielded a p‐value smaller than 1.35e‐06 (Figure 1).


## Results

### Testing Phase
  The model was trained with individuals who had a truncating ANKRD11 variant or a deletion of the ANKRD11 gene and then used to test individuals with ANKRD11 missense variants. A group of individuals with confirmed pathogenic missense variants in ANKRD11 and KBG syndrome, as well as missense variants classified as VUS, were examined to determine the accuracy of the model in classifying the variant as (likely) pathogenic, VUS, or (likely) benign when an individual is associated with a control.

  At this stage, the model produced a PhenoScore prediction score ranging from 0 to 1, along with a facial heatmap generated by LIME. A score below 0.3 indicates no phenotypic match with KBGS, 0.3 to 0.7 is inconclusive, and above 0.7 indicates phenotypic concordance with KBGS. The average prediction score of five iterations is displayed above the facial heatmaps (Figure 2).

  In nine out of 14 individuals, the model identified a phenotypic match, suggesting a strong correlation between the phenotypic and facial features and KBG syndrome. In three individuals, the prediction score was insufficient (0.52, 0.53, and 0.64) to classify it as pathogenic; thus, the model continued to designate it as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS). In two individuals, the model was unable to establish a correlation between the extracted facial features and phenotypic data and the phenotype of KBGS (0.27 and 0.26), resulting in a classification of “No Match.” The mean PhenoScore prediction score for the entire set (n = 14) was 0.76 (Table 2).


### Evaluation of the PhenoScore Framework
  The study compared the prediction scores of PhenoScore with the pathogenicity scores provided by the predictive tools REVEL and AlphaMissense (Table 2). The mean of the PhenoScore output (0.76) for all missense variants was significantly higher than that of REVEL (0.36) (U‐statistic 172, p = 0.0007). However, the comparison between PhenoScore and AlphaMissense indicated no statistically significant difference between the two tools (mean score AlphaMissense 0.68, U‐statistic 100, p = 0.94).

  The effectiveness of the model can be assessed particularly when contrasted with the previous functional work [9] and clinical interpretation of the phenotype by an experienced clinical geneticist. In the individuals where both a loss‐of‐function was established through functional studies [9] and a clinical diagnosis was made by a clinical geneticist, it can be stated with certainty that the missense variant is not a VUS. This conclusion is supported by the geneticist's assessment of the patient's phenotype and the de novo status of the variant, as de novo variants are considered more likely to be pathogenic. In this group of six (n = 6) individuals the mean PhenoScore score was 0.94 versus a mean of 0.86 for AlphaMissense (U‐statistic 14.5, p = 0.63) and 0.47 for REVEL (U‐statistic 36, p = 0.002).

  The results of this subanalysis regarding the evaluation of the framework using five datasets with varying sizes are presented in Figure 3, which illustrates an increasing area under the curve (AUC) and a decreasing Brier score.


### Elaboration on Three 
ANKRD11
 Variants
  Table 2 illustrates that certain missense variants yield different scores in PhenoScore compared to AlphaMissense and REVEL. To gain further insight into the model, we examined these notable variants in greater detail using the matching case, as observed in practice, to identify potential reasons for the discrepancy between the results of PhenoScore, AlphaMissense, and REVEL.


#### Case 1
  A 10‐year‐old Dutch male, with the heterozygous missense variant (c.5162C>T, p.(Thr1721Met), g.89347788G>A) of which the inheritance is unknown, was referred to our clinic with a history of multiple congenital anomalies and neurodevelopmental issues.

  The patient was born with a low birth weight and has exhibited persistent short stature throughout childhood. In terms of development, he has a history of mild intellectual disability and behavioral difficulties, including attention deficit hyperactivity disorder and autism.

  The physical examination revealed multiple dysmorphic features, including macrodontia, a low anterior hairline, and brachycephaly. Abdominal findings included a history of feeding difficulties and recurrent vomiting. Additionally, he has genitourinary anomalies, including hypospadias.

  Furthermore, examination revealed delayed skeletal maturation and an abnormal skin texture. No abnormalities were identified in the cardiovascular, respiratory, or ophthalmological systems.

  A clinical geneticist has determined that the patient's phenotype aligns with the criteria for KBGS. However, genetic testing did not confirm a loss‐of‐function effect [9]. PhenoScore's output for this patient was 0.88; however, the scores obtained with AlphaMissense and REVEL were considerably lower, at 0.21 and 0.42.


#### Case 2
  A 10‐year‐old Dutch female, with a heterozygous de novo missense variant (c.6049G>A, p.(Ala2017Thr), g.89346901C>T) was referred to our clinic with a history of developmental delay, seizures, and behavioral problems.

  The patient was born with a low birth weight and has continued to exhibit a short stature throughout childhood. In terms of her development, she displays mild intellectual disability, along with behavioral issues including attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, autism, and oppositional defiant disorder. Furthermore, the patient experiences both generalized myoclonic and focal seizures.

  Upon physical examination, several dysmorphic features were observed, including an abnormal shape of the eyes, anteverted nares, a prominent nasal bridge, macrodontia, and an abnormal philtrum. Furthermore, a history of recurrent otitis media has been documented.

  A clinical diagnosis of KBGS was made by a clinical geneticist and the pathogenicity of this variant was confirmed [9]; reduced protein stability was demonstrated by using fluorescence assays. These findings are in accordance with the results produced by PhenoScore, which yielded a score of 0.98. AlphaMissense and REVEL yielded considerably lower scores of 0.08 and 0.06.


#### Case 3
  A 16‐year‐old Dutch female, with a heterozygous de novo missense variant (c.1200G>C, g.89351750C>G, p.(Lys400Asn)), was seen in our clinic with a medical history of neurological, developmental, and physical abnormalities.

  In terms of her developmental trajectory, she presented with a moderate intellectual disability, accompanied by a range of behavioral issues, including hyperactivity, attention deficit disorder, and features suggestive of an autism spectrum disorder. She has a history of both generalized and focal seizures.

  The physical examination revealed craniofacial dysmorphisms, including low‐set ears and hypertelorism. Additionally, retinal abnormalities and progressive hearing loss were observed, with a history of recurrent otitis media and conductive hearing impairment.

  Furthermore, a congenital heart defect, urogenital abnormalities, delayed skeletal maturation, and short stature were part of the phenotype.

  According to the ACMG criteria, the variant is classified as VUS [10]. With regard to the clinical interpretation of the phenotype, the features do not appear to be classical for KBGS, since the KBGS phenotype is highly variable. Therefore, KBGS cannot be ruled out purely based on the phenotype. Furthermore, the variant has not been tested functionally. PhenoScore yielded a score of 0.27, while AlphaMissense demonstrated a higher score of 0.86, and REVEL exhibited a lower score of 0.20.


## Discussion
  Although the causal association between variants in the ANKRD11 gene and KBG syndrome has been demonstrated many times [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8], it remains underdiagnosed in practice due to the variability of clinical manifestations. The aim of this study was to investigate the ability of an AI‐based phenomics framework, PhenoScore [17], to identify phenotypic concordance with KBGS in individuals carrying missense variants in ANKRD11, thereby providing phenotypic evidence to support variant interpretation, as these variants are often classified as VUS. By correlating the results of PhenoScore to functional data and validating the results, this study is the next step in bringing AI tools such as PhenoScore to clinical practice. The results of PhenoScore were compared with several predictive tools and the functional work of the missense variants in combination with the clinical‐ and functional interpretation of clinical geneticists to make a statement about the effectiveness of the model.

  First, the results demonstrate that PhenoScore is an effective tool for discriminating between individuals with KBGS and unrelated neurodevelopmental disorders. This is supported by the high accuracy (AUC 0.95) observed in the training phase, which suggests that PhenoScore may have clinical value as the output correlates with the pathogenicity of truncating variants leading to KBG syndrome. Moreover, in the testing phase, PhenoScore predictions for individuals with missense variants in ANKRD11 demonstrated its significant potential to discriminate pathogenic variants from (likely) benign variants. For all missense variants for which pathogenicity was functionally confirmed [9] and for whom a clinical diagnosis of KBGS was available based on their phenotype, PhenoScore generated a score indicating pathogenicity—with an average predictive score of 0.94.

  The comparison of the mean PhenoScore results (0.94) with the pathogenicity scores provided by REVEL (0.47) and AlphaMissense (0.86) revealed a number of interesting observations. The observed differences in scoring between these tools highlight the multifaceted nature of variant pathogenicity assessment.

  It is worth noting that some missense variant scores exhibited significant discrepancies between PhenoScore, AlphaMissense, and REVEL. Functional testing was conducted on Case 1, but the variant did not show a loss‐of‐function effect. However, the patient's phenotype met the criteria for KBG syndrome. These findings are reflected in the scores, as PhenoScore rated the variant as pathogenic based on the phenotype, while AlphaMissense and REVEL generated scores that allow the variant to be classified as benign and VUS. It demonstrates that PhenoScore may generate a false‐positive result, if the phenotype of the patient is highly suggestive of the syndrome or that a causative variant is yet to be identified.

  In Case 2, both AlphaMissense and REVEL provided low scores (0.08 and 0.06 respectively), although functional testing demonstrated a clear loss‐of‐function effect for this variant. PhenoScore accurately provided a high score (0.98) for this individual, corresponding with the functional testing—and demonstrating its clinical utility, since molecular prediction scores were incorrectly low.

  In Case 3, Phenoscore classified the variant as benign (0.27) with respect to the phenotype. This was despite the clinicians' finding that the patient's phenotype was suggestive of KBG syndrome, however lacking the “typical” facial dysmorphic features. This result demonstrates that facial features may have played a more significant role in the classification process than other phenotypic characteristics. This discrepancy may be attributed to the quantity of phenotypic data available for the individual in question. To achieve a score that aligns with the clinicians' findings, it may be necessary to obtain additional data from this subject. Furthermore, it is noteworthy that AlphaMissense classified this variant as pathogenic (0.86) and REVEL as benign (0.20).

  Nevertheless, the higher mean prediction score of PhenoScore for the six clinically confirmed pathogenic missense variants is indicative of the added value of integrating facial and phenotypic data, which encompasses the broader spectrum of clinical manifestations. This integrative approach appears to be a more robust method for capturing the complexity of genotype–phenotype correlations than tools that analyze genetic data in isolation.

  The results of this study demonstrate a notable enhancement of the predictive capacity of PhenoScore in comparison to a previous analysis of ANKRD11 variants based upon 22 patients [17]. The area under the curve (AUC) increased from 0.78 to 0.95, which can be partly attributed to the extended number of patients included in the analysis. However, the number of patients in that study was limited due to the lack of Biobank consent or the availability of facial photographs, which restricted the scope of the study. Moreover, the number of missense variants that were tested for pathogenicity using functional assays was limited, thus precluding a comprehensive assessment of PhenoScore's actual accuracy. Additionally, several patients in the cohort carried deletions encompassing not only ANKRD11 but also neighboring genes. As these flanking genes may also influence the phenotype, this could have affected the clinical presentation and, consequently, the PhenoScore results. Although exclusion was not deemed necessary (since the patients were diagnosed with KBG syndrome), this should be considered when interpreting the phenotypic variance within the cohort.

  It is important to acknowledge a key conceptual distinction: PhenoScore assesses phenotypic concordance with KBGS, not variant pathogenicity directly. A high PhenoScore indicates that an individual's phenotype closely matches KBGS, which provides supporting evidence for variant interpretation—specifically strengthening application of the PP4 criterion (phenotype highly specific for a disease with single genetic etiology) within the ACMG framework. However, phenotype matching alone does not establish that a specific variant is causal. Theoretically, an individual could present with a KBGS‐concordant phenotype while carrying a benign missense variant alongside an undetected causal event (such as a structural variant not identified by standard sequencing). Therefore, PhenoScore should be used as one line of evidence within a comprehensive variant interpretation framework, integrated with molecular prediction scores such as AlphaMissense and, where available, functional studies. This approach aligns with Bayesian variant interpretation principles, where phenotypic evidence can appropriately increase the weight of PP4 from supporting to strong or very strong when quantified objectively.

  It is worth comparing PhenoScore with Face2Gene (FDNA Inc.), a widely used AI‐based tool for clinical diagnosis of genetic syndromes. Face2Gene also employs facial recognition technology and has been used to support clinical diagnosis, particularly in patients carrying VUS. Face2Gene provides a ranked list of potential syndrome matches with qualitative scoring (high, medium, or low), which has proven clinically useful for syndrome identification. However, there are key differences between the two tools. PhenoScore generates a continuous quantitative prediction score between 0 and 1, enabling more granular and objective assessment that can be directly integrated into the ACMG variant classification framework as supporting evidence for the PP4 criterion. In contrast, Face2Gene's qualitative output is less amenable to formal variant interpretation frameworks. Additionally, PhenoScore integrates both facial features and HPO‐based phenotypic data in a combined model, whereas Face2Gene primarily relies on facial analysis with syndrome suggestions. Furthermore, PhenoScore is open‐source, allowing for transparency and reproducibility, while Face2Gene is a proprietary commercial platform. A limitation of PhenoScore is that it requires a syndrome‐specific training set, whereas Face2Gene covers a broader range of syndromes. Both tools share the inherent limitation that phenotypic similarity does not prove variant causality.

  An important consideration for the clinical utility of PhenoScore is its ability to distinguish KBGS from phenotypically overlapping conditions, particularly Cornelia de Lange syndrome (CdLS). CdLS shares several features with KBGS, including intellectual disability, limb abnormalities, short stature, and craniofacial dysmorphisms such as synophrys and prominent eyebrows. However, the PhenoScore framework is specifically designed to discriminate between individuals with KBGS and controls with other neurodevelopmental disorders, as the control group in our training phase consisted of individuals with various NDDs evaluated at our outpatient clinic, which may include individuals with CdLS. The high AUC of 0.95 suggests that PhenoScore can effectively distinguish KBGS from other NDDs, including those with overlapping features. Nevertheless, a dedicated analysis comparing PhenoScore performance specifically in KBGS versus CdLS cohorts would further strengthen the clinical validity of the tool and is an important direction for future research.

  Further studies are therefore required to validate these performance results, particularly given the greater ethnic variation in the study cohorts. It is also of importance to determine the influence of missense variants that have not been subjected to functional testing. One possible method could be to examine the DNA methylation signature of ANKRD11 as a supplementary approach to the current methodologies [23]. This could potentially enhance the interpretation of genotype–phenotype correlations and strengthen the accurate results of PhenoScore with greater significance. Moreover, it is essential to conduct multiple prospective validation studies before PhenoScore can be integrated into clinical practice.

  In conclusion, the integration of an AI‐based framework with traditional genomic approaches, as exemplified in this study, represents a promising avenue for the accurate identification and interpretation of genetic variants. Future research should include the expansion of training datasets and the inclusion of a broader range of ethnicities. Nevertheless, PhenoScore can assist clinicians in the interpretation of variants of uncertain significance (VUS), thereby improving diagnostics for individuals with KBGS, especially when combined with molecular scores such as AlphaMissense.
