# miR-223-3p improves macrophage polarization balance and mitochondrial metabolism to ameliorate uveitis by orchestrating P65Lys310 acetylation through RBPJ/HDAC1 axis


## Background
  Uveitis is an autoimmune disease characterized by iris, ciliary muscle, and choroid inflammation. miR-223-3p, an anti-inflammatory microRNA, can regulate the expression of inflammatory genes involved in the disease process. However, the role and potential mechanism of miR-223-3p against uveitis remain unclear.


## Methods
  STRING website prediction, molecular docking, and co-IP experiments were performed to verify whether there was an interaction between RBPJ-HDAC1, HDAC1-P65, and P65-ARG1. Based on ex vivo and in vivo experiments, we detected NF-κB P65lys310 acetylation, P65 nuclear translocation, and the level of M1/M2 macrophage polarization. In addition, we also determined the levels of mitochondrial pressure and calcium flux under different conditions. Regulation of NF-κB P65lys310 acetylation via the RBPJ/HDAC1 axis affects macrophage polarization and mitochondrial function.


## Results
  We first found reduced miR-223-3p expression, elevated RBPJ and total acetylation levels, and an imbalance in macrophage polarization in peripheral blood monocyte-derived macrophages from patients with uveitis. Co-IP experiments supported the interaction between RBPJ and HDAC1, and HDAC1, as a key deacetylase, could inhibit NF-κB P65lys310 acetylation. Notably, overactivation of NF-κB P65lys310 acetylation levels in uveitis leads to elevated polarization of M1 macrophages and mitochondrial dysfunction. miR-223-3p can attenuate NF-κB P65lys310 acetylation and P65 nuclear translocation levels through the RBPJ/HDAC1 axis in uveitis, effectively reducing pro-inflammatory macrophage levels and mitigating mitochondrial damage, thereby reducing ocular inflammation and positively regulating the intraocular microenvironment in uveitis.


## Conclusion
  miR-223-3p can inhibit P65lys310 acetylation and enhance mitochondrial function to improve M1/M2 macrophage polarization balance to ameliorate uveitis through RBPJ/HDAC1 axis.

## Introduction
  Autoimmune uveitis is a chronic systemic disease mainly characterized by intraocular inflammation that seriously threatens patients’ visual health and even leads to blindness [1, 2]. Currently, the most effective clinical treatments rely on hormonal therapies that exert a strong anti-inflammatory effect. However, such hormonal treatments may cause significant ocular side effects (e.g., elevated intraocular pressure and cataracts) as well as systemic complications (e.g., adrenocortical insufficiency and osteonecrosis) [3]. Therefore, these treatments are not recommended as a suitable long-term option.

  Experimental autoimmune uveitis (EAU) primarily targets retinal antigens, which are considered immunologically privileged, and serves as the most widely used animal model for studying autoimmune uveitis. Despite this, clinical trials targeting interleukin (IL)-17A for the treatment of uveitis have not yielded successful results [4]. Consequently, there is an urgent need to investigate the pathogenesis of autoimmune uveitis further and identify new therapeutic targets to provide effective treatment options in clinical settings.

  Macrophages can be divided into two main phenotypes: classically activated (M1) and selectively activated (M2) under different conditions. Macrophage polarization is a dynamic process, with M1 and M2 being the two extremes in the continuous spectrum of macrophage polarization states. M1 macrophages are activated by lipopolysaccharides (LPS) or interferon (IFN)-γ and secrete pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-6, IL-1β, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, and produce large amounts of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), contributing to inflammatory responses [5]. M2 macrophages are usually activated by IL-4 or IL-13 and produce the anti-inflammatory factors IL-10, transforming growth factor (TGF)-β, and arginase 1 (Arg1), which inhibit the inflammatory response. Numerous studies have shown that macrophage polarization imbalance is one of the essential causes of the development of autoimmune diseases, such as Crohn’s disease, rheumatoid arthritis [6–8], and multiple sclerosis. Although uveitis is primarily driven by T cells, its onset, progression, and resolution are closely linked to the active involvement of innate immune cells. Notably, M2-like macrophages, known for their anti-inflammatory metabolic profile, can directly inhibit pathogenic T cells and promote the generation of regulatory T cells (Tregs) by secreting cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β. Hence, macrophages may serve as upstream targets for T cell regulation. Investigating how drugs regulate macrophage metabolism can provide valuable insights into the broader immune microenvironment.

  Modulating macrophage polarization and managing the balance between M1 and M2 phenotypes represent promising therapeutic strategies for autoimmune diseases [9]. Recent advancements in immunometabolism research have highlighted that the functional characteristics of immune cells are closely linked to their metabolic programs. Specific to macrophages, the pro-inflammatory (M1) phenotype relies on glycolysis, rapidly producing adenosine triphosphate (ATP) to meet the energy needs of its acute inflammatory response. The M2 phenotype relies more on oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and fatty acid oxidation to maintain its long-term tissue repair function. Therefore, the energy metabolism state of a cell is the “central controller” of its functional activity [10].

  Multiple networks of signaling pathways are now well established to be involved in macrophage polarization homeostasis, including the PI3K-AKT, NF-κB, and JAK-STAT signaling pathways [11, 12]. Here, we open a new perspective on macrophage polarization, focusing on the involvement of the RBPJ/histone deacetylase 1 (HDAC1)/NF-κB P65 axis in macrophage polarization and mitochondrial injury.

  Emerging evidence suggests that epigenetic modifications are strongly associated with macrophage polarization. Lysine acetylation, the posttranslational addition of acetyl groups to the ε-amines of lysine residues, is a widespread posttranslational modification (PTM). The acetylation state is a reversible process driven by HDAC and histone acetyltransferase (HAT) [13]. The balance between these two enzymes controls the expression of a wide range of genes, affecting various biological processes [14]. In mammals, HDACs are classified into four distinct classes. Class I includes HDACs 1–3 and 8, class II comprises HDACs 4–7, 9 and 10, class III consists of sirtuin 1–7, and class IV includes HDAC 11 [15].

  HDAC1 is a class I HDAC that is ubiquitously expressed, predominantly located in the nucleus, and exerts its function primarily by deacetylating histone and nonhistone proteins [16], coordinating cell signaling networks. HDAC1, like all deacetylases, lacks a DNA-binding domain and fulfills its function by interacting with transcription factors as a homo- or heterodimer or as part of a multicomponent repressor complex [17]. As a result, HDAC1 need to associate with other proteins to be directed to its chromatin target. However, the regulatory mechanisms by which HDAC1 operates in autoimmune uveitis are largely unknown.

  MiR-223 is an evolutionarily conserved anti-inflammatory microRNA expressed mainly in neutrophils and macrophages and is involved in the control of maintaining myeloid homeostasis [18, 19]. As a negative feedback mechanism, miR-223 regulates the expression of inflammatory genes at the post-transcriptional level. miR-223-3p reduces the expression of the NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, thereby attenuating inflammatory monocyte infiltration and alleviating the inflammatory response [20, 21]. Additionally, miR-223-3p activates the macrophage MAPK pathway and modulates macrophage polarization [22], inhibiting the core inflammatory NF-κB pathway and exerting beneficial anti-inflammatory effects. However, the molecular mechanisms through which miR-223-3p regulates macrophage polarization homeostasis in autoimmune uveitis remain poorly understood.

  In this study, we first identified elevated levels of RBPJ and total acetylation in peripheral blood monocyte-derived macrophages from patients with uveitis. To investigate whether these molecules are involved in the pathogenesis of uveitis, we examined protein interactions and confirmed that NF-κB P65 undergoes acetylation. Furthermore, we identified that P65 is associated with mitochondrial metabolism and macrophage polarization. On the basis of these findings, we hypothesized that miR-223-3p may modulate mitochondrial oxidative stress and macrophage polarization by regulating the RBPJ/HDAC1/NF-κB P65 signaling axis, thereby alleviating uveitis. Our study offers new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying the anti-inflammatory effects of miR-223-3p in the treatment of uveitis.

  Blood samples (2–4 ml) were collected from all healthy volunteers and patients with uveitis to isolate monocytes. The Human Monocyte Extraction Kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China) was used for this purpose. Monocytes were then stimulated with macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF). The cells were cultured in cell culture dishes (NEST Biotechnology, Wuxi, China) for 6 days before undergoing various interventions.


### Experimental animals
  This study purchased 6–8 weeks Lewis rats (160–180 g) from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The rats were randomly assigned to five groups: the NC group, the EAU group, the miR-223-3p empty vector (miR-223-3p-N) group, and the different doses (5 μl or 10 μl) of miR-223-3p-carrying lentivirus (virus titer: 4.68E8 TU/ml) groups. Mixtures containing interphotoreceptor vitamin A-binding protein (IRBP, Shanghai Bioengineering, Shanghai, China), complete Freund’s adjuvant (CFA, Sigma, USA), Mycobacterium tuberculosis (TB, Difco, USA), and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were vortexed for 10 min with a handheld high-speed disperser (Xinzhi Bio., Ningbo, China) to make a celiac fluid. Rats in the NC group were injected with 200 μl of a mixture containing only CFA and TB (without IRBP peptide) at the same site. Meanwhile, rats in the EAU group, miR-223-2p-N group, and miR-223-3p group were injected subcutaneously with approximately 200 μl of chyme at the bilateral abdomen, the soles of the feet, and the posterior part of the neck to induce EAU. After immunization, rats in the miR-223-2p-N group received an injection of the lentiviral empty vector on day 0, while rats in the miR-223-3p group received injections of either 5 μl or 10 μl of the miR-223-3p-carrying lentivirus (Genomeditech, Shanghai, China) directly into the vitreous cavity.

  After 12 days of immunization, the spleen, lymph nodes, and eye tissues from the rats in each group were collected for subsequent analyses. The eye tissues used in this study were obtained after removal of the cornea and lens, mainly comprising the retina, choroid, ciliary body, and iris.


### Scanning laser ophthalmoscopy (SLO) and swept-source optical coherence tomography angiography (SS-OCTA)
  The inflammatory reaction in the fundus was observed using a scanning laser detector (Optos, Shanghai, China) in each group of rats after 12 days of immunization. The blood flow density in the retina was measured using SS-OCTA (TowardPi BMizar, TowardPi Medical Technology, Beijing, China), and the measurements were repeated three times. The software can calculate the average retinal blood flow density in each quadrant.


### Molecular docking
  The three-dimensional (3D) structures of the proteins RBPJ, HDAC1, P65, and ARG1 were obtained from the PDB Protein Database. Each protein was imported into ZDOCK 3.0.2, where rigid docking was applied to predict interactions across the entire protein. The results of the docking were analyzed using Discovery Studio.


### Rat primary macrophage cell culture
  Twelve days post-immunization, rats in the NC and EAU groups were euthanized, the spleen, lymph nodes, and eye tissues were isolated to prepare cell suspensions. Monocytes were collected using a rat monocyte extraction kit from Solarbio (Beijing, China). We prepared a medium containing 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS) from NEST Biotechnology (Wuxi, China) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin from Servicebio (Wuhan, China). Additionally, the medium was supplemented with 50 ng/ml of M-CSF from Novoprotein (Suzhou, China).


### miRNA transfection
  Primary cultured macrophages were transfected with miR-223-3p-carrying lentivirus or shRNA-RBPJKD (viral titer: 4.87E8 TU/ml), respectively. The transfected cells were incubated in cell culture dishes (NEST Biotechnology, Wuxi, China) at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 48–72 h.


### Real-time fluorescent quantitative PCR (Q-PCR)
  Total RNA was extracted from rat tissue and human macrophage cells according to the SparkEasy RNA Extraction Kit (Shandong Sparkjade Science Co., Ltd., Jinan, China) and reverse transcribed according to the manufacturer’s instructions using the reverse transcription reagent (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). Real-time PCR was conducted in a 96-well plate (NEST Biotech., Wuxi, China) using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix on a Roche LightCycler® 480 fluorescence quantification system. β-Actin served as an internal reference for the genes iNOS, Arg-1, RBPJ, and HDAC1. ### Histopathology, immunofluorescence, immunohistochemistry, and laser confocal microscopy
  Rat tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin–eosin (H&E). The clinical inflammation and pathology scores were performed according to previously established criteria [23]. For immunostaining, tissue sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following antibodies: anti-RBPJ (Bioss, Beijing), anti-HDAC1 (ABclonal Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China), anti-P65Lys310 acetylation (Ac-P65Lys310) (Abmart Shanghai Co., Ltd., China), anti-P65 (proteintech, Wuhan, China), and anti-Arg1 (ABclonal Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China), and anti-iNOS (ABclonal Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China). After incubation, the sections were washed with PBS, and a secondary antibody was applied for 50 min at room temperature. A laser confocal microscope (Zeiss) was used for observation.


### Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
  The macrophages in each group were cultured after different treatments for 48–72 h. Subsequently, the supernatants were collected, and the levels of IL-1β, TNF-α, Arg1, and IL-10 were measured using ELISA kits (Jianglai Biotechnology Co. Ltd., Shanghai, China).


### Flow cytometry
  Macrophages were initially stained with antibodies against F4/80-FITC and CD11b-APC (Bioss, Beijing, China) for 40 min. After staining, the cells were fixed for 1 h. Subsequently, staining was performed using CD86-PE and CD206-PE (eBioscince, Inc., USA), respectively. Finally, the levels of macrophages were analyzed using flow cytometry (Agilent Technologies, USA).


### Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)
  Protein lysates were collected using IP lysate (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). The cell lysates were incubated overnight with Protein A/G magnetic beads (Vazyme, Cat: PB101-02) and antibodies at 4 °C. Then, the protein complexes on the beads were eluted for the indicated experiments.


### Western blot
  Total protein extraction from rat tissues and human macrophages was collected using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA). The samples were loaded onto a 10% one-step colorful sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) gel (Shandong Sparkjade Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Jinan, China) and subsequently transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. After being blocked with 5% skim milk at room temperature, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with the following antibodies: RBPJ (abcam, UK), NF-κB P65, iNOS, HDAC1 (Abclonal, Wuhan, China), Ac-P65Lys310 (Cell Signaling Technology, USA), acetylation lysine, and β-actin (Proteintech, Wuhan, China). Afterward, the membrane was washed with Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST), and a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-coupled secondary antibody was added and incubated for 1 h. Finally, the target proteins were visualized using a Sparkjade ECL star Kit (Shandong Sparkjade Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Jinan, China) with a FUSION-FX7 imaging system (Vilber Lourmat, France).


### Noninvasive microtest technology (NMT)
  We utilized non-invasive micro-test technology (Xuyue (Beijing) Sci. & Tech. Co., Ltd, Beijing, China) to measure the Ca2+ flux in isolated rat eye tissue. The test solution is first added to the Petri dish, then the rats are sacrificed, and the eye tissue is collected and quickly placed into the precooled, oxygenated test solution. The Ca2+-sensitive microelectrodes were filled with a liquid Ca2+ exchanger and calibrated accordingly. The Ca2+ flux data were collected at a sampling rate of one point every 4 s, and real-time data were recorded for a total of 6 min.


### Cell Mito stress test
  Eighty microliters of macrophages (1.25 × 106 cells/ml) were seeded in XF96 Pro cell culture plates. Cells were cultured overnight in a 5% CO2 incubator. The next day, the cells were rinsed twice with Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) medium. The drugs Oli (oleomycin), FCCP (phenylhydrazone), and Rot|AA (Rotenone and antimycin A) were added to the probe plate. Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was measured in real-time using the Seahorse XF96 Pro extracellular flux analyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA).


### Mitochondrial membrane potential, ROS, mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I (NADH-CoQ reductase) activity, and ATP content
  Mitochondrial membrane potential, ROS, NADH-CoQ reductase activity, and ATP content were measured using the JC-1 assay kit (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), the ROS assay kit (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology), and the ATP content kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China), respectively. All assays were conducted following the manufacturer’s protocol.


### Statistical analysis
  All experiments were conducted at least three times. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 7.0 software and are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For comparisons among two or more groups, statistical significance was assessed using SPSS 26.0. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed for this purpose, with P-values less than 0.05 considered significant.

  The visual field examination demonstrated significant visual field defects in patients with uveitis compared with normal subjects, with darker colors indicating more severe visual field defects) (Fig. 1B). SLO imaging results clearly visualized the vitreous cavity in normal subjects. In contrast, in cases of uveitis, the fundus structure was obscured, and there were notable inflammatory exudates (Fig. 1C). Additionally, ultrasound results showed inflammatory exudates in the vitreous cavity of patients with uveitis (Fig. 1D).

  To study macrophage polarization in uveitis, we examined the expression of macrophage polarization marker iNOS, Arg1 in uveitis. First, we identified abnormalities in gene and protein expression of iNOS, Arg1 in peripheral blood monocyte-derived macrophages of uveitis, with high expression of Arg1 and low expression of iNOS (Fig. 1E, F). This finding suggests a shift in macrophage polarization toward M1 macrophages in uveitis. Subsequently, we examined the total protein acetylation levels in patients’ peripheral blood monocyte-derived macrophages. We found that the overall level of protein acetylation in macrophages was elevated (Fig. 1G–J).

  Acetylation is an essential posttranslational modification of proteins. HDAC1, a deacetylase, is distributed on almost all cell surfaces and can affect the level of protein acetylation. To verify whether HDAC1 is involved in the pathogenesis of uveitis, we further examined HDAC1 expression and found that HDAC1 was lowly expressed in peripheral blood monocyte-derived macrophages of uveitis (Fig. 1K, L). These results suggest an imbalance in macrophage M1/M2 polarization and an abnormal level of pan-acetylation in macrophages of patients with uveitis, which may be associated with HDAC1.


### MiR-223-3p negatively regulates RBPJ expression
  We found that miR-223-3p was lowly expressed in peripheral blood monocyte-derived macrophages of patients with uveitis and eye tissues of EAU rats (Fig. 1M, N). In contrast, RBPJ mRNA was highly expressed in eye tissues of EAU rats (Fig. 1O), and that RBPJ protein was highly expressed in peripheral blood monocyte-derived macrophages of patients with uveitis and eye tissues of EAU rats (Fig. 1P–R). Additionally, a dual luciferase assay showed that miR-223-3p negatively regulates the RBPJ expression (Fig. 1S, T).

  It is noteworthy that several sites where HDAC1 docks with the P65 molecule include the lysine 310 site of P65, which is one of the vital acetylation sites of P65 [24]. Building on the aforementioned experiments, which indicated abnormal levels of total acetylation in peripheral blood monocyte-derived macrophages in uveitis, we hypothesized that the lysine 310 residue of P65 may undergo acetylation in the context of uveitis. Co-IP assay of human peripheral blood monocyte-derived macrophages revealed that P65 was indeed acetylated (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, our analysis revealed that HDAC1 interacted with both P65 and acetylated P65 at lysine 310 (Fig. 2D).

  We further determined whether there were abnormal P65 and Ac-P65Lys310 expression levels in peripheral blood monocyte-derived macrophages of uveitis by western blotting (Fig. 2G–I). Recent studies have shown that Ca2+ can exacerbate oxidant-induced mitochondrial damage [34], highlighting its close association with mitochondrial dysfunction. Therefore, we monitored Ca2+ flux in live eye tissues of EAU rats using noninvasive microtest technology (NMT) (Fig. 9E–G). ## Discussion
  miR-223 plays a crucial role in regulating various inflammatory signaling pathways in macrophages. In this study, we observed a significant reduction in miR-223-3p levels in macrophages from patients with uveitis. Supplementation with miR-223-3p was able to mitigate fundus vasodilation, congestion, and inflammatory exudation associated with uveitis by restoring the M1/M2 polarization balance and reducing inflammatory cell infiltration in eye tissues. Consistent with this finding, reduced miR-223-3p occurred in various diseases such as periodontitis, syphilis spirochete infection, and LPS-induced sepsis, and restoration of miR-223-3p levels can attenuate inflammatory damage [37–39].

  The recombinant signal-binding protein jκ (RBPjκ) is a key transcriptional regulator of the Notch signaling pathway [40]. Upon Notch activation, the intracellular domain of the Notch receptor (NICD) translocates to the nucleus, where it binds to RBPjκ, thereby activating the transcription of target genes, including those of the Hes family. In contrast, in the absence of Notch signaling, RBPjκ associates with co-repressor complexes, such as HDAC-containing histone deacetylase complexes, to repress transcription and modulate the expression levels of downstream target genes. Histone deacetylase (HDAC) tightens chromatin structure and represses transcription and gene expression by removing acetyl groups from histones or nonhistones [17]. In our study, molecular docking and protein interaction assays revealed that RBPJ has a high binding capacity to HDAC1 in human and rats. HDAC1 is markedly reduced in uveitis, indicating its potential protective role in the disease. While HDAC1 is crucial in the pathogenesis of uveitis, its role in regulating inflammation remains controversial, as it may exhibit either anti-inflammatory or pro-inflammatory effects depending on the disease context. For instance, in atherosclerotic endothelial cells, HDAC1 suppresses the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α [16]. However, Li et al. reported that HDAC1 is a key pro-inflammatory molecule in a depression model [41]. Consistent with our results, Wang HK et al. observed that HDAC1 deficiency in the brain leads to elevated ROS levels and inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-1β, exacerbating neuroinflammation [42]. Our data similarly support that HDAC1 dysfunction can activate ROS and thus exacerbate inflammation. Importantly, we build upon previous studies and present new evidence demonstrating that HDAC1 directly regulates Ac-P65Lys310 in uveitis, thereby driving macrophage mitochondrial oxidative stress and inflammatory responses. Additionally, miR-223-3p-induced inhibition of NF-κB activity is largely dependent on the activation of HDAC1 in macrophages. Collectively, our findings suggest that HDAC1 deficiency in uveitis plays a pivotal role in the polarization imbalance and mitochondrial dysfunction observed in M1/M2 macrophages.

  HDAC1 inactivates NF-κB signaling by binding to and deacetylating P65 [28]. Similar to phosphorylation, P65 acetylation is crucial for the nuclear activity of NF-κB [24]. Acetylation at distinct lysine residues on P65 can differentially modulate NF-κB function. Zhang et al. identified seven acetylated lysine residues on P65, namely 122, 123, 218, 221, 310, 314, and 315 [43]. This is consistent with our molecular docking results, which show that HDAC1 specifically targets the lysine 310 and 314 sites on the P65 subunit. Moreover, studies have reported that acetylation of the lysine 310 site of the P65 subunit is essential for full transcriptional activation of NF-κB [25]. Therefore, our study mainly focused on exploring the P65lys310 site in uveitis.

  Recent reports have highlighted the activation of NF-κB as a significant factor in the polarization of M1 macrophages. However, a direct target of the NF-κB pathway that influences macrophage polarization has yet to be confirmed. Initially, we hypothesized that NF-κB P65 might directly regulate iNOS to promote M1 polarization; however, Co-IP experiments did not yield the expected results. Consequently, we conducted additional experiments to investigate the interaction between P65 and Arg1. Interestingly, the Co-IP analysis revealed that the NF-κB subunit P65 interacts with Arg1, a key macrophage surface marker. A possible explanation is that P65 has a higher binding activity with Arg1 because the binding energy value of P65 with Arg1 (−10.1 cal/mol) is lower than that of P65 with iNOS (−9 cal/mol). This experiment provides new insights into the direct regulation of macrophage polarization by NF-κB. It helps to explain the inhibition of the macrophage M2 polarization program during inflammation through increased levels of P65 acetylation.

  The NF-κB signaling pathway is activated in various chronic inflammatory conditions, including rheumatoid arthritis, periodontitis, and rosacea-like dermatitis. It plays a pivotal role in promoting M1 macrophage polarization while inhibiting M2 macrophage differentiation [44–46]. NF-κB-mediated transactivation of the nuclear compartment is essential for initiating inflammation in response to multiple stimuli. Historically, research targeting the NF-κB pathway has largely focused on the phosphorylation events that drive its activity. However, recent studies have highlighted the acetylation of NF-κB as an equally crucial regulatory mechanism in the pathogenesis of inflammatory diseases. Acetylation of the NF-κB subunit P65, for example, has been shown to directly influence its transcriptional activity [47]. Notably, chaperone-mediated autophagy has been identified as a protective mechanism in neurons, where it reduces P65 acetylation via the degradation of P300, thereby mitigating inflammatory damage [26]. Similarly, berberine has been shown to alleviate LPS-induced metabolic disturbances and inflammatory responses by decreasing P65 acetylation in macrophages [48]. Furthermore, Zhang et al. demonstrated that inhibition of P65 acetylation in macrophages significantly attenuated colitis-associated sepsis [43]. In our study, we observed elevated acetylation levels of P65 at lysine 310 in uveitis. Moreover, miR-223-3p was found to exert anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting P65 acetylation at lysine 310, reducing M1 macrophage polarization, and restoring mitochondrial function.

  We observed that mitochondrial damage occurs in uveitis. The damaged mitochondria led to Ca2+ efflux, increased production of ROS, decreased function of mitochondrial respiratory chain complex, reduced ATP levels associated with energy metabolism, and overall mitochondrial respiratory dysfunction. This phenomenon is linked to the activation of NF-κB. In a previous study, it was found that miR-155 activates NF-κB in endothelial cells, contributing to mitochondrial damage [49]. Similarly, Li et al. demonstrated that inhibiting mitochondrial dysfunction and inflammatory response by reducing NF-κB activation alleviates septic kidney injury [32]. Capece et al. suggested that NF-κB influences mitochondrial dynamics and respiration [50]. NF-κB activation has been well-established as a crucial pro-inflammatory signaling event in the context of mitochondrial damage. Interestingly, damaged mitochondria themselves can also activate the NF-κB pathway, thereby amplifying the inflammatory response [51, 52]. This suggests a reciprocal interaction between mitochondrial dysfunction and NF-κB signaling, with both contributing to the amplification of the inflammatory process. Additionally, Mills et al. demonstrated that macrophages initially perceive inflammatory stimuli, which lead to alterations in their mitochondrial metabolism. These changes drive the pro-inflammatory phenotype of macrophages [53]. In summary, NF-κB activation is implicated in the pathogenesis of uveitis, leading to mitochondrial metabolic dysfunction. Furthermore, stressed and damaged mitochondria further amplify NF-κB activation, thereby promoting the polarization of pro-inflammatory macrophages.

  Nevertheless, this study does have some limitations. First, our experiment revealed that HDAC1 can target two sites on the P65 protein: lys310 and lys314. Lys310 is a core site for NF-κB, so we focused on the regulation of P65 Lys310 by miR-223-3p. However, we did not explore the Lys314 site, which warrants further investigation. In addition, we selected P65 to assess the interaction with iNOS and Arg1, and we found that P65 only interacted with Arg1. However, we cannot rule out the possibility that P65 interacts with other surface markers in M1/M2 macrophages. In future studies, we plan to investigate other acetylation sites on P65 and examine its potential interactions with additional macrophage surface markers.


## Conclusions
  Our results suggest that M1 macrophage polarization shifts and inflammatory injury occur in uveitis. miR-223-3p has a protective effect on the inflammatory response in uveitis, especially in inhibiting P65 acetylation through the RBPJ/HDAC1 axis to improve mitochondrial metabolism and thus restore M1/M2 balance. Collectively, miR-223-3p may be a promising anti-inflammatory drug candidate, providing new therapeutic targets and options for treating uveitis in clinical practice.
