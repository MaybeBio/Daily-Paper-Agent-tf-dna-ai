# Gentiopicroside inhibits HMGCR/NF-κB pathway to alleviate renal fibrosis


## Background
  Gentiopicroside (GSP) has been shown to effectively ameliorate renal fibrosis (RF), yet its underlying mechanisms are poorly characterized. This study investigates how GSP alleviates RF using RNA-seq and in vitro experiments.


## Method
  First, an RF cell model was established through TGF-β induced HK-2 cells. The therapeutic effect of GSP on RF was evaluated in vitro through CCK-8 and Transwell assays. RNA-seq was used to identify potential targets of GSP in treating RF, followed by PPI analysis, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses to explore the mechanisms of GSP in alleviating RF. Finally, the mechanisms were comprehensively validated in vitro, and a rescue experiment was conducted by overexpressing HMGCR in HK-2 cells.


## Result
  GSP inhibited TGF-β-induced HK-2 cell proliferation and migration and reduced Collagen I and α-SMA expression in vitro. Differential analysis of control vs. model groups revealed a total of 911 differentially expressed genes, including 374 downregulated and 537 upregulated genes. Of these, 225 genes were reversed after GSP treatment. PPI analysis indicated that HMGCR is the core target of GSP in alleviating RF, and molecular docking demonstrated a strong binding between GSP and HMGCR, which was further confirmed by CETSA. GO enrichment analysis revealed that the improvement of RF by GSP is closely related to biological processes such as cholesterol biosynthesis and metabolism. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis highlighted the significant roles of cholesterol metabolism and the NF-κB signaling pathway in GSP’s effect on RF. Western blot results confirmed that GSP significantly inhibited expression of HMGCR and activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway in TGF-β-induced HK-2 cells. This effect was significantly reversed upon overexpression of HMGCR, indicating that GSP exerts its anti-RF effects by downregulating HMGCR and inhibiting NF-κB pathway activation.


## Conclusion
  This study confirms that GSP can inhibit the HMGCR/NF-κB pathway to protect against TGF-β-induced HK-2 cell injury, exhibiting favorable therapeutic potential for RF.

## Introduction
  Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) is a common renal disorder primarily characterized by progressive decline in kidney function, which, if left untreated, eventually leads to complete renal failure and progresses to end-stage renal disease [1]. Research statistics indicate that approximately 13.4% of the global population is affected by CKD, and its incidence continues to rise annually, placing a heavy burden on socio-economic systems and public health [2]. CKD is associated with multiple etiological factors and a complex pathogenesis, including both primary and secondary causes, such as nephrotic syndrome, gout, and hypertension [3]. Therefore, for CKD caused by different pathogenesis, in addition to symptomatic treatments such as immunosuppressants, corticosteroids, and diuretics, it is also important to consider therapies targeting the underlying conditions, such as antihypertensive and uric acid-lowering treatments [4]. Despite significant efforts over the past decades in managing CKD, the current treatment options remain suboptimal. As the disease progresses, the majority of CKD patients develop renal fibrosis (RF) [5]. RF is the most common irreversible organic lesion of renal tissue, characterized by abnormal extracellular matrix deposition, tubular atrophy, and tubulointerstitial fibrosis [6]. Currently, there are limited methods and drugs available to ameliorate or treat RF [7, 8]. Therefore, it is necessary to develop therapeutic drugs for targeting RF and CKD.

  As a chronic metabolic disease, the pathogenesis of RF is complex, including inflammatory cascades, oxidative stress, hyperuricemia, and lipid accumulation. Among these, hypercholesterolemia and its related inflammation play significant roles in RF [9]. Recent studies have shown that the incidence of RF in CKD patients with hypercholesterolemia is 1.5 times higher than in those with normal cholesterol levels, suggesting that dysregulated cholesterol metabolism is an independent risk factor for RF [10]. Research has confirmed that during RF progression, the plasma concentrations of total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), and very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) are elevated, while the plasma concentration of high-density lipoprotein (HDL) is reduced, which are among the major contributors to the exacerbation of RF progression [11, 12]. 3-Hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) is a key rate-limiting enzyme in cholesterol synthesis. Its expression level and activity directly affect the rate of cholesterol synthesis, playing an important role in regulating cholesterol levels in the body [13]. It is well-known that inhibiting HMGCR lowers the plasma concentrations of TC, TG, LDL, and VLDL while increasing the plasma concentrations of HDL, thus alleviating hypercholesterolemia. Studies have reported that abnormal high expression of TC, TG, VLDL can promote inflammation and exacerbate renal fibrosis, indicating that inhibiting HMGCR is an effective therapeutic strategy for alleviating RF [14]. Statins, such as atorvastatin, are the most common HMGCR inhibitors and have long been used to treat CKD. For example, atorvastatin alleviates CKD by inhibiting oxidative stress [15, 16]. Another study showed that atorvastatin alleviates UUO-induced renal interstitial fibrosis by inhibiting the transcriptional activity of YAP [17]. Furthermore, studies have confirmed that other statins, such as simvastatin and rosuvastatin, also offer significant protective effects against RF [18–20]. In addition to inhibiting HMGCR, statins exhibit pleiotropic effects downstream of the mevalonate pathway, including alleviating fibrosis, lowering blood glucose, and reducing blood pressure. These pleiotropic effects may contribute to the enthusiasm for statin use in the treatment of CKD and RF [21]. However, a drawback of statin therapy is the well-recognized muscle-related side effects, including myalgia, myasthenia, and immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM). These drugs have also been reported to have varying degrees of hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity [13]. Overall, inhibiting HMGCR to improve hypercholesterolemia is indeed an effective strategy to alleviate RF, but statins as HMGCR inhibitors remain suboptimal for the treatment of RF due to their inevitable toxic side effects. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop safer and more effective HMGCR inhibitors for the management of RF.

  Many studies has confirmed the unique advantages of TCM in the prevention and treatment of RF, making it an effective therapeutic strategy for RF [22, 23]. Gentiopicroside (GSP), an iridoid glycoside, is the major bioactive component of Gentiana manshurica Kitag. Phytochemical studies have also confirmed that gentiopicroside is the main active compound in other Gentiana species, such as Gentiana macrophylla [24, 25]. Currently, studies on GSP primarily focuses on anti-inflammatory activity. Studies have shown that GSP exhibits excellent anti-inflammatory effects [26–28]. Another study demonstrated that GSP accelerates diabetic ulcer wound healing by inhibiting wound inflammation [29]. Notably, a study by Xiao et al.. showed that GSP can ameliorate diabetic nephropathy by activating the TGR5 receptor and inhibiting the NF-κB pathway [30]. In a subsequent study by the same team, GSP was found to ameliorate tubulointerstitial fibrosis through the AT1R/CK2/NF-κB axis [31]. These results suggest that GSP may be a potential therapeutic drug for RF treatment. GSP has been shown to significantly inhibit the NF-κB pathway, which may be its primary mechanism to alleviate RF, consistent with our research findings. In conclusion, GSP has been confirmed to alleviate RF through multiple pathways, but our understanding of its potential molecular mechanisms in alleviating RF remains insufficient, warranting further investigation. In this study, RNA-seq was used to analysis the targets and mechanisms of GSP in treating RF and validated these results in vitro. The findings indicate that GSP alleviates RF by regulating cholesterol metabolism through the inhibition of the HMGCR/NF-κB axis. It provides additional evidence for GSP treatment of RF.


## Materials and methods

### Reagents
  Gentiopicroside (Lot: HY-N0494, purity > 99%) was obtained from MedChemexpress LLC (MS, USA); MEM culture medium (PM150410) was purchased from Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China); Fetal Bovine Serum (Lot: A5256701) was purchased from Gibco Life Sciences Co., Ltd.(Brazil); SDS-PAGE precast gel (Lot: MA0284) and RIPA Lysis Buffer (Lot: MA0151) was purchased from Meilun Biotechnology (Dalian, China); Crystal Violet Solution (Lot: C0121), CCK-8 reagent kit (Lot: C0038), and ECL chemiluminescence assay kit (Lot: P0018HS) were purchased from Beyotime Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China); Collagen I antibody (Lot: A21059, Dilution ratio 1:1500), α-SMA antibody (Lot: A17910, Dilution ratio 1:1500), β-actin antibody (Lot: AC026, Dilution ratio 1:3000), HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Lot: AS014, Dilution ratio 1:3000) were acquired from ABclonal Biotechnology (Wuhan, China); NF-κB antibody (Lot: #8242, Dilution ratio 1:1000), p-NF-κB antibody (Ser 536) (Lot: #3033, Dilution ratio 1:1000), IκBα antibody (Lot: #4812, Dilution ratio 1:1500), p-IκBα antibody (Ser 176) (Lot: #2078, Dilution ratio 1:1500) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China); HMGCR antibody (Lot: ab242315, Dilution ratio 1:1000) was obtained from Abcam Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Total Cholesterol (TC) assay kit (Lot: E-BC-K109-M) and Triglyceride (TG) assay kit (Lot: E-BC-K261-M) were purchased from Elabscience Biotechnology Co.,Ltd. (Wuhan, China).


### Cell culture
  HK-2 cell was purchased from Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Referring to previous studies [32, 33], HK-2 cells were cultured in MEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin double antibody (MEM complete medium), which without mycoplasma contamination. Complete culture medium containing 10 ng/mL TGF-β was used to culture HK-2 cells to establish RF cell model. The cells were cultured in a incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2.


### GSP solution preparation
  The 7.12 mg GSP was accurately weighed and dissolved in 100 µL DMSO and 900 µL PBS to prepare a 10 mM stock solution. Subsequently, 100 µL of this stock solution was diluted with 900 µL of complete MEM to obtain a 1 mM GSP solution, and serial dilutions were similarly performed to obtain GSP solutions at 200, 100, 50, 20, 10, 5, 2, and 1 µM. The final DMSO concentrations in the 200 µM and 100 µM GSP solutions were 0.1% and 0.05% (v/v), respectively, which are within the acceptable range for cell culture. Furthermore, a PBS solution containing 10% DMSO was diluted in an identical manner to generate complete medium containing 0.1% DMSO for use as a vehicle control in CCK-8 assays. Additionally, complete medium containing 0.05% DMSO was prepared as a vehicle control for subsequent experiments.


### CCK-8 assay
  Firstly, HK-2 cells (4 × 10³ cells/well) were plated in 96-well plate, and proliferation was assessed via CCK-8 after GSP treatment. Model and GSP groups received 10 ng/mL TGF-β in complete medium, with the GSP group additionally treated with GSP. The control group was treated with complete medium containing 0.1% DMSO. After 24 h, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution (A solution mixed with B solution at a 1:1 ratio) was added per well and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was then measured under enzyme labeling, and GraphPad software was used to calculate the results.


### Western blotting
  HK-2 cell proteins were extracted with RIPA lysis buffer (supplemented with 1% PPI and 1 mM PMSF) and quantified using BCA assay, and the protein concentrations were normalized using RIPA Lysis Buffer. Equal amounts of protein samples were then loaded into the wells of SDS-PAGE gels for electrophoretic separation, with the program set to 80 V for 30 min and 120 V for 2 h. After electrophoresis, the proteins were transferred to a PVDF membrane, with the transfer program set to 350 mA for 2 h. PVDF membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk (1 h), incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C, washed thrice with TBST, then incubated with secondary antibody (2 h, RT). The membrane was then washed using TBST, and ECL solution was applied for obtaining images. The gray values of the bands were quantified using ImageJ, and the relative expression of proteins was calculated with β-actin as the internal reference.


### Transwell migration assay
  The effect of GSP on the migration of TGF-β-induced HK-2 cells was assessed using the Transwell assay. First, HK-2 cells (5 × 10⁴ cells in 200 µL serum-free MEM) were seeded into 8 μm pore upper chambers. The lower chamber contained 700 µL MEM containing 20% FBS. All groups, except for the control group, were treated with 10 ng/mL TGF-β, and the experimental groups were further treated with different concentrations of GSP. After 24 h of free migration, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min and then stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution for 15 min. After washing thoroughly with PBS, the results were visualized under a microscope, and quantification was performed using ImageJ software.


### EdU assay
  Cell proliferation was assessed using an EdU cell proliferation kit. TGF-β-treated HK-2 cells were treated with GSP at concentrations of 25, 50, and 100 µM for 24 h. Following treatment, cells were incubated with 10 µM EdU solution for 2 h. After washing with PBS, cells were fixed and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100. The additive solution was then added, and cells were incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. Images were acquired under an optical microscope.


### RNA-seq and differential analysis
  HK-2 cells were divided into three groups: control, model (TGF-β 10 ng/mL), and GSP treatment (TGF-β 10 ng/mL + 100 µM GSP). Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol, and samples that passed QC (OD260/280 = 2 ± 0.2, OD260/230 ≥ 2.0) were subjected to library preparation and sequencing by Shanghai OE Biotech Co., Ltd. Poly(A). The mRNA was enriched using oligo(dT) magnetic beads, followed by fragmentation, first-strand and second-strand cDNA synthesis, end-repair, A-tailing, adaptor ligation, size selection, and PCR enrichment. Library concentration was quantified using Qubit and qPCR, and qualified libraries were sequenced using the Illumina PE150 platform.

  For transcript abundance estimation, StringTie was used to assemble transcripts and calculate FPKM values. FPKM values were not used for statistical testing or DEG identification, and which were used only for visualization and expression profiling.

  For differential expression analysis, the raw gene-level count matrix (generated using featureCounts) was imported into the limma package. Raw counts were normalized using TMM followed by voom transformation, and differential expression was assessed using empirical Bayes moderated statistics. Genes with log2|FC| ≥ 1.5, P < 0.05, and FDR < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed. Venn diagrams were subsequently used to identify GSP-responsive genes, defined as genes whose changes induced by TGF-β were reversed by GSP treatment.


### Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis
  PPI analysis of potential targets was was performed through STRING database. Filtering conditions: species set to Homo sapiens, confidence level set to 0.9, and removal of isolated nodes. The PPI network information was then exported and visualized using Cytoscape software. Additionally, the MCC algorithm in the CytoHubba plugin was used for topological analysis of the PPI network to identify the core targets of GSP in alleviating RF.


### Real-Time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)
  HK-2 cells were washed with PBS after GSP exposure, and total RNA was extracted using the CellAmp™ Direct RNA Prep Kit. cDNA was synthesized with PrimeScript RT Master Mix, followed by SYBR®-based qPCR using gene-specific primers (Sangon Biotech; sequences listed in Supplementary Table S1). Relative expression was determined by the 2^(−ΔΔCt) method, normalizing to GAPDH.


### GO and KEGG enrichment analyses
  The GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were employed to predict mechanisms related to potential targets using ClusterProfiler package, and results can quickly elucidate the underlying mechanisms of GSP’s effect on alleviating RF. The results were filtered with a threshold of p < 0.05, and the species was set to human. Finally, the results were sorted by p-value and visualized using the ggplot2 package.


### Molecular docking
  The human HMGCS1 (PDB ID: 2P8U) and HMGCR (PDB ID: 8PKN) protein structures were retrieved from the PDB database. HMGCS1 and HMGCR were selected as receptors and pre-processed by adding polar hydrogens, removing water molecules, and assigning Kollman charges. The docking box was defined using the CASTp 3.0 tool; briefly, the docking box for HMGCR (center_x, y, z = 121.693, 98.381, 108.196; size_x, y, z = 46.5, 47.25, 47.25) and HMGCS1 (center_x, y, z = 20.487, 27.467, 63.92; size_x, y, z = 47.25, 47.25, 47.25) were defined based on the ligand-binding pockets in the protein crystal structures. Then, the binding of GSP to HMGCS1 and HMGCR was evaluated by molecular docking with AutoDock 1.5.7 software. Finally, PyMOL software was used to visualize the 3D binding conformations of GSP with HMGCS1 and HMGCR.


### The cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)
  HK-2 cells were divided into control and GSP groups. Cells were treated with 100 µM GSP (GSP group) or an equal volume of 0.05% DMSO solution (control group) for 12 h. After treatment, Cell lysates were prepared from HK-2 cells using RIPA buffer containing 1 mM PMSF and split into two equal portions. Then, each lysate was aliquoted into six tubes and heated for 10 min at 35, 40, 45, 50, 55–60 °C. Samples were then centrifuged at 17 400 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were subjected to western blot. Melting curves were generated by fitting the band intensities with the Boltzmann sigmoidal function in GraphPad Prism.


### Overexpression of HMGCR
  Human HMGCR cDNA was cloned into the pcDNA3.1-HA vector, and then the pcDNA3.1-HMGCR plasmid was transfected into HK-2 cells using Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent. Twenty-four hours after transfection, the cell culture medium was replaced and cells were further cultured. Finally, the efficiency of overexpression was verified by Western blotting.


### Statistical analyses
  All data were presented as Mean ± SEM. The t-test was used to calculate the differences between two groups, while one-way ANOVA was employed for comparisons among multiple groups, and Dunnett’s method was used to post hoc test. The p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant: * P < 0.05; ** P < 0.01; *** P < 0.001; **** P < 0.0001.


## Results

### GSP suppresses proliferation and migration of TGF-β-induced HK-2 cells
  The impact of GSP on the proliferation of TGF-β-induced HK-2 cells was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. The findings indicated that cell proliferation was notably enhanced in the RF model group, whereas treatment with GSP notably inhibited HK-2 cell proliferation in a dose-dependent manner within a certain range. The most significant reduction in proliferation was observed at a GSP concentration of 100 µM (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, the effect of GSP on the migration of TGF-β-induced HK-2 cells was assessed using the Transwell assay. Results revealed that GSP treatment substantially reduced cell migration, with migration activity much lower than that of the RF model group (Fig. 1B and Fig S1A-B). Finally, Western blot analysis was performed to measure the levels of Collagen I and α-SMA, which are established markers of fibrosis (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, EdU staining showed that TGF-β-induced HK-2 cell proliferation was inhibited after GSP treatment (Fig. 1D). Finally, these findings align with previous research and suggest that GSP could be a promising therapeutic agent for treating RF, significantly mitigating fibrosis in TGF-β-induced HK-2 cells.

GSP inhibits the expression of fibrosis-related proteins Collagen I and α-SMA in HK-2 cells (n = 3); C. GSP inhibits HK-2 cell migration (n = 3); D. The effect of GPS on HK-2 cell proliferation by EdU assay (n = 3). All data are presented as Mean ± SEM; compared with the Model group: * P < 0.05; ** P < 0.01; *** P < 0.001; **** P < 0.0001


### RNA-seq analysis of GSP in alleviating RF
  RNA-seq analysis of HK-2 cells treated with GSP was performed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms of GSP treating RF. First, PCA analysis revealed that GSP significantly reversed the transcriptional profile changes induced by TGF-β in HK-2 cells (Fig. 2A). Compared to the control group, a total of 911 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified in the model group, including 537 upregulated and 374 downregulated genes (Fig. 2B). Similarly, compared to the model group, the GSP treatment group showed a total of 244 upregulated and 373 downregulated genes (Fig. 2C). Finally, 225 potential targets of GSP in alleviating RF were identified by Venn analysis (Fig. 2D).

  Fig. 2RNA-seq analysis. A. PCA analysis of the effect of GSP on the transcriptional profile of HK-2 cells (n = 3); B-C. Volcanic diagram for differential analysis; D potential targets of GSP alleviate RF were screening by Venn analysis


### Identification of core targets of GSP in alleviating RF
  PPI network analysis was performed, and sorting based on the Degree value. The results showed that the improvement of RF by GSP was primarily associated with FASN, TLR4, HMGCR, HMGCS1, and CYP51A1 (Fig. 3A). The PPI network was constructed using Cytoscape software, followed by topological analysis of PPI network using the CytoHubba plugin and the MCC algorithm. The results indicated that HMGCR and HMGCS1 had the highest scores (Fig. 3B). Additionally, we performed a topological analysis of the PPI network using three algorithms—EPC, Degree, and Closeness—and all results consistently positioned HMGCR and HMGCS1 at the core of the network (Fig S2). These results suggest that they may be core targets of GSP in the treatment of RF. Thus, HMGCR and HMGCS1 were preliminarily identified as the core targets of GSP in alleviating RF.

### GSP could target and bind to HMGCR
  To evaluate the effects of GSP on the core targets HMGCR and HMGCS1, we first performed qPCR assay. The results showed that GSP significantly reduced the mRNA expression levels of both HMGCR and HMGCS1, but the inhibitory effect was modest (Fig S3). Thus, we hypothesized that GSP might exert its function by directly binding to the HMGCR and HMGCS1 proteins. Then, molecular docking was further employed to assess the binding affinity between GSP and these two targets. The protein structures of the two core targets were obtained from the PDB database for molecular docking analysis. The results showed that the binding energy of GSP with HMGCR was − 8.7 kcal/mol, while the binding energy with HMGCS1 was only − 6.8 kcal/mol (Fig. 4A-B). Additionally, across three independent molecular-docking runs, the RMSD values for the binding of GSP to both HMGCR and HMGCS1 remained consistently stable (Table S2 and Table S3). Then, the results indicated that GSP and HMGCR could form hydrogen bonds at positions SER-775, CYS-777, ASN-750, ser-740, and SER-745, suggesting that HMGCR tightly interacts with GSP and is a core target molecule for GSP in alleviating RF. Furthermore, CETSA experiments observed that after GSP treatment, the thermal degradation of HMGCR protein was reduced, and HMGCR exhibited higher thermal stability (Fig. 4C), with the CETSA curve shifting to the right (Fig. 4D). This suggests that a targeted interaction may exist between GSP and HMGCR, and GSP may alleviate RF by binding to HMGCR.

  Fig. 4Molecular docking analysis and CETSA assay of GSP binding to HMGCR. A. Molecular docking conformation of GSP with HMGCR; B. Molecular docking conformation of GSP with HMGCS1; C-D. CETSA assay showed GSP can bind tightly to HMGCR


### GO and KEGG enrichment analyses
  GO enrichment analysis revealed a total of 461 BP terms, 26 CC terms, and 26 MF terms. The major BP terms included sterol, cholesterol, secondary alcohol biosynthetic processes. The CC included the endoplasmic reticulum chaperone complex and endoplasmic reticulum lumen, while the MF involved signaling receptor regulator activity, cytokine activity, and receptor ligand activity (Fig. 5A). The GO chord plot results showed that the core targets HMGCR and HMGCS1 were primarily enriched in cholesterol biosynthesis and cholesterol metabolic processes (Fig. 5B). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis highlighted the critical roles of steroid biosynthesis, cholesterol metabolism, and the NF-κB signaling pathway in the effect of GSP in alleviating RF (Fig. 5C). Subsequently, Western blotting results confirmed that GSP significantly inhibited TGF-β-induced HMGCR expression level and NF-κB pathway activation in HK-2 cells (Fig. 5D and Fig S1C-E). These results suggest that GSP exerts anti-RF effects by downregulating HMGCR levels and inhibiting NF-κB pathway activation, which is closely associated with cholesterol metabolism.

### GSP exerts therapeutic effects on RF via the HMGCR/NF-κB pathway
  Based on the results of PPI, molecular docking, CETSA, and KEGG enrichment analysis, we observed that cholesterol metabolism and the NF-κB signaling pathway play critical roles in the effect of GSP in alleviating RF. Thus, we measured TG and TC levels in HK-2 cells after GSP treatment and found that GSP significantly reduced both (Fig S4). This process is associated with inhibiting HMGCR-mediated cholesterol metabolism, and the regulatory role of cholesterol metabolism in RF progression has been widely reported. Additionally, we hypothesized that GSP exerts its anti-RF effects by targeting HMGCR to inhibit the NF-κB signaling pathway. To test this mechanism, we performed rescue experiments by overexpressing HMGCR in HK-2 cells (Fig S5). The Transwell assay showed that overexpression of HMGCR significantly reversed the inhibitory effect of GSP on HK-2 cell migration (Fig. 6A-B). Western blotting results showed that the inhibitory effects of GSP on Collagen I, α-SMA, and the NF-κB signaling pathway were reversed upon overexpression of HMGCR, these results demonstrate that overexpression of HMGCR can rescue the anti-RF effect of GSP (Fig. 6C and Fig S1F-J). Additionally, selective over-expression of HMGCR in HK-2 cells was sufficient to activate the NF-κB pathway and to up-regulate the fibrosis-related proteins Collagen I and α-SMA, indicating that HMGCR can modulate NF-κB signaling pathway independently of GSP (Fig S6). Collectively, these findings further support the notion that GSP exerts its anti-renal-fibrotic effects primarily by inhibiting HMGCR/NF-κB pathway.

## Discussion
  Renal fibrosis (RF) is the most severe complication in the progression of Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD), characterized by irreversible organic lesion of renal tissue. RF is the primary cause of poor prognosis in CKD patients and represents a major urgent challenge to be addressed. As a chronic metabolic disease, the complex pathogenesis of RF is a major obstacle in the development of therapeutic drugs. As a result, clinical benefits for RF treatment remain limited. Gentiopicroside (GSP), the main bioactive component in Gentiana manshurica Kitag., has been shown to have a promising therapeutic effect on RF in previous studies [30, 31]. In this study, a TGF-β-induced HK-2 cell model was established to investigate the molecular mechanisms of GSP in alleviating RF through RNA-seq and various other experiments. The results demonstrated that GSP can inhibit the HMGCR/NF-κB pathway, showing promising potential for RF treatment. KEGG analysis revealed that this process is closely associated with cholesterol biosynthesis and metabolism. The findings provide further evidence supporting the research and application of GSP in the treatment of RF.

  It is well known that HMGCR is a key rate-limiting enzyme in the regulation of cholesterol synthesis and metabolism, playing a crucial role in regulating cholesterol levels in the body. Notably, previous studies have reported that CKD patients with hypercholesterolemia are more likely to progress to RF, making it an independent risk factor for RF. This suggests that targeting HMGCR is an effective therapeutic strategy for ameliorating RF [9, 12, 14]. Statins, which are widely recognized HMGCR inhibitors, have also been extensively reported to have promising therapeutic potential for RF. However, muscle-related side effects are nearly inevitable, which significantly limits the application of statins. Our study confirms that GSP can targets HMGCR to inhibit the NF-κB pathway and alleviate RF, indicating that GSP may serve as an alternative to statins. The NF-κB pathway is a classical inflammation-related pathway, and the benefits of inhibiting inflammation in the progression of RF are well recognized. Furthermore, the anti-inflammatory activity of GSP has been widely reported, with two previous studies showing that GSP alleviates RF by inhibiting the NF-κB pathway [30, 31]. The results of our study are consistent with these previous findings, and notably, we demonstrate that HMGCR is the target of GSP in inhibiting the NF-κB pathway. Extensive research has confirmed the correlation between HMGCR and the NF-κB pathway. For example, recent studies have shown that activation of the NF-κB pathway in liver cancer cells promotes the expression level of HMGCR [34], which has been verified in other cell types [35, 36]. These results suggest that HMGCR is a downstream effector of the NF-κB pathway. However, in our study, we found that overexpression of HMGCR in HK-2 cells activated the NF-κB pathway and upregulated the expression of Collagen I and α-SMA. Our findings indicate that HMGCR is a key target for activating the NF-κB pathway and suggest that GSP exerts its anti-RF effects mainly by inhibiting the HMGCR/NF-κB pathway.

  This study reveals the molecular mechanisms by which GSP alleviates RF, but several limitations should be addressed. First, while previous studies have reported the good therapeutic effects of GSP for RF, this study did not include in vivo experiments. Future studies should explore the mechanism of GSP in vivo. Second, although we confirmed the binding of GSP to HMGCR through molecular docking and CETSA, further evidence is needed to support this conclusion. Additionally, the mechanism by which GSP targets and inhibits HMGCR is still not well understood. It remains unclear whether GSP’s inhibition of HMGCR operates in the same manner as statins. Future research should further clarify the mechanism by which GSP inhibits HMGCR. Overall, this study provides insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying GSP’s improvement of RF. It also offers additional evidence supporting further research and therapeutic applications of GSP in RF and reports a promising HMGCR inhibitor.


## Conclusion
  This study shows that HMGCR is key target of GSP in protecting HK-2 cell injury induced by TGF-β. CETSA assays further revealed that GSP binds strongly to HMGCR. RNA-seq analysis and in vitro validation highlighted the significant role of cholesterol metabolism and HMGCR/NF-κB pathway in GSP protects HK-2 cell injury. In summary, our findings suggest that GSP can inhibit the HMGCR/NF-κB pathway can protect against TGF-β-induced HK-2 cell injury, exhibiting promising therapeutic potential for RF.
