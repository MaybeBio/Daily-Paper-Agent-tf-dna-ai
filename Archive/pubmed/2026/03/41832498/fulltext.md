# Germacrone suppresses renal cancer growth by regulating c-Fos-mediated lipid metabolism


## Background
  Germacrone, a major bioactive component of Curcuma zedoaria, exhibits promising anticancer activity across various preclinical models. However, its molecular mechanisms in renal cell carcinoma (RCC), particularly regarding metabolic regulation, remain largely unexplored. This study aimed to investigate the therapeutic potential and underlying mechanisms of germacrone in RCC, specifically focusing on lipid metabolic reprogramming.


## Methods
  This study integrated network pharmacology and RNA sequencing transcriptomics to identify potential molecular targets of germacrone in 786-O and Caki-1 cells. In vivo antitumor efficacy was evaluated using the subcutaneous xenograft tumor model in BALB/c nude mice. In vitro, cell proliferation, apoptosis, migration, and cell cycle distribution were assessed using CCK-8, flow cytometry, transwell, and colony formation assays. To elucidate the underlying mechanism, de novo fatty acid synthesis was examined by quantifying intracellular triglyceride (TG) and total cholesterol (TC) levels. Interactions between germacrone and the c-Fos/SREBP1 signaling axis were analyzed using Western blot analysis, cellular thermal shift assays (CETSA), and molecular docking. Functional validation was performed using c-Fos overexpression plasmids to assess the extent to which germacrone’s effects were reversible.


## Results
  Germacrone significantly inhibited RCC growth in vivo, as evidenced by reduced tumor volume and weight, without detectable systemic toxicity. In vitro, germacrone suppressed proliferation, migration, and invasion in 786-O and Caki-1 cells, while promoting apoptosis and cell cycle arrest. Transcriptomic and molecular analyses indicated that germacrone targets the c-Fos transcription factor, leading to the downregulation of the SREBP1 signaling pathway and key lipogenic genes (FASN and ACC1). This regulation resulted in a marked reduction in intracellular fatty acid and cholesterol biosynthesis. Notably, c-Fos overexpression significantly reversed the germacrone-induced suppression of lipid metabolism and cell viability, confirming that germacrone functions primarily by modulating the c-Fos/SREBP1 axis.


## Conclusion
  Germacrone serves as a novel modulator of the c-Fos/SREBP1 pathway, effectively inhibiting the growth of renal cancer by disrupting c-Fos-mediated lipid metabolism. These findings identify c-Fos as a potential therapeutic target and highlight germacrone as a promising candidate for RCC treatment strategies targeting metabolic reprogramming.

## Introduction
  Globally, renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is the most predominant urological tumor and the most lethal malignancy of the urogenital tract, particularly among individuals with advanced or metastatic RCC [1]. Partial nephrectomy, along with surgical resection, conventional radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, remains the most effective treatment for localized RCC. Although diagnostic and treatment strategies for RCC have advanced over the past decade, the long-term prognosis remains poor. In particular, among patients with stage IV RCC, the average 5-year survival rate is only approximately 28% [2]. Consequently, there is an increased need for innovative and efficient therapeutic approaches for RCC.

  Abnormal lipid metabolism, especially increased de novo lipogenesis (DNL), has emerged as a hallmark feature of cancer. Enhanced lipogenesis supplies malignant cells with crucial components required for membrane biosynthesis, signaling, and post-translational modification substrates, thereby facilitating oncogenic growth [3]. Regulation of DNL primarily occurs at the transcriptional level through sterol regulatory element-binding proteins (SREBPs), which stimulate genes encoding various lipogenic enzymes, including fatty acid synthase (FASN), acetyl-CoA carboxylase-1 (ACC1), and stearoyl-CoA desaturase-1 (SCD1) [4, 5]. Recently, increased attention has been directed towards targeting abnormal lipid metabolism in renal cancer. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that inhibiting SREBP1 with PF-429,242 [6] and ring finger protein 20 can potentially reduce clear cell RCC (ccRCC) cell growth both in vitro and in vivo [7, 8]. These findings imply that targeting SREBP1 and related lipogenic enzymes could be effective for therapeutic intervention and overcoming chemoresistance in renal cancer.

  The Fos transcription factor family (c-Fos, Fra-1, Fra-2, and FosB) forms heterodimers with Jun proteins (c-Jun, JunB, and JunD) to generate the activator protein 1 (AP-1) complex, which binds TPA-responsive elements in promoter or enhancer regions of target gene loci [9]. The c-Fos proto-oncogene, a human homolog of the retroviral oncogene v-Fos, is highly conserved and regulates cell survival, growth, differentiation, and transformation. In colon cancer, c-Fos enhances stemness and 5-fluorouracil resistance by transcriptionally upregulating NANOG [10], while in non-small cell lung cancer, the G-quadruplex ligand BMVC-8C3O restores osimertinib sensitivity by suppressing c-Fos [11]. Conversely, c-Fos overexpression (c-Fos OE) inhibits tumor progression. It induces apoptosis in ovarian cancer by disrupting adhesion proteins and glycosylation enzymes [12], and functions as a tumor suppressor in liver cells by blocking the cell cycle [13]. These findings indicate that c-Fos exerts context-dependent roles across cancer types, although its specific targets in RCC remain undefined.

  Curcumae Rhizoma has been utilized for centuries in traditional Chinese medicine for its anticancer, hepatoprotective, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory properties [14–16]. Germacrone, one of the key active constituents of Curcuma zedoaria, has been demonstrated by numerous studies to exert cytotoxic effects on various solid and hematologic tumor cells [17]. For instance, germacrone demonstrates anti-proliferative effects in breast cancer by triggering G0/G1 and G2/M cell cycle arrest and apoptosis via the caspase pathway mediated by mitochondria [18]. It also suppresses MCF-7 cell proliferation by regulating ERα-dependent gene transcription by inhibiting ERα and coregulator recruitment to target promoters or enhancers [19]. In hepatoma cells, Liu et al. reported that germacrone induces G2/M arrest and apoptosis through increased ROS generation, inhibition of JAK2/STAT3 activation, and regulation of STAT3-targeted genes, such as Bcl-2 family members and p53 [20, 21]. Other studies indicate that germacrone suppresses the growth of U87 and U251 human glioma cell lines by modulating proteins linked to G1 cell cycle arrest and apoptosis [22]. Zhang et al. further demonstrated that germacrone significantly prevents the proliferation and movement of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma cells while inducing apoptosis through intrinsic apoptotic signaling and suppression of STAT3 [23]. While these studies highlight the anti-tumor activity of germacrone, its effects and molecular mechanisms on renal tumor growth remain unverified, and its modulatory impact on lipid metabolism has yet to be experimentally validated.

  This study demonstrated the first evidence that germacrone effectively inhibited renal cancer in vitro and in vivo, while suppressing fatty acid biosynthesis. The findings highlight the potential of targeting c-Fos-mediated lipid metabolism as a treatment strategy for renal cancer and provide new insights into germacrone’s biological properties.


## Materials and methods

### Cell cultures
  The human renal cancer cell lines 786-O and Caki-1 were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Committee on Type Culture Collection of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). The cells were cultured in the RPMI-1640 culture medium (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) with 10% FBS (Cell-box, Hunan, China), and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution (Solarbio, Beijing, China) at 37 °C in an atmosphere of 5% CO2. Germacrone (purity: > 98%, A14488) was procured from AdooQ Bioscience (Irvine, CA, USA).


### Cell proliferation assay
  The CCK-8 assay (APExBIO, Houston, TX, USA) was employed to measure cell proliferation. Cells were plated (3 × 103/well) in a 96-well plate with germacrone concentrations of 30, 60, 120, 180, and 240 µM germacrone. Figure 1A illustrates the chemical structure of germacrone. Each well was incubated with 10 µL of CCK-8 solution for an additional 2 h period.

  Fig. 1Germacrone suppressed the proliferation of 786-O and Caki-1 cells. A Structural formula of germacrone. 786-O and Caki-1cells were treated with 0-240 µM germacrone for 24, 48 and 72 h, respectively. Cell viability was determined by CCK-8 assay (B) and the IC50 values were calculated from the dose-response curves (C). The proliferative ability of 786-O and Caki-1 cells treated with germacrone was examined via a colony formation assay (D) and an EdU assay (E). Statistical significance was determined by one-way ANOVA. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001


### Cell clone formation assay
  Cells were cultured in six-well dishes (500 cells/well) and incubated for 24 h. After treatment, cells were cultured for 10 days until visible clone clusters (defined as > 50 cells/clone) formed. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and dyed with 0.1% crystal violet. Images were captured, and colony numbers were quantified using ImageJ software.


### Cell cycle analysis
  Flow cytometry with a cell cycle detection kit was used to evaluate cell cycle distribution (MAIGE, Jiangsu, China). The 786-O and Caki-1 cells were firstly stimulated at different germacrone solution concentrations (0, 120, 180 and 240 µM) for 48 h. The cells were then collected and washed with PBS. Each tube received 1 ml 70% ethanol and was incubated at -20 °C overnight for fixing. Following that, the fixed 786-O and Caki-1 cells were harvested and cleaned using PBS chilled to 4 °C, and 500µL PI/RNase dye solution was added into each tube and incubated for 10 min at room temperature (RT) in the dark. Finally, samples were measured by Beckman CytoFLEX S (Beckman Coulter, Brea, USA) and analyzed by FlowJo software (Tree Star Inc., Ashland, USA).


### Apoptosis analysis
  Cell apoptosis was evaluated via an Annexin-V/PI detection kit (CellorLab, China). Cells were suspended in 400 µL binding buffer, and an incubated with Annexin V-FITC (5 µL, 15 min) and PI (10 µL, 5 min) at 37 °C in the dark. The flow cytometry was utilized to analyze the stained cells immediately (Beckman Coulter, Brea, USA).


### Scratch wound-healing assay
  786-O and Caki-1 cells from all groups were cultivated in six-well plates for 24 h. At approximately 80% confluence, a linear wound was formed using a 200 µL sterile pipette tip. After medium removal, cells were rinsed twice with PBS, and an incubation was conducted in serum-free RPMI-1640. Cell migration was visualised at 0, 12, and 24 h using an inverted microscope (Olympus CKX53, Tokyo, Japan), and the migration area (µm²) was quantified with ImageJ v.1.53 (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).


### Transwell assay
  The Transwell assay utilized 24-well chambers from Corning Inc (Corning, NY, USA). 786-O and Caki-1 cells (2 × 104 cells/well) were placed in the top chamber, while 500 µL of RPMI-1640 enriched with 10% FBS was applied to the bottom chamber to serve as a chemoattractant. Following incubation with different concentrations of germacrone solution (0, 120, 180, and 240 µM) for 48 h, 4% paraformaldehyde was employed to fix the cells migrating to the lower surface of the filter at RT for 15 min, and 0.1% crystal violet was applied to stain them at RT for 5 min. Images were captured by microscopy as described above.


### EdU assay
  Cell growth was evaluated using an EdU staining kit (Beyotime, Jiangsu, China). Briefly, the 786-O and Caki-1 cells (1 × 104 cells/well) were grown in 12-well plates, and an overnight incubation was conducted at 37 °C. The cells were exposed to germacrone solution (0, 120, 180, and 240 µM). An incubation of cells was conducted with EdU for 2 h, then 4% paraformaldehyde was utilized to fix them for 15 min, and 0.2% Triton X-100 was employed to permeabilize them for an extra 15 min. A 30 min incubation of cells was conducted with the Click Reaction Mixture at RT in darkness, then a 10-min incubation was conducted with Hoechst 33,342.


### Atomic force microscopy (AFM) analysis
  The cell suspension of 786-O and Caki-1 cells (1 × 105 cells/well) was planted in 2 cm culture dishes and incubated with germacrone (0, 120, 180, and 240 µM) for 48 h. Young’s modulus was detected by AFM (Bruker-JPK, Berlin, Germany). To determine the Young’s modulus, force-distance curves were collected and examined using JPK Imaging Process Software.


### In vivo tumorigenicity assay
  Male BALB/c nude mice (4 weeks old) were obtained from GemPharmatech (GemPharmatech Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China). Mice were subcutaneously injected with 1 × 107 786-O cells in the right side of the abdomen. Germacrone treatment commenced 8 days after tumor inoculation. All mice were euthanized after 24 days of the treatment regimen. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: V (mm3) = ½ × length × width2. Mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 5/group) and administered germacrone (60 or 90 mg/kg/day) via intraperitoneal injection. The positive group was treated with 40 mg/kg/day sunitinib (Aladdin, Shanghai, China) by oral gavage. The control group received an intraperitoneal injection of a 100 µL blend of PEG400, DMSO, and sterile deionized water in a 1:1:3 proportion. Mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation at the end of the 24 days treatment period.


### Hepatic and renal function analysis
  Serum aspartate aminotransferase (AST), serum creatinine (CR), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) were evaluated using a fully automatic biochemical analyzer (Hitachi-7180, Tokyo, Japan).


### Transcriptome sequencing analysis
  The Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, California, USA) was utilized to assess the total RNA concentration and purity in 786-O cells treated with germacrone. After rRNA removal and mRNA enrichment with Oligo (dT) magnetic beads, a reverse mRNA transcription into cDNA was conducted using random hexamer primers. The adapted cDNA sequencing was carried out using the DNBSEQ-T7 platform (Benagen Inc., Wuhan, China) following the reverse transcription of RNA fragments for cDNA library creation. The VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit for MGI® (Vazyme, Jiangsu, China) was utilized as the library preparation kit. After determining the gene alignment rates, expression was quantified as FPKM using the RSEM software. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were determined via NOISeq with criteria of fold change ≥ 2 and P.adj ≤ 0.05. The R cluster profiler was utilized to conduct KEGG (http://www.genome.ad.jp/kegg) and WikiPathways analyses (https://www.wikipathways.org/index.php/WikiPathways) for DEGs.


### Triglyceride (TG) and total cholesterol (TC) assay
  Total lipids were extracted with isopropanol, and the clear supernatant comprising the lipids was extracted. TG and TC were quantified following the manufacturer’s instructions (Beyotime, Jiangsu, China).


### Lipid droplet staining
  Per the manufacturer’s guidelines, neutral lipids were visualized with BODIPY 493/503 fluorescent dye (Beyotime, Jiangsu, China). A 4% paraformaldehyde solution was employed to fix the cells, and then BODIPY was applied to stain them for 20 min at RT away from light. Images were obtained using a Nikon confocal laser microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). ImageJ was utilized to quantify the neutral lipid drops staining in 786-O cells.


### Oil red O staining
  Oil Red O staining was implemented with the Modified Oil Red O Staining Kit (Beyotime, Jiangsu, China), as directed by the manufacturer. In brief, frozen tissue sections of tumors were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde solution in PBS. Upon rinsing with the staining detergent, sections underwent incubation with modified Oil Red O solution at RT for 20 min.


### Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining and immunohistochemistry (IHC)
  Specimens of tumors and organs were sliced into 4 μm-thick slices. Organ tissues were subjected to HE staining, while tumor tissue slices underwent deparaffinization, rehydration, and incubation with primary antibodies. Then, the slices were exposed to a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody and diaminobenzidine for final analysis.


### Western blot analyses
  Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA kit (Beyotime, Jiangsu, China). After separation via SDS-PAGE, 20 µg of each protein sample was transferred to PVDF membranes (ISEQ00010, Millipore, MA, USA), and a 2 h blockage was conducted with 5% non-fat milk at RT. An incubation of membranes was performed at 4 °C overnight with primary antibodies against Bax (AF0057, Beyotime, Jiangsu, China), Bcl-2 (AG1225, Beyotime, Jiangsu, China), cleaved caspase-3 (ET1602-47, HUABIO, Hangzhou, China), SREBP1 (HA722160, HUABIO, Hangzhou, China), FASN (ET1701-91, HUABIO, Hangzhou, China), ACC1 (AF1867, Beyotime, Jiangsu, China), or β-actin (66009-1-Ig, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) in antibody dilution buffer (P0023A, Beyotime, Jiangsu, China). After TBST washes, a 1 h incubation of membranes was conducted with HRP-linked anti-rabbit (A0208, Beyotime, Jiangsu, China) or anti-mouse (A0216, Beyotime, Jiangsu, China) secondary antibodies at RT and imaged using ChemiDoc XRS+ (Bio-Rad, CA, USA). ImageJ was utilized to measure the band intensities.


### Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)
  CETSA was implemented following the specified protocol [24]. Briefly, 786-O cells were subjected to control (DMSO) or germacrone, and a 2 h incubation was conducted at 37 °C. Post-incubation, cells were rinsed once and partitioned into portions containing 0.5 × 106 cells each. The control and drug-treated samples were heated in pairs for 3 min at 43–61 °C, with 3 °C increments between each pair, using a Bio-Rad T100TM Thermal Cycler. Following heating, cells were maintained at RT for 3 min before being placed on ice. Cell collection and lysis were conducted in Ripa lysis buffer enriched with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Beyotime, Jiangsu, China), followed by SDS-PAGE and Western blot analysis via anti-HA (c-Fos) and β-actin antibodies.


### Plasmids and transient transfection
  Transfections were executed with the Lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagent, adhering to the instructions provided by the manufacturer. For this purpose, the c-Fos plasmids created by WZBIO (Wuhan, China) were utilized. Following a 24 to 48 h incubation, the transfection solution was swapped out for a new, complete culture medium. The pEnter-kana‐c-FOS‐FLAG/His plasmid was introduced into 786-O cells as per the provided specifications.


### Microscale thermophoresis (MST)
  To confirm germacrone’s binding capability to wild-type c-Fos, 293T cells were transfected with a plasmid encoding the GFP-target protein, and cell lysates were harvested 48 h post-transfection.These were then analyzed using MonolithTM NT.115 MST equipment (Nano Temper, Munich, Germany).


### HPLC-MS/MS Analysis
  786-O and Caki-1 cells were treated with 240 µM germacrone or vehicle for 48 h, followed by PBS washing to remove extracellular germacrone before harvest. The cells were lysed with a 50% water and methanol solution, underwent several freeze-thaw cycles, and the supernatants were collected for further analysis. The liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS) system (Agilent HPLC-1200 tandem mass spectrometry 6495 C) was used to measure germacrone content.


### Statistical analysis
  GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA) was utilized for IC50 calculation and statistical analyses. The two groups’ variations were analyzed with either unpaired or paired Student’s t-tests; comparisons among multiple groups employed one-way analysis of variance with Tukey’s test. In contrast, two-way analysis of variance with Tukey’s adjustment was applied for analyses involving groups across time points. P < 0.05 was regarded as significant.


## Results

### Germacrone inhibits the growth of 786-O and Caki-1 cells
  To evaluate germacrone’s inhibitory effect on renal malignant cell growth, CCK-8 and colony formation assays were conducted. The results indicated that germacrone decreased 786-O and Caki-1 cell viability in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1B). A significant decrease in cell viability was observed after treatment with 120–240 µM germacrone for 24, 48, and 72 h. The IC50 values for germacrone after 24, 48, and 72 h were 178.9, 193.1, and 175.8 µM in 786-O cells, 168.7, 180.3 and 159.2 µM in Caki-1 cells, respectively (Fig. 1C). Germacrone effectively decreased the growth of 786-O and Caki-1 cells, as confirmed by colony formation and EdU assays (Figs. 1D, E). These results illustrate that germacrone exerts strong anti-tumor activity in 786-O and Caki-1 cells.


### Germacrone induces apoptosis and cycle arrest in 786-O and Caki-1 cells
  Flow cytometry revealed a dose-dependent increase in apoptosis in 786-O and Caki-1 cells following germacrone treatment (Fig. 2A), confirming its inhibitory effects on renal cancer cells. Germacrone also significantly increased the proportion of cells in the G1 phase while reducing those in G2/M, indicating G1/S arrest and inhibition of cell cycle progression (Fig. 2B). The Bcl-2 family, comprising prosurvival proteins (Bcl-2) and pro-apoptotic factors (Bax), regulates apoptosis by maintaining mitochondrial integrity and promoting cytochrome c release [25]. Caspase-3 cleavage and activation further mediate apoptosis through proteolysis of essential proteins [26]. Consistently, germacrone treatment upregulated cleaved caspase-3/caspase-3 and Bax/Bcl-2 expression (Fig. 2C). TUNEL apoptotic staining confirmed that the apoptosis index was significantly higher in germacrone-treated groups compared with controls (Fig. 2D). These findings indicate that germacrone effectively promotes apoptosis in vitro. Subsequent investigations further examined whether germacrone could alter mitochondrial morphology. Intracellular mitochondria were stained red and nuclei were stained blue (Fig. S1), and mitochondrial morphology was characterized using the relative mean branch length. These results revealed that mitochondrial fragmentation and apoptosis increased in response to germacrone administration.

### Germacrone suppresses migration, invasion and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)
  Dynamic cytoskeletal reorganization influences cellular biomechanical properties, including migration, adhesion, and metastasis [27–29]. Malignant cells must remodel the actin cytoskeleton to deform and facilitate metastasis [30–32]. The invasion and migration capacities of 786-O and Caki-1 cells were markedly reduced in the presence of germacrone (Figs. 3A-B).

  EMT signaling contributes to tumor metastasis. To determine whether EMT inhibition was associated with germacrone treatment, EMT markers in 786-O and Caki-1 cells were assessed. Germacrone treatment resulted in a dose-dependent increase in E-cadherin expression, associated with epithelial morphology, and a decrease in vimentin and N-cadherin expression, associated with mesenchymal morphology (Figs. 3C-E).

  Furthermore, malignant tumors commonly exhibit variable cellular stiffness [33, 34]. AFM was utilized to assess the Young’s modulus values of 786-O and Caki-1 cells exposed to varying germacrone concentrations. This study revealed that the Young’s modulus of 786-O and Caki-1 cells was significantly increased after germacrone treatment in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3F), indicating enhanced cellular stiffness and thereby reducing metastatic potential.


### Germacrone repressed RCC xenograft tumor growth in vivo
  To evaluate germacrone’s effect on RCC cell growth in vivo, 786-O cells were administered into nude mice via subcutaneous graft, and germacrone or sunitinib was administered at selected concentrations daily after 8 days of transplantation (Fig. 4A). The Figs. 4B-C illustrate that administration of germacrone or sunitinib markedly delayed the growth of xenograft tumors. During this period, body weight did not significantly differ between the germacrone-treated and control groups (P > 0.05), suggesting no adverse effects on overall health (Fig. 4D).

  The 90 mg/kg dose of germacrone significantly reduced tumor weight to 48.8% of the control, compared to a reduction to 67.6% with the 60 mg/kg dose (Fig. 4E). To evaluate organ toxicity, kidney and liver tissues were collected. Blood samples were analyzed for AST, ALT, BUN, and CR levels. The control and germacrone-treated groups demonstrated no significant differences in these parameters, suggesting no negative impact on kidney and liver function (Figs. 4F). HE staining illustrated no significant pathological alterations in the kidney or liver tissues of either the control or germacrone-treated groups. Kidneys displayed normal glomeruli and renal tubules without epithelial detachment, cast formation, inflammation, or fibrosis. Hepatocytes were orderly arranged with uniform nuclei and intact hepatic cords, without evidence of significant necrosis (Figs. 4G-H). IHC confirmed reduced expression of the proliferation marker Ki-67 following germacrone treatment (Fig. 4I). The hypothesis was formulated stating elevated doses of germacrone could markedly suppress RCC growth.


### Network pharmacology analysis of germacrone
  A systematic network pharmacology analysis elucidated germacrone’s anti-renal clear cell carcinoma mechanisms. Venn diagram analysis (Fig. 5A) identified 81 overlapping targets from germacrone-associated proteins (95) and renal cancer-related targets (2,068). Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network mapping (Fig. 5B) revealed a scale-free topology, with FOS emerging as a critical hub in the core subnetwork (Fig. 5C). This transcription factor (FOS) occupied a central position as a red-highlighted node, exhibiting extensive connectivity to key regulators including ESR1, HSP90AA1, and EGFR, suggesting its pivotal role in germacrone’s pharmacological activity.

B, C PPI network of the common targets according to degree value analyzed by Cytoscape software. D GO enrichment analysis of network pharmacology. E Top 20 most enriched KEGG categories for the common targets according to network pharmacology analysis

  The gene ontology (GO) and kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses of these 81 targets demonstrated GO terms (Fig. 5D): Significant enrichment of intrinsic apoptotic signaling (biological process) and sterol regulatory element binding (molecular function), implicating stress response and lipid regulation. KEGG pathways (Fig. 5E). Fatty acid metabolism (highlighted by a deep red bubble indicating the highest significance, P < 0.001) was prominently enriched alongside pathways in cancer and PI3K-Akt signaling.

  ​ Critically, the co-localization of FOS in the interaction network (Fig. 5C) and the robust enrichment of fatty acid metabolism (Fig. 5E) suggest that germacrone targets FOS to modulate lipid metabolic reprogramming in renal cancer cells. This integrated analysis confirms germacrone’s dual targeting of apoptosis and cancer-specific lipid metabolism through the FOS-centered regulatory network.


### Transcriptomic analysis of germacrone-induced damage in 786-O cells
  To elucidate germacrone’s molecular mechanism in RCC, transcriptome sequencing was performed on 786-O cells. Fifty-five genes were identified as DEGs, with 13 upregulated and 42 downregulated based on a 1-fold change and P.adj < 0.05 (Fig. 6A). Figure 6B highlights the top DEGs classified by fold change; notably, FOS and SREBF1 were significantly downregulated. GO enrichment analysis revealed that cell proliferation regulation, DNA damage response, and mitochondrial fission were overrepresented in the BP class; the nuclear envelope, Golgi apparatus, and synaptic membrane were overrepresented in the CC class; kinase activator function, calcium ion binding, and ubiquitin ligase activity were overrepresented in the MF class (Figs. 6C-D). KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that lipid metabolism, atherosclerosis, metabolic pathways, and apoptosis were significantly associated with germacrone-mediated cytotoxicity in 786-O cells (Figs. 6E-F). This predominant enrichment is consistent with network pharmacology predictions (Fig. 5E). These findings strongly support the hypothesis that germacrone-induced apoptosis may rely on lipid metabolic reprogramming.

### Germacrone binds to c-Fos and suppresses lipid biosynthesis in 786-O cells
  The binding interactions between c-Fos and germacrone were studied through molecular docking. Figure 7A illustrates proteins as blue cartoons and the compound as yellow sticks. Docking revealed excellent binding energies, with germacrone fitting into the protein’s active pocket. Specifically, germacrone formed a hydrogen bond with THR-169 and hydrophobic interactions with LEU-165 and LEU-172, stabilizing the protein-ligand complex.

  CETSA confirmed the direct association of germacrone with c-Fos, leading to a shift in the thermal stability of the c-Fos protein (Fig. 7B) [35]. Afterward, further direct binding analysis between germacrone and c-Fos was performed by MST using exogenous GFP-tagged c-Fos protein. As anticipated, germacrone easily attached to c-Fos proteins with Kd values estimated to be 27.22 ± 0.35µM (Fig. 7C).

  Given that lipid metabolism was implicated in germacrone’s antitumor effects (GO and KEGG analyses), the impact of germacrone on lipid homeostasis was subsequently examined. Germacrone treatment decreased protein levels of lipid synthesis-associated genes (FASN, ACC1, SREBP1, and c-Fos) (Fig. 7D). Immunofluorescence confirmed reduced c-Fos and SREBP1 staining (Figs. 7E-F). Staining with the lipophilic dye BODIPY 493/503 demonstrated a significant reduction in neutral lipid droplet accumulation (Fig. 7G). Consistently, germacrone exposure decreased TG and TC synthesis in 786-O cells (Fig. 7H). Collectively, these results indicate that germacrone disrupts lipid biosynthesis in RCC cells.

  Thus, subsequent investigations were directed at examining whether germacrone could enter cells to interact with c-Fos. Detection of intracellular fractions by HPLC-MS/MS analysis [36] revealed the presence of a significant amount of germacrone in germacrone-incubated RCC cells, indicating that germacrone can indeed be taken up by the cells (Fig. 7I). Furthermore, cells were exposed to the proteasome inhibitor MG-132, which resulted in a significant restoration of germacrone-induced c-Fos depletion (Fig. 7J). These data indicate that germacrone likely reduces protein expression by inhibiting c-Fos synthesis.


### Germacrone attenuates lipid biosynthesis in vivo
  Following the in vitro studies, lipid modulation by germacrone was evaluated in tumor-bearing nude mice. IHC staining demonstrated significantly decreased c-Fos expression in tumor tissue (Fig. 8A), linking germacrone’s anti-tumor activity to c-Fos suppression. Staining with BODIPY 493/503 (Fig. 8B) and Oil Red O (Fig. 8C) revealed a marked decrease in neutral fatty acids, including TGs, in the germacrone-treated group. To assess the impact of exogenous fatty acids on germacrone’s anti-tumor activity, 786-O cells were treated with palmitic acid (PA). Notably, co-treatment with PA and germacrone significantly rescued cell viability compared to germacrone alone (Fig. 8D). This finding indicates that suppression of lipid metabolic pathways is a key mechanism through which germacrone inhibits the growth of renal cancer cells. Furthermore, quantification of TC and TG levels in xenograft tumors revealed that germacrone treatment significantly reduced intratumoral lipid accumulation (Fig. 8E), consistent with its suppressive effect on lipid metabolism.

### Germacrone induces cytotoxicity by inhibiting c-Fos expression in 786-O cells
  To further confirm whether c-Fos is critical for germacrone’s anti-tumor effects and lipid metabolic reprogramming, 786-O cells with transient c-Fos OE were generated, and transfection efficacy was validated by Western blot analysis(Fig. 9A). c-Fos OE partially counteracted the germacrone-induced reduction in viability, proliferation, and migration, as demonstrated by CCK-8, colony formation, and scratch assays (Figs. 9B–D). ## Discussion
  Germacrone is a monocyclic sesquiterpene and the key bioactive constituent of the volatile oil derived from Curcumae Rhizoma. Numerous studies have demonstrated that germacrone exerts a pro-apoptotic effect in various solid and hematologic malignancies, such as liver cancer [20], breast cancer [18, 19, 37], gliomas [22], and other cancer types. Germacrone’s anti-tumor activity involves cell cycle arrest, tumor growth suppression, apoptosis induction, and reversal of drug resistance, collectively suppressing metastasis and invasion. However, its efficacy against renal cancer remains largely unexplored. In this study, germacrone significantly induced apoptosis and cell cycle arrest in 786-O renal malignant cells. The 786-O cell line is a well-established human renal clear cell adenocarcinoma cell line obtained from a primary tumor of a 58-year-old male patient. It exhibits critical molecular features of RCC, including the absence of the von Hippel-Lindau tumor suppressor gene, which causes constitutive HIF pathway stimulation, and it demonstrates broad resistance to conventional chemotherapeutic agents and radiation therapy [38]. This study demonstrated that subcutaneous administration of 1 × 107 786-O cells into BALB/c nude male mice resulted in tumor formation, and intraperitoneal injection of germacrone (60 and 90 mg/kg) significantly suppressed xenograft tumor growth. These findings illustrated that germacrone successfully inhibits renal malignancy, with only mild hepatic and renal toxicities observed.

  After clarifying the effects of germacrone on 786-O and Caki-1 cells through cell experiments, network pharmacology was employed to predict germacrone’s targets in RCC. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed pathways associated with lipid metabolism, atherosclerosis, and cancer. Using RNA sequencing and bioinformatics analysis, germacrone’s impact on pathway enrichment in renal cancer was further investigated, which identified DEGs primarily associated with lipid metabolism. The lipid biosynthesis pathway was prioritized for validation over other pathways (for example, PI3K-Akt) for three key reasons: First, it emerged as one of the most significantly enriched pathways in multi-omics analysis. Second, unlike generic cancer signaling, aberrant lipogenesis is a defining metabolic hallmark of ccRCC, offering a disease-specific therapeutic target. Third, this study hypothesized that disrupting upstream lipid homeostasis acts as the primary driver triggering the observed downstream apoptosis and cell cycle arrest. In the germacrone-treated group, key genes involved in lipid metabolic reprogramming—including FOS, ACSM2A, AMACR, UCP1, ABCA12, and the transcription factor SREBF1—were downregulated (Fig. 6B), suggesting that the anti-tumor effects of germacrone in renal cancer may be related to a decrease in fatty acid synthesis. Cancer cells rely heavily on elevated levels of energy substrates, including fatty acids and cholesterol [39, 40], to support rapid growth, proliferation, and metastatic capacity [40–42]. This metabolic shift has also been correlated with resistance to therapies, including sunitinib, in renal cancer [41–43]. Additionally, this metabolic shift was correlated with resistance against therapies, including sunitinib, in renal cancer [44–46], targeting this pathway holds significant promise for not only suppressing tumor growth but also overcoming drug resistance.

  Studies demonstrate that, unlike healthy cells, malignant cells actively produce cholesterol and fatty acids, with de novo fatty acid synthesis accounting for up to 93% of total lipid content in some cancer types [47]. These findings demonstrate that germacrone significantly reduces the protein levels of c-Fos and its downstream lipid synthesis targets, including SREBP1, FASN, and ACC1 (Fig. 7D). Furthermore, experiments using the proteasome inhibitor MG132 revealed that this reduction in c-Fos abundance is primarily driven by the inhibition of its synthesis rather than the acceleration of its degradation (Fig. 7J). Functionally, germacrone strongly reduced the accumulation of neutral lipid droplets (Fig. 7G) and decreased cellular TG and cholesterol levels (Fig. 7H) in 786-O cells. Collectively, these outcomes indicate that germacrone disrupts lipid biosynthesis in RCC cells by directly targeting c-Fos, downregulating the SREBP1/FASN pathway, and confirming that suppression of de novo fatty acid synthesis is a primary mechanism of its anti-tumor effect.

  As discussed earlier, lipid metabolic reprogramming, a hallmark of malignant tumors, supplies cancer cells with fatty acids essential for membrane synthesis, signaling molecules, and overall tumor survival, growth, and metastasis. Recent findings highlight fatty acid metabolic reprogramming as a key factor in promoting oncogenesis and chemoresistance in RCC. As an immediate-early protein, c-Fos associates with nuclear proteins from the c-Jun family to bind the AP-1 motif, a specific cis-acting DNA sequence, thereby regulating the transcription of target genes [48]. c-Fos, a central regulator of lipid metabolism, is upregulated in many cancer types [49, 50]. Binding to TPA-responsive factors in the SREBF1 promoter activates SREBF1 transcription, increasing the expression of lipid biosynthesis-related genes and counteracting apoptosis by maintaining lipid homeostasis [51]. This study reveals a novel mechanism: germacrone suppresses SREBF1 transcription by inhibiting the binding of c-Fos to the SREBF1 promoter. This disruption of lipid homeostasis contributes to the observed apoptotic cell death. The critical role of the c-Fos/SREBP1 axis was further validated: c-Fos OE effectively rescued germacrone-induced cell death and inhibition of lipid metabolism in RCC cells. It restored cell viability (Fig. 9B), colony formation (Fig. 9C), migration ability (Fig. 9D), and partially reversed the downregulation of FASN, ACC1, and SREBP1 protein levels (Fig. 9E). These findings definitively establish that germacrone’s anti-RCC activity is mediated through inhibition of the c-Fos/SREBP1 axis, thereby suppressing proliferation, enhancing apoptosis, and downregulating fatty acid synthesis.

  A critical consideration in targeting transcription factors like c-Fos is the potential for off-target effects on normal physiological processes. However, this study suggests that germacrone exerts a selective inhibitory effect on RCC cells primarily by targeting the tumor-specific metabolic vulnerability. Malignant cells, unlike normal counterparts, are often characterized by metabolic reprogramming—specifically elevated de novo lipogenesis driven by the c-Fos/SREBP1 axis—to support their rapid growth and membrane synthesis requirements [40–42, 46]. This creates a therapeutic window where RCC cells are disproportionately sensitive to c-Fos inhibition compared to normal tissues, which possess lower basal c-Fos activity and rely primarily on dietary lipid uptake rather than de novo synthesis to maintain homeostasis [52, 53]. Consistent with this, the in vivo toxicity assessment (Figs. 4F-H) demonstrated that germacrone administration did not induce observable hepatotoxicity or nephrotoxicity in mice. This aligns with recent findings that targeting FASN or SREBP pathways effectively suppresses tumor progression with minimal systemic toxicity [52], further supporting germacrone as a safe therapeutic agent that specifically targets the metabolic addiction of renal cancer cells.

  Beyond its direct anti-tumor activity, increasing evidence suggests that germacrone may confer protective effects on normal tissues, further enhancing its clinical value. Chemotherapy-induced toxicity, particularly nephrotoxicity caused by agents like cisplatin, remains a major challenge in cancer treatment. Notably, Soodvilai et al. reported that germacrone functions as a potent inhibitor of Organic Cation Transporter 2, thereby reducing cisplatin accumulation and cytotoxicity in renal proximal tubular cells without compromising its anti-cancer efficacy [54]. This ‘renoprotective’ potential is highly significant for RCC treatment, where preserving remaining renal function is paramount. Furthermore, germacrone has been demonstrated to exert hepatoprotective effects against xenobiotic-induced oxidative stress via the Nrf2 signaling pathway [55]. These findings are consistent with in vivo observations (Fig. 4F-H), where high-dose germacrone treatment (90 mg/kg) potently suppressed tumor growth yet caused no detectable histological damage or functional impairment in the liver and kidneys. Collectively, these findings support the concept that germacrone possesses a favorable therapeutic index, selectively targeting cancer cells through lipid metabolic reprogramming while sparing or even protecting normal tissues.


## Strengths and limitations
  This investigation presented persuasive evidence that germacrone can successfully treat renal malignancy. An in vivo efficacy test using a renal cancer xenograft mouse model validated germacrone’s influence on tumor growth. It demonstrated its suppression of aberrant lipid metabolism, as determined through network pharmacology and RNA sequencing analyses. This study is the first to establish that germacrone triggers apoptosis and cell cycle arrest by suppressing c-Fos/SREBP1-mediated fatty acid synthesis. This study identified a novel therapeutic target and highlighted germacrone as a promising natural lead compound for developing new treatments, particularly for patients with renal cell cancer.

  This study has several limitations. First, only a xenograft tumor model was utilized to evaluate germacrone’s anti-RCC function in vivo. Patient-derived xenograft models and organoid technology, which better recapitulate the tumor microenvironment and intercellular heterogeneity of primary tissues, may provide a more precise evaluation of germacrone’s drug response under physiological conditions. Second, this study primarily relied on 786-O and Caki-1 human ccRCC cell lines for in vitro experiments. RCC exhibits significant heterogeneity, encompassing various subtypes (for example, papillary RCC, chromophobe RCC) [56], which may differ in molecular characteristics and metabolic reprogramming patterns. Future studies should validate germacrone’s effects and mechanisms in a broader panel of RCC cell lines (including different subtypes) and patient-derived primary cells. Third, while preliminary evidence for direct germacrone–c-Fos interaction was provided through molecular docking, CETSA and MST, these findings require further substantiation. The precise binding site of germacrone on the c-Fos protein remains unidentified, and it is unclear how this binding impacts c-Fos’s transcriptional activity and DNA binding affinity to the SREBF1 promoter. Moreover, as a natural sesquiterpene, germacrone likely faces drug-likeness challenges such as poor aqueous solubility, low bioavailability, and rapid systemic metabolism [57]. This study did not assess its pharmacokinetic properties (for example, absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion). Additionally, novel delivery approaches (for example, nanocarriers) were not explored to enhance tumor targeting and efficacy and potentially reduce systemic toxicity. Addressing these factors will be critical for its future clinical translation.


## Conclusion
  For the first time, this study demonstrated that germacrone inhibits 786-O cell growth and motility by inducing apoptotic cell death. Network pharmacology and transcriptome sequencing analyses illustrated that c-Fos suppression and lipid metabolism disruption are key contributors to germacrone-induced cytotoxicity. Furthermore, this study demonstrated that c-Fos OE effectively reversed apoptosis and suppression of lipid metabolism caused by germacrone in 786-O cells. Based on these findings, the c-Fos/SREBP1 axis represents a potential therapeutic target for RCC, and germacrone could be a valuable natural compound for treating RCC in clinical settings.
