# Foxn3 is part of a transcriptional network that regulates primary cilia in the developing retina


## Abstract
  Retinas from mice with a targeted disruption of the gene encoding forkhead transcription factor Foxn3 contained additional displaced amacrine interneurons and retinal astrocytes in the inner plexiform and ganglion cell layers, as well as ectopic primary cilia on bipolar and amacrine interneurons. Foxn3 is a transcriptional repressor and numerous genes linked to cilia structure or assembly were upregulated in embryonic retinas with disrupted Foxn3. CUT&RUN analysis revealed that many upregulated retinal genes were bound by the Foxn3 and Rfx3 proteins. A short hydrophobic motif (LXXLXWL) shared by Foxn3, Foxn4, and Foxj1 was required for association with Rfx3 and for full transcriptional repression by Foxn3, as well as for full transcriptional activation by Foxj1 or Foxn4. AlphaFold 3 predicted interaction between the hydrophobic motif and the Rfx3 dimerization domain. Mutations in Rfx3 at the predicted interaction site disrupted association of Rfx3 with Foxn3, Foxn4 or Foxj1. These results reveal a new layer of transcriptional regulation of genes required for cilia, with Foxn3 functioning as a repressor of cilia genes and limiting primary cilia formation in the developing retina.


## Introduction
  Cilia are thin, filamentous, membrane-enclosed structures with a microtubule core, present on cells in many eukaryotes (reviewed in Derderian et al., 2023). Motile cilia direct fluid flow or drive cellular movement, while primary cilia (also known as sensory cilia) mediate reception of extracellular signals, including Sonic Hedgehog (SHH), TGFβ, PDGF-A, Wnt, and Notch. Photoreceptor outer segments are specialized primary cilia for phototransduction (Chen et al., 2021). In mouse retinas, retinal ganglion cells, amacrine interneurons, and Müller glia have primary cilia, while most bipolar cells do not (Kowal et al., 2022; Ning et al., 2021); retinal progenitor cells have apical primary cilia (Burnett et al., 2017; Dubaic et al., 2023). The assembly and function of cilia involve proteins produced by hundreds of genes in the ciliome (Elliott et al., 2023); ciliopathies can arise from mutations in many of these genes. Ciliopathies frequently affect photoreceptors, lead to retinal degeneration, and impact vision (Chen et al., 2021). Defects in primary cilia or in signaling pathways localized to cilia alter cell fates or retinal structure during development (Cwinn et al., 2011; Dilan et al., 2019; Fiore et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2005; Zhang and Yang, 2001).

  Members of the forkhead and regulatory factor X (RFX) transcription factor families regulate genes associated with cilia in multiple organisms. Forkhead proteins bind DNA via the forkhead domain and have multiple roles in development (reviewed in Herman et al., 2021). The vertebrate forkhead protein Foxj1 upregulates genes encoding components of motile cilia (Choksi et al., 2014; Patir et al., 2020; Vij et al., 2012; Yu et al., 2008) in cooperation with Rfx2–3 transcription factors (Didon et al., 2013; Lemeille et al., 2020). Foxn4 functions with Foxj1 to allow generation of multiciliated cells; both are expressed in ciliated ependymal cells of the central nervous system (CNS) (Campbell et al., 2016; Quigley and Kintner, 2017; Zheng et al., 2025). In zebrafish, a Foxj1 homolog is necessary for retinal regeneration after injury, but not for retinal development (Lyu et al., 2023).

  Over 40 genes encode forkhead proteins in mice or humans and mutations in forkhead genes are associated with disease (Herman et al., 2021). The FOXN subfamily includes Foxn4, a gene required for the formation of most or all horizontal and many amacrine interneurons in retinas (Li et al., 2004). Foxn4 is essential in retinal developmental timing, promoting interneuron and photoreceptor formation while suppressing retinal ganglion cells (Liu et al., 2020). Foxn4 also functions in spinal cord development (Misra et al., 2014), and is linked to heart development in zebrafish (Chi et al., 2008). The Foxn3 protein functions as a transcriptional repressor in mammalian cells (Huot et al., 2014; Pati et al., 1997; Scott and Plon, 2005). Foxn2 has sequence similarity to Foxn3; both proteins have been reported to function as tumor suppressors (reviewed in Song et al., 2023). Foxn3 regulates glucose metabolism in liver (Karanth et al., 2019) and inflammation in lungs (Yu et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2023). Forkhead factors recognize one of two unrelated DNA sequences, depending on subfamily, but Foxn3 recognizes both sequences, potentially allowing it to regulate more target genes (Nakagawa et al., 2013; Rogers et al., 2019).

  We identified Foxn3 mRNA as a target of microRNA miR-216b in mouse retinas (Zhang et al., 2022). Foxn3 is expressed in retinal progenitor cells, differentiated bipolar cells and cholinergic amacrine cells (Boudreau-Pinsonneault et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2022). When overexpressed in neonatal retina, miR-216b promotes generation of additional amacrine cells and loss of bipolar cells. Co-expression of Foxn3 partially reversed these effects, while inhibition of Foxn3 generated similar phenotypes (Zhang et al., 2022). To investigate the role of Foxn3 in the developing retina, we analyzed retinas from mice in which Foxn3 is disrupted (Birling et al., 2021). We observed additional amacrine cells and astrocytes in the inner plexiform layer (IPL) and ganglion cell layer (GCL) at P21, as well as ectopic primary cilia formation on bipolar and amacrine cells. In developing retinas without Foxn3, numerous genes involved in cilia formation and function were upregulated. We mapped genomic regions bound by Foxn3, Foxn4, and Rfx3 proteins and found that Foxn3 binds at many upregulated genes. We show that Foxn3 or Foxn4 can form a complex with Rfx3, and specific amino acids in these proteins are required for interaction, as well as for transcriptional regulation by Foxn4, Foxj1, or Foxn3 in combination with Rfx3. Our results indicate that Foxn3 is a negative regulator of cilia genes and limits primary cilia formation in the retina.


## Results

### Additional and ectopic amacrine cells and astrocytes in retinas from mice without Foxn3
  Foxn3em1(IMPC)Bay/Mmnc mice, heterozygous for a targeted deletion of Foxn3 exon 3 (Birling et al., 2021), were backcrossed. Homozygous Foxn3em1(IMPC)Bay/Mmnc embryos were present at the expected frequency (25.1%; N= 183) at the end of gestation on embryonic day 19 (E19) and embryos appeared grossly normal, as reported (Birling et al., 2021). Without exon 3, exon 2 was spliced to exon 4 in the Foxn3 mRNA (Supplemental Fig. S1). The resulting coding region is missing part of the DNA binding domain encoded by exon 3. A frameshift at the start of exon 4 prevents translation of the C-terminal transcriptional repression domain (Scott and Plon, 2005) and adds a novel C-terminus (Supplemental Fig. S2A). Foxn3 without exon 3 failed to inhibit activation of a reporter repressed by wild-type (WT) Foxn3 (Supplemental Fig. S2B), indicating the truncated Foxn3 protein is likely nonfunctional. We therefore refer to homozygous Foxn3em1(IMPC)Bay/Mmnc mice as Foxn3 knockout (KO).

  Nearly all Foxn3 KO offspring died at or shortly after birth, consistent with prior reports (Birling et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2023), but we recovered three viable Foxn3 KO mice among mice genotyped after P7 (N=221; estimated survival rate ~3%). Foxn3 KO offspring were smaller than WT offspring (mean±s.d.: Foxn3 KO 4.57±1.14 gm, N=3; WT 13.96±0.47 gm, N=4; p<0.0001, unpaired t test) but appeared otherwise normal at P21. Retina sections from P21 Foxn3 KO mice revealed that while the number of AP2α (Tfap2a)-positive amacrine cells in the inner nuclear layer (INL) did not change, displaced amacrine cells located in the GCL increased compared to WT, and ectopic amacrine cells were found within the IPL (Fig 1A–C). Vsx2 (Chx10)-positive bipolar cells, Sox9-positive Müller glia in the INL, and photoreceptor nuclei in the ONL, did not change number significantly in Foxn3 KO retinas (Fig 1C). However, Sox9-positive glial cells in the IPL and GCL of Foxn3 KO retinas increased (Fig 1D). Sox9/Glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP)-positive astrocytes were present in the IPL and GCL of Foxn3 KO retinas, while Sox9/glutamine synthetase (Glul)-positive Müller glial cell bodies remained in the INL (Fig. 1E, F; Supplemental Fig. S3A, B), indicating that Sox9-positive cells in the IPL/GCL are astrocytes. GFAP-labeled processes from astrocytes extended throughout the IPL in Foxn3 KO retinas, in contrast to WT (Fig. 1E, F; Supplemental Fig. S3A, B). Retina sections from E19 Foxn3 KO embryos did not show obvious changes in amacrine cells or overall structure (Supplemental Fig. S3C–F), indicating changes in amacrine cells at P21 likely arise during postnatal development. To confirm that the phenotype in rare surviving P21 Foxn3 KO retinas is representative, we prepared retinal explants from WT and Foxn3 KO mice at P0 and cultured the explants for 11 days in vitro (DIV). Analysis revealed increased displaced amacrine cells and amacrine cells in the IPL of Foxn3 KO explants (Supplemental Fig. S4). Astrocytes were not significantly increased, possibly due to loss of ganglion cell-derived signals (Fruttiger et al., 1996) after ganglion cell death in explants.


### Upregulation of cilia-associated genes and ectopic cilia in Foxn3 KO retinas
  To investigate retinal gene expression changes after loss of Foxn3, we used RNA-seq to compare gene expression in WT and Foxn3 KO retinas at E19. 1655 genes were differentially expressed in Foxn3 KO retinas (FDR<0.01, Supplemental Table 1) with most mRNAs upregulated, including nearly all mRNAs that changed more than two-fold (Fig. 2A), consistent with Foxn3 repressing transcription. Foxj1mRNA was upregulated ~20-fold and several genes associated with cilia were upregulated over 100-fold (e.g., Cfap65, Cfap44, and Cfap52). mRNAs for Rfx1–3 were significantly upregulated, although each increased less than two-fold (Supplemental Table 1). Foxn3 mRNA without exon 3 was significantly upregulated in Foxn3 KO retinas, although less than two-fold increased. Several genes repressed by FOXN3 overexpression in human cells (Huot et al., 2014) were upregulated in Foxn3 KO retinas (e.g., Pim2, Dryk3, Iqck, and Ift88). Strikingly, most highly enriched GO categories for differentially expressed genes were components of cilia and/or were associated with cilia assembly (Fig. 2B, C). 305 significantly upregulated genes are part of the ciliome (Supplemental Table 1). The ciliome gene fraction increased among highly upregulated genes (Fig. 2D). Foxn3 protein was not detected by western blot, while Foxj1 protein increased in Foxn3 KO retinas at P0 (Fig. 2E); Foxj1 also increased in neuroblast nuclei in Foxn3 KO E19 retinas (Supplemental Fig. S5).

  Foxn3 is expressed in retinal bipolar cells (Zhang et al., 2022), which generally lack primary cilia (Ning et al., 2021). Immunofluorescence analysis of Arl13b, a protein that labels primary cilia, revealed numerous cilia present on Vsx2-expressing bipolar cells in Foxn3 KO retinas at P21, with a 7-fold increase over WT retinas (Fig. 2F–L). Arl13b labeled cilia on presumptive amacrine cells in the inner INL (Ning et al., 2021), with more cilia detected on amacrine cells of Foxn3 KO retinas (Fig. 2M). Ectopic cilia also were present on bipolar cells and amacrine cells in P0 Foxn3 KO retinal explants analyzed after 11 DIV (Supplemental Fig. S6). Primary cilia on bipolar cells at 11 DIV suggests that ectopic cilia may form during or shortly after bipolar cell differentiation. Relatively few cilia were present on amacrine cells in WT explants at 11 DIV, suggesting that loss of Foxn3 may allow premature as well as increased cilia formation on amacrine cells.


### Genomic binding sites for endogenous Foxn3, Rfx3, and Foxn4 in the developing retina
  To identify likely target genes for Foxn3, we performed CUT&RUN analysis (Skene and Henikoff, 2017) to identify genomic regions bound by endogenous Foxn3 in WT mouse retinas at E16, during the peak of amacrine cell formation (Voinescu et al., 2009). 2794 regions with Foxn3 binding were identified (Supplemental Table 2), based on regions of read enrichment consistent between two separate experiments, each with two anti-Foxn3 samples and two IgG controls. Heat maps for individual CUT&RUN libraries showed consistent signals at identified Foxn3 binding regions (Fig. 3A). 90% of the genomic regions bound by Foxn3 overlapped with mouse candidate cis-Regulatory Elements (cCREs) annotated by ENCODE (Abascal et al., 2020), with binding at promoters being most frequent (Fig. 3B). We annotated Foxn3-bound regions based on genes with the nearest transcription start site (TSS). For Foxn3 KO retinas, 49% of upregulated genes and 68% of upregulated ciliome genes were linked to at least one genomic region bound by Foxn3 in the CUT&RUN analysis, indicating that these genes are likely direct targets of Foxn3 repression (Fig. 3C). An example is Foxj1, with a prominent Foxn3 binding peak at its promoter (Fig. 3D). The Foxn3 protein binds to two unrelated DNA sequence motifs: FHL-N and FKH (Nakagawa et al., 2013; Rogers et al., 2019). The FHL-N motif is a subset of the FHL binding motif bound by many Forkhead proteins (Nakagawa et al., 2013). FHL-N, FHL, and FKH motifs were significantly enriched in Foxn3 binding regions (Fig. 3E). Intriguingly, the most enriched de novo motif in Foxn3 binding regions resembles motifs bound by RFX transcription factors. Motifs for Rfx3 (a dimeric RFX binding motif) and Rfx7 (a monomeric RFX binding motif) were enriched in regions bound by Foxn3 (Fig. 3E).

  Prominent Foxn3 binding was present at promoters for Ccdc39, Tekt1, and Cfap206 (Fig. 4A), three genes involved in cilia formation and upregulated in Foxn3 KO retinas (Fig. 2A). Luciferase reporters driven by each promoter region were co-transfected into HEK293 cells with expression vectors for Foxj1, Rfx3, and/or Foxn3. Foxj1 activated all three reporters ~8–15 fold (Fig. 4B–D), while Rfx3 activated the reporters less than two-fold, but modestly increased Ccdc39 reporter activation when co-expressed with Foxj1. Co-expression of Foxn3 blocked activation of all three reporters by Foxj1, with or without Rfx3, consistent with Foxn3 directly repressing these genes (Fig. 4B–D).

  To investigate whether Foxn3 genomic binding overlaps with genomic binding of the Rfx3 or Foxn4 proteins, we used CUT&RUN to identify regions bound by endogenous Rfx3 or Foxn4 in WT E16 mouse retinas (Fig. 5A–B) (Supplemental Tables 3, 4). Individual CUT&RUN samples showed consistent enrichment at called peaks (Supplemental Fig. S7). Remarkably, 81% of Foxn3 bound regions overlapped regions bound by Rfx3, while 40% of the Foxn3 bound regions overlapped regions bound by Foxn4; 32% of the Foxn3 bound regions overlapped both Foxn4 and Rfx3 bound regions (Fig. 5C). For Foxn4, 36% of the bound regions overlapped Rfx3 bound regions. Rfx3 binding motifs were significantly enriched in regions bound by Rfx3, while FHL-N and FHL binding motifs were significantly enriched in Foxn4 bound regions (Fig. 5D).

  The Foxj1 promoter was bound by both Foxn3 and Rfx3 in embryonic retinas, but not Foxn4 (Fig. 5E). We co-transfected a Foxj1 promoter Luciferase reporter into HEK293 cells with expression vectors for Foxn4, Rfx3, and/or Foxn3 (Fig. 5F). Rfx3 weakly activated the reporter, while Foxn4 activated more than 10-fold; co-expression of Rfx3 with Foxn4 further increased activation. However, co-expression of Foxn3 strongly inhibited reporter activation by Foxn4, with or without Rfx3. We tested additional reporters based on intronic enhancers in Ptprf, Meis1, and Notch1, all bound by Foxn3 and Rfx3 in our retina CUT&RUN data (Supplemental Fig. S8A).Meis1 and Notch1 have been linked to the Foxn4 pathway in retina and/or spinal cord (Islam et al., 2013; Luo et al., 2012; Misra et al., 2014); both enhancers were bound by Foxn4 in retinas, while the Ptrpf enhancer was not. However, all three reporters could be activated by Foxn4 in HEK293 cells, and the Ptprf and Meis1 reporters were further activated by co-expression of Rfx3 with Foxn4 (Supplemental Fig. S8B–D). While Ptprf mRNA was upregulated in Foxn3 KO retinas, neither Meis1 nor Notch1 mRNAs changed significantly (Supplemental Table 1). Surprisingly, only the Ptprf reporter was repressed by co-expression of Foxn3, suggesting that additional elements within the enhancers modulate Foxn3 repression.

  Luciferase reporters based on two intronic enhancers located in the Foxn3 gene, each bound by Foxn3, Rfx3, and Foxn4 in retinas, were assayed in HEK293 cells (Fig. 6A–E, Supplemental Fig. S8A, E). Both reporters were activated by Foxn4 and strongly activated by Foxn4 in combination with Ascl1, a basic-helix-loop-helix (bHLH) transcription factor that cooperates with Foxn4 (Luo et al., 2012) (Fig. 6B, Supplemental Fig. S5E). Co-expression of Rfx3 with Foxn4 or with Foxn4 and Ascl1 further increased activation of the reporters, while co-expression of Foxn3 strongly inhibited reporter activity driven by Foxn4, Ascl1, or Rfx3, alone or in combination (Fig. 6B, Supplemental Fig. S8E). For the Foxn3 enhancer 2 reporter, we tested whether binding sites for each factor (Fig. 6A) were required for regulation. Altering the two E-boxes (bHLH binding motifs) present in the reporter prevented Ascl1 cooperation with Foxn4 and Rfx3 (Fig. 6C). Mutation of the FKH and the FHL-N forkhead motifs in the Foxn3 Enhancer 2 reporter greatly reduced activation by Foxn4 or by Rfx3 with Foxn4 (Fig. 6D). However, residual activation by Foxn4 still was strongly inhibited by co-expression of Foxn3 for either the reporter with E-box mutations or the reporter with FKH/FHL-N mutations (Fig. 6C, D). Mutation of the single Rfx3 binding motif in the reporter blocked activation by Foxn4 and greatly reduced activation by Foxn4 with Ascl1 (Fig. 6E). Co-expression of Foxn3 failed to inhibit activation of the reporter without the Rfx3 site by Foxn4 and Ascl1. Taken together, these observations indicate that Rfx3 cooperates with Foxn4 for transcriptional activation and that the Rfx3 site is required for transcriptional repression by Foxn3.


### A conserved LXXLXWL motif in Foxn4, Foxj1, and Foxn3 is required for interaction with Rfx3
  The mouse Foxn4 or Foxj1 proteins can functionally substitute for the FKH-8 forkhead protein to support ciliome gene expression in C. elegans (Brocal-Ruiz et al., 2023). FKH-8 cooperates with DAF-19, the only RFX protein in C. elegans, to activate cilia gene expression, while Foxj1 interacts with RFX proteins to regulate genes involved in motile cilia formation in mammals (Didon et al., 2013). Protein-protein interaction screens detected associations between Foxj1, Foxj2, Foxn2, Foxn3 or Foxn4 and Rfx1–3 (Huttlin et al., 2021; Huttlin et al., 2017; Li et al., 2015). We looked for conserved amino acid sequences outside the forkhead domain in FKH-8, Foxj1, and Foxn2/Foxn3/Foxn4 to identify candidate motifs that might mediate complex formation with RFX proteins. A hydrophobic, leucine rich motif with a conserved tryptophan, LXXLXWL, is located N-terminal to the forkhead domain in these proteins (Fig. 7A). A similar sequence is present in Foxj2/Foxj3. A divergent motif without the central leucine or the tryptophan is present in mouse Foxi1, a protein that cannot substitute for FKH-8 in C. elegans (Brocal-Ruiz et al., 2023). We constructed forkhead expression vectors in which the LXXLXWL sequence was replaced with seven alanine residues (Foxn4–7A, Foxn3–7A, and Foxj1–7A, Fig. 7A). The Foxn3–7A protein expressed in HEK293 cells remains capable of binding to DNA in an electromobility shift assay (EMSA) (Supplemental Fig. S9). Hemagglutinin-(HA-)epitope-tagged versions of Foxn4, Foxn3, or Foxj1 co-immunoprecipitated with Rfx3 when co-expressed in HEK293 cells. HA-tagged versions of Foxn4–7A, Foxn3–7A, and Foxj1–7A were expressed similarly to WT proteins, but failed to co-immunoprecipitate with Rfx3 (Fig. 7B). Expression of Foxj1–7A protein failed to activate the Ccdc39 reporter, with or without Rfx3, while Foxn3–7A repressed Ccdc39 reporter activation by Foxj1 (Fig. 7C), but significantly less than Foxn3. Foxn4–7A failed to activate the Foxn3 enhancer 2 reporter by itself and showed reduced activation when combined with Rfx3 or with Ascl1 and Rfx3. The Foxn3–7A expression vector failed to inhibit Foxn4 activation of the reporter, with or without Ascl1 and Rfx3 (Fig. 7D). Together with previous reports, these observations indicate that the Rfx3 protein forms a complex with Foxn3, Foxn4, or Foxj1 that likely contributes to transcriptional regulation. Disruption of the LXXLXWL motif in the forkhead proteins or lack of an Rfx3 binding site reduced or prevented reporter activation, although the effect differed for Foxn4 and Foxj1. Foxn3 repression without the LXXLXWL motif was reduced or blocked, depending on the reporter, suggesting interactions in addition to Rfx3 may contribute.


### Interaction between the LXXLXWL motif and the Rfx3 dimerization domain
  Complete structures are not available for Foxn3 or Rfx3 proteins, although structures for RFX1 and Foxn3 DNA binding domains have been reported (Gajiwala et al., 2000; Rogers et al., 2019). We used AlphaFold 3 (Abramson et al., 2024) to model the structure of Foxn3 in a complex with a Rfx3 homodimer and a short DNA sequence containing RFX and FKH sites. Interestingly, the predicted structure juxtaposed the Foxn3 LXXLXWL motif to the two Rfx3 dimerization domains, with the LXXLXWL motif as the main contact for Foxn3 (Supplemental Fig. S11A–C). PyMol indicates a pi-pi interaction between the tryptophan (W65) in the LXXLXWL motif and a phenylalanine (F620) in one Rfx3 molecule. However, overall confidence scores for the predicted complex were low, likely due to substantial disordered regions in each protein. We used AlphaFold 3 to generate a model based on the C-terminus of Rfx3, including the extended dimerization domain and adjacent B domain (Emery et al., 1996; Katan-Khaykovich and Shaul, 1998), in combination with the N-terminus of Foxn3 (Fig. 8A–B, Supplemental Fig. S12). This model placed the Foxn3 LXXLXWL motif at the same interface with two Rfx3 dimerization domains and maintained the W65 pi-pi interaction with Rfx3 F620, but with high confidence scores (Supplemental Fig. S11C). AlphaFold 3 models for the N-termini of Foxn4 or Foxj1 with the Rfx3 dimerization domain yielded similar high confidence score predictions for interaction via the LXXLXWL motif, also with pi-pi interactions between the LXXLXWL tryptophan and Rfx3 F620 (Supplemental Figs. S13, S11C). In Foxj1 a second tryptophan (W48) near the LXXLXWL motif, also was predicted to form a pi-pi interaction with Rfx3 F620.

  To investigate Rfx3 amino acids required for interaction with forkhead proteins, we generated expression vectors for Rfx3 with individual substitution mutations: F620A, S616D, or H624A. Rfx3 S616 is predicted to interact with the Foxn3 LXXLXWL motif, while Rfx3 H624 is predicted to interact with Rfx3 F620 in the other chain of the Rfx3 dimer (Fig. 8B). F620 and S616 are conserved among Rfx1–4 and DAF-19 (Fig. 8C). We also tested Rfx3 C544S, which disrupts Rfx3 dimerization (Chen et al., 2018) but is not near the predicted interaction with the LXXLXWL motif (Supplemental Figure S11D). HA-tagged Foxn3 or HA-tagged Foxn4 were co-expressed with Rfx3 or each mutated Rfx3 protein in HEK293 cells. Rfx3 proteins with each mutation showed little co-immunoprecipitation with Foxn3 or Foxn4 (Fig. 8D, E). We evaluated the effect of each mutation on Rfx3 dimerization by co-immunoprecipitation of HA- and Myc-tagged Rfx3 proteins containing the same mutation. F620A, H624A, and C544S substitutions reduced co-immunoprecipitation of Rfx3 dimers, while S616D did not (Fig. 8F). Consistent with these results, F620 and H624 are predicted to be at the Rfx3 dimerization interface and interact with residues in the other Rfx3 chain, while Rfx3 S616 appears to interact only with the LXXLXWL motif (Fig. 8A–B). S616D, F620A, and H624A mutations in Rfx3 did not prevent DNA binding as assessed by EMSA (Supplemental Fig. S17). However, only Rfx3 S616D showed the same pattern of shifted bands as WT Rfx3. The altered EMSA patterns for Rfx3 F620A or Rfx3 H624A resemble EMSA changes reported for Rfx1 proteins that cannot dimerize (Katan-Khaykovich and Shaul, 1998). Co-expression of Foxn4 with either Rfx3 S616D or Rfx3 F620A reduced cooperative activation of a reporter, while co-expression of Foxn4 with Rfx3 H624A or Rfx3 C544S fully activated the reporter (Fig.8G). Taken together, these observations support a direct interaction between the forkhead LXXLXWL motif and the dimerization domains of an Rfx3 dimer. While disruption of Rfx3 dimerization prevented co-immunoprecipitation with Foxn3 or Foxn4, only mutations at Rfx3 S616 or Rfx3 F620, residues predicted to interact directly with the forkhead LXXLXWL motif, prevented cooperative reporter activation with Foxn4.


## Discussion
  Over 300 genes involved in cilia formation or function were upregulated in the developing retinas of Foxn3 KO mice, with some mRNAs increasing over 100-fold. CUT&RUN analysis revealed that most upregulated ciliome genes in the Foxn3 KO retinas were bound by Foxn3 protein, consistent with direct repression (Scott and Plon, 2005). In differentiated retinas, Foxn3 is expressed in bipolar cells and some amacrine cells (Zhang et al., 2022). Strikingly, loss of Foxn3 led to increased primary cilia on bipolar cells and amacrine cells in the INL at P21, as well as in Foxn3 KO retina explant cultures, indicating that Foxn3 is required to prevent ectopic cilia formation in the mouse retina. The consequences of additional primary cilia on cellular function are unclear, but most retinal bipolar cells in the mouse do not have primary cilia (Ning et al., 2021), so bipolar cells with cilia potentially could respond to extracellular signals that might otherwise not be received. These results are consistent with the observation that overexpression of FOXN3 downregulated genes involved in cilium biogenesis and reduced primary cilia formation in human lung carcinoma cells (Huot et al., 2014).

  We observed additional displaced amacrine cells in the GCL of Foxn3 KO retinas at P21, as well as ectopic amacrine cells located within the IPL, although the number of amacrine cells, bipolar cells, and Müller glia in the INL were unchanged. Ectopic amacrine cells were not apparent at E19, suggesting that these amacrine cells form and/or migrate during postnatal retinal development. Amacrine cells located in the central IPL in Foxn3 KO retinas resemble a layer of ectopic amacrine cells observed in the IPL of Plagl1 (Zac1) KO retina explants (Ma et al., 2007), although there are fewer ectopic amacrine cells in Foxn3 KO retinas. The loss of Plagl1 reduces TGF-beta II inhibition of amacrine cell formation. TGF-beta signaling is mediated by receptors in primary cilia and signaling can be impacted by changes within cilia (Christensen et al., 2017), suggesting that altered cilia in Foxn3 KO retinas could alter TGF-beta feedback. However, a recent report linked reduced Foxn3 to the stabilization of Smad4 and increased TGF-beta signaling in lungs (Yu et al., 2025), suggesting Foxn3 may regulate TGF-beta signaling at multiple levels.

  Foxn3 KO P21 retinas had additional retinal astrocytes in the GCL and IPL, with astrocytic processes extending throughout the IPL. Astrocytes migrate into the retina from the optic nerve head (reviewed by Paisley and Kay, 2021) and their dispersal is guided by signals from retinal ganglion cells (O’Sullivan et al., 2017). Transgenic overexpression of PDGF-A in retinal neurons increased astrocyte proliferation and allowed astrocytes to spread into additional retinal layers (Fruttiger et al., 1996), suggesting that astrocyte changes in the Foxn3 KO retinas could arise from altered signaling. Intriguingly, Pdgfa mRNA increased over two-fold in Foxn3 KO retinas at E19 (Supplemental Table 1). Loss of Foxn3 could also affect signal reception through changes in cilia (Bear et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024). In retina, astrocytes guide vascularization (O’Sullivan et al., 2017), suggesting it may be interesting to examine retinal vasculature in the Foxn3 KO in the future. Foxn3 also regulates genes that are not associated with cilia, so it remains possible that developmental changes in astrocytes and/or amacrine cells arise from gene expression changes unrelated to cilia.

  While this manuscript was in preparation, Liu et al. reported retina-specific conditional knockout (cKO) of Foxn3 led to upregulation of cilia-associated genes, including Foxj1, and formation of ectopic cilia on bipolar and amacrine neurons in mature retinas (Liu et al., 2025), consistent with results described here for Foxn3 KO retinas. Liu et al. also observed changes in ERG responses suggesting altered bipolar cell function in Foxn3 cKO retinas. Astrocytes were not analyzed in the Foxn3 cKO retinas, and additional displaced amacrine cells or IPL-localized amacrine cells were not reported. The differences may arise from the models used: in the Foxn3 cKO, Foxn3 exon 2 was deleted in the developing retina with Six3-Cre, while in the model analyzed here, Foxn3 exon 3 was deleted in all cells throughout embryogenesis. In addition, Foxn3 cKO retinas were analyzed at later ages, so potential ectopic amacrine cells may have been eliminated by cell death.

  Forkhead proteins and RFX transcription factors are linked to the activation of cilia gene expression in both vertebrates and invertebrates. The Foxj1 promoter was bound by Foxn3 in embryonic retinas, with Foxj1 mRNA and protein upregulated in Foxn3 KO retinas. Increased Foxj1 likely further activates Foxj1 targets derepressed by absence of Foxn3. Foxj1 cooperates with Rfx3 and we found that Rfx3 genomic binding in the embryonic mouse retina extensively overlaps with Foxn3 binding, and to a lesser extent overlaps with Foxn4 binding. Reporters based on genomic regulatory elements bound by these proteins could be activated by Foxn4 and Rfx3 in cell culture but were repressed by Foxn3. While Rfx3 expression by itself had modest effects on reporters, mutation of the Rfx3 binding site in a reporter both reduced cooperative activation by Foxn4 and prevented repression of the remaining activity by Foxn3, suggesting that different forkhead proteins can interact with Rfx3 to either activate or repress target genes.

  Interactions between RFX proteins and Foxj1, Foxn4, Foxn2, and Foxn3 forkhead proteins have been reported (Didon et al., 2013; Huttlin et al., 2021; Huttlin et al., 2017; Li et al., 2015). We identified an evolutionarily conserved LXXLXWL motif required for association between Rfx3 and Foxj1, Foxn4, or Foxn3 proteins. Mutation of the LXXLXWL motif in Foxj1 prevented activation of a reporter, while mutation of the motif in Foxn4 reduced cooperative reporter activation with Rfx3. Mutation of the LXXLXWL motif in Foxn3 reduced or prevented repression, depending on the reporter. Although these results suggest that interaction between the forkhead proteins and Rfx3 are important for transcriptional regulation, it remains possible that additional factors interact with the LXXLXWL motif to modulate transcription.

  AlphaFold 3 predicted that LXXLXWL motifs in Foxn3, Foxn4 or Foxj1 interact with the Rfx3 dimerization domain in a Rfx3 homodimer. Single amino acid substitutions in Rfx3 at the predicted interface with the LXXLXWL motif, or mutations that disrupt Rfx3 dimer formation, prevented co-immunoprecipitation of Rfx3 with Foxn3 or Foxn4, supporting the predicted interaction. In contrast, only substitutions at either of two amino acids in Rfx3 that are predicted to directly interact with the LXXLXWL motif prevented Rfx3 from cooperating with Foxn4 on a reporter. Mutations that disrupted Rfx3 dimerization altered but did not prevent Rfx3 DNA binding as assessed by EMSA, consistent with prior analysis of Rfx1 (Katan-Khaykovich and Shaul, 1998). Interaction with DNA and/or other proteins may allow dimer formation on DNA for Rfx3 proteins that cannot dimerize in solution. Alternately, an Rfx3 monomer may be able to interact with forkhead proteins when bound to adjacent sites on DNA. Based on our observations, cooperation between Foxn4 or Foxj1 and Rfx3 likely involves both binding to nearby genomic sites and the formation of protein-protein complexes. Since Foxn3 binds to the same sequences as Foxn4 or Foxj1 (Nakagawa et al., 2013; Rogers et al., 2019), Foxn3 may repress genes by competing with Foxn4 or Foxj1 for DNA binding sites, by competing for interaction with Rfx3, and by recruiting transcriptional corepressors (Huot et al., 2014; Scott and Plon, 2005). Such a multifaceted mechanism should allow more potent repression by Foxn3 (see model in Supplemental Figure S18). As Rfx3 residues required for interaction with the LXXLXWL motif are conserved in other RFX factors (Fig. 8C), additional RFX proteins likely interact with forkhead factors with the LXXLXWL motif.

  RFX proteins have been associated with both activation and repression of target genes (Didon et al., 2013; Iwama et al., 1999; Nakayama et al., 2003). Extensive overlap between Rfx3 and Foxn3 genomic binding in developing retina raises the possibility that Foxn3 represses numerous genes in part by interaction with Rfx3. Some overlapping binding locations for Foxn3 and Rfx3 detected by CUT&RUN contain one or more RFX binding motifs but no consensus Foxn3 binding motif (e.g., Ptprf in Supplemental Fig. S8A). Possibly Foxn3 can bind to DNA-bound Rfx3 without direct DNA binding by Foxn3. Alternately, Foxn3 in complex with Rfx3 may be able to bind at a non-canonical DNA binding site (Berkes et al., 2004). Genetic disruptions of RFX genes are associated with human neurodevelopmental disorders (Harris et al., 2021), suggesting that interactions between RFX factors and Foxn3 or other forkhead factors could be important during CNS development.

  Foxn3 and Foxn4 mRNA expression overlaps in retinal progenitor cells (Zhang et al., 2022). Since Foxn4 binds to two intronic enhancers in the Foxn3 gene, and can activate reporters based on those enhancers, Foxn4, with Ascl1 or other factors, may upregulate Foxn3 in retinal cells to restrict activation of Foxj1 and its downstream targets. However, Foxn3 mRNA increased about two-fold in Foxn4 KO retinas (see Supplemental Table 2 in Liu et al., 2020), suggesting additional factors influence Foxn3 expression. Foxn3 mRNA also increases in Foxn3 KO retinas, and Foxn3 can repress reporters based on the Foxn3 intronic enhancers, suggesting negative feedback limits Foxn3 expression.

  We identified Foxn3 mRNA as a target of miR-216b (Zhang et al., 2022). Overexpression of miR-216a/b or inhibition of Foxn3 expression in neonatal retinas led to additional amacrine interneurons and loss of bipolar interneurons among expressing cells. Here we find that disruption of Foxn3 alters amacrine cell localization and displaced amacrine cell number in postnatal retina, although the effects differ from miR-216a/b overexpression. Most labeled amacrine cells observed after miR-216a/b overexpression were in the INL, but we did observe additional displaced amacrine cells (Zhang et al., 2022). Since Foxn3 is disrupted in all cells throughout development in Foxn3 KO mice, it is possible that compensation occurs in these mice, or that ectopic amacrine cell formation with miR-216a/b overexpression depends on the mosaic reduction of Foxn3 in retinas (due to vector electroporation). miR-216a/b also could regulate additional targets that contribute to the observed phenotypes. We proposed a model in which inhibition of Foxn3 expression by miR-216a/b allowed increased Foxn4 activity to promote amacrine cell formation. However, the results presented here suggest that miR-216a/b and Foxn3 could affect amacrine cell development through changes in primary cilia. Consistent with this, Argonaute PAR-CLIP identified a miR-216a/b site in the 3’ UTR of the Rfx3 mRNA in P0 retinas (see Supplemental Table 3 in Zhang et al. 2022), suggesting that miR-216a/b may target multiple regulators of cilia gene expression.

  While our analysis focused on the role of Foxn3 in the retina, most Foxn3 KO mice die at or shortly after birth, with surviving mice smaller than normal, indicating that Foxn3 has additional important developmental functions. Craniofacial defects were reported in mice with a hypomorphic Foxn3 allele and in frogs after morpholino inhibition of Foxn3 (Samaan et al., 2010; Schmidt et al., 2011; Schuff et al., 2007), but the Foxn3 KO mice analyzed here do not show craniofacial defects. However, Foxn3 function has been implicated in the repression of cell growth, acting as a tumor suppressor, regulation of metabolism in liver, and regulation of the inflammatory response in lungs (Huot et al., 2014; Karanth et al., 2016; Li et al., 2017; Xue et al., 2019; Yu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2023). The extensive regulation of genes involved in cilia by Foxn3 suggests that at least some of these roles may be linked to the proper formation or function of cilia.


## Methods

### Mice and processing of retinas
  All mouse (Mus musculus) experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care & Use Committee at the University of Michigan. WT retinas for CUT&RUN analysis were isolated from embryonic day 16 (E16) embryos of CD-1 mice (Charles River). The C57BL/6NJ-Foxn3em1(IMPC)Bay/Mmnc mouse strain (RRID:MMRRC_071094-UNC), with a heterozygous deletion of Foxn3 exon 3, was obtained from the Mutant Mouse Resource and Research Center (MMRRC) at University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, an NIH-funded strain repository, and was donated to the MMRRC by Jason Heaney, Ph.D., Baylor College of Medicine (Birling et al., 2021). C57BL/6NJ-Foxn3em1(IMPC)Bay/Mmnc mice were bred with WT C57/BL6J mice (The Jackson Laboratory). Heterozygous male and female offspring were interbred to maintain the line and to generate Foxn3 KO embryos or offspring. The genotypes of offspring and embryos were determined by genomic PCR (see Supplemental Table 5 for primers).

  For tissue analysis, eyes or retina explants were fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde (ThermoFisher) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Sigma) for 30 min at room temperature and retinas were dissected from the eyes. Retinas were cryoprotected in PBS containing 30% sucrose (Sigma) for 2 hrs at 4 °C, embedded in OCT (Sakura), and sectioned at 15 µm using a cryostat. Serial sections were collected at 300 µm intervals across the retina. All retinas were processed under identical conditions for antibody staining.


### Retina explant culture
  Retinas were dissected from postnatal day 0 (P0) mouse eyes and placed onto Transwell inserts with polyester membranes (Corning; 0.4 µm pore size) in six-well plates, with the ganglion cell layer facing upward. Explants were cultured in 1.2 ml of high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 20% horse serum, 100 units/ml penicillin, and 100units/ml streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 2.5 µM purmorphamine (Sigma) for days 1–3 (Gurdita et al., 2023). Explants for Fig. S6 were cultured without purmorphamine. Cultures were maintained for 11 days prior to fixation and immunofluorescence analysis.


### Immunofluorescence Analysis
  Antibodies used and dilutions are listed in Supplementary Table 5. For retinal sections, samples were blocked for 1 hr at room temperature in PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 (ThermoFisher), 1% unlabeled affinity-purified Fab fragment donkey anti-mouse IgG (H+L), and 5% donkey serum (Jackson ImmunoResearch). Sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies: mouse anti-AP2α, rabbit anti-Sox9, goat anti-GFAP, sheep anti-Vsx2, mouse anti-Arl13b, mouse anti-Foxj1, and mouse anti-GS. The following day, sections were incubated for 1 hr at room temperature with secondary antibodies (1:1000, Jackson ImmunoResearch): Alexa Fluor 647–conjugated anti-mouse, Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated anti-goat, Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated anti-sheep, and Alexa Fluor 568–conjugated anti-rabbit. Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 (Roche). Immunofluorescence was imaged using an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope or Oxford Andor BC43 microscope, and images were analyzed with FV10-ASW 3.1 Viewer or Imaris Viewer 10.2.0. To assemble complete retina sections shown in Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. S3, overlapping images were stitched together using Oxford Instruments Andor Fusion 2.5.0.218. Representative images were cropped, assembled, and labeled using Adobe Photoshop and/or Adobe Illustrator.


### Cell Counts
  For Fig.1, AP2α, Vsx2, and Sox9 INL cells were counted within a 200-µm region in the middle zone between the center and edge on each side of a stitched retina section. Sox9-positive cells in the IPL and GCL were quantified from complete stitched retina sections. Blinded counts were performed manually using the Photoshop count tool. ONL nuclei were quantified from a single 100-µm region on one side. For Fig.2, Arl13b-labeled cilia were quantified from a single 200-µm region on one side. For Figs. S4 and S6 (retinal explants), AP2α-, Vsx2-, and Sox9-positive INL cells, as well as Arl13b-labeled cilia, were quantified from a single 200-µm region located in the middle zone on one side of the retinal explant section. Counts were performed manually using the Photoshop count tool. Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 10 using unpaired t-tests. Quantification was based on 4–6 sections per retina from three P21 Foxn3 KO mice and three P21 WT mice, or from retinal explants cultured from four WT and four Foxn3 KO retinas.


### Expression vectors and reporters
  Plasmids were constructed using standard techniques and are available upon request. Oligonucleotide PCR primers for plasmid construction are in Supplemental Table 5. The pUS2, pUS6, pUS2-MT, pUS2-Foxn3, and pUS2-Foxn4 plasmids have been previously described (Chung et al., 2006; Zhang et al., 2022). DNA oligonucleotides encoding two copies of the HA tag were inserted into pUS2, pUS2-Foxn3, or pUS2-Foxn4 between the BamHI and EcoRI sites, resulting in pUS2-HA, pUS2-HA-Foxn3, and pUS2-HA-Foxn4. The coding regions for mouse Foxj1, Rfx3, and Foxn3 without exon 3 (from C57BL/6NJ-Foxn3em1(IMPC)Bay/Mmnc mice) were isolated by RT-PCR, using RNA extracted from embryonic mouse retinas, and inserted into pUS2, pUS2-HA, or pUS2-MT. The 7A mutants, in which the LXXLXWL motif was replaced with seven alanines (LXXLXWL → AAAAAAA), and Rfx3 point mutants (C544S, S616D, F620A, and H624A) were generated by PCR-based site-directed mutagenesis.

  Enhancer or promoter regions from Foxn3, Tek1, Ccdc39, Cfap206, Notch1, Meis1, Foxj1, and Ptprf were amplified from mouse genomic DNA via PCR and cloned into pGL4.29 (Promega) using the NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly Cloning Kit (New England Biolabs, NEB). The Foxn3_Enh2_FBM construct, which contains mutations in two forkhead binding sites, and the Foxn3_Enh2_EBM construct, with mutations in two E-boxes, were created by inserting synthesized oligonucleotides into pGL4.29: two DNA oligonucleotides with complementary 3′ ends were annealed and extended with Q5 DNA polymerase (NEB) to generate a double-stranded DNA fragment, which was then cloned into the plasmid. The Foxn3_Enh2_Rfx3M construct, with mutation of the Rfx3 binding site, was generated using PCR site-directed mutagenesis.


### RNA-seq
  Retinas from individual genotyped E19 embryos (Foxn3 KO or WT) were isolated, and total RNA from each retina was purified using TRIzol (ThermoFisher). A total of 500 ng of RNA was used to prepare each RNA-seq library using the NEBNext Ultra™ II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina and NEB Unique Dual Index UMI Adaptors (NEB), following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA-seq libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 at the University of Michigan Advanced Genomics Core.


### CUT&RUN
  E16 retinas from CD-1 mice were isolated, pooled, and dissociated into single cells using Accutase (Gibco). Approximately 3×10⁵ cells were used for each CUT&RUN assay, which were performed using the CUT&RUN Assay Kit (Cell Signaling Technologies, CST 86652S) following the manufacturer’s instructions for live cell preparation with modifications: micrococcal nuclease digestion was performed for 1hr in an ice-water bath (0° C), except for the Foxn4 samples and mouse IgG controls, which were digested with micrococcal nuclease overnight (~12 hrs) at 0° C. Genomic DNA recovered from the CUT&RUN assay was used to prepare a DNA library using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit (NEB) for Illumina, adhering to the manufacturer’s protocol with the following modifications: after the addition of End Prep Buffer and Enzyme Mix, samples were incubated at 20°C for 30 minutes and subsequently at 50°C for 30 minutes. For ligation, 2.5 µl of 1:50 diluted NEB Unique Dual Index UMI Adaptors were added. Adaptor-ligated DNA was purified using 1.1X volume of NEBNext Sample Purification Beads. Following PCR enrichment, the adaptor-ligated DNA was again purified using a 1.1X volume of beads. The PCR product was separated on a 6% TBE gel (Invitrogen), and a gel slice corresponding to 200–500 bp (determined using size markers in an adjacent lane) was excised. The DNA from the gel slice was eluted, precipitated using 2-propanol (ThermoFisher), resuspended in Tris-HCl buffer (Invitrogen), and sequenced using an Illumina NextSeq 2000 at the University of Michigan Advanced Genomics Core.


### Bioinformatic analysis
  Paired-end RNA-seq reads (~55nt each) were mapped to mm39 using star (Dobin et al., 2013). Differential expression of genes in RNA-seq data was determined with DEseq2 (Love et al., 2014). Read coverage and splicing maps for Foxn3 in Fig. S1 were generated with IGV 2.19.1 (Thorvaldsdottir et al., 2013). Exon sequences were obtained from the UCSC Genome Browser (https://genome.ucsc.edu). For CUT&RUN analysis, ~55nt paired-end Illumina sequences had adaptors removed using Cutadapt (Martin, 2011) and were then mapped to the mm10 mouse genome and to the Saccharomyces cerevisiae genome (for yeast spike-in DNA) with bowtie2 (Langmead and Salzberg, 2012). Mapped reads were deduplicated based on UMIs using UMI-Tools (Smith et al., 2017). Mapped paired-end reads were filtered for insert sizes of ≤120nt as recommended for transcription factor peak finding with CUT&RUN (Meers et al., 2019b; Meers et al., 2019c). Bedgraph files of read coverage for the mouse genome were generated using bedtools genomecov (Quinlan, 2014; Quinlan and Hall, 2010) and normalized based on total yeast spike-in read counts for each library. Peaks of read accumulation were identified using SEACR with relaxed peak finding (Meers et al., 2019a). Peaks were identified from two independent CUT&RUN libraries for each transcription factor, relative to two matched IgG control libraries. For Foxn3, peaks identified in two separate CUT&RUN experiments (each with two independent Foxn3 samples and two control samples) were intersected to generate a high confidence set of Foxn3 peaks that was used for subsequent Foxn3 binding region analysis.

  The summit of each SEACR peak was defined as the center base in the region with the maximum read count. Peaks were filtered using exclude lists for CUT&RUN (Nordin et al., 2023) and ENCODE Chip-seq (Amemiya et al., 2019); non-chromosomal and chrM mappings also were discarded. Homer 5.1 (Heinz et al., 2010) was used to map DNA binding motifs in each peak. Peaks were linked to a gene annotation based on the nearest TSS from epdNewPromoter_v6.TSS (Dreos et al., 2012) for each peak summit, determined with bedtools closest. ENCODE cCREs (Abascal et al., 2020) that overlapped at least 10% of a SEACR peak were identified using bedtools intersect. Gene symbols for annotated CUT&RUN peaks were updated to current MGI symbols using https://www.informatics.jax.org/batch and matched with gene symbols in the RNA-seq data. Overlaps between peaks for different transcription factors were determined using bedtools intersect with at least 10% overlap.

  Heat maps were generated using Deep Tools (Ramirez et al., 2016) and centered on peaks. Reads mapping at peaks were displayed with IGV 2.19.1 (Thorvaldsdottir et al., 2013). Volcano plot was generated with VolcaNoseR (Goedhart and Luijsterburg, 2020). GO analysis was performed using NIH DAVID: https://david.ncifcrf.gov/tools.jsp (Huang da et al., 2009; Sherman et al., 2022). Venn diagrams were generated with the Broad Institute Venn diagram generator (http://barc.wi.mit.edu/tools/). Figures were assembled and labeled with Adobe Illustrator.


### Structure Predictions for Protein Interactions
  Structure predictions for complexes of full-length proteins with DNA, or protein domains without DNA, were generated using AlphaFold 3: https://alphafoldserver.com (Abramson et al., 2024). See Supplemental Figure S12 for sequences used. Structure predictions were visualized and interactions between proteins were analyzed with The PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Version 3.1.6.1 Schrödinger, LLC.


### Reporter Assays
  HEK293 cells (Takara Bio; tested negative for mycoplasma) were cultured in 96-well plates in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 units/ml penicillin, and 100 µg/ml streptomycin (all from ThermoFisher). For each well, 20 ng of reporter DNA (Foxn3Enh2_WT, Foxn3Enh2_RfxM, Foxn3Enh2_FBM, Foxn3Enh2_EBM, Foxn3Enh1, Tek1, Ccdc39, Cfap206, Foxj1, Meis1, Notch1 or Ptprf) was co-transfected with 20 ng of either individual or combinations of the following DNA constructs: pUS2, pUS2-Foxn3, pUS2-Foxn3_7A, pUS2-Foxn4, pUS2-Foxn4_7A, pUS2-Foxj, pUS6, pUS2-HA-Foxj1, pUS2-HA-Foxj1-7A, pUS2-HA-Foxn3, pUS2-HA-Foxn3-7A, pUS2-Foxn3KO-Truncation, pUS2-Ascl1, pUS2-Rfx3, pUS2-Rfx3-S616D, pUS2-Rfx3-F620A, pUS2-Rfx3-H624A or pUS2-Rfx3-C544S. Additional pUS2 was added as needed to maintain the same total DNA amount. An additional 20 ng of pRL-SV40 was included as an internal control for transfection efficiency. Transfections were carried out using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) or TransIT-VirusGEN (Mirus) per manufacturer’s instructions. Reporter activity was measured 24 hrs post-transfection using the Dual-Glo® Luciferase Assay System (Promega). Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity to account for transfection efficiency. Luminescence intensity was measured with a FLUOstar OPTIMA plate reader. The relative expression data shown in the figures include individual data points and means for 3–6 independent transfections. Statistical analyses were performed using one-way or two-way ANOVA in GraphPad Prism 10.


### Co-immunoprecipitation and Western Blot Analyses
  HEK293 cells in 10-cm dishes were co-transfected with 2 µg of each plasmid and 2 µg of the combination plasmid DNA using Lipofectamine 2000 or TransIT-VirusGEN (per manufacturer’s instructions). After 24 hrs, cells were lysed in NETN buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0 (Invitrogen), 100 mM NaCl (Lonza), 0.5% NP-40 (Sigma), 0.5 mM EDTA (Invitrogen) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Sigma). Lysates were cleared by centrifugation (13,000 rpm, 5 min, 4 °C). For immunoprecipitation, 20 µL of Pierce™ Anti-HA Magnetic Beads (ThermoFisher) were incubated with lysates for 2 hrs at 4 °C with rotation, washed three times with NETN buffer, and eluted in 1× NuPAGE™ LDS sample buffer (Invitrogen) by boiling for 10 min. Proteins were resolved on 10% NuPAGE™ gels, transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (Invitrogen), and probed with rabbit anti-HA, mouse anti-Myc, or rabbit anti-Rfx3 (see Supplemental Table 5). Secondary antibodies included IRDye® 800CW goat anti-rabbit (1:8000, Li-COR), goat anti-rabbit StarBright™ Blue 700, and goat anti-mouse StarBright™ Blue 520 (1:5000, Bio-Rad). Blots were imaged using a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc™ MP system.

  Protein extracts from P0 retinas were prepared in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors (Sigma). Proteins were resolved on 10% NuPAGE™ gels, transferred to nitrocellulose membranes, and probed with rabbit anti-Foxn3, mouse anti-Foxj1, or mouse anti-β-actin (Supplemental Table 5). Corresponding secondary antibodies were used, respectively, as goat anti-rabbit-HRP (1:2000), goat anti-mouse-HRP, and goat anti-mouse StarBright™ Blue 520 (1:5000; all Bio-Rad) and imaged as above.


### Electrophoretic Mobility Shift Assay (EMSA)
  30-bp oligonucleotides containing consensus Rfx3 and Foxn3 binding sites based on Foxn3 enhancer 2 were synthesized (IDT; see Supplemental Table 5), with or without a 5′-biotin-label, and annealed to create DNA duplex probes for EMSA. For Rfx3 binding, HEK293 cells in 6-cm dishes were transfected with 4 µg pUS2-MT-Rfx3 or point mutants using TransIT-VirusGEN. Protein extracts were prepared 24 hrs later by lysing cells in 30 µL of NETN buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Lysates were cleared by centrifugation (13,000 rpm, 5 min, 4 °C). Rfx3 EMSA binding reactions (10 µL) contained 15 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.9, Gibco), 40 mM KCl (Sigma), 1 mM DTT (Invitrogen), 4% glycerol (Sigma), 50 ng/µL poly(dI-dC)(ThermoFisher), 400 fmol probe, and 2 µL extract, and were incubated at 4 °C for 30 min. For Foxn3 binding, HEK293 cells in 10-cm dish were transfected with 10 µg pUS2-HA-Foxn3 or pUS2-HA-Foxn3–7A. Extracts were prepared in NETN buffer, clarified, and HA-tagged Foxn3 proteins were purified using Pierce™ Anti-HA Magnetic Beads. The Foxn3 proteins were eluted with 20 µl of 2 mg/mL HA peptide (ThermoFisher) at 37 °C for 10 min. Foxn3 EMSA binding reactions (10 µL) containing 15 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.9), 40 mM KCl, 1 mM DTT, 5 mM MgCl2 (Ambion), 400 fmol probe, and 3 µL purified Foxn3, were incubated at room temperature for 30 min. For competition assays, 40 pmol of unlabeled competitor oligonucleotide duplex was added before the labeled probe. Complexes were resolved on a 6% native polyacrylamide gel (ThermoFisher) in 0.5× TBE (Invitrogen) at 100 V for 70 min at 4 °C, transferred to a nylon membrane (Amersham) overnight at 50 V and 4 °C, UV-crosslinked, and blocked for 15 min with 1× blocking buffer (250 mM NaCl, 17 mM Na2HPO4, 7.3 mM NaH2PO4, 1% SDS (Sigma)). Membranes were incubated with streptavidin–HRP (Vector Laboratories) in blocking buffer for 10 min, washed three times with 0.1× blocking buffer (5 min each), and visualized by chemiluminescence using Clarity Max™ Western ECL substrates (Bio-Rad).
