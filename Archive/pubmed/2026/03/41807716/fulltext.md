# HIF3A-mediated aberrant activation of TXNIP promotes Alzheimer’s disease progression


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43404-z.


## Introduction
  Dementia has emerged as a global health challenge1. AD, the primary form of dementia, exhibits parallel epidemiological trends and stands as an urgent, escalating global concern2,3. Its etiology is intricate and multifactorial, arising from the interplay of aging, genetic, and environmental factors, yet the precise mechanisms underlying its pathogenesis remain incompletely elucidated4.

  Current academic hypotheses regarding AD primarily include the amyloid cascade hypothesis, inflammation hypothesis, metal ion hypothesis, tau protein hypothesis, and OS hypothesis. Collectively, these hypotheses underscore that OS plays a pivotal role in AD pathogenesis5,6.

  The brain is particularly vulnerable to OS due to its high oxygen consumption paired with low antioxidant levels7. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are primarily generated via the mitochondrial electron transport chain, redox reactions of metal ions, and enzymatic processes. Under normal physiological conditions, ROS contribute importantly to signal transduction pathways and transcriptional activation. However, when ROS accumulate beyond the capacity of antioxidant defense systems, they target cellular biomacromolecules—including phospholipid membranes, proteins, and nucleic acids—disrupting normal cellular function8. This cascade further stimulates mitochondria to release pro-apoptotic proteins, ultimately inducing apoptosis of central nervous system (CNS) neurons. Notably, OS can damage cells and trigger mitochondrial dysfunction9, while mitochondria themselves act as a key source of ROS, creating a deleterious feedback loop10.

  Although the central role of OS in the pathological process of AD has been widely recognized, traditional research methods mostly focus on population-level cellular analysis11, making it difficult to accurately capture the heterogeneous responses of different cell types in the brain (such as neurons, astrocytes, microglia, etc.) under the action of OS. This has, to a certain extent, restricted the in-depth elucidation of the pathogenesis of AD. However, the emergence of single-cell sequencing technology has provided a powerful tool to break through this limitation12,13. This technology can perform high-throughput sequencing analysis of the transcriptome at the single-cell level14, thereby accurately identifying specific cell subsets in brain tissue that are sensitive to OS and parsing their unique gene expression patterns and molecular regulatory networks15. Therefore, this study integrates scRNA-seq data with bulk sequencing data to conduct an in-depth investigation into the hub genes with enhanced OS activity after the occurrence of AD. We employed ML algorithms to screen and optimize these genes, and finally identified the genes associated with enhanced OS activity. Experimental validation confirmed the reliability of our analysis results. This study seeks to deepen the understanding of OS mechanisms in AD and to provide a foundation for developing precise therapeutic strategies.


## Materials and methods

### Data collection
  We obtained the GSE138852 single-cell transcriptome dataset from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo), which includes entorhinal cortex samples from both healthy controls and AD patients across 12 individuals. In addition, we downloaded the conventional RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) dataset GSE5281 from GEO for analysis, which contains 87 AD samples and 74 brain samples from normally aging individuals. Meanwhile, we downloaded the GSE132903 dataset, which comprises 97 samples from AD patients and 98 samples from non-demented controls (ND) of the middle temporal gyrus, for validation analysis.

  We acquired OS-related genes from this literature and ultimately screened out a total of 1399 OS-related genes. We performed Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA, Version 2.0.7, https://git.bioconductor.org/packages/GSVA) on the AD samples and control samples in the GSE138852 dataset to assess the changes of OS.


### scRNA-seq dataset analysis
  In this scRNA-seq dataset, we used the Seurat16 (Version 4.4.0, https://satijalab.org/seurat) R package to process the raw data. we conducted principal component analysis (PCA) on highly expressed genes and retained the first 20 principal components. Subsequently, we conducted clustering analysis using the “FindCluster” function, setting the resolution parameter to 0.1, which resulted in 6 cell clusters, and visualized the results via UMAP. Meanwhile, we obtained the highly expressed genes of each cluster using the “FindAllMarkers” function. Cell types were identified based on canonical marker genes.


### Evaluation of OS score
  In this study, the AUCell17 (Version 1.28.0, https://git.bioconductor.org/packages/AUCell), Ucell18 (Version 2.10.1, https://git.bioconductor.org/packages/UCell), ssgsea, and AddModuleScore algorithms were employed to evaluate the OS activity of each cell at the single-cell level, and comparisons of OS activity were made between AD samples and normal controls (NC). Based on the median of the OS scores, cells were divided into high OS activity groups and low OS activity groups for differential gene analysis (DEG). Additionally, genes closely associated with OS were obtained through correlation analysis. The intersection of genes identified by correlation analysis and DEG was determined.


### GO enrichment analysis
  We carried out GO analysis to explore the functional roles and pathways linked to the intersecting genes. In addition, the GeneMANIA (https://genemania.org/) website was used to assess the functions of genes and the interactions between them. Meanwhile, we performed GSEA enrichment analysis on the HIF3A gene using the Reactome and Hallmark gene sets.


### ML algorithms identify for the optimal feature genes
  Four ML algorithms, including eXtreme Gradient Boosting (xGBoost, Version 3.1.2.1, https://github.com/dmlc/xgboost), Support Vector Machine-Recursive Feature Elimination (SVM-RFE) (sigFeature R package, Version 1.24.0, https://git.bioconductor.org/packages/sigFeature), Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) (glmnet R package, Version 4.1–10, https://glmnet.stanford.edu) and Random Forest (RF) (randomForest R package, Version 4.7–1.2, https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~breiman/RandomForests/) were employed to identify the candidate genes. The input gene set consisted of 54 genes from overlap between positive-regulated OS genes and highly correlated OS genes from the GSE5281 bulk transcriptome dataset, and each sample was labeled as AD or control.

  For LASSO regression, the optimal penalty parameter (λ) was determined through tenfold cross-validation, yielding eight genes with non-zero coefficients. The RF model ranked variables based on mean decrease in accuracy and identified 12 genes with an importance score > 0. SVM-RFE iteratively eliminated features using a linear kernel and selected 28 genes with the lowest classification error. XgBoost quantified feature contributions using gain scores and selected the top 28 genes. To ensure robustness, we intersected the feature genes identified by all four algorithms, resulting in seven optimal OS-related biomarkers.

  Subsequently, the candidate genes were validated using the transcriptome dataset GSE132903.


### Validation of HIF3A genes at the single cell level
  We performed validation at the single-cell level using UMAP plots and density plots, and detected the high expression of HIF3A in astrocytes in the single-cell transcriptome data. In the analysis of cell trajectories using the Monocle (Version 2.34.0, https://git.bioconductor.org/packages/monocle) package, high expression of the HIF3A gene was also found in astrocytes, as well as high expression in the late stage of development.


### GTRD and KnockTF database
  HIF3A targets were predicted using the Gene Transcription Regulation Database (GTRD, http://gtrd.biouml.org) and the KnockTF Database (http://www.licpathway.net/KnockTF/index.php). GTRD integrates genome-wide transcription factor binding sites identified by ChIP-seq experiments, providing comprehensive regulatory information for human and mouse genes. KnockTF contains target genes of transcription factors derived from knockdown or knockout experiments, allowing identification of genes regulated by specific TFs under perturbation conditions.


### Animals
  All mice were bred in-house and maintained on a C57BL/6 background. We used male mice throughout the entire study between 8 weeks of age. Selecting male-only mice is to avoid the possible confounding effects of hormonal fluctuations during the estrous cycle of female mice on the OS pathway and gene expression. This approach simplifies the model and enhances the consistency of experimental results during the initial mechanism exploration stage. Mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups. The mice were raised in an SPF animal room to ensure that the room temperature (24 °C ± 2) and humidity were suitable. Streptozotocin (STZ; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was dissolved in 0.1 M citrate buffer (pH 4.5) to a final concentration of 10 mg/mL. AD-like pathology was induced via daily intraperitoneal injection of STZ (50 mg/kg) for 5 consecutive days. Post-injection, body weight and fasting blood glucose were monitored weekly to validate model induction. Eight weeks after the final STZ injection, mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane and euthanized by cervical dislocation. Brains were immediately dissected, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C for molecular assays. The protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Yellow River Sanmenxia Hospital (approval no. KY231220).


### Cell culture
  Human astrocytes SVGp12 was purchased from Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences. These cells were cultured in MEM medium containing 10% FBS. All cell lines were incubated and maintained in a humidified incubator with an atmosphere of 5% CO2 at a temperature of 37 °C.

  An in vitro AD model was established by inducing SVGp12 cells with Aβ25-35 (Aβ25–35; ≥ 97% purity; CAS No. 131602-53-4; Yuanye Bio-Technology, China; Cat. No. S41991). Prior to use, Aβ25-35 was dissolved in sterile physiological saline and incubated at 37 °C for 72 h for activation. Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were collected and seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well in 100 μL of medium, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 24 h. After removal of the old medium, the cells were treated with 100 μL of 20 μM Aβ25-35 solution and incubated for another 24 h before sample collection and subsequent analysis.


### Western blot
  Cells were lysed in RIPA, and protein concentration was detected by a BCA Kit (Beyotime). Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and further transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, MA, USA). Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with HIF3A (Proteintech, 1:1000, 27,650–1-AP), β-actin (Proteintech, 1:5000, 66,009–1-Ig) and TXNIP antibody (Proteintech, 1:1000, 18,243–1-AP). Membranes were incubated with secondary antibody (Proteintech, 1:10,000, SA00001-1). The proteins were detected using a GEL imaging system (Bio-Rad, CA, USA).


### RT-qPCR
  Total RNA isolation from cells was carried out with the TRIzol RNA reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). Complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis was performed using the HiScript II Q Select RT SuperMix (Vazyme, China). For real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) detection, SYBR Green fluorescent dye (Takara Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) was utilized. Quantitative real-time PCR reactions were conducted on an ABI StepOnePlus real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems). GAPDH was used as an endogenous reference gene for normalization of target gene expression levels.


### ChIP-qPCR
  ChIP-qPCR assays were performed following standard protocols with minor adjustments. Briefly, exponentially growing cells were fixed with 1% formaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich) for 10 min at room temperature to crosslink protein-DNA complexes, quenched with 0.125 M glycine (5 min). Cells were lysed in SDS lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 10 mM EDTA, 1% SDS, protease inhibitors) on ice for 10 min, then sonicated (Bioruptor, 30 s on/30 s off, 15 cycles) to generate 200–500 bp DNA fragments. Sheared chromatin was diluted 1:10 in ChIP dilution buffer, pre-cleared with Protein A/G magnetic beads (1 h, 4 °C), and incubated overnight at 4 °C with 5 μg anti-HIF3A antibody or IgG control. Immune complexes were captured with magnetic beads (2 h, 4 °C), washed sequentially with low/high salt buffers, LiCl buffer, and TE. DNA was eluted (1% SDS, 0.1 M NaHCO₃, 65 °C, 30 min), crosslinks reversed with 5 M NaCl/proteinase K (overnight, 65 °C), and purified using QIAquick kit. Enrichment of TXNIP promoter was quantified by qPCR with gene-specific primers (SYBR Green method).


### Luciferase reporter assay
  Exponentially growing cells (5 × 104 cells/well) were seeded into 24-well plates and transfected with 200 ng of firefly luciferase reporter plasmid (containing target promoter/enhancer elements) and 20 ng of Renilla luciferase internal control plasmid using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. After 6 h of transfection, cells were treated with fresh culture medium for 24 h. Cells were then lysed with 100 μL passive lysis buffer (Promega) on ice for 15 min, and lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. Firefly luciferase activity was measured using a GloMax 20/20 luminometer (Promega) with 50 μL of lysate mixed with 50 μL luciferase assay reagent (Promega). Renilla activity was normalized to firefly activity to correct for transfection efficiency.


### Cell transfection
  The stable cell line exhibiting optimal growth was selected, trypsinized, and plated at a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL. After 24 h of culture in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator, when the cells reached the logarithmic growth phase at approximately 50% confluence, they were subjected to transfection. For the transient transfection, siRNA (obtained from GenePharma) was prepared by first mixing 250 μL of serum-free medium with 8 μL of Lipofectamine 2000 (Lip2000, Invitrogen) and incubating the mixture for 5 min. Simultaneously, 4 μg of plasmid (siRNA construct) was combined with 250 μL of serum-free medium and also incubated for 5 min. The culture medium in the wells was then aspirated, and the cells were gently rinsed three times with serum-free medium. The two mixtures were combined, added to the cells, and each well was supplemented with 1.5 mL of fresh serum-free medium before being returned to the incubator for 6–8 h. Following this incubation, the transfection mixture was removed and replaced with 2 mL of fresh medium containing 10% serum. The cells were cultured for an additional 24 h, after which the supernatant was carefully aspirated and the cells were harvested for subsequent Western Blot or PCR analysis.The siRNA sequence targeting human HIF3A (siHIF3A) was GATTGCAGAAGTGGCTGGCTA.


### Oxidative stress test
  Determination of GSH/GSSG and MitoSOX activity in Astrocytes: RIPA lysate was added to SVGp12 cells with different treatment, and the sample was lysed on ice. After centrifugation at 12000 g at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected. The protein concentration was determined by the BCA method. The GSH/GSSG and MitoSOX content in Astrocytes were determined according to the kit instructions.


### Seahorse detection assay
  Seahorse XF analyzer assays for OCR and ATP production rate were performed as follows: Cells (5 × 104 cells/well) were seeded into XF24 microplates and incubated overnight at 37 °C, 5% CO2 for attachment. Culture medium was replaced with 500 μL pre-warmed XF base medium (pH 7.4, 2 mM glutamine, 10 mM glucose, 1 mM pyruvate) 1 h before assay. Oligomycin (1 μM), FCCP (1 μM), and antimycin A &Rotenone (0.5 μM) were sequentially injected into reagent ports. Real-time OCR was monitored under basal conditions followed by drug injection, with each cycle including 3 min mixing and 3 min measurement (3–5 replicates). OCR (pmol O2/min/μg protein) and ATP production rate (pmol ATP/min/μg protein) were calculated using Wave software based on OCR changes post-inhibitors, with cell number normalized by CCK-8 assay.


### Statistical analysis
  All data processing and statistical analyses were conducted with SPSS 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Quantitative variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For data meeting normality and homogeneity of variance assumptions, paired t-tests were used to compare AD-affected brain tissues with NC; independent samples t-tests were employed for comparisons between other experimental groups. Inter-group differences across multiple experimental groups were assessed using one-way ANOVA, with Tukey’s post-hoc test for pairwise comparisons. For time-dependent comparisons involving multiple groups, two-way ANOVA was applied; to evaluate AD-related pathological changes over time, repeated measures ANOVA was used, followed by Bonferroni correction for post-hoc tests. Disease progression analysis was performed via the Kaplan–Meier method, with group differences in progression assessed using the Log-Rank test. Statistical significance was defined as a p value less than 0.05.


## Results

### Exploring OS-associated characteristics in AD through scRNA-seq analysis
  Using an OS gene set19,20, we calculated OS scores for AD cells and found that the scores in the AD group were significantly higher than those in the control group (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, 37,818 high-quality cells were selected from the scRNA-seq dataset for further analysis. UMAP analysis revealed that these cells were clearly grouped into six distinct clusters (Fig. 1B). Manual annotation based on canonical marker genes identified six distinct cell types: Oligodendrocytes, astrocytes, OPC, neurons, microglia, and a population of unidentified cells. Notably, the proportion of astrocytes was significantly reduced (Fig. 1D–F). Functional enrichment analysis indicated that astrocytes were primarily associated with the regulation of protein localization to membranes and the regulation of protein kinase A signaling pathways (Fig. 1G).

  To assess OS activity at the single-cell level in AD, we applied the AddModuleScore, AUCell, Ucell, and ssGSEA algorithms. The outcomes revealed notable heterogeneity in OS activity across cells. Astrocytes had the highest OS activity, while neurons and microglia cells exhibited comparatively lower activity (Fig. 2A). UMAP analysis revealed elevated OS activity on Astrocytes (Fig. 2B). Therefore, we selected astrocytes for the subsequent analyses. Based on the median of overall OS activity, cells were stratified into high-OS and low-OS groups (Fig. 2C). Differential expression analysis between the high-OS and low-OS groups identified 136 upregulated genes potentially contributing to elevated OS activity. Correlation analysis further identified 60 genes whose expression levels were significantly associated with OS scores. The overlap between these two gene sets is presented in Fig. 2D. A full list of both groups of genes has been provided in the Supplementary Table 1. Finally, a total of 54 genes were retained for subsequent analysis. We plotted a volcano plot to illustrate the expression profiles of all 136 OS-correlated genes from the GSE5281 dataset (Fig. 2E).

### Findings from GO and PPI analyses
  We performed GO analysis to explore the functional roles of the 54 positively regulated OS genes. Up-regulated genes were significantly enriched in forebrain development and acidic amino acid transport in the Biological Process (BP) category, and predominantly associated with the sarcolemma in the Cellular Component (CC) category. Importantly, the cellular response to hypoxia pathway was highlighted, comprising key genes including HIF3A, FOS, and RORA. These genes are known regulators of hypoxia signaling and may provide insights into mechanisms relevant for subsequent functional analyses. Down-regulated genes were enriched in synapse assembly and regulation of synapse organization in BP, and mainly associated with the synaptic membrane in CC (Fig. 3A). The PPI network of the 54 retained genes was constructed to explore their potential functional associations. As shown in Fig. S1,S2, these genes formed a highly interconnected network, with multiple functional modules highlighted by distinct biological processes. Specifically, the up-regulated genes were enriched in processes such as cardiac conduction, contractile actin filament bundle, and multicellular organismal signaling, whereas the down-regulated genes were primarily associated with synapse organization and the synaptic membrane. Additionally, we examined the differential expression of these 54 genes between the Control and AD groups using bulk transcriptome data from the GSE5281 dataset (Fig. 3B).

### Results of ML algorithms and validation at bulk level
  We employed four ML algorithms to identify the optimal feature genes. Eight key genes were identified as diagnostic markers for OS using the LASSO algorithm (Fig. 4A, Fig. S3A). We identified 12 key genes with an importance score > 0 after utilizing the RF algorithm (Fig. 4B, Fig. S3B). We identified 28 key genes by SVM-RFE algorithm (Fig. 4C). By applying the XGBoost algorithm, 28 key genes were selected from the initial set of 54 genes (Fig. S3C). Subsequently, integrating the results from all four ML approaches allowed us to pinpoint seven optimal feature genes (Fig. 4D). The reliability of these seven genes was then validated using the GSE132903 dataset. The results showed that HIF3A, GLIS3, MT2A, PARD3, and SORBS1 were significantly up-regulated in AD, whereas GAPDH and MAPK10 showed markedly decreased expression in AD (Fig. S3D). Biologically, these genes have been implicated in OS responses, neuronal survival, or AD-related pathological processes. HIF3A, a hypoxia-inducible factor, plays a central role in cellular adaptation to OS21. GLIS3, a Krüppel-like transcription factor, is known to regulate neuronal function22. MT2A, a member of the metallothionein family, shows consistently elevated expression in AD samples23. PARD3 regulates microtubule stability and organization, which is critical for neuronal polarization24. Previous studies have reported that SORBS1 is upregulated in the hippocampus of AD patients compared to unaffected controls, supporting its potential role in cognitive impairment25. GAPDH, a known target of PAD-mediated (PAD, peptidylarginine deiminase) citrullination, has been implicated in neurodegenerative diseases26. In contrast, MAPK10 has been reported to be upregulated in astrocytes of AD patients, potentially contributing to AD-associated neurotoxicity27.

  In addition, in the GSEA enrichment analysis, HIF3A had a strong correlation with the previously identified OS-related pathways, which further supports its potential role as a key regulator in the progression of Alzheimer’s disease by mediating OS responses. Therefore, we conducted an in-depth analysis of HIF3A gene.


### Validation of HIF3A gene at the single-cell level
  To further clarify the specific cell type in which the HIF3A gene exerts its function, we performed validation at the single-cell level. Results from UMAP analysis showed that HIF3A was predominantly expressed in astrocytes, with relatively low expression in neurons and microglia (Fig. 5A). Additionally, both violin plot and bubble plot results indicated elevated expression of HIF3A in astrocytes (Fig. S3E,F). Astrocyte cells were divided into Astrocyte_High and Astrocyte_Low groups based on the expression level of the HIF3A gene. To investigate the developmental dynamics of major brain cell populations in AD, we performed pseudotime inference using the Monocle algorithm. Monocle reconstructs continuous cellular trajectories by reducing high-dimensional single-cell transcriptomic data into a low-dimensional manifold and ordering cells based on transcriptomic similarity. This unsupervised approach approximates temporal progression and identifies branching points that reflect potential state transitions. The reconstructed trajectory originated from the Oligodendrocyte population and diverged toward two terminal branches corresponding to Astrocytes and OPCs (Fig. 5B-C). Along these trajectories, we examined the expression dynamics of HIF3A, which consistently exhibited persistently high expression within the astrocyte lineage across the entire pseudotime continuum, mirroring our previous observation that HIF3A is predominantly enriched in astrocytes (Fig. 5D).

### Identification of the HIF3A-TXNIP Axis
  To investigate the regulatory mechanism of the transcription factor HIF3A in relation to OS in AD, this study first retrieved potential target genes of HIF3A from the KnockTF and GTRD databases, based on the well-documented property of HIF3A as a transcription factor. Subsequent intersection analysis between these target genes and OS-related gene sets identified 8 candidate downstream genes, namely ALDH3A2, ATXN1, CDK1, CPOX, ITGAL, NRAS, SLC40A1, and TXNIP.

  To verify the expression characteristics of these downstream genes in AD and their regulatory association with HIF3A, further analyses were performed using two AD-related datasets (GSE5281 and GSE132903). The results showed that among the 8 candidate genes, TXNIP exhibited significant and consistent features: first, TXNIP was persistently overexpressed in AD patient samples, with consistent results across both datasets (Fig. 6A,B); second, the expression level of TXNIP was significantly positively correlated with that of HIF3A (correlation coefficient r = 0.54, p = 1.95E−16) (Fig. 6C,D). The remaining 7 genes did not show consistent and significant results in either differential expression or correlation analyses.

  Based on the above findings, we focused on the HIF3A-TXNIP axis as the core direction for subsequent investigations into the mechanism by which HIF3A-mediated OS acts in AD.


### HIF3A mediated aberrant TXNIP activation in AD progression
  In order to further analyze the regulatory relationship between HIF3A and TXNIP in AD, the expression level of HIF3A in the brain tissues of mice in the NC group and the AD model group was detected by Western blot and qRT-PCR. The success of the AD modeling was first evaluated through behavioral tests. In the place navigation trial, after five consecutive days of training, the escape latency of WT mice on day 6 was significantly shorter than that of AD mice (Fig. S4A,B). In the spatial probe trial, compared with the WT group, AD mice showed significant reductions in the time spent, distance traveled, and number of platform crossings in the target quadrant (Fig. S4C–F), while no significant difference was observed in swimming speed between the two groups (Fig. S4G). The Western blot and qRT-PCR results showed that the protein (Fig. 7A) and mRNA (Fig. 7B) expressions of HIF3A in the AD model group were significantly higher than those in the NC group (p < 0.001), suggesting that HIF3A was abnormally expressed in AD. As a nuclear factor with transcriptional regulation function, HIF3A may participate in the pathological process of AD by targeting and regulating the transcription of downstream genes. To verify its direct regulatory effect on TXNIP, we employed chromatin co-immunoprecipitation quantitative PCR (ChIP-qPCR) experiments, and the results showed that HIF3A could specifically bind to the TXNIP gene promoter region (Fig. 7C). The effect of HIF3A on the activity of TXNIP promoter was further examined by dual-luciferase reporter assay. The results showed that when the HIF3A binding site in the TXNIP promoter region was mutated, the promoter activity was significantly reduced (Fig. 7D), it was confirmed that HIF3A regulated its transcriptional activity by binding to TXNIP promoter. In addition, Western blot results showed that the expression level of TXNIP protein was significantly up-regulated in HIF3A-overexpressing cells (Fig. 7E), which further verified the transcriptional activation function of HIF3A on TXNIP. Furthermore, compared with the normal group, the protein expression level of TXNIP in the brain tissue of AD mice was also significantly increased (Fig. 7F).

  Previous studies have shown that TXNIP can inhibit the antioxidant function of thioredoxin (Trx) by direct binding, and then promote the generation and accumulation of ROS, inducing endoplasmic reticulum stress and mitochondrial stress, ultimately, it leads to inflammation or apoptosis28,29. To assess the OS status of astrocytes in the AD model, we constructed an in vitro AD model in human astrocytes SVGp12. It was observed that TXNIP levels were significantly elevated in the model group (Fig. 7G). Further detection of glutathione (GSH) and oxidized glutathione (GSSG) in SVGp12 showed that the ratio of GSH2/GSSG in the AD model group was significantly lower than that in the NC group, suggesting that the level of OS in the astrocyte was increased, and the ratio was significantly increased after HIF3A knockdown (Fig. 7H–J), indicating that HIF3A mediated the enhancement of astrocyte OS in the AD model. Mitochondrial OS level detected by mitochondrial ROS-specific probes showed that knockdown of HIF3A could significantly reduce the elevated mitochondrial ROS level in the AD model group (Fig. 7K). In addition, the mitochondrial function of SVGp12 cells was detected by cell energy metabolism analysis system. The results showed that the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and ATP production were significantly reduced in AD model group, whereas oxygen consumption rate and ATP production were significantly restored after HIF3A knockdown (Fig. 7L–N). The above results suggest that the abnormally high expression of HIF3A in astrocytes exacerbates mitochondrial stress by TXNIP activation, which in turn promotes AD progression.


## Discussion
  AD is a devastating neurodegenerative disorder among the elderly30, with age being the greatest risk factor31. Accumulating evidence implicates mitochondrial dysfunction and OS as central contributors to AD pathogenesis. Mitochondrial impairment increases ROS production32, disrupts calcium homeostasis, damages both mitochondrial and nuclear DNA, and promotes β-amyloid (Aβ) accumulation—hallmarks of AD pathology33–35. The mitochondrial cascade hypothesis highlights how age-related mitochondrial decline creates a pro-oxidative environment conducive to neurodegeneration36.

  In this study, we confirmed and extended these observations by integrating single-cell and bulk transcriptomic data. Our scRNA-seq analysis revealed that OS scores were significantly elevated in AD cells compared to controls, reinforcing the central role of OS in AD. Importantly, we uncovered striking cell-type heterogeneity, with astrocytes exhibiting the highest OS activity. This finding highlights astrocytes as key mediators of redox imbalance in AD and sheds new light on the cell-type specificity of OS-induced damage.

  Among OS-related genes, HIF3A emerged as a robustly upregulated feature gene in AD. Traditionally regarded as a hypoxia-responsive transcription factor37, the role of HIF3A in neurodegeneration and OS regulation has been largely unexplored. Our single-cell trajectory analysis showed that HIF3A is predominantly expressed in astrocytes and maintains high expression during disease progression, suggesting a sustained contribution to astrocytic dysfunction. While HIF1α has been implicated in neuroinflammation and Aβ accumulation38, our data reveal a previously unrecognized and significant role for HIF3A in modulating astrocytic redox homeostasis.

  Further analysis identified TXNIP as a potential downstream target of HIF3A. TXNIP, a well-known inhibitor of Trx39, is a critical regulator of intracellular ROS detoxification40. Consistent with this biological function, our integrative transcriptomic analyses revealed that TXNIP was markedly upregulated in AD samples and showed a strong positive correlation with HIF3A expression, suggesting the presence of a transcriptionally regulated HIF3A–TXNIP axis. Previous studies have shown that TXNIP overexpression is associated with increased ROS production, synaptic dysfunction, and neuroinflammation in AD41. Our results support a novel mechanistic model in which HIF3A transcriptionally upregulates TXNIP in astrocytes, leading to impaired ROS clearance, exacerbated oxidative damage, and mitochondrial dysfunction. This HIF3A–TXNIP pathway bridges transcriptional dysregulation and astrocyte-mediated OS, offering a mechanistic explanation for redox imbalance in AD. This newly defined pathway expands the current conceptual framework of AD pathogenesis and may offer novel therapeutic opportunities aimed at restoring redox homeostasis.

  The strengths of this study include the integration of single-cell and bulk transcriptomic data, allowing us to dissect OS heterogeneity at cellular resolution and validate findings in larger cohorts. The application of complementary bioinformatics approaches (AddModuleScore, AUCell, LASSO, RF) further enhances the robustness of our results. By identifying the HIF3A–TXNIP pathway, we fill an important gap in understanding how astrocyte-specific transcriptional programs drive OS and neurodegeneration in AD.

  Therapeutically, this axis represents a promising target. Inhibiting HIF3A-mediated TXNIP upregulation may attenuate OS and protect neurons. Notably, TXNIP inhibitors such as resveratrol analogs42 have demonstrated neuroprotective effects in preclinical models and warrant further evaluation in the context of AD. Future studies should also explore how this pathway interacts with other AD-related processes, including Aβ aggregation, tau pathology, and neuroinflammation, to develop multi-target intervention strategies.

  This study is subject to certain limitations, primarily due to the fact that the function of the HIF3A-TXNIP axis has been validated only in a male mouse model. It is important to note that AD exhibits significant sexual dimorphism in terms of prevalence, pathological progression, and associated molecular pathways. Therefore, the conclusions drawn from the present study may not be directly generalizable to female individuals. Given this discrepancy, whether HIF3A exerts a similar regulatory role in female models represents an important direction for future research.

  In summary, our findings highlight the astrocytic HIF3A–TXNIP pathway as a key mediator of OS and disease progression in AD, providing novel insights into disease mechanisms and potential therapeutic avenues.


## Conclusion
  In conclusion, our study highlights the cell-type-specific nature of OS in AD, identifies astrocytic HIF3A as a key regulator of OS, and proposes the HIF3A-TXNIP pathway as a critical mechanism linking transcriptional regulation to redox imbalance in AD. These findings advance our understanding of OS-mediated pathogenesis in AD and provide a foundation for developing novel therapeutic approaches targeting redox homeostasis.
