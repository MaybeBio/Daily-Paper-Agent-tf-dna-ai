# Mechanisms of enhanced or impaired DNA target selectivity driven by protein dimerization


## Abstract
  Successful DNA transcription demands coordination between proteins that bind DNA while simultaneously binding to one another to form dimers or higher-order complexes. For proteins with numerous DNA targets throughout the genome, measurements that report on their dwell time or occupancy thus represent a convolution over a population interacting with specific DNA, nonspecific DNA, or protein partners on DNA. Dimerization is known to add contacts that can help a protein monomer to stably bind DNA. However, we show here that dimerization can also impair measured dwell times and occupancy on target sequences because the population redistributes across DNA. We combine spatial stochastic simulations of pairwise reversible reactions between proteins and DNA with mass-action kinetic models and theory to isolate the role of reversible dimerization on observed DNA dwell times, occupancy, and spatial distribution of proteins on DNA. Three key themes emerge: (i) Protein–protein interactions, in addition to protein–DNA interactions, can localize a protein to DNA, and rates of (un)binding can thus widely tune dwell times. (ii) Dimensional reduction achieved through nonspecific binding and subsequent 1D diffusion controls the order of magnitude of enhancements despite nucleosome barriers. (iii) Dimerization enhances selectivity for locally clustered targets and often impairs binding to widely spaced targets by sequestration. Compared with ChIP-seq data, our model explains how the distribution of the essential GAGA factor monomer throughout the genome is highly selective for clustered targets due to protein interactions. This model framework predicts when even weak dimerization can redistribute and stabilize proteins on DNA as a necessary part of transcription.


## Introduction
  The transcription of DNA into RNA is essential for all living systems and relies on the concerted action of a variety of DNA-binding proteins (1–4). While some of these proteins are highly specialized and recognize approximately one target in the genome, here we focus on “generalist” proteins, motivated by the essential GAGA factor (GAF) (5), that bind specific DNA sequences or motifs that are widely distributed in the genome. These proteins may therefore have multiple “choices” about where or when to bind, and key metrics of proper function include dwell times (1, 6) and occupancies on DNA (7, 8). These multidomain proteins can arrive at DNA sites as monomers and thus do not require dimerization to localize stably to DNA (5, 9–11). However, dynamic recruitment of monomers can be facilitated not only via specific or nonspecific DNA interactions (12–15) but also via protein–protein interactions (12–14). Experimental measurements that report on the lifetime and occupancy of populations of such multidomain proteins on DNA thus reflect the combined effects of both DNA and protein interactions (16–18). Theory and modeling for proteins searching DNA focus on the predicted behavior of monomers (6, 19–23), and thus two-state models for interpreting experiments of a protein on DNA typically assume only specific and nonspecific binding modes, independent of protein interactions (10). Models that incorporate dimerization during the target search are thus far restricted to only 1D dynamics (24). However, without incorporating protein–protein interactions in combination with reversible localization from 3D to 1D sequences, these approaches cannot predict responses to mutations in protein interacting domains (10), responses to expression level changes that must impact dimerization, or coupling between clustered targets and oligomerizing proteins. A key insight from our modeling approach is that protein localization and immobilization on DNA can arise primarily from protein–protein interactions, without requiring direct DNA binding. Our focus here is to systematically isolate and understand how dimerization will quantitatively enhance or impair DNA target occupancy, dwell time, and selectivity toward clustered targets. We define a minimal model of reversible protein dimerization, incorporating DNA binding and continuous diffusion in 3D and 1D. Using particle-based reaction-diffusion (RD) simulations, mass-action kinetics, and theory, we show how diverse outcomes emerge dependent on the ratio of proteins to targets, dimensional gains of 1D interactions, and relative domain–domain binding rates.

  Our model explicitly captures a key feature of many DNA-binding proteins: their nonspecific association with the charged DNA backbone (25), which facilitates 1D diffusion (20, 26, 27). This sliding or hopping along DNA accelerates a protein search for a target sequence, a mechanism exploited by prokaryotic (26–29) and eukaryotic proteins (30), despite the latter's frequent interruption along the 1D DNA path by nucleosomes (31). Nonspecific association causes a longer lifetime and stronger affinity to DNA when compared with binding isolated target sequences (31, 32). When two proteins localize to DNA, their dimerization becomes a 1D search as long as at least one partner can slide. Like on 2D membranes, this dimensional reduction will typically increase the collision probability compared with 3D (33), promoting significantly more stable and long-lived dimer and membrane- or DNA-bound populations at equilibrium (34, 35). Building from the seminal work of Berg et al. (6, 20) and others studying monomer searches for targets (15, 19, 22, 23, 36), our model allows nonspecifically adhered and thus 1D diffusing proteins to encounter and bind DNA targets using 1D rates. Our proteins can also encounter one another while diffusing on DNA and dimerize using 1D rates, with 1D stretches restricted to between nucleosomes to mimic eukaryotic DNA, where L spans ∼15–100 nm (37–40). To support reversible binding and retain detailed balance, we assume collisions are not always productive for binding. This is consistent with evidence of proteins sliding over targets without binding (29, 41) and with protein–protein association rates, which are rarely diffusion limited (42). With both 1D and 3D association in our model, the ratio of a protein's local volume V to the accessible DNA length L between nucleosomes represents a critical factor controlling lifetimes and occupancy, but we will consider this fixed based on the Drosophila nucleus and genome size at V/L = 1,000 nm2 (see SX.B in SI). The association rates in 1D, kon1D, reflect a 1D search and therefore must be specified relative to the 3D rate kon3D, with their ratio, h2=kon3D/kon1D, having the same units as V/L. The magnitude of h2 will depend on the specific binding pair, and here we will constrain it to the 1–1,000 nm2 range, which is reasonable when localization to low-dimensional spaces entropically restricts configurations without significant allosteric effects (43–45). For example, this encompasses estimates for a purely diffusion-limited reaction of h2≈(4πσD3D)/(3D1D/L), where σ is a molecular binding radius, D is the translational diffusion coefficient, and a 1D macroscopic rate can only be approximately defined relative to a domain length scale L (46) due to re-entrant diffusion, unlike in 3D (47). These physically essential parameters of V, L, and h2 are all independently tunable in our spatial simulations and control the impact of dimensional reduction.   Although dimerization between transcription factors is known to bring together DNA-binding domains that effectively switch on DNA binding (48) (e.g. bZIP monomers (49, 50)), not all DNA-binding monomers need to form dimers or higher-order complexes to stably bind DNA, including GAF (5). Our model therefore addresses a broader range of mechanisms to establish where or when proteins can or cannot benefit from dimerization to impact target selectivity, beyond simply preformed dimers. We focus here on monomers that form homodimers to directly compare with GAF experiments. Genome-wide measurements report on a population of competing proteins, not single molecules, and our approach establishes regimes where dimerization impairs target binding via sequestration and where dimerization has minimal impact on mean dwell times despite altering the distribution of proteins on and off DNA. Critically, we quantify how dimerization (or higher-order oligomerization) significantly enhances selectivity for clustered targets, beyond what is expected based solely on an increased density of target sites. While clustered targets in the genome (commonly found in promoters and enhancers (51, 52)) are known to enhance transcription factor binding (51–53), even monomers will naturally select for a cluster over a single target due to the higher target density. However, we here quantify how protein–protein interactions can dramatically enhance dwell times on DNA and show that this population effect diminishes for widely spaced targets (54, 55). For the oligomer-forming GAF protein, ChIP-seq data supports selectivity for clustered targets in Drosophila (10, 56), and our model directly explains how protein–protein interactions are responsible for enhancing these localization inhomogeneities throughout DNA. While our model is much simplified compared with the in vivo environment where, for example, heterogeneity in nontarget sequences (15), 3D genome structure (21, 57), and energy-consuming reactions (58) contribute to transcriptional control, we expect measurements over multiple cells do help average out heterogeneity in these nontracked variables to justify our focus on reversible oligomers and target spacing. By isolating the impact of dimerization from the stabilizing effects of specific and nonspecific DNA binding in a coupled 3D and 1D system, our model provides a way to improve interpretation of single-particle tracking measurements and predict how both DNA-binding and protein–protein interaction domains control DNA target selection.


## Models and theory

### Model description
  Our rate-based models contain two objects: proteins and DNA in a 3D volume. We implement a stochastic spatial RD model and map this to a nonspatial mass-action kinetic model of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) to facilitate theoretical analysis and efficient phase-space exploration. Before defining their reaction network, system geometry, and parameter ranges, we overview properties of all models. Protein species P has three interaction sites: one site binds another protein partner, one nonspecifically binds to DNA, and one binds to specific DNA sites (Fig. 1). DNA is modeled as a 1D line composed of two discrete monomeric species, specific sites S and nonspecific sites N (Fig. S1). If a protein binds N, the species PN performs 1D diffusion continuously along this line. To capture the sliding (diffusion) of proteins along the 1D DNA to search for specific sites or other proteins, we assert in our spatial stochastic models simulated using the NERDSS software (59) that the nonspecific sites N themselves are diffusible, with Dt1D=1μm2/s (see SXII.C in SI). Although nonspecific sites do not physically translate along the DNA, only the proteins do, this definition produces only a random rearrangement of equivalent N sites with the same density. Technically, this simplifies our simulations by supporting continuous diffusion of proteins in 1D as in similar RD models (23), just as when the molecules are in 3D, while still defining a discrete number of total nonspecific sites (59). On the other hand, our specific sites S remain stationary, as proteins bound to their target motifs stop diffusing. In 3D, protein monomers diffuse with Dt3D=1.5μm2/s, and in 1D the monomer has Dt1D=0.6μm2/s. Higher-order assemblies diffuse more slowly consistent with Einstein–Stokes. The locations of the interfaces on the protein are at coordinates slightly offset from the protein center of mass, which supports simultaneous formation of all contacts (see SI and Fig. 1e). To be concrete, our model parameters are based on GAF from Drosophila (10). All simulation details in SI along with complete parameter lists in Dataset S1.


#### System setup
  The system geometry is focused on the “free” DNA between two nucleosomes, which has variable length of ∼15–100 nm in flies (31, 37–40). We treat nucleosomes as blocks to diffusion and assume 1D accessible stretches of ∼60 nm (∼180 nucleotides) in most simulations as a reasonable intermediate length to maintain up to two specific sites and a surplus of nonspecific sites, matching recent in vitro experiments on GAF binding to the Drosophila hsp70 promoter, where we note that in vitro experiments also explore much longer segments out to 5,000 nm (31) (see SX.C in SI). The system volume is then defined as 60,000 nm3 based on a V/L ratio of 1,000 nm2 for Drosophila (see SX.B in SI for justification), or y,z box lengths of ∼31 nm. We use reflecting boundaries at box edges for both 3D and 1D diffusion, and for simplicity, we do not consider more complex chromatin topology, which can impact target search times (21, 23). We note that although increases to V increase γ (Eq. 1), with fixed copy numbers, it also changes concentrations, so we here assume changes to γ arise from h2 (see SI for extended discussion).


#### Copy numbers
  For species concentrations, we define the number of N sites based on a line density of (nnon/l)∼3/5nm−1 (Fig. S1). The number of specific targets S in a segment of DNA (between two nucleosomes) is highly variable throughout the genome. For example, GAF has ∼105 segments and ∼15,000 target motifs (see SX.C and SX.E in SI). Excepting the last results section where we compare model predictions with ChIP-seq data (which reports on the DNA sequence occupancy for GAF from many cells), we model DNA segments with discrete integer copies of sites, either S=0 or S=2. We thus define models representing these distinct different DNA environments (Fig. 1c), and solve for the local equilibrium of these microenvironments, before considering competition between them. We assume an equilibrium steady state because it dramatically simplifies the possible solution space and represents an important reference state for future comparison to nonequilibrium systems. For our “DNA” model, S=0. For the “DNA + targ” model, S=2, but the sites are not adjacent, and thus a single protein dimer cannot bind both targets simultaneously. For the “DNA + clusTarg” model, S=2 and they are clustered, allowing a single dimer to bind both sites simultaneously. All three models have the same segment length, same N+S, and the same V/L ratio of 1,000 nm2 for Drosophila (see SX.B in SI for justification). NERDSS tracks integer copy numbers while the ODEs can readily probe a continuum of concentrations. For concentrations, we typically use [S]=0or158μM and [N]=948.9or791μM. The protein concentration for tracking two discrete proteins represents a high “local” concentration in the simulation volume of [P]=158μM. With the ODE model/theory we evaluate lower and higher concentrations as well.


#### Mapping from spatial to nonspatial (well mixed)
  We quantitatively map the particle-based RD model to a nonspatial system of ODEs following mass-action kinetics. The number of distinct species and the reaction network is the same in both models by design, as we use a microkinetic framework where every possible species that can form is tracked (P,PN,PSN,PP,…). The ODE models do not track diffusion or spatial geometry, so we must absorb these features into the model parameters or reaction network. Dimensional reduction, or the transformation of 3D interactions to a 1D space, is captured in both models via necessary changes to the (i) system size and (ii) reaction on-rate. For our coupled ODEs, all species are assumed well mixed, but reactions restricted to 1D must capture both (i) and (ii). To interconvert between all species we track them in the same units, choosing 3D units, e.g. [N] has units of copies/volume. All 1D association reactions must therefore have the reaction rate scaled by γ (Eq. 1). This can be shown mathematically (see SI for detailed derivation). The off rates vary at most by a scalar, kb1D=ckb3D, since they do not involve a search. To minimize additional parameters, we assume that the off rates are the same from 3D to 1D, kb1D=kb3D, and we use the same value of h2 for all 1D reactions (see SX.B in SI for further details). Key assumptions for expecting agreement between the spatial simulations and our ODE models/theory are therefore that diffusion is not limiting nor are the distances to reach DNA; hence, measured timescales are controlled by well-mixed concentrations and rates. The ODE models absorb the effects of diffusive travel time into macroscopic rate constants konX,koffX, which differ from the intrinsic or microscopic rates kaX,kbX, used in the NERDSS simulations. In 3D, this conversion to a single macroscopic rate is well established and accurate (60), whereas in 1D, a single macroscopic rate is an assumption because diffusion is reentrant (46). These assumptions are reasonable and valid in the rate regimes we study (binding is far from diffusion limited), and in the spatial domains, we consider (protein diffusion is fast enough to keep the system well mixed) and supported by our results showing close agreement between survival time distributions in both models (see also Fig. S2). We note that the fraction of proteins bound to DNA θ and the target occupancy ϕ are independent of diffusion and kinetic rates because we operate at equilibrium, and KAX=(konX/koffX)=(kaX/kbX)(60).


#### Reaction network
  Our model has seven rate parameters. Six of the rate parameters are from the fundamental pairwise or bimolecular interactions between our species: protein–protein dimerization P+P⇌PP with macroscopic rates konP and koffP, protein nonspecific binding to DNA (P+N⇌PN) with rates konN and koffN, and protein binding to specific DNA (P+S⇌PS) with rates konS and koffS (Fig. 1a). We note that the fraction of proteins bound to DNA and the target occupancy are independent of diffusion and kinetic rates because we operate at equilibrium. Because each protein P has three distinct and noncompeting interaction sites, reactions with P,S, and N sites combine to form multiple states, and in our microkinetic framework, we enumerate all of these states, with some of these reactions occurring in 1D. Shown in Fig. 1b, the monomer P can bind to DNA using both specific and nonspecific sites, denoted as state PSN. Here, either the nonspecific or specific site may bind DNA first, followed by the other. The distinction between state PSN and PS maintains detailed balance since proteins can both slide onto S in 1D or directly bind from 3D. From a molecular perspective, the PS+N reaction is like a conformational change (61, 62), or unimolecular reaction. However, since the N species can diffuse, the change must be represented in our model via a bimolecular reaction. The rate becomes pseudo-first order, because we assume the concentration of N is in excess and thus constant.

  To briefly contrast our three microenvironments (Fig. 1c), “DNA” model contains six species or microstates: [P],[PP],[N],[PN],[PPN],[PNPN], that participate in six reactions (Fig. S3). For “DNA + targ” model, in addition to the six species, it contains an additional seven species: [S], [PS], [PSN], [PPSN], [PPS], [PNPS], and [PNPSN], and 21 total reactions (Fig. S4 and SI SV.D). PNPS is a dimer where each constituent monomer binds DNA once. PPSN is also a dimer that forms two DNA interactions but both are on the same monomer. The species PSPS does not exist in this model because a dimer cannot simultaneously span to bind two targets at once, enforcing the spacing between targets. For “DNA + clusTarg” model, to capture the spatial adjacency of targets in the well-mixed ODEs, we define the target cluster by a new species S2. The protein dimers can now bind two targets at once. There are 10 additional species beyond the six species in model DNA, and 27 total reactions (Fig. S5 and SI SV.E).

### Dimerization generates selectivity of proteins for clustered targets on DNA
  We here combine two DNA microenvironments, one with a pair of clustered targets and one with a pair of separated targets, to calculate the spatial selectivity of proteins in this inhomogeneous environment theoretically (see SXI in SI) and compare with spatial stochastic simulations (Figs. 1e and 5a). We quantify the selectivity for environment A vs. B as the ratio of recruitment fractions to each DNA segment, selectivity(A)=(θ(A)/θ(B)), which is >1 when proteins select for A. By keeping all parameters identical in both environments except the separation between two targets, we see that monomers show no selectivity for A clustered vs. B separated targets, in theory or explicit spatial simulations (Fig. 5c). For even weak dimers, however, with KAP in mM−1 scale, we see a dramatic increase in protein selectivity for the clustered targets (Fig. 5b and c). The selectivity increases as expected for stronger dimers (Fig. 5b). However, these simple monotonic trends are not retained when protein copies match target copies, or NPtot=NStot. In this case, once the clustered target is occupied, proteins/dimers remaining in solution will now bind the separated targets. While there is still selectivity for the clustered targets, it is reduced with stronger dimerization as once θ(A)→1 on the clustered segment, dimerization can now only increase θ(B) (Fig. 5b). Lastly, we note that the particle-based stochastic NERDSS simulations (59) show a similar trend in selectivity to the theory but with some deviations particularly for strong dimers (Fig. 5b). This is primarily driven by the discrete individual molecules tracked in the NERDSS simulations, whereas in the continuum analytical theory, we allow fractional distributions of proteins throughout the system. By explicitly capturing spatial heterogeneity and diffusion, the NERDSS simulations show an even stronger switch-like behavior in selectivity for clustered sites as dimerization strength increases above ∼102M−1.


### Simulation results reproduce ChIP-seq data on oligomer selectivity for clustered targets
  The GAF protein forms higher-order oligomers of up to six-mers and possibly larger (10, 31, 63) and has thousands of targets in the genome (Fig. S10). In vivo, GAF occupancy as measured via ChIP-seq (56) is higher at clustered targets (10) (Fig. 5a). Through spatial simulations, we therefore challenged our proteins to now select between targets organized as a single target, cluster of two targets, or four-in-a-row clusters (Fig. 5a). We inevitably expect more proteins at the four-site cluster compared with the one-site cluster, and indeed if the proteins do not dimerize, their occupancy is increased by exactly 4-fold (Fig. 5b). More generally, the occupancy ϕn on a cluster of n targets is ϕ1=(ϕ2/2)=(ϕ3/3)=(ϕ4/4) for monomers (Fig. S9). Critically, this sets a theoretical upper bound on the distribution of monomers at cluster sites. However, when we allow dimerization, we now see effectively no proteins binding to the one-site cluster, and an increasing fraction from two to four sites. Normalizing by n, the dimer is clearly selective for a cluster of size two over one, whereas n=4 only increases due to a higher target density, ϕ1≪(ϕ2/2)=(ϕ4/4), producing a plateau (Fig. 5c). This result implies that proteins that form a tetrameric oligomer would be maximally selective for a cluster of four targets, and indeed this is what we recover with our model, ϕ1≪(ϕ2/2)<(ϕ4/4) (Figs. 5d and S9).

  These model results are consistent with the GAF ChIP-seq results only when GAF is forming higher-order oligomers, and not simply binding as monomers, as the results exceed the monomer upper bound. In other words, GAF occupancy is higher in genome regions with clusters of larger n target sites, even after normalizing the intensity by n, i.e. ϕ1<(ϕ6/6) (Fig. 5a). This normalized intensity even shows a plateau at ∼n=6 for GAF six-mers. This result is only consistent with the in silico oligomerization experiment and not the monomer behavior. We also observe the in silico selectivity appears to be higher than GAF (Fig. 5c and d), but this makes sense when considering the frequency of distinct clusters. Our simulated clusters (n = 1, 2, 3, or 4 sites per DNA segment) were present at equal amounts, to simplify the simulation setup (Fig. S10). In contrast, for GAF targets, cluster abundances are highly nonuniform (Fig. S10d and e); the majority of target sequences appear alone (n=1), and >90% are present at n<3 (64). The numerous sequences with small target clusters compete for proteins and impair the overall selectivity for sequences with large clusters but do not eliminate it, as we verify using simulations (Fig. S10f and g). Hence, the GAF shows remarkable selectivity for these large but infrequent clusters over single-target sites.


## DISCUSSION
  Our results show that dimerization between DNA-binding proteins will only universally enhance DNA target occupancy and selectivity when the target sites are clustered to the same or higher degree than the protein dimer. Thus, although dimerization always increases the recruitment fraction (SVII.C in SI), driving more proteins onto DNA from solution, the intuition that these proteins are more effective at occupying DNA targets oversimplifies the role of population dynamics (Fig. 6). A critical control parameter for all models is the strength of 1D partitioning to the nonspecific DNA, or γKAN[N]tot. Without nonspecific binding, all trends are relatively straightforward—for isolated targets, dimerization impairs, and for clustered targets, it enhances (SVIII in SI). With nonspecific binding, a long-lived dimer (slow koffP) that binds an isolated single target will enhance its own dwell time on DNA via nonspecific interactions, but by sequestering proteins from distant targets, the population can report a reduction in the mean dwell time (Fig. 6). A short-lived dimer (fast koffP) can also reduce the mean dwell time by recruiting proteins to DNA via transient protein interactions. Mutations that eliminate protein–protein interactions (10) can thus have unpredictable effects on dwell time. For isolated targets, dimerization can also simultaneously impair dwell time while still enhancing occupancy (black star in Fig. 6). While this may seem counterintuitive, it points to distinct driving factors for these two metrics; dimers can increase flux onto target DNA (higher effective on-rate), despite shortening the mean lifetime of the population across DNA (higher effective off rate). Such isolated target motifs are common in the genome for proteins like GAF (64), and with a sufficiently transient protein–protein bond lifetime and moderate target binding, dimerizing proteins can occupy these isolated motifs more frequently but for short intervals (Fig. 6), supporting “trials” of binding that may help ultimately identify clustered targets.

  By extending beyond previous quantitative models for predicting DNA dwell time and occupancy via the explicit addition of dimerization, we significantly expand the state space (up to 16 states) beyond the monomer bound to specific or nonspecific DNA (three to four states) (20, 28), and beyond models of protein dimerization on 1D (24). This provides a new framework for quantitatively interpreting in vitro DNA-binding experiments on multidomain proteins (31). With a thermodynamically consistent framework, our steady-state equations for dwell times and occupancies are relatively interpretable. Transferrable binding parameters (e.g. koffP,koffS) can be extracted from data despite the convolution of both protein and DNA contributions. Our application of the model to the GAF system has integrated out more accurate representation of the in vivo environment, where the genome organization is both structured and dynamic (57, 65, 66), and the dense packing of chromatin can both enhance and impair monomer search times (21, 23). In addition, the mechanics of DNA contributes to affinities (67, 68), nonspecific regions can induce variable association strengths (15, 69, 70), and macromolecular interactions induce higher-order assemblies (62) or liquid-like droplets (63). Further, we exclude energy-consuming processes that, like dimerization, can nonmonotonically enhance the response to concentration variations (58). Nonetheless, because these additional sources of variability exist throughout the genome, the data and our model clearly demonstrate that even weak protein–protein interactions can dramatically enhance selectivity for clustered targets, which is particularly notable when they are infrequent in the genome as is the case for GAF (Fig. S10). This produces what is effectively a spectrum of affinities for different regions of the genome as suggested previously (71), that is not a linear function of the target cluster size but rather a nonlinear function of (also) the protein–protein affinity. Predicting this emergent spectrum for oligomers from the fundamental pairwise interactions, including both 3D and 1D association, is a key outcome of our model (Fig. 5).

  For generalized transcription factors with widespread target sites, one expects selectivity for the sequences of highly expressed genes to be higher to enhance transcription (72), and that should fundamentally depend on collectively stronger affinities for DNA emerging not just from sequence recognition but via protein self-assembly (18). Dimerization is then an essential building block for identifying the correct sequence for transcription with the right frequency, but our work shows that it must be tuned to help rather than hinder target binding. This tunability can be encoded in nonspecific interactions or the 1D protein–protein binding rates, particularly the h2 factor from γ, in addition to the 3D rates. While we here considered h2 to span 1,000-fold variation, the significance of this 3D-to-1D binding parameter in controlling DNA interactions urges more careful quantification, as it has not been measured, and similar to membrane-associated interactions (44), allosteric effects could make assembly much more (or less) favorable in 1D vs. 3D (45, 73). Our model here considered only monomers that form homodimer and homo-oligomers, but heterodimers are also quite common among transcription factors (17, 73); with expansions of this model to include heterogeneity in protein interactions and diverse components, we predict stoichiometry then provides further nonlinear tunability for which genes are “switched on” for transcription either constitutively or in response to stimulus. This increasing design space for parameters will support more effective classification of DNA sequences, like a deep neural network, with assemblies of diverse DNA-binding domains improving selectivity for longer sequence tracts of distinct motif clusters. The models used here will help predict how competition and cooperation between binding partners control selectivity as it varies nonlinearly with concentrations, binding rates, and spatial localization throughout the genome, which is necessary to understand proper transcriptional control (74).
