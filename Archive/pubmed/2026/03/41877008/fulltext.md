# DcSE: an improved densenet with enhanced attention fusion for super-enhancer prediction


## Background
  Super-enhancers are critical cis-regulatory elements that play a central role in modulating gene expression and driving cellular identity. Their dysregulation is closely associated with the development of numerous major human diseases, particularly cancer. In this study, we propose DcSE, a novel deep learning framework designed for efficient and precise super-enhancer prediction. The core architecture is based on an enhanced DenseNet featuring an improved Convolutional Block Attention Module. Unlike standard serial processing, our module employs a dynamic fusion mechanism that adjusts the contributions of channel and spatial attention through learnable parameters. To further enhance robustness, DcSE adopts an ensemble learning framework utilizing cross-validation and multiple initializations.


## Results
  DcSE demonstrates exceptional performance on benchmark datasets for both human and mouse, surpassing existing state-of-the-art models across all evaluation metrics. It achieves 80.81% ACC and 87.86% AUC on the human dataset, along with 80.16% ACC and 87.04% AUC on the mouse dataset. Visual analysis through t-SNE confirms that the model learns highly separable, high-order feature representations from raw sequences. Furthermore, cross-species validation experiments prove the robust generalization capability of the framework. Motif analysis utilizing mask-based attribution methods successfully identifies species-specific key transcription factors, such as ZKSCAN3 in humans and STAT1 in mouse, providing clear biological interpretability.


## Conclusions
  DcSE is a high-performance, robust, and interpretable computational tool. By accurately capturing key sequence features and providing biological insights into transcription factor regulation, it offers a reliable framework for super-enhancer identification and the study of genomic regulatory mechanisms.


## Introduction
  Super-enhancers (SEs) are a class of cis-regulatory regions characterized by exceptionally high densities of transcription factors (TFs), cofactors, and active histone modifications. They play a crucial role in determining cellular identity and regulating gene transcription programs. Typically, super-enhancers are formed by the aggregation of multiple typical enhancers (TEs) within a specific genomic distance, spanning regions of 8,000 to 20,000 base pairs, whereas conventional enhancers are generally only 200 to 300 base pairs in length. The typical enhancers that constitute a super-enhancer are believed to act synergistically to significantly amplify the transcriptional activity of key genes. Furthermore, super-enhancers harbor a high concentration of trait-associated genetic variants, with mutations strongly linked to various diseases, including cancer, Alzheimer’s disease, and autoimmune disorders [1]. Early identification of super-enhancers primarily relied on ChIP-seq experiments [2], which involved detecting signal enrichment for transcription mediator complex subunits (MED1), master regulatory transcription factors, or histone modification markers such as H3K27ac [3]. The ROSE algorithm developed by the Young laboratory remains one of the most widely used bioinformatics tools for identifying super-enhancers. It achieves this by merging adjacent enhancers within a specified genomic distance threshold and ranking them based on ChIP-seq signal intensity [4]. However, such experimental approaches are not only costly and time-consuming but also dependent on high-quality antibodies specific to particular markers, which are not readily available for all cell types. Consequently, the development of computational methods for genome-wide super-enhancer identification has become a prominent research topic.

  In recent years, machine learning and deep learning technologies have exhibited remarkable potential in the study of super-enhancers. For example, Improse integrates 36 genomic features and utilizes various machine learning algorithms to identify super-enhancers [5]. Additionally, DeepSEN employs convolutional neural networks to differentiate super-enhancers from typical enhancers within epigenomic data [6]. Although these models showcase excellent predictive performance, they are heavily dependent on expensive and time-consuming ChIP-seq experimental data, which poses challenges for large-scale applications under constrained experimental conditions.

  To overcome the limitations imposed by experimental dependencies, researchers have begun exploring methods for super-enhancer identification that rely solely on DNA sequence information. Compared to approaches that utilize multi-omics features, DNA sequence data is readily accessible and inherently rich in regulatory signals. For instance, the DeepSE [7] model employs DNA2Vec [8] to statically encode DNA fragments into fixed-dimensional embedding vectors, followed by the use of a convolutional neural network to perform classification tasks. However, this approach neglects nucleotide positional information during encoding, making it challenging to model long-range dependencies. Additionally, its static embedding vectors cannot be optimized through backpropagation. While SENet [9], which is based on the Transformer architecture, enhances the modeling of global contextual relationships, it still struggles to fully capture fine-grained positional information within ultra-long DNA sequences. MuSE [10] attempts to achieve a more comprehensive sequence representation by integrating multiple features, such as one-hot and k-mer embeddings.

  Nevertheless, these advanced models still have room for improvement: on one hand, most approaches rely on fixed attention mechanisms or simple feature concatenation strategies, which fail to adaptively balance the importance of different features, thereby limiting the precision of feature extraction [11]. At its core, this issue arises from the design of the feature fusion module. Feature fusion is a critical component in deep learning, facilitating the integration of multi-scale or multi-view representations. Traditional deep learning models for genomic data typically utilize static fusion methods, such as direct concatenation or element-wise summation. While these approaches are computationally efficient, they treat all features equally, neglecting the inherent differences in information content and discriminative power across feature dimensions. To address these limitations, researchers have introduced standard attention mechanisms, such as SE-Net [12] and CBAM [13], which weight feature channels or spatial dimensions. However, these mechanisms generally adhere to fixed processing pipelines, lacking adaptability to sample- or instance-specific variations. In recent years, the broader deep learning field has increasingly shifted its focus toward dynamic feature fusion paradigms, where feature fusion strategies adapt based on the input data. For instance, Selective Kernel Networks (SKNet) dynamically adjust neural receptive fields through selection mechanisms [14]; CondConv [15] generates convolutional kernels adaptively for different samples; Adaptively Spatial Feature Fusion (ASFF) learns spatial weight maps for multi-scale feature fusion [16]; and Attentional Feature Fusion (AFF) enhances feature representation capabilities through attention-based fusion [17]. Although these dynamic fusion methods have shown promising results in computer vision tasks, they have not been fully integrated into super-enhancer identification. In this context, achieving a fine-grained balance between channel and spatial dimensions remains a critical issue that requires urgent attention. on the other hand, these models are predominantly employed as single predictors, exhibiting sensitivity to data partitioning and initialization, resulting in insufficient model stability and robustness [18].

  To address the aforementioned challenges, this study proposes a novel deep learning framework, DcSE. The core innovation of DcSE lies in its enhanced network architecture and robust ensemble strategy. Architecturally, we build upon the enhanced DenseNet framework and innovatively introduce a modified Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM) [13] between Dense Blocks and Transition Layers. Unlike traditional CBAM, which processes channel and spatial attention sequentially, our module employs a dynamic fusion mechanism. Learnable parameters dynamically adjust the contribution ratio between channel and spatial attention, enabling a more precise capture of key signal features within sequences. To further enhance model stability and generalization, DcSE employs an ensemble learning strategy based on cross-validation and multiple initializations. By randomly partitioning the data into 10 stratified splits and training multiple submodels with distinct initializations, it achieves multi-model fusion of prediction results and appreciable performance improvement.

  We systematically evaluated DcSE on human and mouse benchmark datasets. Experimental results demonstrate that DcSE consistently outperforms existing mainstream models across all key performance metrics. Ablation studies validate the effectiveness of the improved CBAM module and dynamic fusion strategy; t-SNE visualization reveals DcSE’s outstanding hierarchical feature learning capability; cross-species validation further confirms the model’s excellent generalization performance. Furthermore, model-based motif analysis indicates that DcSE possesses strong biological interpretability and successfully identifies species-specific key transcription factors [19]. In summary, DcSE provides a high-performance, robust computational model for super-enhancer identification with biological interpretability, offering a novel research perspective for understanding regulatory mechanisms at the DNA sequence level.


## Methods

### Data
  This study utilized the open-source DNA sequence dataset provided by Luo et al. [9], aiming to offer comprehensive and reliable data support for super-enhancer identification research. They extracted publicly available super-enhancer data from the SEdb 2.0 database [20] (http://www.licpathway.net/sedb/), encompassing both human genome (hg38) [21] and mouse genome (mm10) [21] sequences to ensure that the extracted sequences were highly representative. After meticulous processing of the raw data, Luo et al. extracted 10,341 SEs and 16,666,905 TEs) from the human dataset, and 11,677 SEs and 6,991,312 TEs from the mouse dataset. To address potential classification bias stemming from the clearly higher number of TEs in the raw data, Luo et al. randomly selected samples from TEs that matched the SE count and exhibited similar length distributions. This approach resulted in balanced benchmark datasets for both humans (10,341 each) and mouse (11,677 each). To comprehensively evaluate model performance and ensure the stability and reliability of experimental results, this paper employs a repeated stratified data-splitting evaluation strategy. Specifically, we performed ten mutually independent stratified random splits on the benchmark dataset. In each split, 90% of the samples were allocated for model training, while the remaining 10% constituted an independent test set. This approach ensured consistent category proportions of SE and TE across both training and test sets. Each data split utilized a distinct random seed to mitigate any accidental bias arising from specific partitioning patterns. It is important to note that this evaluation strategy aims to assess model performance stability through multiple independent training-testing iterations, as opposed to traditional cross-validation. All experimental results presented in this paper are reported as the mean ± standard deviation across the ten independent splits. Specific details of this benchmark dataset are presented in Table 1.

### Model structure
  In this study, we developed a deep learning model named DcSE for the identification and classification of super-enhancer sequences. This model can automatically learn complex feature information from DNA sequences and effectively handle the characteristics of enhancer data. The overall framework of the DcSE model is divided into four key components: (A) Sequence Embedding. We employ a pre-trained embedding matrix to transform DNA sequences into 100-dimensional continuous vector representations [19, 22]. These embeddings can be further optimized during training to effectively capture semantic sequence information [23]. (B) Enhanced Dense Connection Network. We utilize an improved DenseNet [24] architecture to process the embedded sequences. This network integrates an enhanced CBAM mechanism between Dense Blocks and Transition layers, building upon the traditional DenseNet framework. This enhancement enables the model to adaptively focus on critical feature regions, improving feature extraction efficiency. The network features a growth rate of 32 and a structure consisting of two Dense Blocks, where each block contains five layers. It employs a 0.3 dropout rate to prevent overfitting, thus more effectively extracting key sequence feature patterns from DNA sequences. (C)Feature Processing and Classification. Extracted features undergo batch normalization, followed by compression from 256-dimensional to 64-dimensional features via a fully connected layer with ReLU activation. Batch normalization [25] is reapplied to enhance training stability, while a 0.2 dropout rate further prevents overfitting [26]. Finally, the processed features generate prediction probabilities through an output layer with sigmoid activation, determining whether the input sequence constitutes a super-enhancer. (D)Ensemble Module. To enhance prediction accuracy and robustness, we designed an ensemble learning framework based on cross-validation [27]. Specifically, we partition the benchmark data into five stratified folds. An ensemble of five submodels is then created, where each submodel is trained on four folds. During the prediction phase, we utilize an averaging strategy to amalgamate the predictions from all five models, thereby effectively mitigating the variance and bias inherent in individual models [28].Detailed information is illustrated in Fig. 1.

#### Feature embedding scheme
  In this study, we employ advanced sequence embedding methods to process DNA sequence data from super-enhancers and typical enhancers. While traditional one-hot encoding is intuitive, it suffers from significant drawbacks: it generates highly sparse matrices that waste computational resources and completely ignores contextual relationships between bases and sequence patterns [29]. Although k-mer-based feature encoding partially addresses these issues, it faces the curse of dimensionality (4^k dimensions) and loses the original sequence order information.

  To overcome these limitations, we draw inspiration from the SENet approach and adopt a pre-trained embedding technique based on dna2vec [8]. This method utilizes human genomic sequences as a learning corpus, enabling the mapping of k-mer fragments within DNA sequences to a low-dimensional, dense vector space. We selected k = 6 and a sliding step size s = 1 as parameter configurations, which represent the optimal balance between information complexity and computational efficiency as demonstrated by the PEP-WORD experiment [30]. Unlike static embeddings, we set the embedding layer weights as trainable parameters, initialized solely with dna2vec, allowing these vectors to be further optimized through backpropagation during training. Through this dynamic embedding approach, each DNA sequence is transformed into a 3000 × 100 dimensional feature matrix. This not only significantly reduces feature dimension but also effectively preserves sequence semantic information and base-pair association patterns while capturing long-range dependencies. This efficient representation enhances the model’s ability to distinguish super-enhancers from typical enhancers.


#### Improve DenseNet
  In our proposed enhanced DenseNet architecture, we have optimized the feature learning and propagation mechanisms of the original DenseNet. Renowned for its unique “dense connections,” DenseNet comprises multiple Dense blocks interconnected through Transition layers. Within each Dense block, every layer is directly linked to all subsequent layers, facilitating efficient feature reuse and information flow. In our enhancement, we have innovatively introduced a specially designed CBAM after each Dense block and before the Transition layer. The improved DenseNet architecture is illustrated in Fig. 2.

  Fig. 2An improved DenseNet architecture based on an improved CBAM attention mechanism

  Specifically, after a Dense block completes its internal dense feature extraction, the resulting feature maps are first processed through this enhanced CBAM module. Unlike standard CBAM modules, which typically apply channel attention and spatial attention mechanisms sequentially, our key improvement lies in adopting a dynamic feature fusion strategy that effectively combines the previously independent channel and spatial attention mechanisms. This dynamic fusion mechanism enables the model to integrate important information from both the channel and spatial dimensions in a more collaborative and adaptive manner. This approach allows for more precise learning and emphasizes the most information-rich regions and channels within the feature map, while simultaneously suppressing relatively minor feature responses. Consequently, the network’s ability to understand and capture key features is enhanced. Feature maps optimized through this fusion attention mechanism are subsequently passed to the transition layer. Transition layers typically perform feature dimension reduction and pooling operations to manage model complexity. Therefore, introducing the CBAM enhanced by dynamic feature fusion before the feature maps enter the transition layer ensures that the network prioritizes and preserves the most finely filtered, discriminative feature information before dimensionality compression and spatial downsizing occur.

  In DenseNet, the Dense Block serves as the core building block, characterized by its internal ‘dense connection’ mechanism. This design significantly enhances feature reuse and improves gradient flow throughout the network, thereby mitigating the vanishing gradient problem. #### Improve CBAM
  The CBAM attention module comprises both a channel attention module and a spatial attention module. In the original CBAM design, input features are typically first processed by the channel attention module, whose output is subsequently fed into the spatial attention module to ultimately produce weighted feature maps. However, this sequential processing approach has inherent limitations: the attention modules compute in a fixed order, and the weight calculations of subsequent modules may be adversely affected by changes in the feature patterns from preceding outputs. This can lead to imprecise weight computations in the spatial attention module, or result in a partial loss of weight information for certain channels in the final feature map.

  To address the potential limitations of traditional attention mechanisms or simplistic fusion approaches, we propose an adaptive weighting fusion mechanism that introduces dynamic weighting strategies for both channel and spatial attention. Unlike conventional fixed-weight fusion or simple concatenation of attention features, our approach dynamically adjusts the importance of feature representations generated by channel and spatial attention based on the intrinsic characteristics of the input features. This is achieved through two learnable parameters, weight_channel and weight_spatial, in conjunction with a feature-aware adaptive module. The channel attention stream focuses on identifying feature channels with greater informational content (emphasizing ‘what’ is important), while the spatial attention stream prioritizes locating more critical spatial regions within feature maps (emphasizing ‘where’ is important). The structure of the dynamic fusion mechanism is illustrated in Fig. 4.

  Fig. 4Internal structure of dynamic feature fusion

  In implementation, these two learnable parameters are initially set to balanced values and subsequently optimized through backpropagation. To ensure a reasonable and stable weight distribution, we employ the Sigmoid function to constrain the final fusion weights within the range [0,1]. This enables the model to flexibly adjust the influence of each attention dimension’s feature flow based on the characteristics of the input data. For instance, when global channel information is more critical for recognition tasks, the model may automatically increase the influence of weight_channel; conversely, when the spatial layout of features is more important for localization, it will enhance the impact of weight_spatial. This weight allocation is entirely determined by the model’s internal learning mechanisms without explicit rule intervention, allowing the fusion mechanism to adaptively select the optimal combination of channel attention and spatial attention features for different input data or task requirements. #### Sequence prediction
  In this study, we constructed a fully connected module consisting of 100 neural units, primarily designed to extract features from input data and map them to the target output dimension. This structural design effectively captures latent patterns within the data, providing a robust foundation for subsequent analyses. To mitigate overfitting, we employed batch normalization techniques to standardize the input data, significantly enhancing the stability and convergence speed of model training. Additionally, we implemented Dropout regularization, which randomly deactivates a portion of neurons during training. This approach reduces the model’s over-reliance on training samples and enhances its generalization capabilities.

  Regarding the selection of activation functions, the ReLU function was applied after batch normalization. This nonlinear function effectively models complex relationships within the data, thereby enhancing feature learning efficiency. Finally, the feature representations extracted by the fully connected module are passed to output neurons equipped with Sigmoid activation functions, generating probability values between 0 and 1 to quantify the likelihood of a sample being a super-enhancer.


#### Ensemble model
  This study employs model averaging as an ensemble learning strategy to enhance prediction robustness and accuracy. As a core method in ensemble learning, model averaging effectively mitigates prediction biases inherent in individual models by integrating outputs from multiple independently trained models. The theoretical foundation lies in the observation that different models typically exhibit distinct types of error when applied to identical test samples; averaging these predictions significantly reduces variance and improves generalization capability.

  In the experimental design, to further enhance the stability of model predictions and reduce variance caused by model initialization and data partitioning, this paper proposes an ensemble learning framework based on five-fold stratified cross-validation within the training set. Specifically, for each independent data partition described in Sect.  2.1, we further divide the corresponding training set into five stratified subsets. Within each fold, the model is trained on four of these subsets and validated on the remaining one, yielding five mutually independent submodels. During prediction, these five submodels generate prediction probabilities for the same independent test set. The final result is obtained by taking the arithmetic mean of the prediction probabilities produced by these five submodels, thereby forming a five-model ensemble prediction result. This ensemble learning strategy is executed within each independent data partition, enabling the model to benefit from both data diversity and ensemble performance gains while maintaining complete invisibility of the test set.


### Performance evaluation
  In bioinformatics, the area under the ROC curve (AUC), area under the PR curve (AUPR), accuracy (ACC), recall (REC), precision (PRE), and F1 score are commonly utilized metrics for classification evaluation. ACC reflects the overall correctness of a model's predictions. REC, often referred to as sensitivity, measures a model's ability to accurately identify positive instances, while PRE indicates the proportion of samples correctly predicted as positive that are indeed positive. The F1 score, which is the harmonic mean of PRE and REC, provides a comprehensive evaluation of model performance. Similarly, AUPR is frequently employed to assess scenarios involving imbalanced data, as it holistically reflects the model's precision performance across all recall levels, thus offering greater sensitivity to the model's predictive capability for minority positive classes. AUC evaluates the model's performance across all possible classification thresholds, effectively assessing its overall ranking capability and ability to differentiate between positive and negative samples. These evaluation metrics provide a scientific foundation for model optimization, enhancing prediction accuracy and reliability while advancing the progress and application of this research. ### Selection of model hyperparameters
  To achieve efficient model tuning, this study employs Optuna [31], an open-source automated hyperparameter optimization framework. A key feature of Optuna is its define-by-run API, which allows researchers to dynamically construct the search space during optimization, thus providing exceptional flexibility. The framework incorporates advanced optimization algorithms, including the Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) and Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES), and integrates seamlessly with mainstream deep learning libraries such as PyTorch and TensorFlow. By automatically exploring parameter combinations, Optuna assists researchers in discovering optimal model configurations within complex search spaces. This method not only replaces time-consuming manual tuning but also significantly enhances the performance and generalization capabilities of the final model.

  In our experiments, we employed Optuna to optimize three critical hyperparameters: learning rate (l), training epochs (e), and batch size (b). The learning rate was evaluated over a discrete set of values: 5e-5, 1e-5, 5e-4, 1e-4, 5e-3, 1e-3, 5e-2, 1e-2, and 1e-1. The search interval for the training epochs ranged from 20 to 100, with a step size of 10, while the batch size was explored within the range of 16 to 64. In contrast, to achieve a balance between computational efficiency and model complexity, the architecture hyperparameters were fixed at specific values. Based on preliminary empirical studies, we configured the DenseNet backbone with a growth rate of 32 and two layers per DenseBlock, adhering to the design principles and empirical configurations outlined by Huang et al. [24]. These settings were selected to mitigate overfitting on genomic sequence inputs. Concurrently, in accordance with the standard recommendations from the original research, we established the channel reduction ratio of the CBAM module at 16 [13], which has been demonstrated to provide an optimal trade-off between parameter efficiency and attention performance. Following the optimization process conducted by Optuna, the optimal parameter combination was identified as follows: learning rate of 1e-4, training epochs of 30, and a batch size of 64.


### Comparison of different model frameworks
  In this study, we conducted ablation experiments to thoroughly investigate the contributions of each network module within the DcSE model. ### Comparison of different feature fusion methods
  Feature fusion plays a pivotal role in deep neural networks, significantly enhancing data representation capabilities by integrating complementary information from diverse sources or hierarchical levels. To systematically validate the effectiveness of our dynamic fusion strategy, which is the core of our improved CBAM, this study designed an ablation experiment comparing it against three other representative fusion methods: Simple concatenation involves directly merging feature vectors from channel attention and spatial attention along the feature dimension. Although this method is straightforward to implement and has been effectively utilized in DeepSEA [32] for integrating DNA sequence features, it assigns uniform weights to all features, neglecting the variations in feature significance Fixed-Weight Fusion involves the combination of features derived from channel attention and spatial attention through predetermined fixed weights. The DeepDTI model [33] utilizes this approach to merge features of drugs and proteins. By maintaining constant weights throughout both training and inference phases, this method ensures computational efficiency; however, it may restrict adaptability to diverse input patterns.Attention-Based Fusion is a strategy that dynamically assigns importance weights to features extracted through spatial and channel attention mechanisms. For example, iEnhancer-ECNN [34] utilizes these attention mechanisms in enhancer recognition tasks to effectively fuse multi-scale features. This approach allows the model to concentrate on the most relevant features based on the input contextDynamic Fusion is an advanced fusion strategy that adaptively adjusts fusion coefficients based on input features. The EPI-DynFusion model [35] has effectively demonstrated the utility of a similar dynamic feature fusion method in predicting enhancer-promoter relationships. Given these advantages, this dynamic fusion approach is the strategy we adopted and enhanced within our DcSE framework.

  As illustrated in Fig. 8, the four fusion methods demonstrate considerable differences across various evaluation metrics when applied to the Human and Mouse datasets.

  Fig. 8A Comparison of performance evaluation metrics for different feature fusion methods on the Human dataset. B Comparison of performance evaluation metrics for different feature fusion methods on the Mouse dataset

  In the Human dataset, the dynamic fusion strategy achieved the best performance across all six evaluation metrics, particularly approaching 0.88 in AUC and AUPR. This result demonstrates its adaptive modeling capability for feature importance. Given the relative stability and high evolutionary conservation of human genomic regulatory patterns, dynamic fusion effectively integrates complementary information from channel attention and spatial attention. This integration enables the capture of key sequence features closely associated with enhancer activity, such as transcription factor binding sites and chromatin accessibility signals. In contrast, methods such as simple concatenation, fixed-weight fusion, and attention fusion fail to fully leverage the differences between these hierarchical features, resulting in limited improvements across metrics.

  On the Mouse dataset, dynamic fusion maintained superiority in metrics like ACC, AUC, AUPR, and Precision; however, Recall notably declined to approximately 0.70. We hypothesize that this decline may stem from the stronger tissue specificity and condition dependency of mouse gene regulation. Compared to humans, mouse exhibit greater regulatory diversity across developmental stages and tissue types, resulting in higher variability in enhancer activity patterns. When learning primary regulatory features, dynamic fusion may prioritize amplifying globally consistent signals while underemphasizing locally active, weakly regulated elements. This bias leads to omissions in identifying some true positive samples. This tendency reflects the model’s focus on high-confidence biological signals rather than comprehensive coverage of all potential enhancer regions.

  Overall, dynamic fusion exhibits a trait of ‘conservative reinforcement’ at the biological level: it captures core regulatory patterns more accurately in evolutionarily conserved and stably regulated species (e.g., humans). However, its emphasis on primary signals may diminish the recognition rates for minor or conditional regulatory regions in systems characterized by more plastic regulatory networks and pronounced tissue specificity (e.g., mouse). This phenomenon indicates that fusion strategies employed across species should consider variations in regulatory element conservation and tissue specificity to achieve a balance in the model’s biological performance, optimizing both sensitivity and specificity.


### Quantitative explanatory analysis of dynamic fusion mechanisms
  To further investigate the intrinsic contribution of the dynamic fusion mechanism to enhancing super-enhancer prediction performance, we conducted a quantitative analysis of the fusion weights learned during model training. Specifically, we statistically analyzed the distribution of fusion weights corresponding to each sample across 10 independent experiments of DcSE on the Human and Mouse datasets, visualizing the results as violin plots as shown in Fig. 9. The quantitative results reveal that in the Human dataset, the average fusion weights for channel attention and spatial attention were 0.709 ± 0.118 and 0.291 ± 0.118, respectively. In the Mouse dataset, the corresponding average fusion weights were 0.714 ± 0.12 and 0.286 ± 0.122. These results indicate that although channel attention generally carries a higher weight, both attention branches exhibit significant weight fluctuations across different samples. This demonstrates that the dynamic fusion mechanism does not rely on fixed fusion ratios but instead adaptively adjusts the relative contributions of the two attention types based on the feature patterns of different sequence samples.

  From a distributional perspective, the Human and Mouse datasets exhibit consistent overall trends in fusion weights while showing subtle differences in weight dispersion, reflecting potential variations in the feature organization of super-enhancer sequences across species [36]. This coexistence of cross-species consistency and divergence provides quantitative support for the stability and generalization capabilities of dynamic fusion mechanisms in sequence modeling tasks.

  From a biological perspective, super-enhancer sequences typically exhibit two key features: first, they possess motif combination patterns driven by transcription factor binding sites; second, they demonstrate the spatial organization of functionally distinct segments within the sequence [37]. Channel attention is particularly effective at emphasizing motif combinations and higher-order feature interactions, while spatial attention excels in capturing position-dependent regions of importance. In contrast to traditional CBAM, which employs a serial structure that may result in the sequential propagation of attention information and the introduction of bias, the dynamic fusion mechanism proposed in this paper integrates both complementary types of information in parallel. This mechanism flexibly allocates contribution ratios based on sample characteristics, rendering it more suitable for the task of enhancer sequence modeling.

### Significance analysis
  In this study, we aimed to validate the statistical robustness of the performance improvement of DcSE over the comparison models. To achieve this, the original dataset was randomly partitioned into 10 independent splits using 10 distinct random seeds. For each partitioned training and test set, both DcSE and the comparison models underwent independent training and evaluation, resulting in 10 sets of paired performance metrics. The results are illustrated in Figs. 10 and 11. A statistical significance analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism based on these experimental results. Given that the performance distributions across the 10 independent experiments may not meet the normality assumption, and considering that this study seeks to determine whether DcSE’s performance is significantly superior to that of the baseline models we employed the one-tailed Wilcoxon [41] matched-pairs signed-rank test for statistical inference.

  On the Human dataset, statistical results demonstrate that DcSE exhibits extremely significant performance advantages (p < 0.001) over all comparison models across four key metrics: ACC, AUC, AUPR, and Precision. This finding underscores its ability to effectively balance recall capability while maintaining high precision.

  Unilateral tests conducted on the Mouse dataset further substantiate these conclusions. Specifically, DcSE demonstrated a significant advantage over all comparison models (p < 0.01 or p < 0.001) across AUC, AUPR, F1, and Precision metrics. Although the differences between SVM and MuSE on individual metrics (e.g., ACC or Recall) did not achieve statistical significance, the overall distribution across 10 independent experiments indicated that DcSE exhibited a higher median performance and superior stability, thereby reflecting its robust generalization capability across diverse species datasets.

Compared to the intra-species prediction performance shown in Tables 3 and 4, the cross-species prediction performance exhibits a decline, which aligns with expectations. For instance, AUC values consistently exceeded 0.82 in intra-species predictions, whereas in cross-species experiments, the ‘Human-> Mouse’ direction achieved an AUC of 0.78 ± 0.02 and ‘Mouse-> Human’ reached 0.80 ± 0.01. Nevertheless, all key metrics—including AUC and AUPR—remained above 0.6, robustly demonstrating that the DcSE model effectively captures shared biological sequence patterns between human and mouse species. This confirms the feasibility of cross-species prediction and the model’s strong generalization capability. When further comparing the two prediction directions, we observed that ‘Mouse-> Human’ achieved slightly superior overall performance. As shown in Fig. 10, this direction outperformed the other in ACC (0.72 vs. 0.64), AUC (0.80 vs. 0.78), AUPR (0.80 vs. 0.79), Precision (0.72 vs. 0.66), and F1 score (0.73 vs. 0.69), while only slightly underperforming in Recall (0.77 vs. 0.79). This result indicates that the DcSE model demonstrates strong knowledge transfer capabilities from one species to another. In our experiments, features learned from mouse data appear to exhibit slightly greater cross-species generalizability than those learned from human data.

### Motif analysis
  To investigate the biological interpretability of the DcSE model in distinguishing SE regions and validate whether it captures meaningful regulatory signals, we performed motif analysis. Previous studies indicate that SE typically exhibit high-density enrichment of specific transcription factor (TF) binding sites, where these TFs often serve as key master regulators driving cellular identity. Therefore, we hypothesize that high-importance sequence patterns identified by the DcSE model during prediction should correspond to the binding motifs of these critical TFs.

  To validate this hypothesis, we employed a masking-based attribution method to identify the sequence regions that contribute most significantly to model predictions [48]. Specifically, this process utilizes a sliding window approach (window size: 50 6-mer motifs, stride: 25) that traverses the input sequence. At each window position, the motifs within the window are temporarily masked, and the DcSE model predicts the masked sequence anew. We quantified the importance score of each region by calculating the difference between the prediction probability of the original sequence and that of the masked sequence. Regions with higher scores indicate a greater contribution to the model’s SE prediction. To further evaluate the sensitivity of motif discovery results to regional selection thresholds, we conducted a systematic robustness analysis. Specifically, we selected the top 50, 100, 150, and 200 high-importance regions, ranked by importance scores, as inputs. After aggregating these sequences, we performed de novo motif discovery using the MEME tool [49]. To rigorously distinguish genuine regulatory motifs from background noise, we employed a zero-order Markov model constructed from the input sequences as the background model. Subsequently, the discovered motifs were compared against a database of known transcription factor binding sites using the TomTom tool [50], and statistical significance was assessed via its built-in null model.

  Results indicate that the identified core modules exhibit high consistency across all tested threshold conditions, as illustrated in Figs. 14A–D and 15A–D. This consistency is quantitatively supported by statistical significance: in the Human dataset, the TomTom matching p-values for core modules at thresholds ranging from Top 50 to Top 200 were 3.70 × 10⁻³, 8.40 × 10⁻⁴, 3.41 × 10⁻², and 7.07 × 10⁻³. In the Mouse dataset, the corresponding p-values were 4.72 × 10⁻³, 3.55 × 10⁻⁵, 5.10 × 10⁻⁴, and 4.81 × 10⁻³. Notably, the Top 100 threshold demonstrated optimal or highly competitive statistical significance across both species (Human: 8.40 × 10⁻⁴; Mouse: 3.55 × 10⁻⁵), further validating its suitability as a representative set. Collectively, these analyses demonstrate that the modular signals identified by DcSE reflect intrinsic and stable regulatory features within sequences, rather than biases introduced by specific selection thresholds. The final detailed modular alignment and annotation results are presented in Figs. 14 and 15.

  Using this analytical workflow, we successfully identified key sequence motifs leveraged by the DcSE model during prediction. As illustrated in Fig. 14, regions identified by the model as highly significant in the human dataset demonstrated substantial enrichment of a de novo motif (bottom figure). The TomTom tool aligned this motif with MA1973.1 from the JASPAR database (top figure) [51], which is a recognized binding site for the transcription factor ZKSCAN3 (Zinc finger with KRAB and SCAN domains 3). Concurrently, as depicted in Fig. 15, the same analysis conducted on the mouse dataset al.so revealed a significantly enriched de novo motif (bottom figure). This motif was matched by TomTom to STAT1_MOUSE.H11MO.0.A in the HOCOMOCO database (top figure) [52], corresponding to a binding site for STAT1 (Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 1). These findings illustrate that the DcSE model effectively learned biologically relevant transcription factor binding patterns associated with super-enhancer function in both species. Notably, the most significant motif identified varies between species: ZKSCAN3 in humans versus STAT1 in mouse. This observation offers profound insights into the cross-species prediction results discussed in "Performance of DcSE on imbalanced datasets" Section. On one hand, the success of cross-species predictions indicates that DcSE captures deeper regulatory syntax or conserved features shared across species. On the other hand, the motif analysis in this section highlights that the model also learns potent species-specific signals. This underscores the complexity of the DcSE model: it relies not solely on a single conserved feature but rather captures and utilizes both evolutionarily conserved universal rules and biologically critical species-specific regulatory factors simultaneously, thereby achieving high-precision predictions.


## Conclusion
  In this study, we propose a novel deep learning framework named DcSE for accurately identifying super-enhancer regions within DNA sequences. The core innovations of DcSE lie in its unique network architecture and robust ensemble strategy. Architecturally, DcSE is an enhanced version of DenseNet, wherein we innovatively integrate an improved convolutional block attention module between Dense Blocks and Transition layers. This module employs an adaptive weight fusion mechanism with learnable parameters, enabling the model to dynamically adjust the contributions of channel and spatial attention based on input features. At the framework level, to enhance stability and generalization, we designed an ensemble learning framework based on cross-validation and multiple models, effectively reducing the bias and variance associated with a single model.

  Extensive experimental results validate the effectiveness of the DcSE model. Ablation studies and feature fusion comparisons demonstrate that our proposed enhanced CBAM and its dynamic fusion strategy clearly outperform alternative methods. In systematic comparisons with existing state-of-the-art predictors, DcSE achieves optimal performance across all key evaluation metrics on both human and mouse datasets. t-SNE visualization analysis intuitively illustrates DcSE’s robust hierarchical feature learning capability, demonstrating how the model progressively refines highly confounded raw sequence features into sharply delineated, distinguishable clusters. Cross-species validation further confirms DcSE’s strong generalization ability to capture shared regulatory syntax across species. Finally, motif analysis not only validates the model’s biological interpretability but also reveals that DcSE simultaneously learns evolutionarily conserved universal rules and biologically critical species-specific regulatory factors, such as ZKSCAN3 in humans and STAT1 in mouse.

  Despite the encouraging results, several limitations warrant discussion. First, concerning input data representation, super-enhancer sequences typically span broad genomic regions (8 kb to 20 kb). Due to computational constraints, our model employs a fixed input length, necessitating the truncation of longer sequences, which may lead to the loss of distal regulatory information. Second, while our sequence-based approach avoids reliance on costly ChIP-seq data, it inherently lacks the cell-type-specific dynamic context provided by epigenetic markers. Finally, the proposed ensemble framework introduces a trade-off between performance and efficiency, as training five independent submodels increases computational overhead compared to single-model approaches.

  Future work will focus on addressing these challenges. We aim to explore Transformer-based architectures capable of handling longer contexts to mitigate information loss due to truncation. Additionally, we plan to investigate lightweight model distillation techniques to reduce computational burden while preserving the robustness advantages of the ensemble strategy. Furthermore, we intend to extend DcSE to predict the functional impact of non-coding variations within identified super-enhancers, bridging the gap between sequence identification and the interpretation of disease mechanisms. In summary, despite these limitations, DcSE provides a high-performance, stable, and biologically interpretable computational tool for super-enhancer identification, offering new research perspectives for understanding regulatory mechanisms at the DNA sequence level.
