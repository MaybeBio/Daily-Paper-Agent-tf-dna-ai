# Screening of peptide inhibitors targeting YAP-TEAD4 interaction: affinity evaluation and anti-AML cell activity


## Abstract
  Aberrant activation of YAP-TEAD4 drives tumorigenesis, progression, and chemoresistance. Disrupting their interaction serves as an alternative anticancer strategy, with peptides better adapting to the large, flat interaction interface. In this study, the peptides 1–4 were screened from the peptide database via pharmacophore modelling, molecular docking, and interaction analysis. Subsequently, affinity experiments showed that among the peptides 1–4, peptide-4 possessed the lowest Kd values (Kd = 5.08 ± 0.42 nM) measured by MST and exhibited the binding affinity for TEAD4. MD simulations further demonstrated that peptide-4 stably bound to the TEAD4. MTT assays showed that peptide-4 suppressed AML-193 cell viability with an IC50 of 0.65 ± 0.04 μM. RT-qPCR assays demonstrated that Peptide-4 significantly downregulated the mRNA expression levels of CTGF and CYR61. In conclusion, the data demonstrated that the peptide-4 may serve as a promising candidate to disrupt the YAP-TEAD4 interaction and enhance biological activity in AML-related cellular models.


## Introduction
  Acute Myeloid Leukaemia (AML) cancer is a comprehensive manifestation resulting from dysregulation of multiple signalling pathways, and has emerged as one of the major threats to human health and safety1,2. As an evolutionarily conserved signalling pathway, the Hippo pathway plays a pivotal role in regulating organ size, tissue development, and homoeostasis maintenance3. Dysregulation of the Hippo pathway, particularly the aberrant activation of YAP (yes-associated protein), is closely associated with the initiation, progression, metastasis, and chemoresistance of various tumors4. Although chemotherapy and emerging therapeutic strategies-including immunotherapies such as antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs), recombinant fusion proteins, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), and CAR-T cell therapy-can inhibit cancer progression to a certain extent, these approaches are not without limitations5. These drawbacks mainly manifest as poor durable efficacy, severe adverse effects, and a paucity of targeted therapeutic options in clinical practice4,6. Current cancer intervention strategies still face considerable challenges, highlighting the need for alternative methods to regulate cancer-related biological processes7.

  When the Hippo pathway is activated, YAP/TAZ (Transcriptional Coactivator with PDZ-binding Motif) are phosphorylated, resulting in their cytoplasmic retention or degradation8. Conversely, pathway inactivation enables YAP/TAZ nuclear translocation, where they form complexes with transcription factors such as TEAD4 (transcriptional enhanced associate domain 4) to promote tissue growth9. Furthermore, the key downstream target genes of the Hippo signalling pathway, CTGF (Connective Tissue Growth Factor) and CYR61 (Cysteine-Rich Angiogenic Inducer 61), exhibit significant upregulation in cancer10. YAP/TAZ overexpression drives Hippo pathway dysregulation and unregulated cell proliferation in multiple malignancies, including AML, chronic lymphocytic leukaemia (CLL), breast cancer, glioblastoma, non-small cell lung cancer, and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma8,11. In AML patients, YAP exhibits aberrant activation and is functionally involved in leukemic progression, while TAZ is maintained in a non-functional, inactive state12. Furthermore, TAZ is overexpressed in patients with CLL13. Thus, YAP is identified as the promising therapeutic target for AML. YAP is a transcriptional coactivator without enzymatic activity or binding pockets, making it a difficult-to-drug target14. Therefore, inhibiting TEAD4 to block YAP function represents a viable strategy. A number of TEAD-targeting inhibitors have been identified in cancer-related studies, such as flufenamic acid, niflumic acid, vinylsulfonamide, and MGH-CP115,16 However, TEAD inhibitors are limited by poor specificity, resulting in low binding affinity17. Therefore, inhibitors targeting the YAP-TEAD4 interaction hold promise as alternative approaches for modulating cancer-associated signalling pathways.

  The YAP-TEAD4 complex has three interfaces, where YAP binds to TEAD4 via three distinct regions: β-strand (interface 1), α-helix (interface 2), and Ω-loop pocket (interface 3)18. The isolated β-strand and α-helix sequences of YAP exhibit low affinity for TEAD4, posing challenges for drug discovery19. In contrast, the isolated Ω-loop sequence of YAP shows micromolar affinity for TEAD420. Consequently, compounds derived from YAP’s Ω-loop sequence can disrupt the YAP-TEAD4 interaction21. Interface 3 of the YAP-TEAD4 interaction features a large, flat surface without deep pockets, rendering the development of small-molecule inhibitors challenging22. IAG933, a small-molecule inhibitor targeting the YAP-TEAD4 protein–protein interaction (PPI), was in Phase I clinical trials for treating mesothelioma in 20214. However, no updated results have been reported to date. VT3989 and IK-930 exhibited differential binding affinities by inhibiting the palmitoylation of TEAD proteins14. Given the inherent properties of peptides, the discovery of peptide inhibitors targeting YAP-TEAD4 holds significant importance for modulating YAP-TEAD4-associated biological activity.

  Peptide 17, derived from the interface 3-targeting peptide fragment YAP84-100 and modified via disulphide bond-mediated conformational optimisation, has been applied in therapeutic studies on murine ovarian cancer models. Peptide-17 bound TEAD1 with a Kd value of 15 nM23. The cysteine-rich peptide TB1G2 has been demonstrated to specifically block the YAP-TEAD interaction24. The YAP-derived Ω-loop sequence, cyclized via disulphide bond modification (designated as Stapled sYAPm1), exhibits enhanced binding affinity for TEAD. Stapled sYAPm1 had a Kd of 37.0 ± 5.4 μM25. However, such YAP-TEAD4-targeting peptide inhibitors generally suffer from insufficient affinity. Peptide 10, which incorporates unnatural amino acids into the YAP85-99 sequence to enhance affinity, exhibits more potent inhibition of the YAP-TEAD4 interaction and was selected as the positive control compound in this study due to its high affinity. Peptide 10 bound with a Kd of 12.16 ± 0.93 nM22. However, in-depth cellular and in vivo studies of it in tumours have not been conducted. Thus, improving their binding affinity has become a critical issue requiring urgent resolution for AML therapy.

  Structure-based virtual screening enables more efficient identification of active molecules from compound databases26. Additionally, our research group has consistently adopted a strategy involving pharmacophore model construction based on structure and protein-ligand interactions, followed by active compound screening and validation of their activity through in vitro and in vivo experiments27,28. The pharmacophore model constructed based on the structural characteristics and interaction mode of YAP-TEAD4 was used to screen the active peptide (peptide-4) from database, and its activity was validated via in vitro experiments. Thus, peptide-4 shows activity in AML-related cell models by interacting with the YAP-TEAD4 complex.


## Materials and methods

### General
  The peptides 1–4 and Peptide 10 were synthesised by Nanjing peptide Biotech Co. Ltd. (Nanjing, China). The dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was obtained from Aladdin Reagent (Shanghai, China). The recombinant human TEAD4 protein was acquired from Abcam (Cambridge, MA, USA), with the catalogue number is ab238222. The full-length amino acid sequence of human YAP protein was retrieved from UniProt (Accession No. P46937). Its sequence was provided in Table S1.


### Cell culture
  AML-193 cells (Catalogue No.: CRL-9589) were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific), with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin added, in the incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Cells were routinely subcultured every 2–3 days. All experiments were conducted in cells in the logarithmic growth phase.


### Establishment of pharmacophore model
  The crystal structure of the YAP-TEAD4 complex (PDB ID: 3KYS) with a resolution of 2.8 angstroms (Å, 1 Å = 0.1 nm) was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB). It was opened using the Molecular Operating Environment (MOE, Chemical Computing Group Inc., Montreal, Quebec, Canada) software. To begin with, the structure was preprocessed using the Structure Preparation tool for structural correction under the Amber 10:EHT force field. The interaction of the YAP-TEAD4 complex was analysed in MOE. Based on the interactions between key amino acids in the TEAD4 active site and YAP, a pharmacophore model was constructed. In constructing the pharmacophore model of TEAD4, hydrogen bond acceptors, hydrogen bond donors, and hydrophobic centres were prioritised as key features. The constructed pharmacophore features were combined to form the TEAD4 pharmacophore model. It was served for virtual screening of peptide inhibitors targeting YAP-TEAD4 PPI.


### Validation of the constructed pharmacophore model
  The validation method for the constructed pharmacophore model was adopted from this reference. Seven active peptides were derived from two separate references22,23. A test set comprising 500 molecules was constructed by combining active and inactive peptides. Based on the constructed pharmacophore model, the 500 molecules were screened using the docking tool of MOE. The screened molecules were classified into active and inactive compounds, with their respective counts recorded. Two evaluation metrics for the pharmacophore model-Enrichment Factor and Goodness of Hit Score (GH)-were calculated simultaneously.


### Construction of peptide library
  Peptide library construction methods reference previously published literature28,29. A combinatorial peptide library was generated using the QuaSAR-CombiGen algorithm with short peptide fragments as building blocks, where each fragment library comprises 39 peptide sequences. The peptide library containing 59,319 decapeptides was constructed by integrating two tripeptide fragments and one tetrapeptide fragment for the virtual screening of YAP-TEAD4 peptide inhibitors. Attachment sites were defined as “An” terminal atoms, where n is a positive integer. Tripeptide and tetrapeptide libraries contained specific site labels, with tripeptide fragments’ “A0” connecting to “A1” and “A2” of tetrapeptide fragments, respectively. Finally, the database of 59,319 decapeptides was output, with their two-dimensional (2D) structures converted to three-dimensional (3D) via energy minimisation in MOE for subsequent virtual screening.


### Virtual screening
  The relevant sequences of YAP within the YAP-TEAD4 complex were deleted. Next, Peptide 10 was converted into a 3D structure and docked into the TEAD4 domain using the Dock tool to form the TEAD4-Peptide 10 complex. The TEAD4-Peptide 10 complex was processed using the QuickPrep tool, encompassing the following: (1) Hydrogen atom addition via Protonate 3D; (2) Removal of water molecules beyond 4.5 Å from the TEAD4 domain or Peptide 10; (3) Energy minimisation until the root-mean-square (RMS) gradient reached 0.1 kcal/mol/Å. Peptide 10 was used as the positive control to guide and validate the entire virtual screening workflow.

  Based on the constructed TEAD4 pharmacophore model, a preliminary screening of the 59,319-peptide library was performed to identify potential YAP-TEAD4 PPI inhibitors. The selected peptides were docked into the active pocket of TEAD4 using the Dock module in MOE. The ligand conformations were sampled at a density of 1.0 within the TEAD4 binding site. The initial ligand conformations were generated via the Triangle Matcher algorithm and pre-screened using the London dG scoring function. Subsequently, conformations were refined under the Rigid Receptor framework (rigid receptor-flexible ligand docking mode), and further prioritised using the GBVI/WSA dG scoring function. The top four peptide conformations, ranked by docking scores, were selected for subsequent in vitro evaluation experiments.


### MST assay
  The MST experiment was performed following literature and established protocols24,28. Using Peptide 10 as the positive control, recombinant human TEAD4 protein was fluorescently labelled at a final concentration of 10 μM according to the manufacturer’s instructions with the Monolith™ RED-NHS 2nd Generation Protein Labelling Kit (Nano Temper Technologies GmbH, Munich, Germany). The labelled TEAD4 protein tubes were wrapped in aluminium foil and stored on ice; peptide samples were dissolved in PBST (Phosphate Buffered Saline with Tween) and serially two-fold diluted to generate 16 concentration gradients. For peptides with high lipophilicity, an appropriate amount of DMSO could be added for solubilisation, with the final DMSO concentration required to be ≤ 5% to ensure experimental reliability. Subsequently, the samples were incubated in the dark for 5 min. After capillary loading, fluorescence intensity was measured using the Monolith NT.115 instrument (Nano Temper Technologies GmbH).


### Molecular dynamics simulations
  To assess peptide-4 binding stability with TEAD4, 50 ns MD simulations were performed using GROMACS (GROningen MAchine for Chemical Simulations, version 2021.5). The most favourable conformer complex of TEAD4-peptide-4 was derived from docking results. The TEAD4-peptide-4 complex was subjected to preprocessing using the QuickPrep tool, parameterised against the Amber 10:EHT force field. The topology generation of the TEAD4-peptide-4 complex was performed using the AMBER99SB-ILDN force field. The TEAD4-peptide-4 complex was then positioned in a 1.0 nm cubic box, with solvation performed via the extended simple point charge (ESP) model. To maintain the system’s electrostatic neutrality, sodium and chloride counterions were added, which serves to replicate the physiological environment and guarantee the scientific integrity and stability of the simulation. The system was subjected to 5000-step energy minimisation using the steepest descent algorithm. Subsequently, equilibration simulations were carried out in the NVT (temperature: 300 K) and NPT (pressure: 1.0 bar) ensembles to facilitate stable MD simulations. The result was submitted to the BeiKun Cloud platform to conduct the relevant computation (https://www.bkunyun.com/v2/console/create-job). In the MD simulation, the leap-frog algorithm was employed as the integrator with a time step of 2 fs. Additionally, energy and trajectory data were sampled at 10 ps intervals. Root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), secondary-structure, radius of gyration (Rg) and hydrogen bond number plots were generated using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.5).


### MM/PBSA calculations
  In the MD simulation, the final 100 conformations of the TEAD4-peptide-4 complex were utilised for binding free energy calculation via the Molecular Mechanics/Poisson–Boltzmann Surface Area (MM/PBSA) method. In this study, the single-trajectory approach was employed to calculate the binding free energy, using the formulas:  ΔG = ΔH −T × ΔS  ΔH =ΔEele+ΔEvdW+ΔGPB+ΔGSA

  Gibbs free energy change (ΔG) refers to the binding free energy, which is defined as the difference between the free energy of the complex and the sum of the free energies of its individual components (the TEAD4 protein and peptide-4). Enthalpy change (ΔH) represents the change in the thermal effect of the system and consists of the electrostatic interaction energy (ΔEele), van der Waals interaction energy (ΔEvdW), polar solvation free energy (ΔGPB), and nonpolar solvation free energy (ΔGSA). The configurational and solvent entropy change (– T (thermodynamic temperature) × ΔS (entropy change)) denote the thermodynamic contribution term of entropy. The ΔG of the TEAD4-peptide-4 complex was computed to quantitatively evaluate the binding affinity between the protein and ligand.


### Free energy landscape
  The RMSD and Rg values of the TEAD4-peptide-4 complex at corresponding time points were integrated. The free energy landscape was generated via GROMACS, followed by conversion of the landscape data into numerical files using Python and subsequent rendering of the 3D landscape plot with the Matplotlib library. Next, the 2D interaction profile of the optimal conformation of the TEAD4-Peptide-4 complex was generated using LigPlot.


### MTT assay
  AML-193 cells were inoculated in 100 μL of the RPMI-1640 medium at the density of 5 × 10³ cells/well in 96-well plates. After allowing the cells to attach for 12 h, add the peptides at the specified concentration. The peptides were subjected to two-fold serial dilution with a concentration range of 0.1 ∼ 4.2 μM. Cells were incubated at 37 °C for 72 h in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2. Then, 10 μL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL in PBS) was added to each well and incubate at 37 °C for 4 h. 100 μL of DMSO was added to each well to dissolve the formaldehyde crystals. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy HT). Cell viability was used to calculate IC50 values for the peptides. All experiments should be conducted three times, with each experiment repeated independently at least three times.


### Reverse transcription quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction
  The mRNA expression levels of CTGF and CYR61 were detected by RT-qPCR, following the method described in the relevant references10. AML-193 cells treated with Peptide-4 at 0.2 and 5 μM were washed twice with PBS, then centrifuged at 13,000 rpm to collect the cell pellets. TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) was added to the pellets, followed by thorough mixing and lysis for total RNA extraction from the cells. The concentration and purity of the isolated RNA were determined subsequently. First-strand cDNA was synthesised from the extracted RNA as the template for real-time PCR using the PrimeScript RT Kit, with all procedures performed strictly in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The synthesised cDNA was stored at −80 °C. Thereafter, PCR amplification was conducted on a PCR instrument using the SYBR Premix Ex Taq Kit and gene-specific primers. The PCR protocol was set as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, annealing at 60 °C for 60 s, and extension at 72 °C for 20 s. The relative mRNA expression levels of target genes were normalised to the internal reference gene GAPDH. The relative mRNA expression was presented as the fold change and calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method. Furthermore, the upstream and downstream primer sequences used for the detection of CTGF and CYR61 are provided in Table S2.


### Prediction of in vitro physicochemical properties
  The amino acid sequence of Peptide-4 was submitted to the Expasy ProtParam platform (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/) for prediction of its physicochemical properties. These properties include: theoretical pI, negative and positive residues, half-life (hours), Aliphatic index, and GRAVY (Grand Average of Hydropathicity) value.


### Statistics analysis
  One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to compare group means, where a p values < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data were presented as mean ± SEM (standard error of the mean), and each experimental condition was replicated a minimum of three times.


## Results and discussion

### Pharmacophore model of TEAD4
  Interaction interface 3 of the YAP-TEAD4 complex was selected for interaction analysis. Residues 85–95 derived from the YAP sequence are crucial for YAP-TEAD4 complex formation, adopting an “Ω”-like conformation. The carbonyl oxygen of YAP residue Phe95 formed a hydrogen bond with the amino group of TEAD4 active pocket residue Lys274. Thus, Phe95 was defined as a hydrogen bond acceptor, and the F1 (Acc) feature was constructed. The primary amine of YAP residue Lys90 formed two and one hydrogen bonds with TEAD4 residues Asp411 and Asp243, respectively. Consequently, Lys90 was designated as the hydrogen bond donor, leading to the construction of the F3 (Don) and F4 (Don) features. Additionally, Lys90 played a key role in maintaining the “Ω”-like conformation. Most residues 85–95 of YAP were hydrophobic amino acids, forming a large internal hydrophobic core, which prompted the construction of the F2 (Hyd) feature. The combination of these pharmacophore features yielded the TEAD4 pharmacophore model. The pharmacophore model of TEAD4 is presented in Figure 1.


### Validation of TEAD4 pharmacophore model
  The ability of the TEAD4 pharmacophore model to identify inhibitors that disrupt the YAP-TEAD4 interaction was validated in our in-house dataset containing 500 compounds. Results for the metrics evaluating the TEAD4 pharmacophore model are presented in Table S3. Pharmacophore model screening identified 8 molecules, including 7 active ones. The results showed that the active molecule yield from model screening was 88%, with an active molecule ratio of 100%. The enrichment factor was calculated to be 63, indicating a strong ability of the model to enrich active molecules and high screening efficiency. Concurrently, the model exhibited 0 false negatives and 1 false positive, demonstrating excellent comprehensiveness and specificity in active molecule identification. A GH score closer to 1 indicates superior predictive performance of the model. The TEAD4 pharmacophore model in this study achieved a GH score of 0.9, demonstrating its excellent predictive capability. Collectively, validation demonstrated that the constructed TEAD4 pharmacophore model could effectively identify YAP-TEAD4 interaction-disrupting inhibitors in the 500-compound test set.


### Virtual screening
  The workflow for virtual screening of peptide inhibitors targeting YAP-TEAD4 interaction disruption is presented in Figure 2. Based on interaction analysis of YAP-TEAD4 complex interface 3, four pharmacophore features were constructed to form the TEAD4 pharmacophore model. Using this model, the established database of 59,319 peptides was subjected to primary screening. In silico screening yielded 126 hit peptides that fulfilled all pharmacophore characteristics. Following docking of the hit peptides into the TEAD4 active pocket, the top four candidates, ranked by docking scores relative to Peptide 10, were chosen for further in vitro assessment. The docking scores for peptides 1–4 and Peptide 10 are presented in Figure 3.


### Interaction analysis
  The optimal binding modes of peptides 1–4 with the TEAD4 active pocket were output during docking (Figure 4). Their interactions were analysed in MOE, focusing on hydrophobic and hydrogen bond interactions. A summary of interactions between peptides 1–4 and key amino acids in the TEAD4 active pocket is presented in Table 1. The peptides 1–4 formed hydrogen bonds with TEAD4 key residues Asp243, Gln246, Asp249, Lys274, Glu368, Glu393, and Asp411, and hydrophobic interactions with TEAD4 active residues Val242, Leu272, and Val391. Compared to peptide-1, peptide-2 established an additional hydrogen bond with TEAD4 residue Lys250. The peptide-3 formed more hydrogen bonds than peptide-2 with TEAD4 residues Gln246, Asp249, Glu240, and Tyr406. In comparison with peptide-3, peptide-4 exhibited one more hydrogen bond each with TEAD4 residues Glu393 and Glu240. The higher number of hydrogen bonds indirectly indicated stronger interactions and more stable binding modes between peptides 1–4 and the active pocket of TEAD4. Thus, these peptides were advanced to the subsequent in vitro validation phase.


### MST analysis
  Given the strong hydrogen bond and hydrophobic interactions between peptides 1–4 and TEAD4 key residues, affinity assays were performed between these peptides and recombinant human TEAD4 protein. The Kd values were determined using the Monolith NT.115 instrument, with lower Kd values. The Kd values of Peptide 10 and peptides 1–4 are presented in Table 2. Furthermore, the binding curves of the peptides are presented in Figure S1. As the positive control, Peptide 10 exhibited the Kd value of 12.16 ± 0.93 nM. Compared to Peptide 10, the screened peptides 1–4 showed lower Kd values and superior affinity. Among these, peptide-4 displayed the lowest Kd value (Kd = 5.08 ± 0.42 nM). Subsequently, peptide-4 was subjected to MD simulations to evaluate its binding stability with TEAD4 under physiological conditions.


### MD simulations
  A 50 ns MD simulation was performed on the optimal binding mode of peptide 4 with TEAD4 to evaluate their binding stability, using RMSD, RMSF, Secondary structure, Rg values and number of hydrogen bonds as evaluation metrics. The RMSD values of the TEAD4-peptide 4 complex reached equilibrium and fluctuated around 0.45 nm (Figure 5(A)). The RMSF values of key amino acid residues in TEAD4 were all below 0.15 nm (Figure 5(B)). In addition, the secondary structure of TEAD4 was mainly composed of Structure and β-sheet structures (Figure 5(C)). The Rg values of TEAD4 fluctuated within the range of 1.7 ∼ 1.8 nm (Figure 5(D)). The formation of six strong hydrogen bond interactions contributed to the conformational stability of the complex (Figure 5(E)). Collectively, these results demonstrated that peptide-4 interacts stably to TEAD under physiological conditions during the 50 ns MD simulation.


### MM/PBSA analysis
  The binding free energies of the TEAD4-peptide-4 complex was calculated using the MM/PBSA method, which is decomposed into four components: van der Waals energy, electrostatic energy, polar solvation energy, and non-polar solvation energy. Herein, the first three energy components served as driving forces for complex formation, whereas the polar solvation energy was unfavourable for binding. The total binding free energies of the TEAD4-peptide-4 complex was −35.89 ± 0.43 kJ/mol, indicating thermodynamic stability of the complex. Additionally, the core driving forces for TEAD4-peptide-4 binding were intermolecular van der Waals and electrostatic interactions.

  Given the thermodynamic stability of the TEAD4-peptide-4 complex, the contribution of TEAD4 active-site residues to binding was subsequently analysed. These results are presented in Figures 5(F,G). The energy components of TEAD4 active-site residues (Gln246, Asp249, Lys274, and Tyr406) stabilise the complex binding (Figure 5(F)). Additionally, the electrostatic energy of residues Gln246, Asp249, Lys274, and Tyr406 makes a major contribution to complex binding. The polar solvation energy of residue Lys250 also supports the stability of the complex (Figure 5(G)). Collectively, these data identify Gln246, Asp249, Lys250, Lys274, and Tyr406 as core binding residues.


### Free energy landscape analysis
  The free energy landscape (FEL) serves as a core analytical tool for characterising the conformational space distribution and stability of protein-peptide complexes. By mapping conformational descriptors (RMSD and Rg) to free energy values, it intuitively illustrates the thermodynamic probability and energy barriers associated with distinct conformations. The free energy of the TEAD4-peptide-4 complex was calculated based on the RMSD and Rg values at corresponding time points. The FEL plots of the TEAD4-peptide-4 complex were presented in Figure 6. The blue region corresponds to the most stable conformational state of the system-lower energy and a more concentrated region indicate higher conformational stability. Conversely, the red region represents the highest energy and least stable conformation of the system. Figures 6(A) (2D contour plot) and 6(B) (3D surface plot) correspond to two distinct visual representations of the identical FEL dataset. A distinct “energy basin” (blue core region) was observed for the TEAD4-peptide-4 complex, indicating stable conformation after their binding.

  Given the optimised conformation of the TEAD4-peptide-4 complex, its interaction profile was visualised using the LigPlot tool. The interaction between TEAD4 and peptide-4 was mainly mediated through hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions. The hydrogen bonds between TEAD4 and peptide-4 ranged from 2.52 to 3.95 Å. These results are consistent with the binding measurement results obtained in vitro.


### MTT assay
  After 72 h, the cytotoxic effects of peptides 1–4 and peptide 10 on AML-193 cells were evaluated by the MTT method. Peptide dose-response curves are presented in Figure S2. Among the tested peptides, the IC50 value of peptide-4 was the lowest, at 0.65 ± 0.04 μM, indicating significant activity in AML-193 cells. The IC50 values of peptide-3, peptide-2 and peptide-1 were 0.78 ± 0.03 μM, 0.94 ± 0.05 μM and 1.16 ± 0.09 μM respectively. The IC50 of the positive control peptide 10 was 1.34 ± 0.11 μM. These results indicate that the activity of all peptides in AML-193 cells was superior to that of the control peptides, among which peptide-4 shows the highest effect under experimental conditions.


### RT-qPCR analysis
  The relative mRNA expression levels of CTGF and CYR61 were determined by RT-qPCR. The results showed that their mRNA expression levels were significantly downregulated in peptide-4-treated AML-193 cells, and the downregulation exhibited a concentration-dependent enhancement with the increase in peptide-4 concentration (Figure 7). Collectively, Peptide-4 could significantly reduce the mRNA expression levels of CTGF and CYR61, the core downstream target genes of the Hippo signalling pathway.


### Physicochemical property analysis
  The physicochemical properties of Peptide-4 were predicted and summarised in Table S4. With a theoretical pI of 12.01, Peptide-4 carried a strong positive charge under physiological conditions, which was conferred by Lys and Arg residues and expected to enhance its membrane permeability. Furthermore, Peptide-4 exhibited excellent stability, as evidenced by an in vitro half-life of 30 h in mammalian reticulocytes. Its aliphatic index of 39 indicated basic thermal stability at physiological temperature (37 °C), while a GRAVY value of −1.110 confirmed favourable hydrophilicity. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Peptide-4 possesses favourable physicochemical properties, supporting its potential as an anti-AML candidate peptide.


## Conclusions
  The peptide inhibitors directly interfering with the YAP-TEAD4 PPI represent the most promising strategy for the treatment of cancer. The peptides 1–4 were screened from the peptide database based on pharmacophore modelling, molecular docking, and interaction analysis. Subsequently, affinity experiments indicated that peptide-4 possessed the lowest Kd value (Kd = 5.08 ± 0.42 nM) for TEAD4 among the Peptide 10 and peptides 1–4. MD simulation demonstrated that peptide-4 stably bound to the TEAD4. Moreover, Gln246, Asp249, Lys250, Lys274, and Tyr406 were identified as core binding residues within the TEAD4 active site. Moreover, Peptide-4 exhibited potent inhibitory activity against AML-193 cells with the IC50 of 0.65 ± 0.04 μM and significantly downregulated the mRNA expression of Hippo signalling pathway downstream genes CTGF and CYR61. In conclusion, peptide-4 may be a promising candidate for anti-AML therapy on modulating YAP-TEAD4 interactions. The core sequence of YAP that interacts with TEAD4 is P85MRLRKLPDSFFKPP99. Compared with Peptide 10, amino acid alterations in Peptide-4 (MRSRKLPNSF) resulted in enhanced binding affinity and therapeutic efficacy. This finding lays a foundation for further in-depth investigations into YAP-TEAD4 interaction-targeted therapy.

  Computational prediction results demonstrated that Peptide-4 exhibited excellent physicochemical properties, indicating that it possesses favourable membrane permeability, solubility and stability (Table S4). Furthermore, these predicted physicochemical characteristics lay a solid foundation for the further optimisation of Peptide-4. Subsequently, in vitro experiments will be conducted to further validate these properties. In vivo evaluations of peptide-4 will be further conducted, encompassing anti-AML efficacy assays, toxicity studies, and pharmacokinetic experiments.
