# Synthetic fluorescent MYC probe: Inhibitor binding site elucidation and development of a high-throughput screening assay


## Abstract
  We report the discovery of a fluorescent small molecule probe. This probe exhibits an emission increase in the presence of the oncoprotein MYC that can be attenuated by a competing inhibitor. Hydrogen–deuterium exchange mass spectrometry analysis, rationalized by induced-fit docking, suggests it binds to the “coiled-coil” region of the leucine zipper domain. Point mutations of this site produced functional MYC constructs resistant to inhibition in an oncogenic transformation assay by compounds that displace the probe. Utilizing this probe, we have developed a high-throughput assay to identify MYC inhibitor scaffolds. Screening of a diversity library (N = 1408, 384-well) and a library of pharmacologically active compounds (N = 1280, 1536-well) yielded molecules with greater drug-like properties than the probe. One lead is a potent inhibitor of oncogenic transformation and is specific for MYC relative to resistant mutants and transformation-inducing oncogenes. This method is simple, inexpensive, and does not require protein modification, DNA binding, or the dimer partner MAX. This assay presents an opportunity for MYC inhibition researchers to discover unique scaffolds.


## Introduction
  The MYC (myelocytomatosis oncogene) family of oncogenes (MYC, MYCN and MYCL) encodes basic helix-loop-helix leucine zipper (bHLH-LZ) transcriptional regulators that are responsible for moving the cell through the restriction point.1 Throughout this paper, we will use “MYC” to indicate the protein product of the c-MYC gene. Its control of mitogenesis requires association with another bHLH-LZ protein, MAX, which exists in the cell in stoichiometric excess to MYC.2,3 MYC and MAX are both intrinsically disordered proteins (IDP) that heterodimerize and bind preferentially to the palindromic E-box motif in DNA to stimulate or promote transcriptional activity.4–6 Dysregulation of MYC family genes/proteins occurs in over half of all human tumors and is recognized as a general hallmark of cancer initiation and maintenance.2,7–10 Its gain of function in cancer is largely a result of overabundance,11 rather than mutation, stemming from changes in gene copy number, transcriptional overexpression, post-translational modification,12,13 and decreased degradation resulting from suppression of ubiquitin E3 ligases.14 Beyond accelerating proliferation, MYC has been implicated in immune evasion by regulating immune checkpoint genes.15 Furthermore, MYC has been found to have unexpected roles in cancers that nominally have a non-MYC etiology.16,17

  MYC is a pleiotropic transcription factor, yet it possesses broad pathogenic prominence making it a coveted cancer target. This MYC dichotomy has produced a forbidding fear that inhibiting a gene that controls essential cellular activities would lead to unacceptable side effects in vivo. Surprisingly, studies involving the systemic inhibition of MYC through conditional expression of a dominant-negative Omomyc allele in tumor-bearing mice have demonstrated dramatic losses of tumor phenotypes in lung adenocarcinomas,16,18 glioblastomas,19 skin papillomatosis,20 and pancreatic tumors.21 This occurs without intolerable side-effects, and those which do occur, are reversible when MYC inhibition is relieved. Furthermore, this absence of toxicity has to a large extent been mirrored by small molecules at therapeutic concentrations.22–26 Thus, while MYC is a pleiotropic transcription factor, its inhibition has had only mild and reversible side effects to normal tissues in adult animals indicating that a therapeutic window is achievable despite global MYC inhibition.

  Even as one of the most established oncoproteins, decades of drug development have failed to produce a clinically viable MYC inhibitor. As a result, indirect methods of modulating MYC dysregulation through epigenetic silencing, manipulating post-translational regulators of MYC, and by sequestering MAX through small molecule promotion of MAX-MAX dimers have been examined.27–29 Although these methods are promising, many of these targets are not MYC-specific, presenting potential off-target effects and resistance pathways.27 The longstanding strategy has been to directly disrupt the protein-protein interaction (PPI) between MYC and MAX to inhibit dimerization or DNA complexation, ultimately interrupting gene transcription. This strategy has the potential for high specificity with low mutational evasion.27 The challenge of this approach is two-fold: 1. IDPs like MYC lack well-defined binding pockets, and small molecule interactions come at a high entropic cost.30–32 2. PPI interfaces are highly dynamic and expansive areas (flat, featureless and relatively large) that are challenging to disrupt with low molecular weight entities.33 Even so, a number of inhibitors capable of interfering with the MYC-MAX dimer have been identified, demonstrating the feasibility of this strategy.22,23,41,42,25,34–40

  To discover small molecule MYC inhibitors, screening platforms have utilized Förster resonance energy transfer,36 fluorescence polarization,22 bimolecular fluorescence/luciferase complementation,37,41 electrophoretic mobility shift43 and yeast two-hybrid34 methodologies. While these platforms have identified compounds, they exhibit a variety of limitations including cost, complexity, and the potential to yield artificial hits by adulterating the structure of the IDP MYC. We have developed and validated a high-throughput screening (HTS) assay seeking to address these limitations. The innovation of the assay stems from its minimalism wherein it does not require covalent modifications, immobilizations, or the binding elements MAX and the DNA E-box, and it employs a simple-to-prepare fluorescent probe, 1. Hydrogen-deuterium exchange mass spectrometry (HDX-MS) analysis, supported by induced-fit modeling and point mutations, suggest that this probe binds a crucial region of the bHLH-LZ. Furthermore, it disrupts MYC-MAX binding to the DNA E-box in surface plasmon resonance (SPR) studies. The probe is derived from the inhibitor chemical scaffold, KJ-Pyr-9, that has been shown to: 1. specifically bind MYC and MYC-MAX with excellent affinity; 2. inhibit MYC-driven oncogenic transformation and MYC-dependent transcriptional regulation; 3. interfere with tumor growth in a MYC-driven model; 4. be specific for MYC relative to the other oncogenic transformation factors Src, Jun, and PI3KCA; 5. revert the transcriptional signature of MYC to that of a tetracycline-induced knockdown in P493–6 cells.22 Although the mechanism of KJ-Pyr-9 inhibition MYC has not been delineated at a structural level, it is likely comprised of both inhibition of dimer formation and disruption of intact MYC-MAX dimer function. These conclusions are drawn from the high affinity exhibited for both MYC and MYC-MAX by KJ-Pyr-9, and the ability to abolish oncogenic transformation at concentrations well below significant disruption of dimer formation.22 While an effective inhibitor, KJ-Pyr-9 lacks the drug-like qualities (ADME/Tox) required for clinical application and the scaffold is not amenable to extensive derivatization without compromising affinity and specificity.44 With this HTS assay, we have screened a diversity library (N = 1408, 384-well) and a library of pharmacologically active compounds (N = 1280, 1536-well). Hits were vetted by surface plasmon resonance (SPR) and cellular assays. The lead, 5, has more favorable predicted drug-like properties than KJ-Pyr-9 and specifically inhibits MYC-driven oncogenic transformation.


## Results

### KJ-Pyr-9 scaffold fluorophore derivatization
  The cyano derivative of the KJ-Pyr-9 scaffold was chemically appended to the fluorophore 7-diethylamino-4-methylcoumarin (Fig. 1A). This conjugate, 1, was incubated at multiple concentrations of MYC before reading at its maximum excitation and emission. Under these conditions, 1 exhibited a dose-dependent emission increase (Fig. S1) consistent with greater fluorophore rigidity and quantum yeild.45 Coumarin derivatives not coupled to the KJ-Pyr-9 scaffold were saturated at three times greater concentrations of MYC with the opposite effect, an emission decrease, suggesting that conjugating the fluorophore to the KJ-Pyr-9 scaffold is required for the emission increase of 1 (Fig. S2). This phenomenon was further evaluated by the addition of established MYC inhibitors KJ-Pyr-9,22 10058-F4,46 and 10074-G539 to displace 1 and attenuate the emission increase (Fig. S3). To limit the influence of independent fluorescence attenuation, control wells without MYC were measured, and their fluorescence values were subtracted from wells with MYC.


### Labeling malleability of Kröhnke pyridine scaffold
  A pulldown study was performed to investigate the ability of the KJ-Pyr-9 scaffold to sequester MYC within a complex cell lysate and to determine if manipulation of the amide would hinder engagement in this context. Both active (2) and inactive (3) biotinylated probes were prepared based on previously reported inhibitors 5b and 5d 44 (Fig. 1A).

  5b has been shown to mirror the binding affinity of KJ-Pyr-9 by field-effect transistors and the cell activity by CEF while 5d was inactive in both experiments.44 Consistent with these findings, 2 exhibited tighter binding to MYC than 3 by bio-layer interferometry (Fig. S4). When implemented into a pulldown experiment, the immobilized inhibitor 2 enriched MYC from P493–6 cell lysates and eluted it when incubated with the original inhibitor (Fig. 1B–C, S5). Additionally, binding affinity (KD) and ability to inhibit MYC-MAX DNA complexation was determined for 1 by previously described SPR methods.22,44 Surprisingly, 1 exhibited a lower equilibrium dissociation constant for MYC than KJ-Pyr-9 (151 ± 25 nM vs. 486 ± 22 nM) (Fig. S6 and Table S1) and a significantly greater capacity to disrupt DNA complexation (Fig. 5A). The greater KD for the MYC/KJ-Pyr-9 interaction relative to previously reported backscattering interferometry value (KD = 6.5 ± 1.0 nM),22 indicates that immobilization of MYC may have resulted in diminished affinity, highlighting the environmental sensitivity of IDPs.


### Inhibitor binding site
  HDX-MS experiments were performed to determine the binding site. Initially, the MYC monomer was evaluated but exchange rates were not altered by the presence of 1. Suspecting that the highly dynamic nature of the IDP MYC obstructed these studies, the MYC-MAX dimer was evaluated, for which 1 exhibits a comparable affinity for (KD = 50 ± 13 nM). These conditions exhibited a reduction in exchange in residues KKATAYIL (c-MYC397–404) of the MYC sequence relative to DMSO (Fig. 2A, B). This site is within the bHLH-LZ motif (c-MYC351–439), a critical interface for MYC-MAX dimerization.30,47–50 The discovery of this binding site may explain the previously documented ability of KJ-Pyr-9 to disrupt MYC-MAX DNA complexation,22,44 along with the even greater capacity of 1 (Fig. 5A). Although the binding sites of 10058-F4 (c-MYC402–410) and 10074-G5 (c-MYC366–375) have been reported,49,50 the displacement of 1 by these inhibitors is consistent with recent studies that have critiqued or refuted the specificity of 10058-F4 51,52 and have instead suggested a nonspecific affinity for hydrophobic regions across the bHLH-LZ motif.31

  Despite the challenges of computational predictions on disordered proteins like MYC, and the lack of monomeric MYC crystal structures, we attempted to rationalize the binding mode of 1 by induced-fit docking to the KKATAYIL region identified by HDX-MS utilizing a crystal structure of the MYC-MAX-DNA complex. Interestingly, a binding ridge was determined just below the leucine zipper region (Fig. 2C). In good agreement with the HDX-MS data, the furan moiety of 1 occupies this site while the remainder of the inhibitor binds in a kinked fashion around the zipper region. We suggest that the accommodation of the cavity below the ion pair of E373 and K398 by an inhibitor distorts the positioning of the linked helix and hence disrupts the DNA binding properties of the dimeric MYC/MAX protein.

  To probe whether these binding residues influence inhibitor efficacy, the region of MYC blocked by the addition of 1, KKATAYIL, was mutated to replace individual residues with those present in MAX, DKATEYIQ. The MAX protein homodimerizes and is only weakly bound by KJ-Pyr-9.22 Therefore, these mutations would likely be innocuous to MYC-MAX dimerization while altering the modeled binding cavity resulting in resistance to inhibition. Consistent with this, the mutants A401E and L404Q were generated with a mutation near the modeled binding cavity of 1 and both induce transformation when overexpressed in CEF as determined by focus assay. While MYC is inhibited to less than 3% of their normal focus forming ability by 5 (compound identified in pilot screen) at 5 μM, MYC-A401E and MYC-L404Q are not and retain more than 75% of the number of foci present in the untreated cells (Fig. 3). Additionally, 5 displayed greater potency than KJ-Pyr-9 and was less prone to the cytopathic effect than 10074-G5. These data demonstrate the dependence of inhibition on these binding residues and provide evidence for the existence of the modeled binding ridge.


### Application of competition assay to small molecule screening
  To identify inhibitors that share a binding region with KJ-Pyr-9, but possess more drug-like properties, a competition assay was employed that utilizes the displacement of 1 to indicate binding. The competition assay described in 2.1 (Fig. 3S) was optimized for application as a high throughput screening assay. In the competition assay solutions of 1 (0.8 μM) in buffer (50 mM MES, pH 6.0, Tween20 0.01%, BSA 0.002%, DMSO 2%) were prepared with MYC (1 μM) and test compound (10 μM). These mixtures were incubated in the dark (1 h, rt) before emission was measured at 470 nm with a 424 nm excitation. The positive control was set as 10074-G5 (10 μM) and the negative control was vehicle (DMSO). To assess the high-throughput screening potential of this competitive assay, a diversity library (N = 1408) was evaluated in triplicate in a 384-well format (Fig. S7–S8). The competitive assay was scaled to 20 μL and plate dispensers and pin tools were utilized to transfer buffer and compounds. Under these conditions, an acceptable average Z′ factor (0.71), hit rate (0.57%) and high plate-to-plate replicate fidelity (r2 > 0.78) were observed. Six molecules were selected for further evaluation.

  With the 384-well assay operational, we miniaturized the assay to a 1536-well format. The conditions were optimized, and a LOPAC (Library of Pharmacologically Active Compounds) pilot screen was run (N = 1280) in triplicate (Fig. 4, Table 2S). Raw assay data was imported into the Scripps’ corporate database and analyzed using Symyx software. A mathematical algorithm was used to determine active compounds. The assay had excellent reproducibility, Z′ factor, and hit rate (1.25%) and identified 16 candidate inhibitors (Fig. 4). In this HTS assay, 10074-G5 exhibited an IC50 of 1.2 μM and showed no evidence of creating artifactual fluorescence. Plate-to-plate reproducibility was excellent with a correlation of r2 > 0.9 and a slope very close to 1 (Fig. S9). A counter-screen assay was implemented to identify compounds acting as nonspecific chromophores, enhancers of fluorescence, or optical interferents. The counter-screen titration assay is similar in format to the MYC inhibitor assay but employs a method wherein fluorescence in the presence of MYC, the probe (1) and the hit compound is subtracted by the fluorescence in the absence of MYC. Compounds considered inactive (i.e. not artifactually increasing fluorescence) were progressed forward for further consideration and IC50 determination.

  Cheminformatics was used to identify promiscuous compounds by analysis of their activity profiles across other primary screens run against the Scripps Drug Discovery Library. Of the 16 hits, 8 had been noted previously to be promiscuous in other Scripps screening assays53 or were known PAINs (pan-assay interference compounds, a prominent source of false positives in the drug discovery process), yielding 8 compounds of interest (Fig. 4B). These molecules were obtained as powders (LC-MS with > 95% purity and appropriate mass) and demonstrated the same activity when tested in the assay.

  Select compounds from both the 384-well and 1536-well screens (N = 14) were evaluated by several orthogonal assays. Utilizing a previously described SPR assay,44 we assessed the hits for their ability to disrupt MYC-MAX DNA binding by SPR. While all compounds exhibited some level of inhibition, three molecules demonstrated greater inhibition than KJ-Pyr-9. Furthermore, predicted ADME properties display higher cell permeability, oral absorption, and solubility for the selected compounds over KJ-Pyr-9 and with fewer rule of three violations (Table S3). Cellular efficacy and specificity were evaluated by previously described CEF oncogenic transformation assays (Fig. S10–S11).22,44 Six of the compounds demonstrated the ability to reduce foci formation at concentrations 10 μM or lower. These inhibitors were evaluated in the CEF assay with other oncoproteins (Src, Jun and PI3K) and one lead, 5, exhibited significant specificity (Fig. 5B, S12). Induced-fit docking of 5 predicts it engages the same binding cavity of 1 (Fig. 2C).


## Discussion and conclusions
  This fluorescence-based competition assay is simple, inexpensive and does not require covalent manipulations of MYC or the addition of binding partners (MAX or DNA). These features are products of the unique fluorescence probe, 1, which is based on the successful MYC inhibitor KJ-Pyr-9. Although 1 is not commercially available, the starting materials are, and the synthetic route is based on simple methods like the Kröhnke pyridine synthesis and amide couplings. Concerns about loss of affinity and specificity were raised by the addition of the fluorophore tag to KJ-Pyr-9. However, the pulldown and enrichment of MYC, direct SPR binding, and inhibition of MYC-MAX DNA complexation experiments, all suggest significant pliability of the amide site. Surprisingly, 1 has a higher binding affinity for MYC than KJ-Pyr-9 (151 ± 25 nM vs. 486 ± 22 nM) (Fig. S6 and Table S1) and a significantly greater capacity to disrupt MYC-MAX DNA complexation. The introduction of an additional binding mode, via the kinked fluorophore tag revealed by docking, may explain this enhanced activity. Linkage at this amide may be a potential route to capitalize on this feature and generate KJ-Pyr-9 scaffolds with more desirable properties.

  HDX-MS analysis utilizing complete bHLH-LZ domains of the MYC-MAX dimer have revealed that 1 interacts with residues KKATAYIL (MYC397–404). Consistent with this discovery, full-length MYC mutants with single site mutations in this region gained resistance to inhibition intracellularly by compounds that displace 1. The binding mode of 1 was rationalized by induced-fit docking around the surrounding residues to reveal an interaction with a shallow ridge. The furan moiety of 1 primarily occupies this site which may explain why previous efforts to derivatize it significantly hindered inhibition capacity.44 Occupation of this ridge has the potential to perturb the positioning of the linked helix and disrupt the DNA binding properties of the MYC-MAX dimer. MYC mutants with alterations near the region gained resistance to MYC inhibition consistent with the HDX-MS results. Excitingly, these resistant mutants may prove to be an excellent means to counter-screen inhibitors for off-target effects in future studies. In sum, these efforts indicate this region is critical to MYC function and our assay presents an opportunity to fully evaluate the chemical space capable of modulating MYC through this site.

  The assay seamlessly miniaturized to 384-well and 1536-well formats with high reproducibility across multiple laboratories and with Z′ factors and hit rates that are typical for pilot HTS efforts. Identified hits were structurally distinct from 1 and possessed favorable predicted drug-like properties. Furthermore, these molecules disrupted MYC-MAX DNA complexation and several inhibited oncogenic transformation in CEFs. Even with the limited sample size (N = 2688), this assay has produced a lead, 5, with cell potency and predicted ADME properties superior to KJ-Pyr-9. Furthermore, induced-fit docking suggests that 5 engages the same binding cavity as 1. 5, also known as SU4312, is an inhibitor of VEGF receptor-2 tyrosine kinase and has been designed as a candidate drug for cancer therapy.54 It has been shown to reduce proliferation of multiple myeloma and leukemia tumor cells in vitro.55 In addition to the known anticancer pathways, our findings suggest that SU4312 efficacy may in part be a product of MYC inhibition. What the exact contribution MYC inhibition makes to this polypharmacology will be elucidated in future studies. Regardless, these insights may guide the synthetic derivatization of SU4312 to generate homologous compounds that capitalize on the potential multitarget synergy.

  In summary, we have prepared a fluorescent probe, 1, based on the breakthrough MYC inhibitor KJ-Pyr-9. This probe exhibits higher affinity and greater capacity to disrupt MYC-MAX DNA complexation than its progenitor. HDX-MS results, consistent with resistant MYC mutants, suggest that 1 binds a critical region of the MYC protein. A fluorescence-based displacement assay has been developed utilizing the emission increase observed when 1 binds MYC. This assay has proven to be robust in both 384-well and 1536-well pilot screens. Several unique scaffolds have been identified and validated by orthogonal assays. The lead, 5, addresses many of the limitations of KJ-Pyr-9, and our results suggest this cancer drug candidate may elicit its anticancer phenotype in part by inhibition of MYC.

### Bio-layer interferometry
  Streptavidin sensors (ForteBio) were equilibrated in buffer consisting of 50 mM MES, pH6.0, 0.1%BSA, 0.02%Tween-20 and 10 mM NaCl (note: NaCl concentration optimized for individual sensor trays) for five minutes, and this buffer was used throughout the experiment. Sensors were then subjected to real-time kinetic analysis (Fig. S4) in the following steps: baseline (60 s), ligand loading (2 or 3, 250 nM, 1% DMSO, 180 s), quenching (biocytin-10 μg/mL, 60 s), baseline (60 s), association (MYC-150–100 nM, 90 s), dissociation (240 s). Kinetic analysis was carried out using ForteBio Analysis 9.0 software provided with the instrument and the data fitted with a 2:1 (heterogenous ligand) model (R2 = 0.99).


### Fluorescence assays

#### Evaluation of fluorophore conjugate
  1 (5 μM) was incubated at varying concentrations of MYC (6.68 μM–1.6 nM, 1:3 dilution) in MES buffer (50 mM MES, pH6.0, 2%DMSO) in black 96-well half-area plates at 21 °C for 1 h before reading at the max excitation and emission. Emission spectra were overlaid to determine if dose-dependent changes were apparent.


#### Coumarin fluorescence comparison
  Dilution series of MYC (2 μM–0.9 nM, 1:3) in buffer (50 mM MES, pH6.0, 2%DMSO) were prepared with and without fluorophore (0.8 μM). Plates were incubated for 1 h at room temperature before measuring emission at the maximum excitation on a SpectraMax i3x (Molecular Devices): 1 (ex: 427 nm), 7-diethylamino-4-methylcoumarin (ex: 375 nm) and 7-amino-4-methylcoumarin (ex: 339 nm). Emission values from wells with fluorophore were subtracted by the respective wells without fluorophore.


#### Competition assay
  Solutions of 1 (0.8 μM) in buffer (50 mM MES, pH6.0, Tween200.01%, BSA0.002%, DMSO2%) were prepared with and without MYC (1 μM). These solutions were plated in 96-well black half-area plates, and inhibitors from DMSO stocks were added and diluted across (1:3 dilution, 100 μM – 45.7 nM) with a final volume of 90 μL. After incubating (1 h, 21 °C, dark), emission (470 nm) was measured after excitation (424 nm). Emission from wells with MYC were subtracted from the respective wells without MYC to reduce independent fluorescence contributions from the inhibitors. IC50 values were determined by fitting the points to a log(inhibitor) vs. response equation in GraphPad Prism: Y=Bottom+(Top−Bottom)1+10(X−log(IC50)] where X is the log(inhibitor, μM), Y is the emission, and Top and Bottom are the Y Plateaus.


#### 384-well HTS
  The competition assay conditions described in 4.4.3. were followed, but well volume was decreased to 20 μL. Buffer was transferred to plates with an auto-dispenser, before compounds were added using a 100nL pintool head affixed to a PerkinElmer FX instrument. A diversity library (N = 1408) was evaluated in triplicate across three plates. Positive (10074-G5) and negative (DMSO) control wells were run on each plate with 16 replicates. Under these conditions, a high average Z′ factor (0.71), hit rate (0.57%), and plate-to-plate replicate fidelity (r2 > 0.88) were observed (Fig. S7–S8). These values were calculated using previously described equations.56 For the data normalization, activity of each compound was calculated on Avg + 3SD cut-off basis using the following equation: %response=100(Testwell−MedianDataWellsMedianHighControl−MedianDataWells) where the “High Control” represents wells containing MYC + 1 + 10074-G5 and the “Data Wells” represent wells containing both test compounds and the “Low Control” which contains MYC + 1 + DMSO.

  A mathematical algorithm was used to determine active compounds. Two values were calculated: 1) the average percent response of all compounds tested for the screen, and 2) three times their standard deviation. The sum of these two values was used as a cutoff parameter, i.e. any compound that exhibited greater percent inhibition than the cutoff parameter (35.5% in the case here) was declared active. Using this cutoff, the diversity assay yielded 6 active compounds (“hits”).


#### 1536-well HTS
  The Scripps Molecular Screening Center recapitulated the assay in 384-well format. This assay was further validated using the dose-response curve of the reference 10074-G5 and then miniaturized into 1536-well format. See Table S2 for the final HTS assay protocol. Raw assay data was imported into Scripps’ corporate database and subsequently analyzed using Symyx software. The normalization of data and determination of cutoff parameter (23.74%) were performed as described in 4.4.4. Using this cutoff, the LOPAC assay yielded 16 active compounds (“hits”). A “promiscuity index” was calculated; the expression “X out of Y” is interpreted as “this compound was a hit in X out of Y total primary screens”. The X value includes hits in the current screen. Compounds with a high X value relative to the Y value can be considered promiscuous. We proceeded with the top 12 hits for the dose-response follow-up titration assays described below. Of those, 8 were immediately available and were finalized for testing accordingly. Additionally, to verify reproducibility, percent response of all the LOPAC triplicate plates were compared as correlation plots that yielded an r2 value of 0.90 (Fig. S9).


#### Titration assays
  The titration assay employed the same reagents, protocols, and detection systems as the competition assay but tested each of the selected/available compounds as 10-point dose-response titrations (3-fold dilutions) in triplicate. The titration assay performance for inhibitors were consistent with an average Z′ of 0.87 ± 0.01 and a S:B of 8.59 ± 0.12. A four-parameter equation describing a sigmoidal dose-response curve was then fitted with adjustable baseline using Assay Explorer software (Symyx Technologies Inc.). The reported IC50 values were generated from fitted curves by solving for the X-intercept value at the 50% inhibition/activation level of the Y-intercept value. The following rule was used to declare a compound as “active” or “inactive”: Compounds with an IC50 greater than 10 μM were considered inactive. Compounds with an IC50 less than 10 μM were considered active.


### Molecular modeling
  Calculations were performed with modules from the Schrödinger Small Molecule Drug Discovery Suite (Maestro), release 2018–3, using the OPLS3 force field for parameterization.57 The X-ray co-crystal structure of the MYC/MAX/DNA complex (PDB 1NKP) was imported from the protein data bank and prepared with the Protein Preparation Wizard using default settings.47,58 Ligands were imported into and prepared using LigPrep.58 Docking was carried out using the Induced Fit Docking module in Glide.59 A receptor box grid center was built 20 Å around the KKATAYIL peptide sequence of MYC (the binding residues derived from HDX-MS) and compounds were docked with Extra Precision Glide redocking and otherwise default settings, with the lowest energy binding mode represented.60 The binding pocket surrounding the binding region was predicted using SiteMap, and predicted chemical properties were calculated in QikProp.61–63 Figures were generated using PyMol Molecular Graphics System (Schrödinger, LLC).


### Hydrogen-deuterium exchange (HDX) deterred by mass spectrometry (MS)
  Differential HDX-MS experiments were conducted as previously described with a few modifications detailed below.64


#### Peptide identification
  Peptides were identified using tandem MS (MS/MS) with an Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Q Exactive Thermo Fisher). Product ion spectra were acquired in data-dependent mode with the top five most abundant ions selected for the product ion analysis per scan event. The MS/MS data files were submitted to Mascot (Matrix Science) for peptide identification. Peptides included in the HDX analysis peptide set had a MASCOT score greater than 20. The MASCOT search was repeated against a decoy (reverse) sequence, and ambiguous identifications were ruled out and not included in the HDX peptide set.


#### HDX-MS analysis
  MYC-MAX dimer (10 μM) was incubated (1 h, rt) with the respective ligands at a 1:10 protein-to-ligand molar ratio. Next, 5 μL of sample was diluted into 20 μL D2O buffer (50 mM MES, pH7.4; 28 mM NaCl) and incubated for various time points (0, 10, 60, 300, 900, and 3600 s) at 4 °C. The deuterium exchange was then slowed by mixing with 25 μL of cold (4 °C) 0.1 M Sodium Phosphate, 50 mM TCEP and 1% trifluoroacetic acid. Quenched samples were immediately injected into the HDX platform. Upon injection, samples were passed through an immobilized pepsin column (2 mm × 2 cm) at 200 μL min−1, and the digested peptides were captured on a 2 mm × 1 cm C8 trap column (Agilent) and desalted. Peptides were separated across a 2.1 mm × 5 cm C18 column (1.9 μL Hypersil Gold, Thermo Fisher) with a linear gradient of 4–40% MeCN and 0.3% formic acid, over 5 min. Sample handling, protein digestion and peptide separation were conducted at 4 °C. Mass spectrometric data were acquired using an Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Exactive, Thermo Fisher). The intensity weighted mean m/z centroid value of each peptide envelope was calculated and subsequently converted into a percentage of deuterium incorporation. This is accomplished determining the observed averages of the non-deuterated and fully deuterated spectra and using the conventional formula.65 Corrections for back-exchange were made based on an estimated 70% deuterium recovery and accounting for the known 80% deuterium content of the deuterium exchange buffer. HDX analyses were performed in triplicate, with single preparations of each purified protein/complex. Statistical significance for the differential HDX-MS data was determined by t-test for each time point and was integrated into the HDX-MS Workbench software.66 Peptide uptake curves for all measured peptides are included in the supporting information (Fig. S13–S14)


#### Data rendering
  The HDX data from all overlapping peptides were consolidated to individual amino acid values using a residue averaging approach. Briefly, for each residue, the deuterium incorporation values and peptide lengths from all overlapping peptides were assembled. A weighting function was applied in which shorter peptides were weighted more heavily and longer peptides were weighted less. Each of the weighted deuterium incorporation values were then averaged to produce a single value for each amino acid. The initial two residues of each peptide, as well as prolines, were omitted from the calculations. This approach is similar to that previously described.67


### Recombinant protein expression and purification
  His-tagged MYC, MAX, MYC-MAX, and MAX-MAX constructs were expressed in BL21(DE3) E. coli cells and purified as previously described.22


### Biotinylated pulldown and enrichment
  Cell preparation. P493–6 cells were cultured as previously described.68,69 After reaching a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL, cultures were transferred to tubes (1 mL/tube) and pelleted by centrifugation (2500g, 10 min). The pellets were rinsed with HEPES buffer (30 mM HEPES, 50 mM NaCl, 0.005%Tween20, 3 mM EDTA, pH7.4) and centrifuged to remove supernatant (2500g, 10 min). Proteins were extracted from pellets (10 μL) according to the protocol in the M-PER instructions (Thermo Fisher, #78501).


#### Resin preparation
  Pierce high capacity streptavidin agarose (200 μL) was rinsed with MES buffer (600 μL, 50 mM MES, pH6.0) three times before incubation (12 h, rt) with the biotinylated compound (60 nmol, 2 or 3) in 5% DMSO MES buffer (600 μL). The resins were then rinsed with MES as described above to remove unbound compound.


#### Pulldown
  Extracted cell lysate (100 μL) and purified MYC (100 μL, 1.5 μM) were added to the ligand-bound resins and gently mixed (24 h, rt). The mixtures were centrifuged (500g, 2 min, rt) to remove supernatant and the resin was washed three times with MES buffer (600 μL, 15 min, rt) and once with 4% DMSO MES buffer (600 μL, 15 min, rt). The respective competitor (90 nmol, 5b or 5d) was added in MES buffer (600 μL, 3% DMSO) and gently mixed (3 h, rt). The supernatant was isolated by centrifugation (500g, 2 min, rt). All washes and elutions were evaluated by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis with Bolt LDS sample buffer, Bolt premade 17-well gel (4–12%Bis-Tris Plus), and MES SDS running buffer. Gels were visualized by silver stain (SilverSNAP, #24602, Pierce) or western blot (c-Myc 9E10, Horseradish peroxidase, Super Signal West Dura, iBlot 2 NC mini).


#### Quantification
  MYC enrichment was visualized by western blot and quantified relative to lysate by ImageJ to determine the percent retained by the resin.


### Chicken embryo fibroblasts oncogenic transformation assay
  Oncogenic transformation was determined in cultures of CEFs as previously described.70,71 CEF were infected with a series of 10-fold dilutions of the indicated virus. Compounds were added to the nutrient agar overlay. Additional compound-containing overlay was added every three days until experimental end point. ATG-MYC has an upstream non-canonical start site mutated to ATG and was constructed as reported previously.72 The MYC and PIK3CA oncogenes are human in origin while the Src and Jun oncogenes originated in chickens. Transformation with Src is achieved by infection with Rous Sarcoma Virus (Strain PRA) and transformation with Jun is achieved by infection with Avian Sarcoma Virus 17.


### Inhibitor resistant MYC mutants

#### Focus assays
  CEFs were seeded and infected with RCAS(A)-ATG-MYC as previously described.22 Compound treatment was performed with compounds dissolved in DMSO at 50 mM and diluted to 1000x of final concentration before addition to cell culture media. The A401E and L404Q mutants of ATG-MYC in RCAS(A) were prepared by site-directed mutagenesis. Focus assays using these mutants were conducted identically to those involving RCAS(A)-ATG-MYC.


#### Agar colonies
  CEFs were seeded into 100 mm Petri dishes, 2.5 million cells per plate, and infected with the indicated viruses at an multiplicity of infection of 1.0. Cells were passaged three times over one week to allow all cells in culture to become infected. Soft agar colonies were prepared using a base layer of DC3 containing a final concentration of 0.6% Sea Plaque Agarose (SPA) and then overlaid with media containing 0.3% SPA. The soft agar media contains 500 mL F-10 cell culture media (Sigma), 62.5 mL calf serum (Omega Scientific), 25 mL chicken serum (Sigma), 6.25 mL MEM vitamins (Gibco), 18 μM folic acid, 100U Penicillin, 100 μg Streptomycin, 2 mM Glutamine and 0.5%DMSO. The concentration of cells was adjusted to yield approximately 100 colonies per well, and effectiveness of drug treatment was evaluated as the reduction in colony formation vs untreated colonies.


#### Chicken cell culture
  UMNSAH/DF-1 cells, immortalized chicken cells,73 were infected with RCAS(A) virus containing ATG-MYC WT, A401E and L404Q mutations. Cells were passaged three times during which the virus infected the cells. From these infected cells, total RNA was extracted using a Promega Maxwell RSC machine using SimplyRNA Cells kits. The sequence of the region of interest was sequenced by cDNA synthesis using Thermo Fisher high-capacity cDNA kit using random priming followed by amplification using PCR with Phusion polymerase hot start (NEB) and Sanger sequencing (Fig. S15).


### Surface plasmon resonance
  Inhibition of DNA binding experiments were conducted as previously described.44 The kinetic analyses for the interaction of small molecular compounds, KJ-Pyr-9 and 1, with MYC monomer and MYC-MAX dimer proteins were performed on a Biacore 8 K instrument (GE Healthcare Life Sciences) using Series S Sensor Chip CM5 (GE Healthcare Life Sciences, # 29149603). All kinetic analyses were performed at or below of 25 °C using 1x PBS-P + buffer (GE Healthcare Life Sciences, #28995084) supplemented with 1% DMSO as running buffer. All analyses were running at a flow rate of 30 mL/min with DMSO solvent correction and reference and blank subtractions. In brief, MYC monomer protein and MYC-MAX dimer proteins were immobilized onto the CM5 sensor chip surface via amine-coupling methodology aimed at a ligand density around 1,000 and 1,400RUs, respectively. A Biacore 8 K built-in LMW single-cycle kinetics protocol was used for kinetics determination. The contact time was set as 120 s for each concentration, and the entire dissociation time was set as 3600 s. There were 5 concentrations per compound per cycle, each tested compound had its own blank buffer injection as reference. Compound KJ-Pyr-9 was made into stock solution of 30 mM using 100% DMSO, while 1 was made into stock solution of 10 mM using 100% DMSO. Each of the compounds were diluted using 1x PBS-P + buffer with 1% DMSO to get working concentrations of KJ-Pyr-9 compound as 0.625, 1.250, 2.500, 5.000 and 10.000 μM and that of 1 as 0.123, 0.370, 1.111, 3.333, and 10.000 μM. The equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) values were calculated using Biacore Insight Evaluation software (ver. 3.0.11.15423) following the two-state reaction model with global fit parameters for reference, blank and solvent-corrected sensorgrams (Table S1). The two-state reaction model describes a 1:1 binding of analyte to immobilized ligand followed by a conformational or other change that stabilizes the complex and follows the following equation: A+B⇋AB⇋AB*
