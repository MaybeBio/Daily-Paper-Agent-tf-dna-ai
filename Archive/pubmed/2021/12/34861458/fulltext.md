# Identification of cellular proteins associated with Human Cytomegalovirus (HCMV) DNA replication suggests novel cellular and viral interactions


## Abstract
  Upon entry of Human cytomegalovirus (HCMV) into the host cell, the viral genome is transported to the nucleus where it serves as a template for transcription and genome replication. Production of new viral genomes is a coordinated effort between viral and cellular proteins. While the core replication proteins are encoded by the virus, additional cellular proteins support the process of genome synthesis. We used accelerated native isolation of proteins on nascent DNA (aniPOND) to study protein dynamics on nascent viral DNA during HCMV infection. Using this method, we identified specific viral and cellular proteins that are associated with nascent viral DNA. These included transcription factors, transcriptional regulators, DNA damage and repair factors, and chromatin remodeling complexes. The association of these identified proteins with viral DNA was confirmed by immunofluorescent imaging, chromatin-immunoprecipitation analyses, and shRNA knockdown experiments. These data provide evidence for the requirement of cellular factors involved in HCMV replication.


## Introduction
  The initial entry steps of HCMV into the host cell leads to the transport of the viral genome into the nucleus where transcription and genome replication occur (1). The HCMV genome, composed of double stranded (ds) DNA, is one of the largest known viral genomes (~230 kbp) (2). The incoming viral genome is used for de novo transcription for viral protein synthesis, and it is also the template to generate progeny genomes. HCMV genes are expressed in a temporal cascade of transcripts designated as immediate-early (IE), early (E), and late (L) (3). IE and E transcripts produce proteins that are essential for viral transcription and viral DNA synthesis, including the core replication machinery. Although IE genes are expressed shortly after an infection, DNA replication does not occur until approximately 24 hours post-infection (hpi) (4).

  In a unique manner, large DNA virus families (e.g. Herpesviridae, Adenoviridae, and Poxviridae) encode many of their required replication factors (5). Specifically, HCMV encodes six core viral replication proteins analogous to cellular replication factors: UL54 (DNA polymerase), UL44 (DNA polymerase processivity factor), UL70 (primase), UL105 (helicase), UL102 (primase-associated factor), and UL57 (single-stranded DNA-binding protein) (4). The core complex is recruited to the site of origin of lytic replication (oriLyt) within the HCMV genome via viral initiation factors UL84 and IE2 (6). While HCMV encodes the essential factors necessary for viral replication, it is dependent on host factors to successfully complete viral replication.

  HCMV and host interactions have been widely studied, but a comprehensive analysis identifying cellular proteins that contribute to and are required for synthesis of HCMV DNA is incomplete. To address this, we used the innovative technique termed accelerated native isolation of proteins on nascent DNA (aniPOND) (7,8). Similar experiments have been successfully applied to herpes simplex virus (HSV-1), Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), adenovirus (Ad5), and vaccinia virus (VACV) (9-13). Results from these studies yielded valuable information about cellular factors involved in viral genome maintenance, RNA transcription, and genome synthesis.

  These methods utilize nucleoside analogs to label replicating viral DNA. The commonly used analogues are 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxycytidine (EdC) and 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU). In our previous work, we investigated the labeling efficiency of HCMV viral DNA between EdC compared to EdU and identified EdU as the preferred analog, and also optimized the protocol to increase the enrichment of viral DNA while reducing isolation of cellular DNA (14). These nucleoside analogs harbor an alkyne group which is readily available for conjugation to a fluorophore or biotin azide molecule using copper-catalyzed click chemistry for azide-alkyne cycloaddition. After coupling the EdU labeled DNA to biotin, the nascent DNA and its associating proteins are isolated by streptavidin bead capture. Isolated proteins are then identified using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) and further tested by western blot analysis, immunofluorescent imaging (IFA), or chromatin-immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay.

  In vitro transient replication assays were previously used to identify proteins involved in HCMV DNA synthesis (15). However, most of these studies selectively identify viral proteins and cannot easily be used to resolve associated cellular factors. To comprehensively identify viral and cellular proteins associated with viral DNA replication, we applied the aniPOND method to both a laboratory-adapted strain of HCMV (AD169) and a low-passage isolate (TB40E). The use of the TB40E isolate is important because AD169 contains large genomic deletions due to extensive passage in cell culture (16). It has also been discovered that TB40E can replicate in the absence of the known initiation factor UL84, which is essential for replication in AD169 (17). Acknowledging the potential differences in viral and cellular protein-DNA binding partners between strains, we performed the aniPOND method in both AD169 and TB40E.

  Our aniPOND studies identified a cumulation of cellular proteins associated with nascent viral DNA during early time points of infection. These proteins were affiliated with multiple cellular complexes involved in DNA replication, transcription, RNA processing, DNA damage and repair responses, histones and chromatin remodeling, nuclear factors, and cell cycle control. While the focus of this study was to identify previously unknown cellular factors which participate in viral replication, we did detect viral proteins including some of the core replication proteins and initiation factors.


## Methods

### Cell and Viruses
  Human foreskin fibroblasts (HF) and telomerized human fibroblasts (T-HF) cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Media (DMEM) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS). The generation of HF and T-HF cells have previously been described (6). Cells were grown at 37°C and 5% CO2. HCMV strains AD169 and TB40E were propagated and titrated on HF cells. After 6-14 days post-infection, cells were harvested and freeze-thawed three times to release the virus. The lysate was centrifuged at 4,000 RPM for 10 minutes, cell-free supernatant containing virus was collected and viral titer was calculated using standard plaque assay. Viral stocks were stored at −80°C.


### Rate of Replication
  To calculate the rate of HCMV DNA synthesis, HF cells were infected with strains AD169 and TB40E and harvested at 0, 12, 24, 36, 48, 60, and 72 hpi. Total DNA was extracted using lysis buffer (2% sodium dodecyl sulfate, 10mM Tris (pH 7.4), and 10 mM EDTA). DNA was then isolated and purified by incubating with Proteinase K (US Biological) for one hour at 65°C followed by phenol:chloroform extraction. The DNA was precipitated with sodium acetate and ethanol, followed by centrifugation at 13,000 RPM for 10 minutes at 4°C. Ethanol was removed and DNA pellets were washed with 70% ethanol. The residual ethanol was removed and DNA pellets were resuspended in Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 8.0). Isolated DNA was then analyzed by qPCR using SsoAdvanced Universal Probes Supermix (Bio-Rad) with the following cycling conditions: 1 cycle of 95 °C for 3 min, and 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 30s. Primers and probes used to detect specific gene expression were: 7SK Forward, 5’-TGACTACCCTACGTTCTCCTAC-3’; 7SK Reverse, 5’-GTCAAGGGTATACGAGTAGCTG -3’; 7SK Probe, 56-FAM/CCCTGCTAG/ZEN/AACCTCCAAACAAGCT/3IABkFQ/; UL86 Forwards, 5’-TGCGTAAAGTCGAAGAAGGG -3’; UL86 Reverse, 5’-CGCACGGTGAACGAAATAAAG -3’; and UL86 Probe, 56-FAM/CAAGGTGGG/ZEN/CAACATCACGCTCTA/3IABkFQ/. Standard curves were generated using purified AD169 and TB40E DNA. The measurements of cellular 7SK was used to confirm the amount of cells harvests at each time for each replication. The rate of viral DNA synthesis (bp/min) was calculated with the following equation (18); 230,000bp∕genomeduration(min)log2(finalnumberofgenomes)−log2(initialnumberofgenomes)


### aniPOND
  The aniPOND method was optimized from previous protocols (10)(7)(19). A super-confluent monolayer (5 days post-plating) of 12x107 HF cells were infected with AD169 or TB40E (MOI=4). After 2 dpi, infected cells were pulse-labeled with 10 μM EdU at 37°C for 30 minutes. After EdU labeling, cells were washed once with cold 1x PBS and incubated with cold Nuclei Extraction Buffer (NEB) (20 mM Hepes, pH 7.2, 50 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 300 mM sucrose, and 0.5% IGEPAL CA630) for 20 minutes on ice. Cells were scraped and centrifuged at 800 RCF for 10 minutes at 4°C. Nuclei pellets were washed with ice-cold 1x PBS and centrifuged. After PBS wash, nuclei were resuspended in 10 mL of click reaction plus 1/100 of protease inhibitor (Millipore Sigma) for 30 minutes at room temperature while rotating. The Click reaction was prepared using the Click-iT Cell Buffer Kit (Invitrogen) and 5 μM biotin azide (PEG4 carboxamide-6-azidohexanyl Biotin) (Invitrogen). Negative control samples were incubated with 1x PBS and protease inhibitors in place of click reaction cocktail. After the click reaction, pellets were centrifuged and washed with ice-cold 1x PBS followed by centrifugation at 800 RCF for 10 minutes at 4°C and aspiration of PBS. Nuclei pellets were then freeze-thawed by placing samples at −80°C for five minutes and then thawing on ice. The pellets were resuspended in 500 μL Buffer B1 (25 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, and 1% IGEPAL CA630) for 20 minutes on ice to relax chromatin. Chromatin was sheared using a probe sonicator at 25 amp for 30 minutes, 10 seconds on and 10 seconds off on ice. After sonication, sample volume was brought up to 1mL with Buffer B1 and 20 μL was saved for input. Dynabeads MyOne Streptavidin T1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were prepared by washing beads once and blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in 1x PBS and sheared salmon sperm DNA (Thermo Fisher) for one hour at 4°C while rotating. After blocking, beads were washed once with Buffer B1 and resuspended in the starting volume of Buffer B1. Streptavidin beads (250 μL per sample) were then added to sonicated samples and rotated for four hours at 4°C. The lysate was removed and beads were washed three times with Buffer B1 by rotating for 10 minutes each wash at room temperature. Biotin interaction was disrupted and protein was denatured by resuspending beads in Laemmli buffer with β-mercaptoethanol followed by boiling for 5 minutes.


### Immunoblotting
  Viral and cellular proteins were captured and isolated using the aniPOND protocol. Samples were denatured in Laemmli buffer with β-mercaptoethanol, resolved by SDS-PAGE (Bio-Rad) and transferred to a PVDF membrane (Bio-Rad). The membrane was blocked for 10 minutes with 5% BSA in TBST (TBS, 0.01% Tween 20). Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4°C while rocking. Antibodies used were diluted in blocking buffer; UL44 1:1000 (Fitzgerald, cat#10-C50I), H2B 1:2000 (Abcam, cat# 52484), followed by secondary antibody for detection IRDye 680 anti-mouse Ab 1:10,000 (LI-COR, #926-68070).


### Mass Spectrometry and Data Analysis
  Proteins isolated from aniPOND samples were resolved on an Any kD Mini-Protean TGX protein gel (Bio-Rad) for 6 minutes at 165 volts until all the sample was embedded into the gel. The protein gel was then stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue and the protein band was excised. The aniPOND LC-MS was performed by the Mick Hitchcock Ph.D., Nevada Proteomics Center at the University of Nevada, Reno. After LC-MS, the proteome software Scaffold 4 was used to sort peptide counts. The proteomic data were analyzed with a false discovery rate (FDR) of 0.8% with a minimum peptide count of two. For the pie chart data, proteins with a peptide count of at least a three-fold increase as compared to no click control were grouped among the labeled categories. For the heat maps and their associated supplemental tables, proteins with a peptide count of at least a three-fold increase were considered significant and reported.


### Immunofluorescent Assay (IFA)
  To visualize protein localization with EdU-labeled nascent DNA, HF cells were plated onto coverslips and grown to super-confluency. HF cells were infected with AD169 (non-GFP tagged). After 2-5 days post-infection, nascent DNA was labeled by the addition of 10 μM EdU in the culture media for 30 minutes. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized using 0.5% Triton-100. All washes were performed with 1% or 3% BSA in 1x PBS. EdU labeled DNA was conjugated to Alexa Fluor 594 via click chemistry following manufacturers protocol using the Click-It Plus Alexa Fluor 594 imaging kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, ca# C10693). After click chemistry, cells were blocked with either 3% BSA or 3% goat serum in 1x PBS for 30 minutes at room temperature. Antibodies used to detect protein are as follows; UL84 1:1000 (20), IE1 1:2500(21), UL44 1:500 (Fitzgerald, 10-C50I), UL57 1:500 (Virusys, P1209), IE2 1:1000 (Millipore, MAB8140), UL112/113 1:750 (22), H1 1:50 (Santa Cruz, AE-4) H2A 1:200 (Abcam, ab18255), TOP2β 1:100 (Santa Cruz, H286), RBBP4 1:300 (Sigma, R3779), RNA POL II 1:500 (Abcam, ab5095), PARP 1:200 (Cell Signaling, 9542), PCNA 1: 1000 (Abcam, ab29), and MCM3 1:500 (Abcam, ab4460). Fixed cells were incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4°C while rocking. The next day, cells were washed with BSA in 1x PBS and incubated with diluted secondary antibody for 30 minutes at room temperature while rocking. Secondary antibodies used were; Alexa Fluor 594 anti-mouse 1:2000 (Invitrogen, #A21201) and Alexa Fluor 488 anti-mouse 1:2000 (Invitrogen, #A11029). Coverslips were mounted onto glass slides with ProLong Gold Antifade with DAPI (Invitrogen). Cells were imaged at 40X or 63X magnification.


### Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)
  Super-confluent HF cells were infected with AD169 WT (MOI=4). After 2 dpi, cell lysates were harvested and the ChIP was performed using the Diagenode iDeal ChiP-seq kit for Transcription Factors according to manufacturer’s protocol (Diagenode, cat. C01010055). Immunoprecipitation reactions were incubated overnight with IgG, H2A (Abcam, ab18255), PCNA (Abcam, ab29), and RBBP4 (Sigma, R3779) antibodies. Purified DNA was quantified by qPCR analysis using primers and probes specific to UL86 and 7SK as described above.


### shRNA knockdown
  PCNA knockdown was performed using PCNA pSi-mU6 plasmid (Genecopoeia #HSH012420-mU6, NM_002592.2). 2x10^6 HF cells were transfected with 5 ug of either PCNA and scramble pSi-mU6 plasmids, using TurboFectin (OriGene), 24 hrs and 4 hrs before infection. Cells were infected with AD169 (MOI=4). DNA and protein were harvested at 18 hours, 2 and 5 dpi using SureSelect RNA/DNA/Protein isolation kit (Thermo Fisher). Enrichment of viral DNA accumulation was measured by qPCR using primers and probes to UL84 (IDT, FWD primer: 5’-GAAAGAAGAGGACGAGGATGAC-3’, Rev primer: 5’-GTGCGGGAAAGAGAGAGAAG-3’, probe: /56-FAM/TCTAACGAG/ZEN/GATTCT GACGTGCGC/3IABkFQ/) and normalized using cellular 7SK (described above). Protein was used for western blots using immunoblotting and antibodies described above. Experiments were performed in triplicate and Student's t-test was used to calculate statistical significance.

  MCM3 knockdown was performed using TRIPZ doxycycline-inducible shRNA lentivirus (GE Dharmacon, V2THF-151632). To create MCM3-shRNA inducible cell lines, shRNA lentivirus was produced by transfecting HEK293 cells with a plasmid for doxycycline-inducible shRNA MCM3 lentivirus along with lentivirus packaging mix and transfection reagent supplied by the manufacturer. HEK293 cells were incubated for 72 hours before the lentivirus was isolated and concentrated using the Lenti-X concentrator (Clontech) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The purified lentivirus was used to transduce T-HF cells, 48 hours after transduction the T-HF cells were selected with 0.5 ug/ml of Puromycin (Invivogen). The puromycin containing media was refreshed every three days and colonies of puromycin resistant cells were allowed to grow out for 10-12 days before being trypsinized and re-plated into new tissue culture flasks. Once the cells had grown into a confluent T-25 the cells were screened for the expression of dsRED after induction with 2 ug/ml doxycycline, and the cells that had detectable levels of dsRED were used for western blot analysis of MCM3 levels and subsequent experiments. For infection experiments puromycin was removed from the media of the MCM3-shRNA inducible cell lines. Approximately 2x10^6 cells were infected with AD169 (MOI=4), cells were treated with doxycycline 24 hrs before infection, DNA and protein were harvested for qPCR and western blot as described above.


## Results

### Determining the rate of HCMV DNA synthesis
  Enriching viral DNA poses a challenge since EdU can also be incorporated into replicating cellular DNA. This issue was addressed by optimizing the EdU labeling protocol using primary cells, which are sensitive to contact inhibition. HCMV has broad cellular tropism and human fibroblasts (HF) support lytic replication. To halt cellular proliferation, HF cells are grown to a confluent state at which time they undergo arrest due to contact inhibition (23). In our previous study, we performed Fast and Efficient Nascent DNA Isolation (FENDI) followed by qPCR to verify that enriched EdU-labeled DNA is primarily viral and not cellular DNA (14). Additionally, we reported that cellular EdU incorporation is completely diminished by 7-8 days post-plating of HF cells and refer to this as a super-confluent state. We also demonstrated that there is no EdU-related toxicity within the short time pulses used in the aniPOND protocol.

  To accurately determine the resolution window of nascent DNA during the EdU pulsing experiments, we calculated the rate of DNA synthesis in both AD169 and TB40E strains following infection (MOI=0.5) of HF cells (Fig 1A and 1B). To reduce the probability that a cell may receive more than one virion and subsequently start replication with more than one template genome, a lower MOI was used for the experiments to calculate the rate of viral DNA synthesis. Most of the additional experiments in this study were performed at a higher MOI (MOI=4) to ensure that the majority (>90%) of the cells were infected. In cells that are infected by more than one virion, and hence have multiple template genomes utilizing finite cellular resources, there may be the potential for slightly different rate of nucleotide incorporation than with a single starting viral genome. The use of MOI=0.5 ensures that most of the cell infected will only be infected by one virion and will represent a single genome at the beginning of viral DNA synthesis. In order to calculate the change in genome copy number between time points and thus the rate of nucleotide incorporation, a standard curve of HCMV genome copy number was generated using purified HCMV BAC DNA and qPCR was performed using primers and probes specific for HCMV UL86 to measure the copy number of viral DNA at each time point. The rate of nucleotide incorporation was calculated as base pairs (bp) synthesized per minute by dividing the total HCMV genome (230,000 bp) by the difference between log2 of the final genome copy number and log2 of the starting genome copy number.

  Between 0-24 hpi, viral DNA accumulation did not increase. By 36-48 hpi, the rate of DNA synthesis sharply increased; AD169 is ~829 bp/min and TB40E is ~228 bp/min. For TB40E, the rate of DNA synthesis and nucleotide incorporation is slower and did not reach its highest rate of nucleotide incorporation until 60 hrs at ~499 bp/min. In cell culture, one study reported that TB40E has a delay of infection due to less efficient transport of TB40E viral particles compared to AD169 (24). This suggests that there may be a slightly slower rate or a lag in replication kinetics for TB40E compared to AD169. Based on the rate of viral DNA replication, we calculated the amount of labeled viral genome after adding EdU to the growth media for 30 minutes at 48 hpi. For AD169, a 30-minute pulse with EdU results in approximately 24,879 bp or 10% of the genome labeled, and for TB40E about 7,710 bp or 4% of the genome is labeled.

  EdU labeling and addition of fluorescent molecules by click chemistry can also be utilized as a molecular tool to visualize nascent DNA by fluorescence microscopy. In HCMV infected cells, newly synthesized viral DNA was pulse-labeled with EdU followed by a click reaction to covalently link the alkyne containing EdU to an azide containing fluorescent dye (Alexa Fluor 594). We compared EdU incorporation in HCMV infected HF cells (MOI=4) at 48 hpi between AD169 and TB40E by fluorescence microscopy. Infected and mock-infected cells were pulsed with 10 μM EdU by adding the nucleoside analog to pre-warmed growth media for 15, 30, 45, or 60 minutes as indicated (Fig 1C and 1D). EdU labeled genomes were detectable after a 15 minutes pulse. The fluorescent signal was comparable between TB40E and AD169. After 30 minutes, the EdU signal increased and replication compartments became visible for both strains. There was no detectable EdU incorporation observed in mock-infected cells indicating no cellular DNA synthesis.


### aniPOND optimization and detection of proteins
  To optimize pulse-labeling techniques using EdU, the onset and accumulation of viral DNA during infection was visually observed between 0-72 hours post-infection. Super-confluent HF cells were infected with AD169 (MOI=4) from 0-72 hours, starting at 12 hpi cells were incubated with EdU (10 μM for 30 minutes) to pulse label nascent DNA and this continued at 12-hour intervals until 72 hpi (Fig 2A). There was no EdU incorporation in non-infected cells, confirming that nascent DNA labeled in the infected cells is almost exclusively viral DNA. From 0-24 hpi, there was no visually detectable EdU incorporation in the infected cells. This is expected since HCMV DNA synthesis does not begin until approximately 24 hpi. By 36-48 hpi, distinct replication compartments containing EdU-labeled DNA within the host nucleus were observed. Viral replication compartments are necessary for concentrating viral proteins and nucleic acids for efficient replication and assembly (25). These compartments are amorphous and occupy large portions of the nucleus throughout later times of infection (60 hpi and 72 hpi). Based on these results, it was determined that pulsing experiments for performing aniPOND were sufficient using a 30-minute EdU pulsing period at 48 hpi. Although, the rate of nucleotide incorporation for TB40E at 48 hpi was not at the peak (that was noted closer to 60 hpi, Fig 1), the 48 hpi was used for both AD169 and TB40E to limit the variables when comparing the aniPOND of AD169 and TB40E. While using 48 hpi for AD169 and 60 hpi for TB40E would have been the peak of DNA synthesis for both strains, there are differences for cellular factors in response to the initial infection, which may be present or not present at 48 versus 60 hpi, potentially making the comparison less accurate, for those reasons we opted to keep the time point the same for AD169 and TB40E aniPOND.

  A challenge with the aniPOND method is isolating sufficient EdU labeled DNA to detect an appreciable amount of protein. To overcome this, the aniPOND experiments were performed using 12 X 10^7 super-confluent HF cells. Cells were infected (MOI=4) for 48 hpi before pulse labeling for 30 minutes with 10 μM EdU. We confirmed that enrichment and purification of EdU labeled DNA was suitable for downstream LC-MS analysis by first using the aniPOND protocol followed by western blot analysis (Fig 2B). Our controls are defined as “no click”, in which the cells have been pulsed with EdU but there was no azide coupled biotin added to the click reaction, which is necessary to react with the terminal alkyne of EdU forming a triazole linkage between the EdU and biotin. In the western blot, we detected the viral DNA polymerase processivity factor UL44 and the cellular histone H2B in the “click” samples in which biotin was added to the EdU labeled DNA followed by isolation of complexes using streptavidin beads (Fig 2B, “+beads”). In the samples with “no click”, there was no significant enrichment of UL44 or H2B on the streptavidin beads.


### Enrichment and identifying cellular proteins on nascent viral DNA using aniPOND
  We then proceeded with the aniPOND LC-MS infection experiments to identify proteins associated with newly synthesized viral DNA. In these experiments, duplicate sample sets were infected with two different isolates of HCMV, AD169 and TB40E. The reasoning for using two different viruses was to potentially identify differences between HCMV strains, as well as increase the overall rigor for any proteins identified in both AD169 and TB40E infected cells. Infection for aniPOND was performed and harvested at 48 hpi followed by LC-MS, the proteomic data were analyzed using Scaffold 4 software with a false discovery rate (FDR) of 0.8 with a minimum peptide count of two and a three-fold increase as compared to the “no-click” control. Raw data from the LC-MS aniPOND can be found in supplemental Table 1 (S1 Table). The proteins identified in the aniPOND proteome were similar between both AD169 and TB40E, and no appreciable differences were noted between the strains. A pie chart is used to summarize these protein hits by identity and function (Fig 3). Categorizing proteomic data can be unwieldy due to the multifunctional properties of proteins. Since many proteins do not belong to a single defined group, they were sorted pertaining to their most highly associated category. Of the 279 proteins they were grouped into RNA processing (117), nuclear structure/organization (25), chromatin remodeling and histones (22), nuclear ribosomal factors (22), transcription components (15), protein modifying/folding/degradation (14), DNA repair and maintenance (12), cell cycle and cell death (11), viral defense response (10), viral proteins (7), histones (7), DNA replication (5), cytoskeleton (6) and other (6) (Fig 3).

  Interestingly, the majority of detected cellular proteins were associated with RNA processing. This is comparable to the report of HSV-1 aniPOND data (18). Similarly, the next most abundant group of proteins were cellular transcription factors and chromatin remodeling proteins. Overall, this information illustrates a dynamic range of cellular proteins that are associated with viral DNA, and the interconnection between viral DNA synthesis and transcription. It also indicates the likelihood that these cellular proteins are not only present but are functional for efficient replication to take place, especially given the evidence that true late gene expression most likely originates from newly synthesized genome templates (26).

  The two main functions of viral genomes during the early stages of infection are to act as a template for new DNA synthesis and viral RNA production. As such, the broader categories of cellular proteins identified in the aniPOND were divided into those associated with DNA processes or RNA production. To concisely represent the data, peptide counts of enriched samples are shown as heatmaps alongside total calculated enrichment (Fig 4-6, Fig S1-S5). For each heatmap, the color represents the average peptide count of the AD169 and TB40E replicates, “+” indicates samples that had biotin clicked to EdU, and “−” represents control samples that did not have biotin clicked. The enrichment was calculated by taking the average peptide count of the biotin click samples, divided by the control samples.

  In addition to the cellular proteins, we also identified several viral proteins associated with nascent viral DNA. The viral protein with the most pronounced enrichment in AD169 and TB40E was UL44, the viral DNA polymerase processivity factor. In both strains, there was enrichment for gene products of UL112/UL113. UL112/113 has a significant role in viral DNA replication by colocalizing with viral DNA and recruiting UL44 (27)(28). We also detected IE1 (UL123), a multifunctional immediate-early protein involved in regulating transcription and counteracting the host antiviral response (29). Additionally, TRS1 was also enriched in both strains. TRS1 modulates host cell autophagy to prevent a related process called xenophagy, an innate immune defense (30)(31). The fact that not many viral proteins were identified is most likely due to the stringency in the experimental protocol, transient nature of the protein-DNA interaction, or low abundance of the proteins.

  We then focused on assessing cellular proteins identified in the aniPOND from HCMV infected cells, of which there were several known to be essential for DNA replication. Interestingly, some of these proteins have previously been reported to facilitate HCMV DNA synthesis. For example, topoisomerases (TOP2A, TOP2B) are essential proteins required for resolving supercoiled DNA during replication (Fig 4). Other reported factors involved in viral DNA synthesis were mini chromosome maintenance complex component associated protein (MCM3AP), an acetyltransferase that is associated with MCM3 and MCM7, components of the MCM complex which are essential for DNA replication licensing (32-35). We also detected proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA), the cellular processivity factor. PCNA increases with infection and co-localizes to the replication compartments, although it’s exact function in viral replication remains unknown (36,37).

  Viral infections trigger the host cell DNA damage response, and several proteins that participate in DNA repair and maintenance were detected in the aniPOND (Fig 4) (38-40). These include proteins involved in DNA double-strand breaks (DSB) recognition and repair. The proteins identified are key components for non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) and homologous repair (HR) pathways: XRCC5/Ku80, XRCC6, TP53BP1, RAD21, RAD50, MRE11, and PRKDC (41). These data, in conjunction with previous reports, demonstrate that DNA repair proteins interact with nascent HCMV DNA and have a functional role which may extend beyond their characterized role in DNA repair.

  When the viral genome is packaged and encapsulated within the virion it is free of histones. Upon entry of the viral genome to the host cell nucleus, the viral dsDNA circularizes and interacts with cellular histones. We identified several histones enriched in the HCMV aniPOND including variants of the H1 linker and core histones including H2A, H2B, and H3.3. (Fig 5). Along with histones, many chromatin remodeling and histone-modifying proteins were present, including components of the nucleosome remodeling and histone deacetylase (NuRD) complex; HDAC1 and HDAC2, CHD4, MTA2, RBBP4, and RBBP7 (Fig 7B). NuRD is a multiprotein complex that regulates gene transcription by chromatin remodeling (42,43).

  Additionally, cellular factors involved in transcription and transcriptional regulation were enriched on nascent viral DNA (Fig 6). These include subunits of RNA polymerase II (POLR1A, POLR1B, POLR1E). Specific transcription factors and transcriptional regulators identified include YBX1, CEBPZ, UBTF, NRF, and SP100. SND1 was highly enriched, this role of cellular protein in HCMV has not been explored but has been reported to facilitate viral replication in KSHV infected cells (44).

  Cellular proteins involved in the antiviral response were identified, including promyelocytic leukemia (PML) (Fig 6). IE1 is a primary antagonist of PML bodies, in part to counteract the antiviral response (45-47). HCMV modulates intrinsic defenses during initial infection and replication. We also detected cellular proteins that are part of the intrinsic and innate immune response, including IFIT1, IFIT2, IFI16, TRIM25, and G3BP (Fig 6). IFIT1 and IFIT2 have been reported to be upregulated during HCMV infection (48,49). IFI16, a viral DNA sensor, is activated by HCMV infection. IFI16 interacts with pp65 (UL83) to inhibit IFI16-mediated DNA sensing and regulate UL54 expression during lytic replication (48,50-52).


### STRING analysis of proteins associated with nascent HCMV DNA
  It is important to recognize that proteins do not act alone and most function as part of a complex with other proteins. Observed in the aniPOND data, individual proteins that form complexes were often enriched as a whole. To convey the intricacy of these interactions we represent key complexes using a Search Tool of the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING) map (Fig 7). For example, the aniPOND identified proteins associated with SWItch/Sucrose Non-Fermentable (SWI/SNF) and NuRD complexes were isolated collectively. Within these complexes, the protein LC-MS hits included nearly all proteins fundamental to those specific functions and processes, suggesting that aggregated protein complexes were likely isolated during aniPOND experiments and not single protein-DNA interactions. The STRING map also demonstrates that many of these protein complexes are associated with other complementary complexes. For example, proteins identified to be functional for DNA damage and repair, DNA-directed RNA polymerases, RNA processing factors, and the nuclear pore complex interact with each other outside of their main functional realm.


### Localization of viral and cellular proteins with nascent viral DNA
  Select proteins identified in our aniPOND LC-MS experiments were used for additional imaging studies to localize the viral proteins along with nascent viral DNA by IFA (Fig 8A). UL44, the viral DNA polymerase processivity factor, was detectable in clusters throughout the host cell nucleus, with an increased concentration at the borders of the viral replication compartments, which encloses the EdU labeled viral DNA. It is known that UL44 concentrates at the periphery of these compartments (53,54). UL57, the viral single-stranded DNA binding protein, has punctate fluorescent staining associated with viral DNA and is strictly concentrated within viral replication compartments. In a slightly different pattern, UL84 and IE1 were not limited to localization with EdU labeled viral DNA and were instead diffused throughout the host cell nucleus, similar to what has previously been reported at 3 dpi (55,56). IE2 was dispersed throughout the nucleus but accumulated in regions where DNA replication is observed. For UL112/113, the protein was prominently concentrated with nascent DNA. Co-localization analysis was calculated using Coloc 2 Image J software and reported as Pearson’s Correlation Coefficient (r) of EdU labeled DNA fluorescent signal to the protein fluorescent signal to test the association of these viral proteins with nascent viral DNA (Fig 8B).

  We performed additional IFA to detect the localization patterns of cellular proteins known to interact with nascent DNA and identified in the aniPOND experiments (Fig 9A). Core histones were previously shown to be physically associated with viral DNA and congregate in viral replication compartments along with UL44 at 2 dpi (57). We detected H1 and H2A in our aniPOND results and visualized its interaction with replicating viral DNA. In the IFA, H1 and H2A did not show a different localization pattern between non-infected and infected cells. Nuclear staining of TOP2B exhibited a diffuse signal in the mock infected and in infected cells, but did display an area of higher fluorescent signal directed to the periphery of the nucleus and did not exclusively colocalize with viral DNA. However other studies have confirmed that TOP2B directly interacts with viral IE1 and binds to HCMV oriLyt. (58) (59).

  RBBP4, a key component in the NuRD complex, was observed in the nucleus in mock and infected cells. There was a higher signal localized within replication compartments with nascent viral DNA. Another interesting observation is the detection of RBBP4 expression adjacent to the outside of the nucleus only in infected cells, similar to what was observed for TOP2B. Previous reports of shRNA knockdown of NuRD proteins (RBBP4 and CHD4) resulted in reduced viral genome accumulation (60).

  Fluorescent imaging of RNA POL II presented a diffuse speckling pattern throughout the nucleus in both mock and infected HF cells. These specks are associated with concentrated splicing sites that are interacting with newly synthesized RNA (61). Imaging revealed larger and brighter foci in cells with detectable viral DNA replication. While localization is not exclusive to the brighter specks, it suggests an increase of RNA POL Il in association with nascent viral DNA.

  PARP1 is a multifunctional protein involved in DNA repair, chromatin remodeling, and apoptosis (62,63). Noticeably, there is an increased fluorescence intensity of PARP1 overall in infected cells with detectable viral DNA replication staining. It is probable that HCMV induced DNA damage results in an increase of PARP1 expression. Co-localization of these cellular proteins with nascent viral DNA was further confirmed and analyzed by Coloc 2 Image J software as described above (Fig 9B).


### Testing the association between cellular proteins and viral DNA
  To test the association of cellular proteins and nascent viral DNA identified in the aniPOND, a chromatin-immunoprecipitation (ChIP) analysis was performed for select proteins (Fig 10). The cells and infections used for the ChIP analysis were prepared under the same conditions as the aniPOND experiments. For the ChIP, antibodies specific to H2A, PCNA, and RBBP4 were used. After immunoprecipitation, the DNA associated with these cellular proteins were measured by qPCR. The log fold change was plotted and the analysis revealed substantial enrichment of viral DNA compared to cellular DNA for H2A, PCNA and RBBP4. These data confirms that the cellular proteins identified in the aniPOND analysis have specific interactions with viral DNA.

  For additional studies involving colocalization and knockdown experiments, we chose two cellular proteins identified in the aniPOND, which have direct involvement in DNA replication. First, we performed IFA to localize PCNA and MCM3 along with replicating DNA in sub-confluent HF cells, super-confluent HF cells, and super-confluent HF at 3 dpi with AD169 (Fig 11 and 11D). Then to test if these cellular proteins influence viral DNA replication, we performed shRNA knockdown experiments.

  The role of PCNA during HCMV replication is unclear, but it has been documented to colocalize with UL44 at replication compartments (36). Although these proteins have no sequence homology, there is three-dimensional structural homology, and both function as processivity factors (64). For PCNA we used a transient knockdown by transfecting HF cells twice (24 and 4 hours prior to infection) with either a plasmid expressing shRNA targeting PCNA or scramble control. HF cells were infected with AD169 (MOI=4). Protein was harvested at 2 dpi and 5 dpi along with non-infected controls. Protein was used for western blot analysis of PCNA, early viral protein UL84, and GAPDH at each indicated time point (Fig 11B). Cells treated with shRNA targeting PCNA resulted in a reduction in the amount of PCNA protein compared to scramble controls. The accumulation of UL84 protein was not substantially different between the two treatment groups, demonstrating that knockdown of PCNA does not disrupt early viral protein expression. DNA was harvested at 18 hpi, 2 dpi, and 5 dpi. Viral DNA was quantified by qPCR using primers and probes for UL84 and normalized using cellular 7SK. Enrichment of viral DNA at 2 and 5 dpi was calculated using 18 hpi as the initial time point (Fig 11C). There was a significant decrease in the production of viral DNA at 2 and 5 dpi in the samples treated with shRNA targeting PCNA compared to scramble control, with a 31.58% reduction in viral DNA synthesis at 5 dpi

  Although MCM3 was not directly detected in the aniPOND, the protein that acetylates MCM3 was detected, MCM3AP. MCM3AP has previously been demonstrated to play a role in HCMV replication by inhibiting the initiation of cellular DNA synthesis through its interaction with IE2 (65). Interestingly, the IFA showed a clear difference in the accumulation of MCM3 between the sub-confluent, super-confluent, and AD169 infected super-confluent HF cells (Fig 11D). There is very little MCM3 present in the super-confluent cells that are growth arrested. In contrast, both the sub-confluent and infected super-confluent cells clearly show MCM3 within the nucleus. For the MCM3 shRNA knockdown experiments, we generated a doxycycline-inducible shRNA MCM3 cell line in telomerized HF (T-HF) cells using a lentiviral vector which was selected for puromycin resistance. Sub-confluent T-HF shRNA-MCM3 cells were treated with doxycycline for 24 hours before infection with AD169 (MOI=4). Protein and DNA were harvested and analyzed as described above. Protein was used for western blot analysis of MCM3, UL84, and GAPDH at each indicated time point (Fig 11E). The T-HF shRNA-MCM3 cells treated with doxycycline showed a decrease in MCM3 protein compared to controls. Enrichment of viral DNA was calculated and demonstrated a significant decrease, 46.28% reduction in the production of viral DNA at 5 dpi in cells with shRNA to MCM3 (+Dox) compared to the control (−Dox) (Fig 11F). It should be noted that because PCNA and MCM3 are proteins required for cellular DNA synthesis, any long-term reduction in their normal amounts may lead to issues with cell viability which would also indirectly influence HCMV replication.


## Discussion
  Our study applied the aniPOND method to identify viral and cellular proteins associated with nascent HCMV DNA. Additionally, we measured the rate of nucleotide incorporation of HCMV and are able to compare that value to those previously reported for other viruses and organisms. Here we report the rate of DNA synthesis for HCMV at its fastest is ~829 bp/min for AD169 (24-36 hpi) and ~499 bp/min for TB40E (60-72 hpi). For HSV-1, the rate of replication is higher than HCMV and is reported to be ~1,919 bp/min (between 4-8 hpi) (18). The Eukaryotic rate of DNA synthesis has been calculated to be between 750-2,000 bp/min (66-68). The lower rate of TB40E nucleotide incorporation is consistent with the EdU fluorescent imaging, which showed a slightly lower signal intensity during short 15-minute pulses for cells infected with TB40E compared to AD169. The higher rate of nucleotide incorporation of AD169 may be the result of the extensive passage in tissue culture which may have contributed to generating a virus better able to replicate under those conditions.

  In general, the proteins enriched along with nascent HCMV DNA can be grouped into three categories; first are those that have been previously reported to be involved in HCMV replication, second are those that have been reported for other herpesviruses, and third are newly identified proteins. An example that fits into both the first and second category is the NuRD complex, which is reported to facilitate HCMV replication and replication in other herpesviruses, including HSV-1 and EBV (9). Notably, the EBV protein BMRF1 (the DNA polymerase processivity factor and homologue to HCMV UL44) is reported to specifically interact with many of the NuRD subunits that were identified in this study. The proteins found in association with both HCMV and EBV replication include RBBP4, RBBP7, MTA1, MTA2, HDAC1, HDAC2, and CHD4 (69).

  An interesting category of proteins found to be highly enriched were those that participated in nuclear structure and organization (Fig S1). These include TOR1AIP1, an integral membrane protein that resides in the inner nuclear membrane, LEMD2, required to maintain the integrity of the nuclear envelope, and TMEM43, involved in organizing protein complexes at the inner nuclear membrane (NRM). Many proteins which function to maintain and regulate the nuclear pore complex (NPC) were identified. This leads us to speculate that the observed interaction between nascent viral DNA and these complexes may function to keep viral replication products in close proximity to the nuclear envelope for productive assembly and export. HCMV is capable of remodeling the nucleus to form replication compartments during lytic replication to facilitate efficient viral replication (70,71).

  During viral replication, newly synthesized DNA is used as a template for transcription. As such, there was significant enrichment of proteins associated with RNA production and processing. Many ribonucleoproteins, both heterogenous ribonuclear proteins (hnRNP) and small nuclear ribonucleoproteins (snRNP) were identified (Fig S2). hnRNPs are a large family of RNA binding proteins with multiple functions including regulation of transcription, mRNA splicing and transport, and repressing or enhancing protein translational. It was previously reported that hnRNPK is required for efficient HCMV replication and hnRNPA1 interacts with IE2 (59,72,73).

  Many RNA helicases and proteins that function in pre-mRNA, mRNA, and RNA processing were identified (Fig S1). Several DEAD-box and DEAH-box RNA helicases were enriched, including DHX9 and DDX21 which have previously been reported to be involved in HCMV replication (74-76). A few categories of proteins identified with nascent viral DNA did not have a clear function in either DNA replication or transcription. It is important to consider that this may be because the replication compartment contains many proteins that function beyond their traditional capacity (77). These include proteins involved in ribosomal biogenesis and rRNA processing (Fig S4). HCMV is reported to increase the expression of proteins involved in ribosome biogenesis and the steady-state levels of ribosomes to keep up with the demand of translation during viral replication (78-80). We identified nuclear ribosomal protein enriched in association with nascent DNA (Fig S3). While these proteins interact with ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and participate in translation, many have additional functions such as RPS6, a component of the 40S ribosomal subunit, has been documented to interact with KSHV (Kaposi's sarcoma-associated herpesvirus) LANA to increase the protein stability of LANA (81). RPL4 was identified in our HCMV infected aniPOND and interacts with EBV EBNA1 (82). Other ribosomal proteins we detected include those associated with the 60S (RPL) and 40S (RPS) ribosomal subunits as indicated (Fig S3). This suggests that either ribosomal proteins are co-opted during viral replication for additional functions, or viral factors influence ribosomal biogenesis and translation in a previously unidentified manner.

  Also detected were cellular proteins that participate in translation initiation, elongation, and export (Fig S5). As herpesviruses are completely dependent on the host translational machinery for protein production, there are many ways that viruses modulate this mechanism to support the translation of viral proteins, including interacting with specific cellular proteins involved in this process (83). The regulation of translation by the host cells is also part of the antiviral response to prevent viral replication (84). The enrichment of initiation proteins (eIF) and elongation proteins (eEF) may play a dual function in promoting viral translation or circumventing the antiviral response. Some mRNA export factors identified were NXF1 and RAE1. These proteins facilitate the export of mRNAs to the cytoplasm for translation. NXF1 is known to promote the transport of HSV-1 transcripts (85). These proteins have yet to be investigated for HCMV. Lastly, our aniPOND results included proteins involved in proteasomal degradation and SUMOylation (Fig S5). These data emphasize the multitude of host pathways and levels of regulation HCMV utilizes for efficient replication.

  There are inherent limitations to the aniPOND analysis. First, because the DNA is labeled with EdU there is the potential that detection of DNA damage response proteins are due to the use of a nucleoside analogue which may be detected and repaired by that pathway. Although we have previously reported no toxicity related to short EdU pulse time of 30 minutes, we cannot exclude the possibility of DNA damage response due to EdU incorporation (14). Second, the aniPOND method followed by western blot analysis has been used to demonstrate the presence of polycomb repressive complex (PRC) proteins, EZH2 and BMI1, in association with nascent DNA during an HCMV infection (86). While we did not identify these two cellular proteins in our study, a comparison between that study and our findings within this report underscores some of the limitations to the aniPOND with LC-MS analysis. This includes the lack of identifying all core replication proteins (UL54, UL57, UL70, UL102, UL105) along with the identified UL44, which we speculate is due to abundance and sensitivity of the assay. We acknowledge that, although this is a relatively comprehensive analysis, there may be other cellular proteins that interact with nascent viral DNA that were undetected in the proteomics analysis due to low protein abundance or transient interactions that are not captured by this method. Moreover, performing aniPOND analysis at varying times post-infection will provide valuable information on the temporal dynamics of protein-DNA interactions throughout the course of infection, and this may be an important consideration for revealing recruitment differences between strains of HCMV as our data did point to varying peak rate of nucleotide incorporation between AD169 and TB40E. It is important to note that although we have a protocol in place to minimize that amount of cellular DNA enriched in the aniPOND, there is still a small fraction of cellular DNA which may be contributing to the enrichment of cellular proteins identified in the proteomics. We temper any over interpretation of the proteins that were identified, as further studies will be necessary for determining their specific role in HCMV replication.

  A protein identified in our studies that we performed further investigations was cellular PCNA. IFA experiments showed distinct co-localization of PCNA with nascent viral DNA, and shRNA knockdown of PCNA resulted in a significant decrease in the accumulation of viral DNA. PCNA is primarily characterized as a processivity factor. It interacts with replicative polymerases to enhance their activity and is required for duplication of the cellular genome. HCMV, like all herpesviruses, encodes a processivity factor, UL44. It is unlikely that during HCMV DNA replication that PCNA functions in its traditional processivity role. The question remains, what is the function of PCNA during HCMV replication?

  To answer this, we looked at the cellular proteins identified in the aniPOND that are known to modulate PCNA’s function. These include RFC1, RFC3, TOP2A (Fig 6A), PARP1 (Fig 6B), and HDAC1 (Fig 7B). The RFC proteins have direct involvement in loading PCNA onto DNA during active cellular DNA replication and are involved in the mismatch repair mechanism (87,88). PARP1 has also been reported to associate with PCNA during DNA replication and repair, and is proposed to regulate the activity of PCNA (89-91). PCNA also interacts with HDAC1. Although those studies have not defined a clear function for this association, it is proposed that PCNA acts as a scaffold to facilitate the recruitment of factors like HDAC1 to specific locations of the genome for epigenetic regulation (92,93). Taken together this suggests a role for PCNA during HCMV replication that primarily involves DNA repair or facilitating chromatin remodeling.

  The current study identified cellular proteins sthat are co-opted to facilitate efficient viral replication. Our results are consistent with aniPOND experiments performed in HSV-1 (9,18), which reveal a specific enrichment of cellular proteins involved in DNA synthesis and RNA transcription associated with nascent viral DNA. The aniPOND is also a complementary approach to other methods, such as siRNA screens of cellular proteins, which are used to identify factors involved in efficient HCMV replication (94). While the number of different cellular proteins identified by aniPOND may appear to be complex, these interactions provide insight as to how tightly regulated and controlled HCMV replication and transcription are within the host cell nucleus. Our studies elevate what is known about these intricate viral and host interactions and highlight cellular proteins that play a pivotal role in HCMV replication.
