## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text, including abstract, introduction, methods, results, discussion, and funding statements.
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific validity, technical soundness, and clarity of claims based solely on the provided manuscript text. No external data, supplementary figures, or unpublished materials were consulted.
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors report that a photocleavable 6-nitropiperonyloxymethyl (NPOM) adduct on DNA is recognized by the Rad4/XPC damage sensor as a specific lesion, that this binding is abolished by light-induced cleavage of NPOM in a dose-dependent manner, and that the NPOM-DNA retains largely B-form conformation in solution, becoming heterogeneously distorted upon Rad4 binding. These conclusions are supported by UV-visible spectroscopy, competition EMSA, fluorescence lifetime (FLT) FRET measurements, and molecular dynamics simulations.
- **Visible evidence base** Main text figures (Figures 1–6), Tables 1, and referenced supplementary figures/tables (Figure S1–S17, Tables S1–S3) as cited in the text. Methods descriptions for protein preparation, DNA substrate preparation, photo-irradiation, melting temperature measurements, competition EMSA, FLT measurements, and MD simulations are provided.
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Supplementary figures and tables (S1–S17, Tables S1–S3) are cited but not provided. Full MD simulation parameters, force field validation details, and raw fluorescence decay data are not included. The specific DNA sequences for all constructs (e.g., AT1, AT2, AT7, AT10, CH7_NX, CH10_NX) are only partially listed in Table 1, and the exact positions of tC° and tCnitro labels relative to the NPOM site are not fully described in the main text.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript presents a novel application of photocleavable DNA chemistry to modulate and probe the DNA damage recognition process by Rad4/XPC, a key initiator of nucleotide excision repair. The work combines experimental biophysics (FLT-FRET, EMSA, UV-vis) with computational modeling (MD simulations) to characterize the NPOM-adducted DNA and its interaction with Rad4. The central claims are largely supported by the data presented, though several technical details and interpretive ambiguities require clarification. The study is of interest to the DNA repair and photochemistry communities, and the approach has potential for broader application in studying dynamic biomolecular interactions. However, the manuscript would benefit from stronger quantitative comparisons, clearer presentation of the FLT data analysis, and more explicit discussion of the limitations of the MD-derived models.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in the fields of DNA repair, particularly nucleotide excision repair and damage recognition mechanisms, will find the use of a photocleavable lesion as a tool for triggering binding/unbinding of interest. The biophysical community focused on protein-DNA interactions and conformational dynamics may also be interested in the FLT-FRET methodology applied here. Additionally, scientists developing photochemical tools for biological applications may see value in the demonstration of NPOM as a controllable lesion mimic.
- **Major strengths** 1) The concept of using a photocleavable adduct as a controllable DNA lesion to study damage recognition is novel and potentially powerful. 2) The combination of experimental (FLT-FRET, EMSA) and computational (MD) approaches provides complementary perspectives on the system. 3) The dose-dependent photocleavage and its correlation with loss of Rad4 binding is a clean demonstration of the principle. 4) The manuscript is generally well-written and the logic of the experimental design is clear.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **Concern ID** R1-M1
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Technical soundness of FLT-FRET interpretation
  - **Claim pointer** The claim that NPOM-DNA becomes "heterogeneously distorted" upon Rad4 binding, based on the appearance of multiple lifetime components in FLT measurements.
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section "DNA conformation landscape mapped by fluorescence lifetime analyses", Figures 4 and 5, Tables S1 and S2 (not provided).
  - **Concern** The interpretation of the FLT data relies heavily on the decomposition of fluorescence decays into discrete exponential components or continuous lifetime distributions. The assignment of specific lifetime components to distinct conformational states (e.g., "B-DNA-like" vs. "distorted") is not rigorously justified. The authors do not provide a quantitative comparison between the observed lifetime distributions and those expected for known conformations, nor do they account for potential effects of the NPOM moiety on the photophysics of the tC° donor (as they partially acknowledge for the 2.0 ns component in the donor-only construct). The increase in average FRET efficiency upon Rad4 binding (from 0.78 to 0.87) is interpreted as a conformational change, but the direction of change is opposite to that previously observed for other specific lesions, and the authors do not offer a structural explanation for this discrepancy beyond a vague suggestion of a "different binding mode."
  - **Why it matters** The FLT-FRET data constitute a central piece of experimental evidence for the claim that NPOM-DNA is recognized as a lesion and undergoes specific conformational changes. If the interpretation of the lifetime data is flawed or over-simplified, the conclusions regarding the mechanism of recognition are weakened. The lack of a clear structural model for the Rad4-bound NPOM-DNA state limits the impact of the study.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the FLT data, including: (1) a comparison of the recovered lifetime distributions with those of well-characterized DNA conformations (e.g., using the same FRET pair in a known bent or kinked DNA); (2) a quantitative assessment of the goodness-of-fit for different models (e.g., discrete vs. continuous distributions); (3) a discussion of the potential influence of the NPOM group on the donor quantum yield and orientation factor; and (4) a structural rationale for the observed increase in FRET efficiency, possibly supported by additional MD simulations of the Rad4-bound complex.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M2
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Technical soundness of MD simulations
  - **Claim pointer** The claim that MD simulations demonstrate that NPOM can be housed in the major groove of DNA with largely unperturbed base stacking, and that a base-displaced intercalated conformation is also accessible.
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section "Conformational searches and molecular dynamics simulations", Figure 6, Figures S13–S16 (not provided), Table S3 (not provided).
  - **Concern** The MD simulation methodology is described only briefly. Key details are missing: the force field parameters for NPOM-dT (are they derived from quantum mechanical calculations? Are they validated?), the equilibration protocol, the number of replicas, the convergence criteria, and the method for selecting representative structures. The authors state that one trajectory (MJ3) exhibited denaturation and was excluded, but do not discuss whether this indicates a force field artifact or a real instability. The population analysis (66% major groove, 34% transients) is based on a single 1.5 μs trajectory per starting conformation, which may not be sufficient to achieve converged populations. The calculated FRET efficiencies for the modeled conformations (0.96–0.98) are all very similar and close to the ideal B-DNA value, which does not strongly discriminate between the proposed conformations.
  - **Why it matters** The MD simulations are used to provide atomistic models for the NPOM-DNA structure and to support the interpretation of the FLT data. If the simulations are not well-converged or the force field is not properly validated, the structural conclusions are unreliable. The lack of discrimination between conformations based on FRET calculations weakens the link between the computational and experimental results.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should provide full details of the MD simulation setup, including force field parameterization and validation, equilibration and production protocols, and convergence analysis (e.g., RMSD vs. time, block averaging). They should also consider running multiple independent replicas to assess the reproducibility of the population distributions. If possible, they should perform simulations of the Rad4-bound NPOM-DNA complex to directly test the proposed binding mode and the conformational changes observed experimentally.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M3
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Scientific importance and generalizability
  - **Claim pointer** The claim that "photoactivable DNA may be used as a DNA lesion surrogate to study DNA repair mechanisms such as nucleotide excision repair."
  - **Evidence pointer** Abstract and Discussion.
  - **Concern** While the proof-of-principle is demonstrated, the generalizability of this approach is not fully established. The authors show that NPOM-DNA is recognized by Rad4 with affinity comparable to a bona fide lesion, but they do not demonstrate that the downstream steps of NER (e.g., TFIIH recruitment, excision) can be triggered or modulated by light in this system. The discussion of future applications is speculative and lacks concrete experimental evidence that the photocleavable lesion can be used to study the full NER pathway.
  - **Why it matters** The broader significance of the work hinges on its utility as a tool for studying NER. If the approach is limited to studying the initial binding step, its impact is more modest. Demonstrating that the photocleavable lesion can be used to control or monitor downstream events would significantly strengthen the case for its general applicability.
  - **Resolution test** The authors could perform additional experiments to show that the photocleavable lesion is processed by the complete NER machinery, e.g., using a cell-free NER assay or a reconstituted system with TFIIH and nucleases. Alternatively, they could discuss more specifically how the light-triggered unbinding could be used to study the kinetics of the multi-step recognition process, with a clear experimental plan.

- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** R1-M1 (FLT-FRET interpretation) and R1-M2 (MD simulation details) are the primary technical issues that need to be resolved. The lack of full DNA sequence information (R1-m1) is also a significant reproducibility concern.
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** 
  - **Originality** The use of a photocleavable adduct as a controllable lesion for studying DNA damage recognition is a novel concept. While photocleavable groups have been used in other nucleic acid contexts, their application to NER is new. This is a clear strength.
  - **Scientific importance** The study addresses a fundamental question in NER: how the damage sensor recognizes a diverse range of lesions. The finding that a bulky adduct like NPOM is recognized with high specificity, despite retaining B-form conformation, is interesting and may have implications for understanding the mechanism of indirect readout. However, the study does not provide a complete mechanistic picture, and the generalizability to the full NER pathway is not demonstrated. The importance is moderate to high.
  - **Interdisciplinary readership** The work bridges photochemistry, biophysics, and DNA repair. It will appeal to researchers in these fields, but the technical complexity of the FLT and MD methods may limit its accessibility to a broader audience. The potential for the approach to be used in other contexts (e.g., studying other protein-DNA interactions) could broaden its appeal.
  - **Technical soundness** The experimental design is generally sound, but the interpretation of the FLT data is not fully rigorous, and the MD simulations lack sufficient detail and validation. These issues need to be addressed to firmly establish the claims.
  - **Readability for nonspecialists** The manuscript is well-written but assumes significant background knowledge. The abstract is clear, but the results and discussion sections are dense and may be challenging for readers outside the immediate field.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The core concept is novel and the data are promising, but the interpretation of the FLT data and the details of the MD simulations need to be strengthened. The manuscript would be suitable for publication in a specialized journal like RSC Chemical Biology after these revisions.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that the NPOM-DNA is "recognized by the Rad4 protein as a specific substrate" is supported by the EMSA data, but the specificity is inferred from competition with unlabeled DNA. A direct comparison of binding affinity to a known non-specific DNA under the same conditions would strengthen this claim.
- The claim that the FLT data reveal "heterogeneous distortion" upon Rad4 binding is not fully supported without a more rigorous analysis of the lifetime distributions and a clear structural model.
- The claim that the MD simulations demonstrate the NPOM can be "housed in the major groove" is based on simulations that are not fully described or validated.
- The statement that "photoactivable DNA may be used as a DNA lesion surrogate to study DNA repair mechanisms" is a forward-looking statement that is not directly tested in this work. It is a reasonable suggestion but should be framed as a future direction, not a conclusion of the current study.
