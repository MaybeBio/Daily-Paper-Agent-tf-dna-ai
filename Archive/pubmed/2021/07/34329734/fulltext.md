# Identification of a potent Nrf2 displacement activator among aspirin-containing prodrugs


## Abstract
  Aspirin is a desired leaving group in prodrugs aimed at treatment of neurodegeneration and other conditions. A library of aspirin derivatives of various scaffolds potentially activating Nrf2 has been tested in Neh2-luc reporter assay which screens for direct Nrf2 protein stabilizers working via disruption of Nrf2-Keap1 interaction. Most aspirin prodrugs had a pro-alkylating or pro-oxidant motif in the structure and, therefore, were toxic at high concentrations. However, among the active compounds, we identified a molecule resembling a well-known Nrf2 displacement activator, bis-1,4-(4-methoxybenzenesulfonamidyl) naphthalene (NMBSA). The direct comparison of the newly identified compound with NMBSA and its improved analog in the reporter assay showed no quenching with N-acetyl cysteine, thus pointing to Nrf2 stabilization mechanism without cysteine alkylation. The potency of the newly identified compound in the reporter assay was much stronger than NMBSA, despite its inhibitory action in the commercial fluorescence polarization assay was observed only in the millimolar range. Molecular docking predicted that mono-deacetylation of the novel prodrug should generate a potent displacement activator. The time-course of reporter activation with the novel prodrug had a pronounced lag-period pointing to a plausible intracellular transformation leading to an active product. Treatment of the novel prodrug with blood plasma or cell lysate demonstrated stepwise deacetylation as judge by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS). Hence, the esterase-catalyzed hydrolysis of the prodrug liberates only acetyl groups from aspirin moiety and generates a potent Nrf2 activator. The discovered mechanism of prodrug activation makes the newly identified compound a promising lead for future optimization studies.


## Introduction
  Aspirin is one of the most frequently used drugs in high vascular risk patients. People with cognitive dysfunction and dementia also fall into the same category, and the risks and benefits of aspirin for preventing neurodegeneration of various nature are widely examined and debated. There is no consensus on benefits of aspirin for preventing Alzheimer’s disease/dementia (Li et al., 2020) or Parkinson’s disease (Poly et al., 2019), but low-dose aspirin does not pose an additional safety risk in this high-risk population. Development of aspirin-containing prodrugs aimed at targets relevant to neurodegeneration is worth pursuing, because ester prodrugs are easily hydrolyzed under the action of esterase. Liberation of aspirin (acetylsalicylate) from a prodrug competes with simple deacetylation and reflects the esterase preference for a substrate structure, since the aryl ester is inherently more reactive to hydrolysis (Moriarty et al., 2008). A series of aspirin-prodrugs has been previously synthesized and reported, including those with “NO-donor” nitrate moieties and control compounds with the nitrate replaced by groups such as bromo, fumarates, sulfonates, and even a second aspirin moiety (Dunlap et al., 2012; Kastrati et al., 2015). Several of these prodrugs have been shown to inhibit NF-κB signaling and to activate the antioxidant genetic program orchestrated by the Nrf2 transcription factor (Dunlap et al., 2012). Activation of Nrf2-driven programs is a promising target for the treatment of CNS insults of different origin. Nrf2 triggers the expression of hundreds of protective genes. Its protein stability is regulated by its interaction with Keap1 protein, a redox sensor, the conformation, and binding of which into the ubiquitin ligase complex depends on the redox status of its reactive thiols. Upon covalent modification of Keap1 thiols by xenobiotics or ROS/RNS, Nrf2 is released from the ubiquitin ligase complex, translocates to the nucleus, and launches the antioxidant response program. Reversible physiological regulation of Nrf2 stability proceeds via its displacement from the Keap1 Kelch domain by p62 and some other protein clients of Keap1 and results in a moderate shift in the Nrf2 stability equilibrium (Fao et al., 2019). Even though the displacement mechanism is not exclusive for Nrf2 (Hushpulian et al., 2021), it is commonly considered as “benign” compared to covalent modification of Keap1 thiols with alkylating agents like dimethyl fumarate (“Tecfidera”, FDA-approved for multiple sclerosis). Alkylating and pro-oxidant Nrf2 activators exert unavoidable side effects because of their activity with respect to active thiols and selenothiols in various proteins, besides Keap1.

  A typical cell-based screen for Nrf2 activators employs luciferase transcriptional reporters, ARE-luc reporters, however, this screen is not specific for a particular mechanism of Nrf2 activation. Nrf2 protein contains several domains with different functions: a conserved N-terminal regulatory domain termed Neh2, a central transactivation domain, and a C-terminal bZIP domain required for heterodimer formation with members of the small Maf protein family, for DNA binding and nuclear import and export. Among these, Neh2 domain is responsible for recognition by Nrf2 inhibitor, Keap1 protein, forming a complex with cullin III ubiquitin ligase. Neh2 domain of Nrf2 is stretched between 2 Kelch domains in the Keap1 dimer, thus exposing Neh2 lysine residues for ubiquitination. Given the availability of Neh2-luc reporter designed to screen for Nrf2 activators working via Nrf2 protein stabilization by disruption of Keap1 interaction with Neh2 domain of Nrf2 (Smirnova et al., 2011), we screened a library of aspirin prodrugs of various structure (Dunlap et al., 2012) to identify promising candidates for future optimization studies. We found a potent prodrug with a novel asymmetrical scaffold. In accord with LC-MS and modeling data, the novel prodrug generates a mono-deacetylated intermediate working via a displacement mechanism.


## Materials and Methods
  The library of compounds 1–40 (Figure 1 and S1) used in this study was obtained from Dr. Thatcher. The synthetic procedures to make these compounds were previously reported (Dunlap et al., 2012; Kastrati et al., 2017; Kastrati et al., 2015). Bis-1,4-(4-methoxybenzenesulfonamidyl)naphthalene (NMBSA) SML0959 was purchased from MilliporeSigma, 1,4-bis[carboxymethyl-(4-methoxy-benzenesulfonyl)-amino]-naphthalene (CPUY) was purchased from Aobious Inc, Gloucester MA (CPUY192018). Compound 9 was re-synthesized using the published protocol (Dunlap et al., 2012; Kastrati et al., 2017; Kastrati et al., 2015) with modifications. The final product had a purity of 99% as assessed by analytical reversed phase HPLC.


### Endpoint and time-course Neh2-luc reporter assay
  SH-SY5Y cell line stably expressing Neh2-luc (Smirnova et al., 2011) reporter was grown in the DMEM/F12 supplemented with GlutaMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin and plated into 96-well white flat-bottom plates at 15,000 cell/well in 100 μL serum and incubated for 16 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2. The compounds were prepared as 10 mM stock solutions in DMSO. Then, 2 μL aliquots of the compounds were added to the wells, so their final concentrations varied from 2.5 to 40 μM. The plates were incubated for 3 h at 37 °C. The medium was removed, cells lysed with 20 μL Lysis buffer (Promega) for 7 min at RT, and luciferase activity was measured on a SpectraMax M5e plate reader (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA) with 80 μL BrightGlo™ reagent (Promega, Madison, WI). The reporter activation was normalized to the background (only DMSO added). Nordihydroguaiaretic acid was used as a positive control. N-acetyl cysteine (NAC) pre-treatment was performed as follows. NAC stock solution was prepared in 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer, pH 7.5, then 5 μL aliquots were added to each well to the final concentration of 1 mM NAC, and the plate incubated for 30 min. Then the drugs were added, and the plate was incubated and processed as above. For the time-course experiments, 2 μL aliquots of the compounds were added to the wells at different time points, and then at one and the same time, the whole plate was processed as described above. The experiments were performed in triplicates and reported as mean ± SD.


### Fluorescence polarization assay
  Evaluation of displacement activity of novel prodrug was performed using Keap1-Nrf2 inhibitor screening assay kit (BPS Biosciences, San Diego, CA) in accord with the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, fluorescently labelled Nrf2 peptide (0.1 μM final) along with BSA and the assay buffer was added to the black bottom 96-well plate, followed by the addition of either prodrug under study (with varied concentrations from 10 μM to 5 mM) or a commercial displacement activator 1,4-bis[carboxymethyl-(4-methoxy-benzenesulfonyl)-amino]-naphthalene (CPUY192018, Aobious) varied up to 0.1 mM. Then purified human Keap1 (0.1 μM final) was added and the mixture incubated for 30 min. A drop in fluorescence polarization (FP) due to the increased rotation of free fluorescently labeled Nrf2 peptide (Nrf2-FITC) was measured using a plate reader (CLARIO Star Plus). The percentage of bound Keap1-Nrf2 peptide complex was calculated from the FP values and plotted against the concentration of the prodrug or CPUY added. The experiment was performed in triplicate.


### Mass spectrometric analysis of metabolites of compound 9.
  Esterase-catalyzed biotransformation of aspirin prodrug, compound 9, was studied in human plasma (Human Plasma K2EDTA Pooled Gender HUMANPLK2P2N) received from BioIVT. The plasma (190 μL) was placed in a 1.5-mL microcentrifuge tube and pre-warmed to 37 °C. The reactions were initiated by the addition of 10 μL of a 1 mM solution of the test compound in DMSO to each tube to yield a final concentration of 50 μM (final DMSO concentration is 5%). The assays were performed in a shaking water bath at 37 °C and conducted in duplicate. Samples (30 μL) were taken at 0, 30, 60 and 90 min and added to 90 μL of cold acetonitrile. The samples were subjected to vortex mixing for 30 seconds and then centrifugation at 4 °C for 15 min at 14,000 rpm. The clear supernatants were analyzed by HPLC using a Shimadzu LC-20AD HPLC system consisting of a UV/VIS detector (SDP-20AV), degasser (DGU-20A), and an autosampler (SIL-HTA). The separation was achieved on an ACE 3AQ C8 column (150 × 4.6 mm, particle size 3 μm) with detection at 254 and 280 nm; flow rate = 1.0 mL/min; mobile phase gradient of 20–95% acetonitrile in water (both containing 0.1 vol % of FA) in 20 min. Retention times for the first and second metabolite were 10.81 and 11.42 min respectively. Retention time for the original compound was 10.33 min. Experiment with SH-SY5Y/Neh2-luc cell extract (obtained by freeze-thaw cycles from freshly collected cells) was conducted in the similar manner. The samples were taken at 0, 30, 90 min and 5 h. The products were analyzed by mass spectrometry (MS). The MS data was collected on the Bruker Amazon X using Hystar Version 3.2 data acquisition software and processed using Compass Version 4.4 for data analysis. The mass spectrometer front end was configured with an Agilent 1200 Infinity Series HPLC module including Diode Array detector G1315A, and the mass spectrometer was configured with an ESI source and 3D ion trap mass analyzer. Chromatographic separation was achieved on a Poroshell 120 EC-C18 column (3 × 50 mm, particle size 2.7 μm) with detection at 254 and 280 nm; flow rate =0.4 mL/min; mobile phase gradient of 70–100% acetonitrile in water (both containing 0.1 vol % of FA) in 10 min; T = 40 °C. An analytical method employing positive ion electrospray ionization (ESI) was developed to detect the parental compound 9 (m/z 498) and its metabolites, compounds 9i1 and 9i2 (m/z 456) and compound 9i3 (m/z 414).


### Computer modeling
  Docking experiments were performed using the CDOCKER algorithm as implemented in the Discovery studio 3.0 software suite (Accelrys, San Diego, CA), followed by force field minimization and binding energy calculations using the Keap1 Kelch domain crystal structure with the bound NMBSA inhibitor (4IQK.pdb) as a template structure. All ligands (NMBSA, CPUY, prodrug and its deacetylated variants) were imported and minimized using ‘Prepare ligands’ protocol after adding hydrogen bonds. Force field minimization was carried out using the molecular mechanics algorithm CHARMm as implemented in Discovery Studio 3.0. A series of 50 positions were analyzed for each compound. The percentage of high interaction energy positions coinciding with that of NMBSA in the crystal structure has been calculated and taken as a comparative measure of compound affinity for Kelch domain.


## Results
  The library of compounds previously synthesized and screened for anti-inflammatory aspirin-containing prodrugs (Dunlap et al., 2012; Kastrati et al., 2017; Kastrati et al., 2015) is comprised mainly of electrophilic precursors, many of which, upon esterase-catalyzed bioactivation in the cell, generate an electrophilic quinone methide (QM) (Dunlap et al., 2007; Dunlap et al., 2012; Pierce et al., 2016). Among 40 compounds tested in the Neh2-luc reporter assay, 16 demonstrated a strong activation effect, and 10 of those, including compounds 4, 7, 8, 9–11, 13, 27, 28 and 31, were active at low micromolar concentrations (Figure 1). As exemplified in Scheme 1, 4-(bromomethyl) phenolate released from 4-(bromomethyl) phenyl 2-acetoxybenzoate (4), is anticipated to react with cysteine thiols of peptides and proteins via the formation of a p-QM metabolite and resonance-stabilized carbocation or directly by an SN2 mechanism. Covalent modification of proteins by this aspirin prodrug has been demonstrated, including modification of Keap1 cysteines resulting in Nrf2 stabilization (Dunlap et al., 2012; Pierce et al., 2016).

  Most hits from this library fall within the aspirin prodrug class that is bioactivated to a pQM, as shown for compound 4 in Scheme 1, while compounds 27 and 28 are electrophilic derivatives of dinitrophenol; compounds 31–33 are salicylate-fumarate hybrids; and compounds 7 and 8 are aspirin-fumarate hybrid compounds. The bell-shaped titration curve for most of the hits, except compounds 7 and 9, indicates a sharp onset of toxicity, which is expected for compounds acting via the QM formation mechanism shown in Scheme 1. Interestingly, the activation profile of compound 8 with a maleate moiety (cis-form) resembles the QM-type hits, whereas its trans-isomer, compound 7 (fumarate), does not show toxicity at the highest tested concentration of 40 μM, like other hits with the fumarate pharmacophore such as compounds 33 and 34 (Figure 1). It is also noteworthy that compound 33, which has no substitution in the benzyl ring, and compound 34 with an aspirin substituent at the meta-position demonstrate similarity in the titration curves, suggesting that meta-substituted analog is incapable of QM formation and is a weak alkylating agent in accordance with the previously reported results (Dunlap et al., 2007).

  Comparison of titration curves for dinitrophenol derivatives, compounds 26–28, shows that activity of aspirin derivative 26 capable of QM formation is the weakest, as compared to bromo (compound 28) or nitrooxy (compound 27) analogs, and that the presence of a bromo substituent creates the most potent alkylating and, therefore, the most toxic agent. This statement is supported by the activation properties of naphthalene’s 9–11, where the bromo derivative, compound 10, has the highest toxicity. On the other hand, it was surprising to see such remarkable activity of compound 9 in low micromolar range with the observed plateau for the amplitude of activation of ca. 8-fold within the 5–40 μM. The structural geometry of compound 9 with the naphthalene scaffold and two substituents resembles that of NMBSA suggesting that compound 9 may specifically interact with the Kelch domain of Keap1, which in turn may increase its reactivity. To verify whether the mechanism of reporter activation includes the formation of alkylating species because of biotransformation in the cell, we pretreated cells with millimolar concentrations of N-acetylcysteine (NAC), a cell-permeable thiol-containing compound, which can trap intracellularly generated alkylating agents and quench the reporter activation (Figure 2). As expected, the activation induced by compounds 10 and 11 was quenched in the presence of NAC, confirming formation of electrophilic reactive species. However, no effect of NAC was observed for compound 9 (Figure 2B), as well as for the well-known displacement activator NMBSA used as the control (Figure 2A), suggesting that compound 9 may function as a displacement activator, rather than a proto-electrophile. Moreover, based on the results presented in Figure 2, compound 9 with an EC50 value of ca. 40 μM in the Neh2-luc assay is superior to NMBSA (Figure 2). It must be mentioned that, despite the reported low micromolar value for dissociation of NMBSA from Kelch domain of Keap1 determined in FP-assay (Marcotte et al., 2013), a similar offset for EC50 in biological assays (> 50 μM) was observed and reported by others (Marcotte et al., 2013), which agrees with our data. The offset by more than an order of magnitude in the reporter assay may originate from the competition of a displacement activator with the overexpressed Neh2-luc fusion protein, estimated at ca. 60–80 nM from the background luminescence of the reporter cell line (Khristichenko, 2009), and a superior Kd for the full-size Neh2 domain (ca. 50 nM) compared to micromolar dissociation constant for NMBSA.

  To verify whether compound 9 can function as a displacement activator, we performed a direct fluorescent polarization assay with recombinant Keap1 (Figure 3) for compound 9 and CPUY, a structurally optimized analog of NMBSA, in which the addition of two acetic acid “arms” lowered its dissociation constant to 3.59 nM and EC50 in FP-assay for Kelch domain binding to 28.6 nM (Jiang et al., 2014). CPUY was reported to activate ARE-luc reporter in the HepG2 cell line in low micromolar range (Jiang et al., 2014), and the authors noted a very poor membrane permeability of CPUY as a plausible reason for such an offset in its potency. As shown in Figure 3, compound 9 was moderately active in FP, but less potent than CPUY: compound 9 competes with the fluorescently labeled Nrf2 peptide for Keap1 binding only in the millimolar range. The apparent inhibition constant for CPUY in the commercial FP-assay is ca. 80 nM (Figure 3B), only 3-fold higher than the reported value (Jiang et al., 2014). Even if we account for a 3-fold offset in the apparent dissociation constant, the estimated value for compound 9 will be in a millimolar range. This result is very different from the EC50 value of ca. 7 μM (Figure 2) determined for compound 9 in Neh2-luc assay. Such a difference in the FP-assay and cell-based assay points to a plausible biotransformation of compound 9 in the cell that generates a much more active intermediate. This intermediate is not an alkylating agent since the activation effect of compound 9 is not quenched by cell-permeable NAC contrary to compounds 10 and 11 (Figure 2) and DMF (Ahuja et al., 2016).

  The advantage of Neh2-luc reporter over commonly used transcriptional reporters (ARE-luc) is that it permits direct monitoring of the time-course of Neh2-luc fusion protein stabilization upon the action of an activator (Smirnova et al., 2011). The type of the time-course can indirectly indicate the mechanism of Nrf2 stabilization and discriminate between alkylating agents and displacement activators: alkylating activators result in accumulation of the fusion protein, and independent of the drug concentration used, the magnitude of activation reaches the same maximum possible, whereas displacement activators re-equilibrate the system and hit a plateau, which level depends on the drug concentration (Smirnova et al., 2011). The presence of a lag-period points to a possible additional step in the drug action, which could be prodrug biotransformation. The time course of Neh2-luc reporter activation with compound 9, NMBSA and CPUY (Figure 4) clearly indicates that: a) NMBSA and CPUY are functional as they are, and the shape of the time-course with the plateau matches the prediction for displacement activators; b) compound 9 time-course with a 1.5 h lag-period, indicates a requirement for an additional step preceding reporter activation. This step could be a biotransformation of compound 9 yielding its active form, while the decline in the activation seen at the highest concentrations and longer time periods may be a result of either cytotoxicity or sequential transformation of the active metabolites of compound 9.

  To identify the possible metabolites of compound 9, we incubated it with a cell-free extract prepared from SH-SY5Y Neh2-luc neuroblastoma cells collected from 25 mL culture (T75 flask) and blood plasma, which is known to contain butyrylcholinesterase (BuChE), the major cholinesterase that catalysis aspirin hydrolysis. The reaction was monitored by HPLC, and the products formed at different time points were analyzed using LC-MS as described in Materials and Methods. In both media, we identified a mixture of mono-deacetylated compounds 9(i1, i2) as the intermediate converting into the completely deacetylated product, compound 9i3, within 1–2 h of incubation in plasma, and 5–6 h in the cell-free extract (Figure 5). No de-”salicylated” variant of compound 9 was detected within this timeframe. There are two possible mono-deacetylated metabolites (Scheme 2) and the overlapping peaks on the HPLC chromatograms show that one of the variants prevail (see elution profiles in Supporting Information, Figures S2–S4). Since mass-spectroscopy cannot discriminate between the two possible mono-deacetylated metabolites of compound 9, we performed computer modeling to find the most probable version for the Nrf2 displacement activator among various products of compound 9 hydrolysis.

  Since de-”salicylated” forms were not detected upon incubation with human esterase-containing plasma and cell-free extract, we tested the docking scores and positions for the original compound 9 and its three possible deacetylated forms (Scheme 2, Table 1) in comparison with NMBSA, which crystal structure within the Kelch domain is available (4IQK.pdb). As one can judge from the docking energy values (Table 1), all compounds can be successfully docked into the Kelch domain. However, the probability of occupying the same position as NMBSA, was strikingly different. Docking of NMBSA itself generated 50 poses with high docking energy, 20 of which were overlapping with its actual position in the crystal structure, thus giving a 40% probability score, which can be considered as an indicator of a true binding. Compound 9 showed only 4 poses overlapping with NMBSA meaning that compound 9 has only an 8% probability for the NMBSA type of binding. In most docking poses for compound 9 the naphthalene ring was significantly shifted away with a completely different orientation than the original NMBSA ligand. This result may explain why in the FP-assay compound 9 demonstrated displacement activity only at millimolar concentrations (Figure 3A). Among the two mono-acetylated metabolites, compounds 9i1 and 9i2, compound 9i1 had a stronger binding preference, with 17 poses overlapping with NMBSA out of 50 (34% probability), than compound 9i2 with a single generated overlapping pose (2% probability). Surprisingly, the completely deacetylated product was also showing the low probability of coinciding with NMBSA position (1 binding pose out of 50, or 2%). Hence, based on the probabilistic approach, the docking results pointed to compound 9i1 (Figure 6) as the intermediate of the highest resemblance to NMBSA with respect to its binding mode to Kelch domain.


## Discussion
  Cell-based reporter performance is strongly affected by cell membrane permeability and toxicity of the studied compounds. The absence of activity at low concentrations linked to a poor membrane permeability can be illustrated with a well-known alkylating agent and Nrf2 activator monomethylfumarate (MMF) (Ahuja et al., 2016). The comparative studies of dimethylfumarate (DMF) and MMF demonstrated that both esters activate the Nrf2 pathway via S-alkylation of the Nrf2 inhibitor Keap1. Although, Nrf2 activation by DMF was associated with depletion of glutathione and decreased cell viability, whereas slow membrane permeabilization in case of MMF results in mild activation and lesser toxicity (Ahuja et al., 2016). There were no carboxylic acids in this screening library, so the activation data likely reflect the activation properties with respect to Neh2-luc reporter, not affected by cell permeability. Regarding the compound’s cytotoxicity, those compounds previously reported as proto electrophiles, generating quinone methide metabolites (Dunlap et al., 2012; Kastrati et al., 2015), were more active but also more toxic. Fumarate-containing hybrid compounds apparently were working via a mechanism with fumarate acting as the cysteine-alkylating electrophile, since the replacement of the fumarate moiety (as in compound 7) with succinate (as in compound 35) led to loss of activity. The potency dropped when the ethyl ester in compound 31 was substituted with a second aspirin moiety (compound 32) suggesting that fumarate must have a free carboxylic group to exert full alkylating potency, and possibly that esterase-catalyzed hydrolysis of aspirin prodrugs is biased towards the acetyl ester group, generating salicylic acid derivatives (Moriarty et al., 2008). Diethyl maleate is a known activator of Nrf2 (Lee et al., 2001), however, no comparative studies against diethyl fumarate have ever been reported. Our data showed that maleate derivative 8 is more cytotoxic than fumarate 7, likely due to the more significant protein damage and depletion of glutathione levels caused by this electrophile in cells (West et al., 2011).

  The bis-aspirin derivative 9 with a naphthalene moiety has attracted our attention due to the superior activation potency over its phenyl-based analog 3, a known donor of ASA (Figure 1). Both compounds may theoretically react in accord with Scheme 1 with thiol-containing compounds. However, based on the negligible reporter activation in the case of compound 3, one could conclude that the O-substituted variant (in contrast to the bromide) is barely active in thiol modification (as depicted in Scheme 1). Hence, the activity of Compound 9 is unlikely to result from de-salicylation and subsequent quinone methide formation. The resemblance of the molecular geometry of compound 9 to a known displacement activator NMBSA with a naphthalene scaffold guided the next steps, i.e., examination of the effect of cell-permeable thiol-reducing agent, NAC, on the reporter activation, and fluorescence polarization experiments. The absence of NAC quenching effect (Figure 2) supported a mechanism other than thiol alkylation, however, FP-assay results suggested a very poor affinity of compound 9 for Keap1 (Figure 3) contradicting the reporter data. The advantage of luciferase fusion reporter Neh2-luc over common transcriptional reporters such as ARE-luc is that it provides real time monitoring of the fusion protein stability, a time-course of activation, which concentration dependence can be used to discriminate between direct and indirect Nrf2 activators as well as to assume on the mechanism of activation (Smirnova et al., 2011). The observed 1–1.5 h lag-period during activation by compound 9 (Figure 4) directly pointed to a biotransformation step generating an active intermediate, a step preceding stabilization of the fusion protein. The following LC-MS studies on compound 9 bioactivation in the presence of blood plasma or cell-free extract of SH-SY5Y/Neh2-luc reporter cell line demonstrated the formation of one mono- deacetylated intermediate and the double-deacetylated product of Compound 9 (Figure 5). Mass-spectroscopy cannot distinguish between the two possible mono-deacetylated intermediates since they exhibit the same molecular mass and ionization variants, but computer modeling gives a clear preference for only one of the intermediates – compound 9i1 (Scheme 2). Computer docking studies demonstrate that this intermediate and NMBSA have almost the same probability to occupy the position of NMBSA in the crystal structure (Table 1), so one may expect their similar properties in Neh2-luc reporter activation. However, the activation effect of compound 9 is far more pronounced than that of NMBSA (IC50 for Keap1 binding of ca. 2.7 μM (Jain et al., 2015), and moreover, its improved version CPUY (Ka for Keap1 binding of ca. 9.9 nM and IC50=30 nM (Jain et al., 2015). The higher IC50 values obtained for the latter compounds in the utilized cell-based reporter assay may originate from the presence of sulfoxide moiety (in both) and two free carboxy-groups in CPUY structure that create interference with compounds’ transport through the negatively charged surface of the cell membrane. A higher potency of compound 9 in Neh2-luc reporter assay may also point to an additional mechanism, if esterase-catalyzed hydrolysis occurs with compound 9 in its already bound form to Keap1, we can speculate that the observed increase in potency may originate from transacetylation of Keap1 amino acid residues adjacent to compound 9, and hence, Neh2 binding side. Aspirin-induced acetylation of amino acid residues in proteins is well documented by proteomic studies and based on the recently reported aspirin “acetylome”, the most susceptible to acetylation are protein lysines (Tatham et al., 2017), then serines, threonines, and arginines (Ornelas et al., 2017). The putative binding side for Compound 9i1 coincides with the one for NMBSA and has 3 serines, 3 asparagines and 1 threonine residue closely surrounding the bound molecule. The serine residues are located inside the cavity (Figure 6), so their acetylation will unlikely compromise the binding of Neh2 peptide, in contrast to arginine’s located at the surface and participating in Neh2 binding.


## Conclusions
  Aspirin prodrugs, by definition must undergo esterase-catalyzed de-”salicylation” in the cell to release aspirin; however, de-acetylated intermediates may be formed first. Moreover, we observe a clear preference for de-acetylation of one of the two aspirin moieties in compound 9, which indicates an impact of ester bond accessibility on the esterase reaction rate. A novel asymmetrical scaffold with a naphthalene core has been identified as a potent Nrf2 activator prodrug that can be used for future optimization efforts. Previously published structural optimization mainly focused on either replacement of the naphthalene core with more benign ones, like isoquionoline (Zhang et al., 2018) or one amine nitrogen replacement with carbon in NMBSA or its improved version, CPUY (Lazzara et al., 2020). In both cases, the sulfone moiety was preserved to warrant the compound interaction with Ser 508, 555, 602, 363, and 308 residues within the center gap in Kelch domain (Figure 6A, B). The newly identified activator has a perfect fit into the positively charged gap in Kelch domain of Keap1 and is coordinated by Arg 380, 415, 483, and Asn 382 residues (Figure 6C), in addition to Tyr 525 and 334 coordinating phenyl rings of aspirin moieties similarly to two phenyl rings in NMBSA “arms”. The same residues coordinate ETGE Nrf2-peptide in the crystal structure of peptide-bound Kelch domain (4IFL.pdb). Given the dependence of esterase hydrolysis rate on the accessibility of a particular ester bond, the replacement of an aminosulfone linker in NMBSA type activators with an ester bond opens new possibilities, i.e., simpler synthetic protocols and an ability to manipulate with the lifetime of Nrf2 activator in the cell. The latter property is extremely important since both Keap1, an Nrf2 inhibitor, and Bach1, a transcriptional repressor of Nrf2, are well-known Nrf2 targets diminishing the protective effects of Nrf2 activators of any mechanism of action. Pretreatment with Nrf2 activators is beneficial in all scenarios of CNS disorders, whereas showing Nrf2 benefits in post-treatment paradigms is still a challenge. At this point it is not known what type of activation time-course, e.g., short “pulse” in Nrf2 protein stabilization or its persistent stabilization for 24 h or more is better in post-treatment paradigms for CNS insults. The currently available Nrf2 activators don’t provide an opportunity to manipulate with the life-time duration of an Nrf2 activator in the cell and answer this question. Therefore, the newly identified Nrf2 displacement prodrugs will become a valuable tool in solving several fundamental questions relevant to the benefits of Nrf2 activation in post-treatment paradigms.
