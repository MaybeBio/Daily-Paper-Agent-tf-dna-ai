## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text
- **Assessment boundary** Methodological soundness, validation approach, and claims of broad utility for the Bound2Learn machine learning classification approach
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors present Bound2Learn, a random forest-based machine learning approach that classifies single-molecule tracking data to distinguish DNA-bound proteins from diffusing molecules, enabling accurate residence time estimation. The method is validated in silico and in live E. coli and S. cerevisiae, and is claimed to be robust across varying acquisition conditions and applicable to other organisms.
- **Visible evidence base** Main text including Abstract, Materials and Methods, Results, Discussion, Data Availability, Funding, and Acknowledgements. Figures referenced but not provided. Supplementary materials referenced but not provided.
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Figures 1-5, Supplementary Tables S1-S6, Supplementary Figures S1-S4, Supplementary Videos S1-S4, and the software code repository. These are essential for evaluating classification performance metrics, training data quality, and reproducibility.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** The manuscript addresses a genuine and timely problem in single-molecule tracking analysis: robust classification of DNA-bound proteins from diffusing molecules, particularly for slow kinetics where track fragmentation is a major issue. The use of a random forest classifier on track-level features from a widely used tracking software (TrackMate) is pragmatic and potentially useful. However, the evidence provided in the text alone is insufficient to fully assess the method's performance, generalizability, and superiority over existing approaches. Several methodological details are underspecified, and the validation strategy, while reasonable in outline, requires the missing figures and supplementary materials to be properly evaluated. The claims of broad utility are plausible but not fully established from the supplied material.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers using single-particle tracking to study protein-DNA interactions in living cells, particularly those studying slow kinetic processes such as DNA repair, replication, and transcription. The method could also interest method developers in fluorescence microscopy and bioimage analysis, as well as biologists studying chromatin dynamics in organisms beyond E. coli and yeast.
- **Major strengths** The problem addressed is important and the motivation is clearly articulated. The approach leverages existing tracking software output, making it accessible to non-specialists. Validation across two organisms (prokaryote and eukaryote) and comparison to a conventional MSD-based approach strengthens the case. The authors acknowledge limitations and provide practical guidance for users.
- **Major Concerns** 
  - R1-M1: The manuscript does not provide sufficient detail on the feature set used for classification. The text mentions "mean speed, maximum speed, minimum speed, and median speed" and "maximum quality," but the rationale for feature selection, potential correlations, and the contribution of each feature to classification performance are not discussed. This is critical for reproducibility and for understanding why the method works.
  - R1-M2: The training data construction is described as manual classification by looking at raw image data. This introduces potential subjectivity and bias. The manuscript does not discuss inter- or intra-operator variability, nor does it describe any quality control measures for the training labels. The reliability of the ground truth is fundamental to the validity of the supervised learning approach.
  - R1-M3: The comparison to the MSD-based approach is presented as evidence of superiority, but the implementation details of the MSD method are not fully described. It is unclear whether the comparison is fair, i.e., whether the MSD method was optimized to the same extent as the ML approach. The claim that the ML approach "consistently out-performed" the MSD approach requires the missing figures to verify the magnitude and consistency of the improvement.
  - R1-M4: The manuscript claims the method is "less sensitive to track fragmentation" and can use "lower intensity thresholds," but the quantitative evidence for this claim is not presented in the text. The mechanism by which the ML approach achieves this robustness is not explained. This is a key selling point of the method and needs stronger support.
  - R1-M5: The correction for photobleaching and the calculation of bound times rely on several assumptions and equations, but the propagation of errors and the sensitivity of the final estimates to the assumptions (e.g., the 10% variation allowed in the bleach estimate) are not thoroughly analyzed. The robustness of the residence time estimates to these assumptions is not demonstrated.
- **Minor Comments**
  - R1-m1: The abstract states the method is "robust" but does not define what constitutes robustness in this context. A brief operational definition would help readers.
  - R1-m2: The description of the simulation parameters is incomplete. The text mentions "5000 × 5000 array" and cell dimensions, but the number of simulated molecules, the range of diffusion coefficients, and the signal-to-noise ratios tested are not specified in the main text.
  - R1-m3: The manuscript would benefit from a schematic figure or flowchart of the overall workflow, from raw data to residence time estimate, to aid readability for non-specialists.
  - R1-m4: The discussion of limitations is honest but brief. The statement that "choosing the right hyperparameter values... requires some expertise" is vague. Specific guidance or a sensitivity analysis would be more helpful.
  - R1-m5: The Data Availability statement provides a GitHub link, but the manuscript does not state the software dependencies or the expected runtime, which would be useful for potential users.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** R1-M1, R1-M2, R1-M3, R1-M4, R1-M5
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** 
  - Originality: Moderate. The application of random forest classification to track-level features from SPT data is not entirely novel, but the specific focus on long-exposure, slow-kinetics systems and the integration with TrackMate output provides a useful contribution.
  - Scientific importance: Moderate to high. Accurate residence time estimation is a bottleneck in many single-molecule studies, and a robust, accessible method would be widely used.
  - Interdisciplinary readership: Moderate. The method will appeal primarily to biophysicists and molecular biologists using SPT; the broader cell biology community may find it relevant but the technical focus limits its reach.
  - Technical soundness: Not fully assessable from the text alone. The approach is reasonable, but key details on features, training data, and validation are missing. The comparison to MSD methods needs scrutiny.
  - Readability for nonspecialists: Good. The manuscript is clearly written and the motivation is well explained. However, the methods section assumes familiarity with SPT and machine learning concepts.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The core idea is sound and the potential utility is clear, but the current evidence base is insufficient to fully establish the method's validity, robustness, and superiority over existing approaches. The missing figures and supplementary materials are essential for a complete assessment.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that Bound2Learn "has the potential for broad utility and is applicable to other organisms" is plausible but not directly demonstrated; only E. coli and S. cerevisiae are tested.
- The claim that the method is "less sensitive to track fragmentation" is not quantitatively supported in the text.
- The claim that the ML approach "consistently out-performed" the MSD approach is not verifiable without the figures.
- The statement that the method "can obtain estimates in a rapid manner" is not quantified.
- The assertion that the training data from a known DBP (histone H3) can be used to construct a generalizable model for other DBPs is an assumption that is not explicitly tested.
