# Cytotoxic constituents of Glycosmis ovoidea collected in Vietnam


## Abstract
  Compounds derived from natural sources have been major contributors to the area of cancer chemotherapy for decades. As part of an ongoing effort to discover anticancer drug leads from tropical plants, a large-scale collection of Glycosmis ovoidea Pierre (Rutaceae), was made at Nui Chua National Park, Vietnam. Activity-guided fractionation of the chloroform-soluble fractions led to the isolation of nine coumarins, including the new compound, 1-(7-methoxy-2-oxo-2H-chromen-8-yl)-3-methyl-1-oxobut-2-en-2-yl (S)-2-methylbutanoate (1). An close analogue of 1, namely, kincuongin (2), was deemed as non-cytotoxic (IC50 >10 μM) against five different cancer cell lines. However, co-administration of kimcuongin (2) showed an approximately 100 times potentiation of the MCF-7 breast cancer cell cytotoxicity of the previously reported flavonoid, 5,3’-dihydroxy-3,6,7,8,4’-pentamethoxyflavone (10). To provide a mechanistic basis for the cancer cell line inhibition enhancement observed, an initial in silico study on compound 10 indicated that it interacts with isoforms of the NF-κB complex. In a confirmatory western blot experiment conducted, kimcuongin (2) was found to potentiate the effects of flavone 10 in inhibiting both NF-κB and PARP-1. In vivo investigations using a zebrafish (Danio rerio) model showed that compounds 2, 3, 5, and 6 did not exhibit any discernible toxicity at concentrations up to 50 μM.


## Introduction
  Glycosmis ovoidea Pierre belongs to the plant family Rutaceae and was reported originally from Cambodia (Knang-krépeuh Mountains, The Pong Province) [1]. Species of the Rutaceae have been investigated genetically to help classify various plants in their respective phylogenetic trees, from which G. ovoidea was confirmed to belong to the tribe Clauseneae in the subfamily Aurantioideae [2, 3]. Even though G. ovoidea belongs to the well-known Rutaceae family, there are no previous phytochemical or biological studies reported on this species, with the exception of a recent article by our research group [4, 5].

  In the current study, the combined leaves, fruits, and stems of G. ovoidea were collected from Nui Chua National Park in Vietnam. Following an initial report of the isolation of the cytotoxic flavonoid, 5,3′-dihydroxy-3,6,7,8,4′-pentamethoxyflavonone (10) [4], further activity-guided fractionation of the active chloroform-soluble fractions led to the isolation of nine coumarins (1–9). Of these, the new compound 1 was characterized as 1-(7-methoxy-2-oxo-2H-chromen-8-yl)-3-methyl-1-oxobut-2-en-2-yl (S)-2-methylbutanoate. The absolute configuration of this compound (1) was determined by examination of its optical rotation and by comparison of its spectroscopic data with those of kimcuongin (2). Previously, the mechanism of action of 10 in inhibiting the growth of MCF-7 breast cancer cells was examined [4]. Herein, the non-cytotoxic coumarin kimcoungin (2) showed an interesting potentiation (ca. 100-fold) of the cytotoxicity of compound 10, when co-administered to MCF-7 cells. Thus, it is reported for the first time that the coumarin kimcoungin (2) exhibited synergistic effects on the cytotoxicity of the flavonoid, 5,3′-dihydroxy-3,6,7,8,4′-pentamethoxyflavonone (10), with both compounds occurring in the same cytotoxic fraction during the isolation process. Mechanistically, kimcoungin (2) was found also to potentiate the effects of 10 in inhibiting both NF-κB and PARP-1.


## Experimental

### General experimental procedures
  Specific rotations of all optically active pure compounds were recorded at 25 ˚C on an Anton Paar MCP 15 polarimeter (Anton-Paar, Ashland, VA, USA), using MeOH as solvent at the sodium (D) wavelength (589 nm). The ultraviolet (UV) spectra were measured using a SpectraMax M5 spectrophotometer (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA). A JASCO J-810 spectropolarimeter was used to obtain electronic circular dichroism (ECD) spectra on selected chiral molecules. Infrared spectra (IR) of all purified compounds were recorded using a Thermo-Nicolet 6700FT-IR spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Open column normal-phase silica gel chromatography was conducted with 230–400 or 40–63 μm mesh silica gel (Sorbent Technologies, Norcross, GA, USA), and open column Sephadex LH-20 gel (Supelco, Bellefonte, PA, USA). Semi-preparative HPLC was performed on a 21.2 × 250 mm i.d., 10 μm Polaris C18 column (Varian, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and a 21.2 × 150 mm, 10 μm i.d., Sunfire C18 (Waters, Milford, MA, USA) column, connected to a Waters HPLC system equipped with a LSP 717 autosampler and an LSP 2487 UV dual wavelength diode array detector. The NMR spectroscopic data were recorded on either a Bruker NMR spectrometer, operating at 400 MHz for 1H and 100 MHz for 13C, or a Bruker Ascend NMR spectrometer, operating at 700 MHz for 1H and 175 MHz for 13C (Bruker Biospin, Fallanden, Switzerland), with the solvent signals used as reference. The data obtained were processed using TopSpin 3.2 (Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA) and MNova v14.1 software (Mestrelab Research S.L., Escondido, CA, USA). A Thermo LTQ Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was used to obtain high-resolution electrospray ionization mass spectra (HRESIMS) and was operated in the positive-ion mode.


### Plant material
  The leaves, fruits, and stems of Glycosmis ovoidea were collected under acquisition number A06991 in January 2010 at Ninh Thuan Province, Ninh Hai District, Nui Chua National Park, Dahang Village, Vietnam, and under the collection number 2294528 (site no. NCH027). This plant material was collected as a small tree of about 3 meters tall with gray stems. A voucher specimen was deposited (no. 2294528) at the Field Museum, Chicago, Illinois. The plant was identified by D. D. S.


### Extraction and isolation
  The combined leaves, fruits, and stems of G. ovoidea (1.5 kg) were dried and ground, and then extracted extensively by maceration at room temperature with 3 × 2 L of methanol (ACS grade) each overnight. The resultant extract was filtered and evaporated to dryness in vacuo at a temperature between 35–38 ˚C. The collected dried extract was weighed (118.4 g) and reconstituted in 2 L MeOH-H2O (1:9). Next, the MeOH-H2O extract was partitioned initially with hexanes (2 L × 3) and then with CHCl3 (2 L × 3). The CHCl3 partition, obtained from the MeOH-H2O (1:9) extract, was also treated with 1% NaCl (2 L × 3). The three main partitions from each solvent were combined and dried using a rotary evaporator under reduced pressure to yield 11.3 g of a hexanes extract (D0002), 25.6 g of a CHCl3 extract (D0001), and 80.6 g of an aqueous extract (D0003), respectively.

  A portion of the dried CHCl3 extract (D0001 = 12.5 g), found active against HT-29 colon cancer cells (IC50 = 18.4 μg/mL) (Table S1), was dissolved in a mixture of CH2Cl2/MeOH and added to equal amounts of silica gel (70–230 mesh). The sample dried in silica gel was then loaded on top of a glass gravity column and eluted with gradient eluent mixtures of increasing polarity, starting with hexanes/EtOAc and then EtOAc/MeOH. Altogether, ten pooled fractions (F1–F10) were prepared from the sub-fractions collected. The in vitro-active fraction F6 (1.2 g) was further fractionated employing silica gel (70–230 mesh) open column chromatography (3.0 × 45 cm), using a gradient eluent mixture, starting from hexanes-EtOAc (95:5) to EtOAc-MeOH (100:0) and finally to 100% MeOH. Collections of 500 mL were analyzed using thin-layer chromatography (TLC) and ten subfractions with similar profiles were pooled. Fractions of 500 mL (F1−F10) were pooled with similar profiles after analysis using TLC. The previously known flavonoid, 5,3’-dihydroxy-3,6,7,8,4’-pentamethoxyflavone (10) was obtained from fraction F6, as described earlier [4].

  The sub-fraction 3 (254 mg) was further purified by reversed-phase HPLC using a binary eluent mixture (MeOH-H2O; 50:50) with a 21.2 × 250 mm, 10 μm i.d. Sunfire C18 column (Waters, Milford, MA, USA). Several peaks were observed in the chromatogram at a flow rate of 6 mL/min and 7-hydroxycoumarin (4; umbelliferone) and murrayone (5) were collected with tR = 11.2 min (yield of 1.0 mg) and tR = 22.8 min (yield of 1.3 mg), respectively.

  The sub-fraction 5 (237 mg) was purified further by reversed-phase HPLC column chromatography using the eluent mixture MeOH-CH3CN-H2O (25:25:50) and a 21.2 × 250 mm i.d., 10 μm Polaris C18 column (Varian, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Several peaks were observed in the chromatogram at a flow rate of 8 mL/min. The compounds kimcuongin (2) and minutominolate (9) were collected with tR = 36.3 min (yield of 2.5 mg) and tR = 68.7 min (yield of 1.0 mg), respectively.

  The sub-fraction 7 (187 mg) was further separated by reversed-phase HPLC, using an eluent mixture (MeOH-CH3CN-H2O, 20:20:60) with a 21.2 × 250 mm i.d., 10 μm Polaris C18 column (Varian, Santa Clara, CA, USA) column. Several peaks were observed in the chromatogram at a flow rate of 12 mL/min. Murracarpin (3), murragatin (7), minumicrolin (8) and minutominolate (9) were collected with tR = 15.2 min (yield of 9.8 mg), tR = 12.0 min (yield of 10.2 mg), tR = 10.2 min (yield of 6.1 mg), and tR = 68.7 min (yield of 1.0 mg), respectively.

  An aliquot of the larger fraction F8 (1.5 g) was further fractionated using silica gel (70–230 mesh) and gravity open column chromatography (3.0 × 45 cm) with a gradient eluent mixture, starting from hexane-EtOAc (95:5) to 100% EtOAc and then from EtOAc-MeOH (100:0) to 100% MeOH. The sub-fractions 8–11 (187 mg) were purified further by reversed-phase HPLC using the eluent mixture MeOH-CH2CN-H2O (20:25:55) with a 21.2 × 250 mm i.d., 10 μm Polaris C18 column (Varian, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Several peaks were observed in the chromatogram at a flow rate of 8 mL/min. Compounds 1, 2, and 6 were collected with tR = 89.2 min (yield of 2.1 mg), tR = 72.0 min (yield of 20.3 mg), and tR = 21.1 min (yield of 40.4 mg), respectively. The single crystals of 2 were grown in a mixture of ethyl acetate and dichloromethane, and the crystallographic data of kimcuongin (2) have been deposited in the Cambridge Crystallographic Data Centre (CCDC) with the accession number CCDC 2074000. These data are available, free of charge, from the CCDC via http://www.ccdc.cam.ac.uk/data_request/cif (Figure 2, Tables S3–S7).

### Cancer cell cytotoxicity of G. ovoidea secondary metabolites
  All crude plant extracts, fractions, and pure compounds obtained from G. ovoidea were tested for their cytotoxic activity. The panel of cell lines included breast (MCF-7, ATCC® HTB-22™; MDA-MB-231, ATCC® HTB-26™), cervical (HeLa, ATCC® CCL-2™), colon (HT-29, ATCC® HTB-38™), and prostate (PC-3, ATCC® CRL-1435™; DU-145, ATCC® HTB-81™) cancer cells, using the previously published sulforhodamine B (SRB) assay protocol briefly described below [9]. PC3, DU-145, MCF-7 and HeLa cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM). HT-29 and MDA-MB-231 cells were kept in Roswell Park Memorial Institute Medium (RPMI-1640) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 10% antibiotic-antimycotic from Gibco (Rockville, MD, USA). The cells were grown in a T75 tissue culture flasks at 37 °C and 5% of CO2.

  The data obtained for the dried solvent partitions D0001-D0003, the pooled fractions F1-F10 prepared from the dried CHCl3 partition D0001, and the isolated compounds 2–8 and 10 are shown in Tables S1 and S2, respectively. The results are expressed in μM and compared to a DMSO negative control and either paclitaxel or ellipticine as positive control.


### Potentiation of cytotoxicity using combination treatment with compounds 2 and 10 in an SRB assay
  To test the potentiation of compounds exhibiting activity against the MCF-7 human breast cancer cell line, compound 10 was used alone at different concentrations in the range 0.5 nM–50 μM and in combination with compound 2 (10, 20, and 30 μM) (Figure 3), using a slightly modified sulforhodamine B (SRB) assay protocol [9]. Briefly, cells were grown to 70% confluency in the DMEM (Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Grand Island, NY, USA) medium at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Next, cells (1 × 104/well) were added to 96-well plates overnight and treated with serial dilutions of the test compounds for 72 h under the same conditions. To assess the possible potentiation, compound 10 was tested alone using different dilutions from 0.5 nM to 50 μM. Then, this compound was also evaluated at the same different concentrations in combination with compound 2 at three different concentrations (10, 20, and 30 μM). After incubation, cells were fixed with 20% trichloroacetic acid for 30 min at 4 °C and rinsed with water three times. After drying at room temperature, cells were treated with 4% sulforhodamine B for 30 min and the unbound dye was rinsed with 1% acetic acid in water. Once dried at room temperature, each sample was shaken for five min and resuspended in Tris base and, then, the absorbance was read at 515 nm on a Fluostar Optima plate reader (BMG Labtech, Inc., Durham, NC, USA). Data are expressed as the means ± SEM of triplicate samples in two independent experiments.


### Molecular docking of compounds 2 and 10

#### Molecular docking.
  Molecular docking of compounds 2 and 10 with NF-κB p65 and PARP-1 was performed using crystallographic structures (PDB: 1MY5 and 5DS3, respectively) obtained from the Protein Data Bank.


#### Protein:
  The structure of the protein was prepared by adding all hydrogen (polar and no polar) and Kollman charges using AutoDock Tools 1.5.4 [10–12].


#### Ligands:
  The starting conformation of the ligands was an energy-minimized form obtained after a geometric optimization using a semi-empirical force field as implemented in Spartan 10. Compounds 2 and 10 were evaluated by assigning the Gasteiger-Marsilli atomic charges and non-polar hydrogens using AutoDockTools 1.5.4 (http://mgltools.scripps.edu/) [12].


#### Docking:
  Blind docking was carried out with AutoDock4 software (http://autodock.scripps.edu/) using the default parameters, except for the number of GA runs (100) and the Lamarkian genetic algorithm with a local search and 25 million energy evaluations (Long. Evals.) per run. Protein was held rigid during the docking process while ligands were allowed to be flexible. The grid box size was 126 × 126 × 126 points (0.375 Å each) in the x, y, and z dimensions, with the center of the grid corresponding to the protein. The predicted docked complexes (protein–ligand) were those conformations showing the lowest binding energy. Estimated inhibition constant (ki) was calculated from the docking energy displayed by AutoDock following the equation ki=eΔG*1000RT, where ΔG is the docking energy, R is the universal constant of an ideal gas (1.987 19 cal K–1 mol–1), and T is the temperature (298.15 K).


### Western blot analysis
  To determine the protein expression of NF-κB p65, NF-κB p50 and PARP-1, western blot analysis was performed according to a previous procedure [4]. Band densities were analyzed using the ImageJ 1.52a program [13] (Bethesda, MD, USA).


### NF-κB inhibition assay
  Suspension of the cell lysates from HeLa cells treated with DMSO (0.001%, control), 2 (10 or 20 μM), or 10 (0.45 μM) or co-treated with 2 and 10 (10 μM + 0.45 μM or 20 μM + 0.45 μM, respectively) for 24 h followed by a 30 min-treatment with TNF-α (0.035 μg/ml) was transferred to wells in a 96-well plate (100 μL/well equal to 4 × 105 cells/well). Then, the NF-κB inhibitory activity was tested according to the instructions of InstantOne ELISA™ NF-κB p65 (Total/Phospho) kit from Invitrogen by Thermo Fisher Scientific.


### Zebrafish in vivo toxicity study of selected isolated compounds
  Zebrafish (Danio rerio) in vivo experiments were performed under an approved animal protocol (Ohio State University IACUC protocol number 2014A00000006, titled “Vertebrate Animal Model for Natural Product Drug Discovery”). Zebrafish were obtained from the Department of Neuroscience, The Ohio State University. An automated housing system at 28 °C with a 14/10h light/dark cycle was used. At 24 h post-fertilization (hpf), embryos were housed in 24-well plates (five animals per group) and treated for 24 h with the test compounds (50 μM) from G. ovoidea, cycloheximide (50 μM, the positive control), or vehicle (1% DMSO in water). The animals in each well were observed for developmental toxicity symptoms under a microscope (Axiovert 40 CFL Zeiss, magnifier 5 ×) and images were taken using a ProgRes C10 Plus camera.


### Statistical analysis
  Data are represented as the means ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Each assay was carried out in three independent experiments. The IC50 values were calculated using the TableCurve 2D 4v software (System Software Inc., San Jose, CA, USA). Statistical significance values were determined by an analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by a post-hoc Dunett’s test. These analyses were made using GraphPad Prism 5.0 software (San Diego, California, USA). Values of *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001 indicated significant differences between groups against the controls.


## Results and discussion
  The combined leaves, fruits, and stems of G. ovoidea (1.5 kg) were extracted exhaustively by maceration using methanol, and then partitioned into hexanes-, CHCl3-, MeOH-, and aqueous-soluble portions. The dried CHCl3 partition was further fractionated using normal-phase column chromatography. HPLC preparative methods then were used for the purification of these fractions, leading to the isolation of nine additional compounds (1-9), in addition to the previously reported 5,3’-dihydroxy-3,6,7,8,4’-pentamethoxyflavone (10) (Figure 1) [4].

  Compounds 2–9 (Figure 1) were identified by comparison of their NMR, HRESIMS, and ORD or ECD data with literature values as kimcuongin (2), murracarpin (3), 7-hydroxycoumarin (4), murrayone (5), murralongin (6), murragatin (7), minumicrolin (8), minutuminolate (9), respectively [6–8, 14–19]. Of these, the structure of kimcoungin (2) was confirmed by single-crystal X-ray crystallography (Figure 2), and the absolute configuration of 3 was determined by analysis of a Mosher’s esterification reaction [20] and the NOESY NMR spectrum. Compounds 2, 5 and 6 are all known compounds, but their NMR data are reported for the first time herein (Figures S12–S20).

  The isolate 1-(7-methoxy-2-oxo-2H-chromen-8-yl)-3-methyl-1-oxobut-2-en-2-yl (S)-2-methylbutanoate (1) was determined as a new naturally prenylated coumarin ester, which was obtained as a white amorphous substance. The UV spectrum (Figure S1) displayed absorption maxima at 256 (log ε 2.41) and 321 (log ε 2.51) nm. In turn, the IR (NaCl) spectrum showed characteristic bands at νmax 2965 (C-H), 2850 (=C-CH3), 1738 (C=O), and 1605 (C=C) cm−1 (Figure S2). A molecular formula of C20H22O6 was determined for this compound from the sodiated molecular ion peak found at m/z 381.13121 (calcd for C20H22O6Na+, 381.13086) obtained by HRESIMS (Figure S3). The 1H NMR spectrum displayed characteristic signals of a doublet at δ 7.15 (J = 8.8 Hz) due to the H-5 aromatic proton and a doublet at δ 6.31 (J = 9.5 Hz) due to the H-4 aromatic proton. The 1H NMR signal at δ 8.01 (1H, d, J = 9.5 Hz) was ascribed to H-3. Consistent with the molecular formula and the above 1H NMR data analysis (Figure S4), the 13C NMR spectrum (Figure S5) of compound 1, in combination with the DEPT 135 spectrum (Figure S6), showed 20 carbon resonances, including those corresponding to a methoxy group at δ 56.7 (OMe-7), two olefinic carbons at δ 144.1 (C-4) and 112.6 (C-3), as well as three oxygenated quaternary carbonyl carbons at δ 186.6 (C-1′), 173.5 (C-1″), and 159.3 (C-2) [Table 1]. The 1H- and 13C NMR assignments for compound 1 were confirmed using the HSQC spectrum (Figure S7). 1H-1H COSY NMR correlations to assign adjacent functionalities were observed between H-5 (δ 7.77) and H-6 (δ 7.15) as well as between H-4 (δ 8.01) and H-3 (δ 6.31) (Figure S8). In the HMBC NMR spectrum (Figure S9), long-range correlations (Figure S10) were observed from H-4 (δ 8.01) to C-2 (δ 159.3), H-5 (δ 7.77) to C-7 (δ 159.0) and from Me-4″ (δ 0.7) to C-2″ (δ 21.0). A NOESY experiment (Figure S11) was also run and a correlation between the protons of the methyl group at C-2″ and the protons from C-4″ was observed. These data were found to be very closely related to those of a similar coumarin ester using the same NMR solvent, kimcuongin (2) [6] (Figures S12–14), with a planar structure of 1-(7-methoxy-2-oxo-2H-chromen-8-yl)-3-methyl-1-oxobut-2-en-2-yl 2-methylbutanoate.

  To determine the side-chain configuration of compound 1, the specific rotation value was examined. On biogenetic grounds, the absolute stereochemistry of the 2-methylbutanyrate ester portion of this new compound would be expected to be S [21, 22]. Previous investigators have used the optical sign of an intact natural product molecule to assign the configuration of a contained 2-methylbutyrate unit if a positive rotation is observed [21]. The positive optical rotation of compound 1, [α]25D + 15.0; c 0.10, MeOH), was compared with that documented for (+)-(S)-2-methylbutyric acid ([α]25D + 14.6) [22]. Thus, on the grounds of both biogenetic reasoning and the optical rotation value obtained for this new compound, the S configuration could be proposed for compound 1. Accordingly, the structure and configuration of the new ester 1 was assigned as 1-(7-methoxy-2-oxo-2H-chromen-8-yl)-3-methyl-1-oxobut-2-en-2-yl (S)-2-methylbutanoate (1).

  In our previous study, a polyoxygenated flavonoid, 5,3’-dihydroxy-3,6,7,8,4’-pentamethoxyflavone (10) was identified from Glycosmis ovoidea Pierre (Rutaceae), and herein, nine coumarins were isolated from this same plant for the first time. The species of the Rutaceae family have been investigated genetically and phytochemically, and 8-prenylated coumarins and polyoxygenated flavonoids have been regarded as the characteristic second metabolites of Clauseneae and Citreae in this family [2]. Isolation of these two types of natural products from G. ovoidea confirms its classification in the Rutaceae family and the relationships between the Clauseneae and Citreae tribes concluded in the literature [2].

  5,3’-Dihydroxy-3,6,7,8,4’-pentamethoxyflavone (10) was found previously to exhibit cytotoxicity against the human MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 breast, MDA-DU-145 prostate, and HeLa cervical cancer cell lines [4] (Table S2). However, none of the known coumarins (2–8) was cytotoxic (IC50 >10 μM) against those cancer cell lines evaluated in the present investigation (Table S2). Interestingly, the cytotoxic potency of 10 against human MCF-7 breast cancer cells was increased around 100-fold when cells were co-treated with 2 and 10 (Figure 3).

  Nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), one of the most well-studied transcription factors, is a dimer constituted of five subunits, comprising NF-κB1 (p105/p50), NF-κB2 (p100/p52), c-Rel, RelA (p65), and RelB and involved in several biological signaling pathways. NF-κB has been regarded as a principal protein in the inflammatory pathway, and its role in the cancer development and progression has been also investigated widely [23]. Activation of NF-κB is triggered by TNF-α, which generates IκB phosphorylation by the IκB kinase (IKK) complex, resulting in the degradation of IκB by ubiquitination. The activated NF-κB is translocated to the nucleus to bind to specific DNA sequences, and many genes involved in cell differentiation, proliferation, and apoptosis are then expressed. In addition, the enzymatic activity of poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase-1 (PARP-1) and the formation of the complex acetyl transferase p300-CREB binding protein-PARP-1 regulates the activation of NF-κB. Thus, inhibition of PARP-1 has become a promising anticancer target [24, 25].

  Compound 10 was found to induce the intrinsic apoptosis in human MCF-7 breast cancer cells, through the loss of mitochondrial membrane potential and regulation of the BCL-2 protein family [4]. The binding sites predicted by AutoDock for kimcuongin (2) or 5,3’-dihydroxy-3,6,7,8,4’-pentamethoxyflavone (10) and NF-κB p65 are depicted in Figures 4A and 4C [10, 12], while those for 2 or 10 and PARP-1 are illustrated in Figures 4B and 4D. The results suggested that compound 10 binds to NF-κB p65 in a pocket formed by the amino acids Lys-195, Glu-282, Glu-285, Ser-281, Met-284, Asn-202, and Ile-195. The putative binding pocket for this flavone with PARP-1 included the amino acids Tyr-889, Glu-988, Met-890, His-862, His-937, Leu-984, Lys-903, and Tyr-907. However, 2 binds to NF-κB p65 in a pocket formed by different amino acids, including Glu-840, Lys-1000, Glu-842, Gly-843, Gln-846, Glu-844, and Cys-845. The proposed binding pocket for 2 and PARP-1 included the amino acids Tyr-907, Ala-898, Glu-988, Gly-863, His-862, Tyr-896, and Leu-877. In all cases, the main interactions that govern the coupling were hydrogen bonds ranging from 1.9–2.6 Å. Hydrophobic interactions were also observed with non-polar amino acids (Figure 4). The ki values for bindings between 10 and NF-κB p65 (1MY5) and between 10 and PARP-1 (5DS3) were 18.05 μM and 491.71 nM, respectively, (Figure 4), suggesting that 10 shows a higher affinity for PARP-1 (5DS3) than for NF-κB p65 (1MY5). Consistently, the ki value for 2 to bind to NF-κB p65 (1MY5) was 6.79 μM, and that for 2 to bind to PARP-1 (5DS3) was 57.11 nM (Figure 4). These suggest a basis for a possible mechanism of potentiating the cancer cell cytotoxicity of 10 by 2. Based on their docking profiles, it is postulated that the activities of these compounds may be additive to result in a significant potentiating effect.

  A search of NF-κB inhibitors for the treatment of different types of cancer has been prioritized recently [23, 26, 27]. The inhibited phosphorylation of NF-κB p65 observed in HeLa cells treated with 10 (Figure 5A) agreed with those found in the docking studies. Interestingly, a potentiation of the resultant NF-κB inhibition was displayed when cells were co-treated with 2 (10 and 20 μM) and 10 (0.45 μM), of which 2 exhibits a weak activity toward NF-κB. This indicates that the potentiation of the NF-κB inhibition induced by 2 and 10 can be regulated by acting at some of the key points in the NF-κB pathway activation, including the IKKs or NF-κB subunits or proteasome 26S inhibition, or avoiding ubiquitination [27]. Thus, the protein expression of NF-κB subunits p50 and p65 were evaluated using western blot analysis, and both 2 and 10 induced a down-regulation of p50 and p65 NF-κB subunits (Figures 5B–D), on which the effects were potentiated by co-treatment with these two compounds.

  Different stimuli such as oxidative stress results in DNA damage, which provokes genomic instability. As a response to protect the cell integrity, the DNA damage is sensed and repaired in part by the nuclear enzyme PARP-1, avoiding cell death. PARPs are DNA-dependent nuclear enzymes, of which PARP1 is involved in different pathways of DNA repair. This protein could also regulate NF-KB activation through different mechanisms, and thus inhibition of PARP1 may induce cell death [24, 25]. Compound 2 (10 and 20 μM) inhibited protein expression of PARP-1 (Figure 5E), but, conversely, an up-regulation of PARP-1 was induced by 10 (0.45 μM). Interestingly, co-treatment with 2 and 10 induced protein overexpression of PARP-1. It has been reported that an overactivity of PARP-1 is regulated for the executor caspase-3, which breaks PARP-1. Hence, products with 89 and 28 kDa fragments reduce and inhibit the PARP-1 catalytic activity, which is a typical process of cell death by apoptosis [25]. Thus, the overexpression of PARP-1 induced by 2 and 10 may contribute to the synergistic effects observed for their cytotoxicity.

  In our previous investigation, 10 was deemed to be non-toxic to zebrafish embryos [4] when tested in a standardized and approved zebrafish (Danio rerio) in vivo model [28]. Herein, the compounds isolated and available in sufficient quantity, including 2, 3, 5, and 6, were evaluated for their toxicity at a dose of 50 μM against 24 h post-fertilization (hpf) embryos of zebrafish under controlled conditions. Cycloheximide (50 μM) was used as a positive control, and vehicle (1% DMSO in water) as a negative control. All of these compounds were non-toxic or slightly toxic.


## Conclusions
  Nine coumarins (1–9) were isolated from G. ovoidea for the first time, and a synergistic effects of 2 on the cytotoxicity of 10 was observed. This activity could be mediated by targeting NF-κB and PARP-1, as indicated by the docking profiles and western blots performed. The isolates 2, 3, 5, and 6 were not toxic when evaluated in a zebrafish in vivo assay, indicating some promise for discovery of new cancer chemotherapeutic agents from G. ovoidea.
