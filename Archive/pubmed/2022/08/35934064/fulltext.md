# Histone deacetylase inhibition by gut microbe-generated short chain fatty acids entrains intestinal epithelial circadian rhythms


## Background & Aims:
  The circadian clock orchestrates ~24-hour oscillations of gastrointestinal epithelial structure and function that drive diurnal rhythms in gut microbiota. Here, we use experimental and computational approaches in intestinal organoids to reveal reciprocal effects of gut microbial metabolites on epithelial timekeeping by an epigenetic mechanism.


## Methods:
  We cultured enteroids in media supplemented with sterile supernatants from the altered Schaedler Flora (ASF), a defined murine microbiota. Circadian oscillations of bioluminescent PER2 and Bmal1 were measured in the presence or absence of individual ASF supernatants. Separately, we applied machine learning to ASF metabolomics to identify phase-shifting metabolites.


## Results:
  Sterile filtrates from 3 of 7 ASF species (ASF360 Lactobacillus intestinalis, ASF361 Ligilactobacillus murinus, ASF502 Clostridium spp.) induced minimal alterations in circadian rhythms, whereas filtrates from 4 ASF species (ASF356 Clostridium spp., ASF492 Eubacterium plexicaudatum, ASF500 Pseudoflavonifactor spp., ASF519 Parabacteroides goldsteinii) induced profound, concentration-dependent phase shifts. Random forest classification identified short chain fatty acids (SCFA: butyrate, propionate, acetate, and isovalerate) production as a discriminating feature of ASF “shifters”. Experiments with SCFAs confirmed machine learning predictions, with a median phase shift of 6.2 hours in murine enteroids. Pharmacological or botanical HDAC inhibitors yielded similar findings. Further, mithramycin A, an inhibitor of HDAC inhibition, reduced SCFA-induced phase shifts by 20% (P<0.05) and conditional knockout of HDAC3 in enteroids abrogated butyrate effects on Per2 expression. Key findings were reproducible in human Bmal1-luciferase enteroids, colonoids and Per2-luciferase Caco-2 cells.


## Conclusions:
  Gut microbe-generated SCFAs entrain intestinal epithelial circadian rhythms by an HDACi-dependent mechanism, with critical implications for understanding microbial and circadian network regulation of intestinal epithelial homeostasis.


## INTRODUCTION:
  Symbiosis between hosts and microbes is dynamic and dependent on environmental cues1. The circadian clock, a key timekeeper in the dance between hosts and microbes, is a 24-hour biological oscillator coordinating changes in behavior and physiology in anticipation of environmental fluctuations over 24-hour day/night cycles. The clock is centrally controlled by the suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus and entrained by light-dark, sleep-wake and feeding cycles2. The suprachiasmatic nucleus in turn coordinates peripheral clocks, regulating tissue-specific, clock-controlled genes3. In mammals, the molecular basis of the clock is an autoregulatory transcriptional-translational feedback loop involving CLOCK and BMAL1 heterodimers. CLOCK and BMAL1 are transcription factors controlling expression of core clock genes, Period (Per1, Per2, Per3) and Cryptochrome (Cry1, Cry2), which are negative elements expressed during the dark phase4. As PER and CRY proteins accumulate, they bind to CLOCK/BMAL1 thereby inhibiting their transcription5,6. PERIOD2 (PER2) is an important negative element in circadian feedback loops5,7,8.

  Diverse gut functions are regulated by the clock, including nutrient absorption, enterocyte proliferation, and metabolism9,10. Unsurprisingly, circadian disruption is associated with gastrointestinal disorders such as ulcers11, diarrhea12, obesity13 and flares of inflammatory bowel disease14. Recent work shows a connection between host circadian rhythms and the composition, metabolic functions, and localization of the intestinal microbiome, which plays a role in the entrainment and modulation of host circadian signaling15,16. These microbial rhythms, in turn, influence diurnal host gene expression in the intestine and the liver17,1817,19,20.

  More recent work has identified a pivotal role for intestinal microbiota in programming diurnal metabolic rhythms in intestinal epithelial cells through specific histone deacetylases (HDAC) using in vivo approaches21. Previously, we have shown mouse small intestinal epithelial organoids (enteroids) provide a platform for elucidating circadian regulation of homeostasis22,23. Here, we combine organoid approaches with systems metabolomics to demonstrate: 1) entrainment of the host circadian clock by metabolites from the altered Schaedler Flora (ASF), a simplified mouse gut microbiome, 2) machine learning identification of short chain fatty acid (SCFA) production as a key discriminating feature of ”shifters” (ASF taxa whose metabolites entrain circadian rhythms), and 3) an HDAC inhibitory (HDACi) dependent mechanism by which SCFAs induce circadian phase delay.


## MATERIALS AND METHODS

### Altered Schaedler Flora Strain Information
  All strains used in this study originate from the altered Schaedler Flora, a defined mouse gut microbiota. ASF strains were a gift to the Papin lab from Drs. Michael Wannemuehler and Gregory Phillips. The members used in this study are: Clostridium sp. ASF356, Lactobacillus intestinalis ASF360, Ligilactobacillus murinus ASF361, Eubacterium plexicaudatum ASF492, Pseudoflavonifractor sp. ASF500, Clostridium sp. ASF502, and Parabacteroides goldsteinii ASF519. Mucispirillum schaedleri ASF457 was excluded from the study due to lack of detectable growth in the experimental medium.


### Media Formulations
  Full formulations can be found in supplemental methods. Supplemented Brain–Heart Infusion medium (BHI) and modified Lennox Broth medium (mLB) were used to grow ASF. Murine enteroids were grown in murine enteroid growth medium (mEGM) prepared using 70% L-WRN conditioned medium mixed with 30 % basic minigut medium. Human Bmal1-luciferase (Bmal1-luc) enteroids were grown in Stemcell IntestiCult™ Organoid Growth Medium (Human) (OGM) (CAT#06010).


### Bacterial Species Growth, Supernatant Collection, and Metabolite Quantification
  ASF were grown as previously described24. Agar plates containing supplemented BHI media were equilibrated inside an anaerobic chamber for a minimum of 24 hours. Strains were inoculated from frozen stock to grow until dense. Plates were incubated for 3–9 days at 37°C before culturing in 50 mL mLB broth in 500mL glass flasks covered with a Breathe-Easy membrane (Diversified Biotech, Dedham, Massachusetts, USA). After incubating 72 hours at 37°C, cultures were transferred to 50 mL conical tubes, sealed and centrifuged at 15,000 xg for 5 minutes. The supernatant was removed and filter-sterilized (0.22 um pore size, mixed cellulose ester filter) to acquire sterile bacterial supernatant.

### Isolation of Murine PER2::LUCIFERASE and Bmal1-ELuc enteroids
  PER2::LUCIFERASE (PER2::LUC) mice25 on a C57Bl/6J background were obtained from Jackson. Bmal1-ELuc mice were received from David Welsh’s lab at UCSD as gifts from Yoshihiro Nakajima’s lab at the National Institute of Advanced Industrial Science and Technology26. Mice were housed in our institution’s vivarium facility with an ambient temperature of 22°C and a 14-hour light: 10-hour dark cycle. Mice were euthanized using inhaled isoflurane followed by cervical dislocation according to University of Virginia’s Institutional Animal Care & Use Committee guidelines.

  Murine enteroids were isolated as previously described27, plated in 24-well tissue culture plates (Costar® 24-well Clear TC-treated Multiple Well Plates), and grown in culture for 7 days before passage into additional wells or plated in 35mm dishes for bioluminescence experiments.


### HDAC3 inducible knockout enteroids
  Jejunal HDAC3ΔindKO enteroids were generated organoids as above. To induce HDAC3 deletion, organoids were treated with 1µM 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT, Sigma) for 24 hrs then media changed, and cells allowed to recover for 24 hrs. Next, organoids were serum shocked in 50% FBS for 2 hrs, then placed in organoid culture media +/− 1mM butyrate (Sigma) for 18hrs. Organoids were harvested and RNA isolated using RNeasy Kit (Qiagen). cDNA was synthesized via the Verso reverse transcriptase kit (Thermo Fisher). Real-time PCR was performed using SYBR green (Applied Biosystems) and analyzed using the following primers: HPRT forward 5’-GATTAGCGATGAACCAGGT-3’, HPRT reverse 5’-CCTCCCATCTCCTTCATGACA-3’; PER2 forward 5’-CTGGCTCAGCCCTGTCTAGAA-3’, PER2 reverse 5’-AGTCAATGCTTCCAAAGTATTTGCT-3’; HDAC3 Forward 5’-TTGGTATCCTGGAGCTGCTT-3’, HDAC3 Reverse: 5’-GACCCGGTCAGTGAGGTAGA-3’


### Human Bmal1-luc enteroid and colonoid generation; Per2-luc Caco-2 cell generation
  Human intestinal enteroids (HIEs) and colonoids (HICs) (a gift from Noah Shroyer’s lab at Baylor College of Medicine) transduced with pABpuro-BluF (Addgene) plasmid DNA containing 1kb of the mouse Bmal1 promoter fused to luciferase (Bmal1-luciferase)28. Human Caco-2 cells were transfected with Per2-Red-Luciferase-PEST-PGK-purorm which was constructed and cloned into the CSII-EF-MCS lentivirus plasmid by the Viral Vector Core in Cincinnati Children’s Hospital Medical Center. Details of cell lines and transduction protocols are provided in supplemental methods.


### Bioluminescence Recording
  3D murine PER2::LUC and Bmal1-ELuc enteroids, human Bmal1-luc enteroids and colonoids, or 2D Per2-luc Caco-2 monolayers were passaged and plated on 35 mm dishes supplemented with 2mL of either mEGM, IntestiCult OGM or Caco2 media respectively. The next day, PER2::LUC, Bmal1-Eluc or Bmal1-luc expression was synchronized by adding 50% FBS for 2 hours29. After synchronization, medium was replaced with appropriate fresh medium, supplemented with either ASF filter-sterilized supernatants or microbial metabolites sodium acetate, sodium formate, sodium butyrate, isovaleric acid, sodium propionate, choline chloride, nicotinamide, or L-glutamine (GlutaMAX, Gibco). PER2::LUC, Per2-luc, Bmal1-ELluc or Bmal1-luc activity was measured in real-time by bioluminescence recording in a Kronos Dio™ AB-2550 incubating luminometer (ATTO Corporation) for 72 hours. Cell exposure to supernatants or metabolites exposure was apical in Per2-luc Caco-2 monolayers and basolateral in 3D bioluminescent enteroid and colonoid lines.


### Data Analysis and Processing
  R code was created to analyze, filter out noise, and normalize detrended data obtained from the Kronos Dio™ AB-2550 incubating luminometer (ATTO Corporation). Using peak, zero, and trough identification, the instantaneous period and amplitude of the circadian rhythms were calculated and interpolated. Additionally, phase shifts were calculated by comparing two rhythms (i.e. control vs. experimental). Code available at: https://github.com/Tjmoutinho/GI_circadian_rhythms. Differences in amplitude and period between either ASF supernatants or metabolites were compared with 25% mLB or media vehicle controls, respectively, by Mann-Whitney U test.


### Machine Learning with Metabolomics Data
  Metabolomics of ASF spent media were used to identify metabolites associated with circadian shifts24. The R package AUCRF (v. 1.1) was used to generate a Random Forest (RF) model to classify samples as shifters or non-shifters. Normalized metabolomics data were used to train the model by treating all metabolites with a median greater than zero and greater than the non-shifter median as features. Features meeting the described requirements were produced in the majority of samples, allowing in vitro investigation, whereas all other features were removed from the analysis. During the creation of the optimal RF, we used 5-fold cross validation. The optimal forest had 2000 trees with a node size of 5, and classes were manually weighted (using the ‘classwt’ parameter) to account for imbalance between the shifter and non-shifter classes.


## RESULTS :

### Metabolites from ASF species differentially modulate the intestinal epithelial clock
  Incubating PER2::LUC enteroids with bacterial supernatants from ASF356, ASF492, ASF500 and ASF519 significantly altered circadian phase (Figure 1B) whereas supernatants from ASF360, ASF361 and ASF502 did not (Figure 1A). Mann-Whitney U test indicated significant phase delays in hours (h) with ASF356 (10%, median=7.09h, P<0.05; 25%, median=10.89h, P<0.05), ASF492 (25%, median=3.37h, P<0.05), ASF500 (10%, median=2.2h, P<0.05; 25%, median=7.67h, P<0.05), and ASF519 (10%, median=8.62h, P<0.05; 25%, median=14.3h, P<0.05) relative to enteroids incubated with 25% mLB alone (Figure 2). We observed similar findings in murine Bmal1-ELuc enteroids, with significant phase delays at 10% concentrations of ASF356 (median=10.51h, P<0.05) relative to enteroids exposed to 25% mLB alone (Supplementary Figure 1).

  At a 25% concentration, ASF500 dampened the average amplitude of PER2::LUC abundance (median= 0.395, P<0.05) when compared with 25% mLB alone (median=0.7032) (Supplementary Figure 2A). 25% concentration of ASF500 caused significant period changes in PER2::LUC abundance (median= 27.96h, P<0.05) when compared with 25% mLB alone (median=25.52h, p>0.05) (Supplementary Figure 2B ). Among murine Bmal1-ELuc enteroids no significant modulations in period or amplitude were observed (Supplementary Figure 3). With these findings, we conclude phase shifts induced by ASF metabolites arise from PER2 or Bmal1 modulation and not alterations in circadian period.

  We hypothesized specific microbe-generated metabolites present in ASF supernatants play an active role in entrainment of host circadian rhythms. To test this hypothesis, we analyzed metabolomic profiles from ASF bacterial supernatants24 to identify which specific metabolites drive circadian entrainment.


### Machine learning predicts metabolites contributing to circadian phase delay
  Using machine learning applied to a set of 53 detectable metabolites, we explored metabolites most likely to contribute to circadian phase shifts, identifying eleven metabolites as candidates (Figure 3A). Using a feature-reduced random forest model we identified the three microbial metabolites (acetate, butyrate and isovalerate) most highly associated with circadian shifts (Figure 3B&C). These three short chain fatty acids alone gave an accuracy of 98% in predicting ASF phenotype (shifter vs. non-shifter). Our model was not overfit to the dataset according to cross validation results: both cross validation accuracy (cvAUC) and optimal model accuracy (OOB-AUCopt) were nearly equivalent.


### Host microbe-generated short-chain fatty acids alter host circadian clock in the GI tract
  SCFAs arose as a key discriminating feature of phase shifting ASF species. We then tested the SCFAs acetate, butyrate, propionate and isovalerate and other bacterial metabolites such as glutamine, choline and nicotinamide to validate model predictions.

  In mouse enteroids, PER2::LUC enteroids exposed to acetate (10mM, median=3.72h, P<0.05), isovaleric acid (0.5mM, median=5.1h, P<0.05; 1mM, median=8.1h, P<0.05), propionate (1mM, median=3.26h, P<0.05; 10mM, median=11.74h, P<0.05), and butyrate (1mM, median=13.35h, P<0.05) caused significant phase delays in PER2::LUC abundance when compared with untreated controls (Figure 4, 5B). Initially, the experimental SCFA concentrations selected were informed by Leone et al. and Tahara et15,30. NMR metabolic profiles of ASF supernatants allowed us to approximate absolute SCFA concentrations used in ASF experiments (Figure 5A). Because butyrate production was a feature of several “shifters”, we generated a phase response curve for butyrate exposure on PER2 rhythms by exposing PER2::LUC enteroids to 1mM butyrate at multiple circadian timepoints following serum shock synchronization, revealing phase shifts ranging from 2 hour advances to 15 hour delays (Supplementary Figure 4). The extent to which zeitgebers induce a different magnitude and directionality of phase response is dependent on circadian phase at time of exposure31. Thus, phase response curves provides additional evidence for butyrate entrainment of intestinal epithelial between PER2 and Bmal1 rhythms. Formate, glutamine, choline and nicotinamide did not modulate PER2::LUC rhythms (Supplementary Figure 5). Enteroids treated with 10mM hydrochloric acid (median= −2.25h, P<0.05) and 10mM sodium hydroxide (median= −2.02h, P<0.05) displayed modest phase advances of PER2::LUC rhythms when compared to untreated controls (Figure 5B, Supplementary Figure 5) indicating pH changes are not responsible for phase delays due to SCFA’s. 10mM butyrate was not tested as this was toxic to enteroids (Supplementary Figure 12)

  Bmal1-ELuc enteroids exposed to 10mM acetate (median=3.85h, P<0.05), 1mM propionate (median=7.61h, P<0.05), 10mM propionate (median=15.87h, P<0.05), and 1mM butyrate (median=11.08h, P<0.05) exhibited significant phase delays relative to untreated controls (median=0.45h) (Supplementary Figure 6).

  0.5mM isovaleric acid (median=1.069, P<0.05) and 1mM isovaleric acid (median=1.39, P<0.05) generated an increase in the average amplitude of PER2::LUC abundance relative to untreated controls (median=0.732), whereas 10mM NaOH (median=0.45, P<0.05) and 1mM formate (median=0.620, P<0.05) produced a decrease in average amplitude (Supplementary Figure 7A). Bmal1-Eluc enteroids exposed to 10mM formate (median=0.78, P<0.05), 10mM acetate (median=0.507, P<0.05), 1mM propionate (median=0.291, P<0.05), and 1mM butyrate (median=0.407, P<0.05) displayed significant amplitude modulation of Bmal1-ELuc rhythms versus untreated controls (median=0.66, p>0.05) (Supplementary Figure 8A).

  10mM acetate (median=26.35, P<0.05), 10mM propionate (median=27.13h, P<0.05) and 1mM butyrate (median=26.59, P<0.05) caused significant increases in the period of PER2:LUC abundance, whereas 10mM HCl (median=23.7h, P<0.05) and 10mM NaOH (median=24.2h, P<0.05) caused significant decreases relative to untreated controls (median=25.7h) (Supplementary Figure 7B). 1mM propionate (median period=22.78h, P<0.05) elicited a significant period shift in enteroid Bmal1-ELuc expression when compared with untreated controls (median period=24.27h, P<0.05) (Supplementary Figure 8B).


### Histone deacetylase inhibition causes phase shifts in host circadian clock
  Histone modifications to chromatin is an established mechanism of circadian oscillations of gene expression19,32–34. Recent studies demonstrate microbiota-derived SCFAs program diurnal rhythms in the small intestine through histone deacetylases21. To determine whether HDACi plays a role in altering host circadian rhythms in intestinal organoids, we treated enteroids with pharmacologic HDAC inhibitors: trichostatin (TSA)35, SAHA35, LP-41141, MS27543, and HDAC1–242 inhibitor. In addition, we tested sulforaphane36,37 (SFN), a diet-derived isothiocyonate HDACi derived from glucosinolates in the Brassicaceae family (e.g. cabbage, radishes).

  Co-incubating PER2::LUC enteroids with 5uM TSA and 1uM SAHA produced PER2::LUC rhythms strongly resembling phase delay patterns seen with 1mM of butyrate over 10 hours of recording (Figure 6A), suggesting HDACi by butyrate influences circadian entrainment. However, TSA and SAHA proved to be toxic to PER2::LUC enteroids beyond 15 hours of exposure, leading us to search for less potent, yet bioactive HDACis such as LP-41138, HDAC1–2 inhibitor39, and MS27540.

  Enteroids treated with 75nM LP-411 (median=2.454h, P<0.05), 1uM MS275 (median=3.242h, P<0.05), HDAC1–2 inhibitor (median=2.783h, P<0.05) and SFN (5uM, median=3.674, P<0.05; 10uM, median=3.979; P<0.05) showed significant phase delays when compared to untreated controls (Figure 6B).


### SCFA and microbial metabolites alter the host clock through histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibition
  To further probe the role of HDAC inhibition in SCFA entrainment of circadian rhythms we tested mithramycin A, an inhibitor of Sp1/Sp3 transcription41, which abrogates HDAC inhibitor induction of immediate early genes such as PER1 and PER229. Enteroids exposed to 1mM butyrate supplemented with 0.1 uM mithramycin A ) demonstrated significantly reduced phase delay in PER2 expression when compared to enteroids exposed to 1mM butyrate alone (median phase delay of 11.6 vs. 13.4h, respectively, P<0.05 ) (Figure 7). This finding further supports HDAC inhibition as a key intermediary in microbe-derived SCFA induced phase shifts.


### Per2 phase shift response to butyrate is abrogated in HDAC3ΔindKO enteroids
  Recently, gut circadian responses to short chain fatty acids have been shown to be mediated by HDAC3 in mice21,42. We generated HDAC3ΔindKO murine enteroids then measured Hdac3 expression by RT-PCR at 18 hours in the presence or absence of tamoxifen, verifying conditional HDAC3 KO (Supplementary Figure 13). To determine the extent to which effects of SCFAs on PER2 expression are HDAC3 dependent, HDAC3ΔindKO and WT enteroids were then treated with butyrate. PER2 expression was measured 18 hours post-treatment by RT-PCR. In contrast to WT and tamoxifen-naive HDAC3ΔindKO enteroids, tamoxifen-exposed HDAC3ΔindKO exhibited no detectable PER2 phase shifts when exposed to butyrate (Figure 7C), indicating an HDAC3-dependent mechanism concordant with previous in vivo findings.


### Phase shifts in human Bmal1-luc enteroids and colonoids and Per2-luc Caco-2 cells exposed to microbial metabolites
  To explore concordance of our findings across murine enteroids, human enteroids and colonoids, and transformed colonic epithelial cells, we generated human Bmal1-luc enteroids and colonoids and Per2-luc Caco-2 cells. After confirming Bmal1 rhythmicity in human enteroids and colonoids, we investigated the effects of ASF356 spent media, butyrate, and propionate on Bmal1 oscillations. Exposure of human Bmal1-luc enteroids to 25% ASF356 media (median phase advance=3.18 hrs, P<0.05), 10 mM propionate (median phase advance 3.18 hrs, P<0.05), and 1mM butyrate (median phase advance=5.32, P=0.001) (Supplementary Figure 9) produced significant phase advances of Bmal1 abundance versus respective controls. Additionally, butyrate induced a significant phase advance in human Bmal1 colonoids (median phase advance 7.48 hrs, P<0.05) (Supplementary Figure 10). When exposed apically to 1mM butyrate, Caco-2 Per2-luc cells showed a consistent but modest phase delay in circadian rhythms (median=1.59 hrs, P<0.05) (Supplementary Figure 11).


## DISCUSSION:
  In these ex vivo intestinal organoid studies of microbial metabolites and intestinal epithelial circadian timekeeping, we used a combination of tissue culture experiments and systems metabolomics to elucidate gut microbial influences on host circadian rhythms. Recently, Kuang et al.21 demonstrated cross talk between intestinal microbiota and the intestinal circadian clock and role of SCFA and HDAC epigenetic pathways in mice. Our present results complement their findings by revealing: 1) SCFA’s directly entrain intestinal epithelial circadian rhythms ex vivo, in the absence of input from other intestinal cell types (vascular, immune, and neuronal) and independently of the central or other peripheral clocks, further supporting a model of locally controlled crosstalk at the microbe-epithelial interface, 2) Specific SCFAs drive distinct patterns (e.g., magnitude of phase shift, amplitude of circadian rhythms) of circadian entrainment of intestinal epithelial cells, with maintenance of a ~24-hour period, 3) Concordance of SCFA entrainment across mouse enteroids, human enteroids, and human colonoids exposed to microbial metabolites basolaterally, and a human transformed colonic cell line (Caco-2) exposed apically, a HDAC-dependent mechanism for SCFA-induced circadian phase shifts.

  We leveraged a well-curated metabolomic dataset from a simplified and defined mouse microbiome, the altered Schaedler Flora (ASF)24. ASF species prove a uniquely useful model for understanding host-microbe interactions. Mice colonized with this community can remain stably colonized for multiple generations while promoting normal organ physiology and immune system function43. Further, ASF can be cultured in vitro allowing for a more precise understanding of the community24. Measuring bioluminescence of PER2::LUC and Bmal1-ELuc murine enteroids, Bmal1-luc human enteroids and colonoids, and Per2-luc Caco-2 cells, allowed us to explore real-time relationships between host circadian rhythms and microbial metabolites. Using machine learning, we shortlisted metabolite candidates from the ASF metabolome24 for a targeted empirical approach. We observed phase delays in both PER2::LUC and Bmal1-ELuc enteroids exposed to ‘shifter’ ASF bacterial supernatants. Individually testing numerous microbial metabolites to identify key drivers posed a time-consuming challenge. With a systems biology approach, we circumvented this constraint and shortlisted SCFA as discriminating metabolite features.

  Based on these findings, we compared the effects of SCFAs acetate, butyrate, formate, isovaleric acid and propionate against non-SCFA metabolites such as nicotinamide, choline and L-glutamine to validate this model. Nicotinamide, choline and L-glutamine are metabolites differentially utilized by various ASF species and thus appropriate controls for SCFAs. The SCFAs acetate, butyrate, propionate and isovalerate caused pH-independent and dose-dependent phase delays, whereas formate and the remaining metabolites did not. These findings indicate specific SCFAs are key microbe-produced metabolites involved in regulating and entraining host intestinal epithelial circadian rhythms. Intriguingly, the role of butyrate and acetate related to circadian rhythms has been previously described in vivo, with evidence of circadian phase alteration in mouse peripheral tissue following oral administration of SCFAs30. Studies of isovalerate are limited to insomnia and depression, conditions characterized by circadian disruption44 .

  Core circadian genes are controlled through a variety of pathways and impact a diverse repertoire of processes, including immunity, metabolism, and motility45. SCFAs are potent HDACis, with important effects on histones35,46,47. Intestinal microbiota mediate daily metabolic cycles epigenetically via the rhythmic induction of HDACs in epithelial cells of the small intestine21, and HDACs regulate circadian rhythms of hepatic lipid metabolism19 in the context of diet induced obesity48,49. Recent studies have also demonstrated HDAC3-dependent SCFA signaling mechanisms in vivo42 as well as the role of HDAC3 in regulation of circadian and diurnal metabolic rhythms21. Therefore, we hypothesized that HDAC inhibition would play a role in SCFA-induced circadian rhythm entrainment in organoids.

  Our findings provide further evidence that SCFAs drive circadian phase shifts in the intestinal epithelium via HDAC inhibition. Pharmacologic HDAC inhibitors TSA and SAHA mimicked the effects of 1mM of butyrate on PER2::LUC rhythms, and a botanic, synbiotic HDAC inhibitor (sulforaphane) caused similar PER2::LUC phase delays, with less toxicity. Subsequent inhibition of HDACi using Mithramycin A reduced the magnitude of butyrate-induced PER2::LUC phase delay. In addition, conditional deletion of HDAC3 from murine enteroids in HDAC3ΔindKO enteroids abrogated circadian phase responses to butyrate, strongly supporting this conclusion.

  Exposing human Bmal1-luc enteroids and colonoids to either ASF356 supernatant, butyrate or propionate revealed significant phase advances in Bmal1-luc expression. We speculate the contrasting nocturnal and diurnal activities of the murine and human intestinal epithelium may persist ex vivo, as suggested by our current observation of SCFA-induced phase delays in murine enteroids versus phase advances in human intestinal organoids, and our recent findings of anti-phasic gene expression of Rac1, a small GTPase directly inactivated by the C. difficile toxin B subunit, between mouse and human enteroids50. An alternative hypothesis is the possibility of differential circadian phase response curves to short chain fatty acids in mouse vs. human intestinal organoids. Given our hypothesis that metabolites produced by gut microbiota influence circadian rhythms, the use of murine ASF supernatants in human organoids is unlikely to impact the translation of our results. All ASF strains have closely related, human-colonizing counterparts, and the metabolic repertoires exhibited in ASF supernatants are equivalent in human-colonizing strains. For example, ASF519 (Parabacteroides goldsteinii) is a taxa found commonly in both human and mouse gut microbiota. All metabolites identified as clock shifters from our supernatant screens are also produced by human-associated microbes. Given this, the ASF provided two strategic advantages for us. First, we possessed metabolomics data profiling the supernatant of ASF species. Second, these data were produced by growing monocultures of all strains in the same medium under anaerobic conditions, eliminating potential contributions from variable bacterial growth medium on circadian phase shifts.

  Circadian timing is a fundamental component of precision. The presence of robust circadian rhythms in human enteroids and colonoids will likely prove to be a valuable organotypic feature for future studies of the temporal dynamics of host-microbe interactions and for testing chronotherapeutic approaches targeted to the intestinal epithelium. With these findings we illuminate an HDACi mechanism by which the gut microbiota plays a role in maintaining and altering intestinal circadian rhythms. SCFAs and HDACis have been used for their anti-tumor and anti-inflammatory properties and for ameliorating conditions such as diversion colitis51 and inflammation in mouse models of DSS colitis52,51,53,54 . Others have shown depletion of colonic SCFAs increases susceptibility to experimental colitis55. HDACs repress transcription by deacetylating histones but also target nonhistone proteins,21 thereby affecting gene expression. Butyrate, for instance, increases differentiation of colonic T cells by modified histone acetylation of the Foxp3 promoter, leading to protection against colitis56,57. Additionally, sulforaphane acts through a number of signaling pathways including NF-κB and Nrf2 (nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2)58,59.

  Our study sets the stage for potential translational applications. With the advent of chronotherapy60, potential therapeutic avenues to explore including: 1) using ‘shifting’ metabolites to prime the intestinal clock to decolonize invasive bacteria or prepare for chemotherapy and 2) treatment of jet lag and sleep disorders and acclimation of shift workers through microbial metabolite-mediated host clock entrainment,61–65. Interestingly, isovaleric acid (a metabolite also found in valerian, a traditional sleep aid and anxiolytic66 ) induced an increase in the amplitude of PER2::LUC oscillations. Previous studies have shown circadian gene amplitude increases help protect against metabolic syndrome67. With further investigation, circadian rhythm modulation via the microbiome may play an important role in treating disease.

  3D intestinal organoid models contain intestinal stem cells and multiple differentiated cell types in “minigut” structures that recapitulate in vivo features, thus providing a dynamic model for understanding the relationship between the host and microbiota in relation to circadian rhythms. However, due to their complexity, certain limitations arise. First, we did not perform cocultures of live bacteria with organoids because of bacterial overgrowth and incompatibility with organoids over circadian timescales. Second, we did not establish a mechanistic role for specific intestinal epithelial cell types; however, our previous studies have identified Paneth cells as key circadian pacemakers23. Third, in this study, we observed phase shift when IECs were exposed to SCFAs on the basolateral surface of 3D enteroids or colonoids or the apical surface of Caco-2 monolayers, however we did not compare basolateral vs. apical SCFA exposure in 2D monolayers of organoids or Caco-2 cells. These, and approaches such as reverse polarity enteroids68, would test exposure of SCFAs to both the basolateral (where SCFAs transporters are present) and apical surface (where GPR receptors are present and it models the physiology) and enhance our understanding of how SCFAs modulate the intestinal circadian clock. Of note, butyrate induced more modest phase shifts (only 1 to 2 hours) in Per2-luc Caco-2 cells vs. either mouse or human organoids, perhaps as a result of differences in apical vs. basolaterial exposure or transformed vs. primary cell lines. Fourth, we performed the majority of our experiments in jejunal enteroids. The jejunal epithelium has far less exposure to gut microbiota than either the ileal or colonic epithelium69, which utilize SCFAs as a fuel source70. Interestingly, we note that 3 of 4 ASF shifter species (ASF356, ASF492 and ASF500) maintain consistently low levels in the mouse small intestine and show a sharp increase in abundance at the ileocecal valve71. Further, ASF519, which induced the strongest phase shifts in our experiments, is the most dominant ASF species in several mouse models43. Hence, host phase shifts may prove to be an intriguing mechanism by which certain bacterial species establish their niche within the gut. Although the ASF community is a murine gut microbial community, ASF519, the most potent ASF ‘shifter’ in our murine enteroid experiments is a community member of both wild type mouse and human gut microbiota72 with potential commensal properties in human disease73. In most experiments, we observed changes in the phase and amplitude of clock genes by bioluminescence, but did not measure the abundance of these genes by qRT-PCR or proteins by Western blot. In prior work we have shown Per2 mRNA corresponds to PER2-LUC activity in murine enteroids22. Further, in their original description of PER2::LUC reporter mice, Yoo and colleagues validated RNA and protein levels of PER2-Luc across a wide variety of primary cells and tissues25. Lastly, we investigated contributions of individual ASF species and HDAC inhibitors on the clock, however more work is needed to determine how complex communities of microbes impact host circadian rhythms in aggregate and whether HDACi is the central mechanism.

  Future studies are needed to explore microbe-mediated host circadian rhythms in gastrointestinal pathophysiology. Butyrate enemas are a well-studied therapy for diversion colitis and recent studies highlighted the role of gut commensal-produced propionate in mitigating colonization and invasion of Salmonella, a pathogen whose virulence is modulated by the host circadian clock74,75. Given the impact of SCFAs on the host clock, future questions should focus on the extent to which these benefits require host clock entrainment. Several gut immune cell populations are under circadian control1. Intestinal epithelial organoids cocultured with immune cells would provide a more diverse model of circadian gut immunity. Further studies are also needed to uncover the evolutionary benefits of host clock alteration by gut microbes and develop higher fidelity ex vivo models that capture the dynamic circadian flux of gut microbial metabolities in alignment with feeding rhythms.

  In conclusion, our study demonstrates gut microbe-generated metabolites entrain intestinal epithelial circadian rhythms via HDAC inhibition. In addition, we provide a new systems-based framework for linking individual bacterial taxa to specific microbial metabolites in the context of the host circadian clock, with future implications for gut chronotherapeutics.
