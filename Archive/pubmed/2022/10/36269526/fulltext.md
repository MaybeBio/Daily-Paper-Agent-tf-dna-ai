# Use of a supervised machine learning model to predict Oncotype DX risk category in node-positive patients older than 50 years of age


## Purpose:
  The use of the Oncotype DX recurrence score (RS) to predict chemotherapy benefit in patients with hormone-receptor-positive, HER2-negative (HR+, HER2−) breast cancer has recently expanded to include postmenopausal patients with N1 disease. RS availability is limited in resource-poor settings, however, prompting the development of statistical models that predict RS using clinicopathologic features. We sought to assess the performance of our supervised machine learning model in a cohort of patients >50 years old with N1 disease.


## Methods:
  We identified patients >50 years old with pT1-2N1 HR+, HER2− breast cancer and applied the statistical model previously developed in a node-negative cohort, which uses age, pathologic tumor size, histology, progesterone receptor expression, lymphovascular invasion, and tumor grade to predict RS. We measured the model’s ability to predict RS risk category (low: RS ≤25; high: RS >25).


## Results:
  Our cohort included 401 patients, 60.6% of whom had macrometastases, with a median of 1 positive node. The majority of patients had a low-risk observed RS (85.8%). For predicting RS category, the model had specificity of 97.3%, sensitivity of 31.8%, a negative predictive value of 87.9%, and a positive predictive value of 70.0%.


## Conclusion:
  Our model, developed in a cohort of node-negative patients, was highly specific for identifying cN1 patients >50 years old with a low RS who could safely avoid chemotherapy. The use of this model for identifying patients in whom genomic testing is unnecessary would help decrease the cost burden in resource-poor settings as reliance on RS for adjuvant treatment recommendations increases.


## INTRODUCTION
  Oncotype DX is a 21-gene assay predicting the probability of recurrence[1] and the benefit of adjuvant chemotherapy[2, 3] among patients with early-stage hormone receptor (HR)-positive, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-negative breast cancer. The recurrence score (RS) calculated from the assay stratifies patients by risk categories (RS ≤25 vs. >25; scale: 0-100) that inform systemic treatment strategies. Trials have established the validity of Oncotype DX in node-negative disease, and demonstrated a chemotherapy benefit for patients with RS >25.[2, 3]

  While it can spare patients the cost and morbidity of chemotherapy, the Oncotype DX assay is costly and its use is limited by third-party payors; socioeconomic factors such as insurance status have been associated with lower uptake.[4-10] To address this cost limitation, we developed a supervised machine learning (ML) model that uses readily available clinicopathologic characteristics to predict RS category.[11] Data from our validation cohort showed that the model has high sensitivity to identify patients with node-negative disease with a low RS in whom chemotherapy could be omitted. Such a model is expected to be useful in settings where Oncotype DX testing is unavailable or when resource constraints limit its use.

  Recently, the RxPONDER trial showed that postmenopausal patients with 1 to 3 positive axillary lymph nodes and RS ≤25 derived no benefit from chemotherapy, expanding the adjuvant therapy decisions made based on RS.[12] Given these findings, predictive RS models are of interest for assessing node-positive postmenopausal patients. Here we aimed to determine the applicability of the supervised ML model we developed and tested in node-negative patients to predict RS risk category (RS ≤25 vs. >25) in a cohort of node-positive patients >50 years old.


## METHODS
  After Institutional Review Board approval, we retrospectively identified from a prospectively maintained database patients >50 years old with pT1-2N1 HR+, HER2− breast cancer who were treated at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center and had RS testing (Oncotype DX, Genomic Health) from January 2012 through December 2018. We excluded patients who had undergone neoadjuvant therapy. From the medical record, clinicopathologic data, including age at surgery, pathologic tumor size, histologic subtype, tumor grade, presence of lymphovascular invasion (LVI), and hormone receptor expression level as determined by immunohistochemistry (IHC) on the surgical specimen were recorded. We defined tumors as positive for estrogen receptor (ER+) or progesterone receptor (PR+) if ≥1% of tumor cells stained positive for the respective marker. HER2 status was determined by IHC and, if HER2 IHC was equivocal, by fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) according to the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO)/College of American Pathologists (CAP) guidelines. RS testing was ordered for node-positive patients at the discretion of the medical oncologist, and RS results were collected for this study from the original reports from Genomic Health.


### Model development and statistical analysis
  Our supervised ML model to predict RS using clinicopathologic variables has been described previously.[11] Briefly, the random forest model was created using a training cohort of 2587 patients with pT1-2N0 HR+, HER2− breast cancer who underwent RS testing, and the model was validated using a cohort of 1293 patients with node-negative disease. For the current study, we assessed the model’s performance for a cohort with node-positive disease based on the proportion of cases that could be correctly classified as high (RS >25) or low (RS ≤25) risk. The model’s performance was summarized using sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) at a 95% level of confidence (CI). Statistical analyses were performed using the random forest library in R software version 3.6.3 (R Core Development Team, Vienna, Austria).

  For cases in which the model underpredicted RS (i.e., the model predicted RS ≤25 but the observed RS was >25), a breast pathologist (H.Y.W.) reviewed the hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stained slides to identify evidence of biopsy cavity changes or tumor infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) which might have falsely elevated the observed RS.


## RESULTS

### Patient and tumor characteristics
  We identified 401 patients >50 years old with pT1-2N1 HR+, HER2− breast cancer who underwent RS testing; Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of the study cohort. The median age was 63 years and median tumor size was 1.7 cm. Most patients (82%) had invasive ductal carcinoma. All tumors were ER+ with median ER expression of 95%, while 94% of tumors were PR+ with a median PR expression of 80%. The median number of positive axillary lymph nodes was 1; 60.6% of the cohort had macrometastasis while 39.4% had micrometastases.


### Model prediction of RS risk category
  The observed RS was categorized as low-risk (≤25) for 85.8% and high-risk (>25) for 14.2% of patients. A scatterplot of observed and predicted RS is shown in Figure 1. The model’s overall accuracy for predicting RS category was 88.8% (Table 2). The PPV for prediction of the high-risk category was 70.0% (95% CI, 50.6-85.3), with a specificity of 97.3% (95.0-98.8). The NPV for prediction of the low-risk category was 87.9% (84.1-91.0), with a sensitivity for identifying high-risk tumors of 31.8% (20.9-44.4%) (Table 3). Thus, the model identifies patients with low RS with 97.3% accuracy but has an accuracy of only 31.8% in identifying patients with high RS.


### Pathology review of model-underpredicted cases
  The RS category was underpredicted and categorized as low in 36 patients who had an observed RS >25. The median observed RS in these cases was 28 (interquartile range 27-30), with 74% (n=25) having RS between 26 and 30. Of these cases, 29 (81%) had H&E slides of the surgical resection specimen available for re-review. Biopsy cavity changes were identified in 11 of these cases (38%), 9 of which (82%) were either low or intermediate grade. TILs were present in the tumor samples for 2 of the re-reviewed cases (7%), one of which was intermediate grade.


## DISCUSSION
  In this study of patients >50 years old with pT1-2N1 HR+, HER2− breast cancer, our supervised ML model was highly specific for identifying N1 patients >50 years old with low-risk RS. The majority of patients had an observed RS ≤25, and of these patients only 2% had a predicted RS >25, demonstrating that the predicted RS category correctly identifies nearly all patients who can be spared chemotherapy with minimal risk for overtreatment.

  The number of patients for whom RS testing is indicated was recently expanded following the results of the RxPONDER trial, which compared adjuvant endocrine therapy alone to adjuvant chemoendocrine therapy among patients with HR+, HER2− N1 breast cancer and RS ≤25.[12] Among the patients enrolled in that trial who were post-menopausal at the initiation of treatment (3329/5083; 65%), 5-year disease-free survival (91.9 vs. 91.3 months) and distant relapse-free survival (94.4 vs. 94.4 months) did not differ by treatment arm, demonstrating the lack of chemotherapy benefit for this group. Importantly, among many implications of these data for postmenopausal patients with N1 disease, this study highlighted the need for RS analysis to categorize patients who are candidates for de-escalation of adjuvant chemotherapy. Thus, the development of tools to select patients in resource-challenged environments who, based on known clinicopathologic features are likely to have a low-risk RS and can be spared the cost and morbidity of chemotherapy, remains an important task.

  Many models described in the literature use various combinations of clinicopathologic features and a range of methodologies to predict RS. One group, employing breast magnetic resonance imaging, which is equally expensive and more challenging to access compared to the Oncotype DX assay, found that predicting RS risk category with imaging data alone yielded specificity and sensitivity <90%.[13] Several studies have investigated simple correlations between clinicopathologic features and RS, showing that progesterone receptor (PR) status, Ki-67 expression, and tumor grade are fairly consistent predictors of RS.[14-17] Gage et al. developed a simple categorization using only tumor grade and PR status to quite accurately identify patients as high or low risk,[18] and further demonstrated that RS testing is most clinically useful for an intermediate group of patients that did not fit into their predefined high- or low-risk groups.

  Other groups have developed more sophisticated statistical models to predict either exact RS or risk category with a range of accuracy.[11, 19-24] Only two recent studies included patients with nodal disease in the development of their statistical models, although both groups included both pre- and postmenopausal patients: in a study by Lee et al., 26% of patients in the development cohort for had micrometastatic nodal disease,[22] while Marazzi et al. trained their model on a cohort composed of 10.6% micrometastatic and 26.5% N1 disease.[25] The sensitivity of both of these models was <85% and specificity <90%. We applied our ML model, which was developed using a node-negative cohort, to a clinically relevant group of N1 patients age >50 years and demonstrated an excellent specificity of 97.3%.

  Our model’s sensitivity was only 31.8%, indicating that it had poor discrimination between low- and high-risk RS in patients with an observed RS >25. Causes of a falsely elevated observed RS in patients with low-grade tumors (in whom a low RS would be expected) include a mitotically active stroma and the presence of an inflammatory infiltrate in the evaluated specimen.[26] Both biopsy site changes and TILs in the tissue block from which mRNA is extracted for RS may falsely elevate the observed RS since the genomic features of these tissue elements are also being assessed by the assay. Our slide re-review demonstrated that up to one third of underpredicted cases on the ML model were low-to-intermediate grade tumors that contained either biopsy site changes or TILs. This may have caused a discordance between the observed and predicted RS, thus decreasing the sensitivity of the model. This issue may have impacted only a minority of the cases in the study, however, since only 13.7% patients had an observed RS >25.

  The strengths of our study include the large training cohort used to develop our random forest machine learning RS model, and our previously reported analysis of a large validation cohort demonstrating good performance. This node-positive cohort is a clinically relevant group that represents the patient population for whom RS testing is being used to make decisions about adjuvant therapy. Additionally, given that the study was performed at a single institution, central pathologic review and re-review provides consistent reporting of pathologic tumor features. Our ability to re-review slides for biopsy site changes and TILs also allows us to document the real potential for a high-risk observed RS when a low-risk is predicted. Limitations include the low sensitivity of the model, which could lead to undertreatment of high-risk patients if the model is used instead of formal tissue analysis with RT-PCR. The retrospective nature of the study is another limitation, including selection bias since RS was not performed for all N1 patients during the study period. Additionally, one third of the patients in our cohort had micrometastases, which is a higher proportion than were included in the RxPONDER trial[27]; however, our group has previously demonstrated that median RS does not differ based on nodal stage (No, N1mic, N1).[28] Had our cohort included a larger proportion of patients with higher clinical and genomic risk disease, the model’s performance may have improved since it would be less difficult to correctly classify these patients as high risk. Lastly, this study is based solely on data from a single institution (both development and validation). External validation of the model will be important in order to demonstrate fidelity in other patient cohorts.


### Conclusion
  The application of our supervised ML model to predict RS, which was developed in a node-negative cohort, accurately identified patients with low-risk RS age >50 years with pT1-2N1 HR+, HER2− breast cancer (with 97.3% specificity). These patients could then safely forego adjuvant chemotherapy. The development and validation of this and other statistical models yields important quality-improvement tools, particularly for resource-poor settings where decision-making based on clinicopathologic features is useful and as the reliance on RS for decision-making about adjuvant treatment expands.
