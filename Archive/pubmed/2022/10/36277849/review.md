## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript presents a valuable and comprehensive resource: a single-cell multiome atlas of the adult human retina. The integration of this atlas with multiple orthogonal datasets (HiChIP, eQTL) and advanced deep learning models (BPNet) to prioritize noncoding variants is a powerful and timely approach. The authors provide compelling examples of how this integrative strategy can refine the interpretation of GWAS loci, moving from associated SNPs to specific cell types, target genes, and even potential molecular mechanisms. The work is technically sound in its design and execution, and the public availability of the data and models is a significant strength. However, the manuscript's primary claims of "nominating causal variants" are based on computational predictions. The evidence for causality, as opposed to prioritization, is not established. The study would be strengthened by a more explicit discussion of the limitations of the predictive models and by a clearer presentation of the validation status of the nominated interactions. The manuscript is well-written and will be of high interest to the vision research and genomics communities.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** This work will be of significant interest to several groups. First, researchers studying the genetics of complex eye diseases (AMD, glaucoma, DR, myopia, MacTel) will find the prioritized SNP lists and predicted target genes a valuable resource for designing functional validation experiments. Second, the single-cell multiome atlas itself is a major resource for the vision research community, providing a cell-type-resolved map of gene expression and chromatin accessibility in the adult human retina. Third, computational biologists and method developers will be interested in the application of BPNet to single-cell ATAC-seq data and the integrative framework for variant prioritization. Finally, the study's approach is broadly relevant to the field of functional genomics, demonstrating a general strategy for interpreting noncoding variation in any complex tissue.
- **Major strengths**
    1.  **A valuable, publicly available resource:** The generation of a joint scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq atlas of the adult human retina with over 50,000 cells is a significant contribution. The public availability of this data, along with the trained models, will be a lasting resource for the field.
    2.  **A powerful, integrative analytical framework:** The study does not rely on a single method but integrates multiple orthogonal lines of evidence (chromatin accessibility, 3D chromatin interactions, eQTL, and deep learning predictions) to prioritize and interpret noncoding SNPs. This is a robust and sophisticated approach that increases confidence in the nominated variants.
    3.  **Biologically and clinically relevant insights:** The study provides concrete examples of how this framework can generate testable hypotheses, such as the role of rs4821699 in retinal ganglion cells for glaucoma and the cell-type-specific effects of rs1532278 in Müller glia for myopia. These findings directly link genetic risk to specific cell types and potential mechanisms.
    4.  **Clear and logical presentation:** The manuscript is well-structured and clearly written. The results are presented in a logical sequence, from atlas construction to variant prioritization and mechanistic prediction, making the complex analytical pipeline accessible to a broad audience.
- **Major Concerns**
    - **Concern ID** R1-M1
    - **Severity** Major
    - **Blocking** Yes
    - **Axis** Claim vs. Evidence
    - **Claim pointer** The title and abstract state that the study can "nominate causal variants in complex eye diseases." The discussion also refers to "putative pathogenic" SNPs.
    - **Evidence pointer** Results sections "Integration of the single-cell multiome with HiChIP and eQTL data validates SNP-target gene predictions" and "Integration of the single-cell multiome with base-resolution deep learning nominates functional mechanisms for disease-associated SNPs"; Discussion section.
    - **Concern** The term "causal" is a strong claim that is not supported by the evidence presented. The study uses computational methods to *prioritize* variants and predict their functional effects. While the integration of multiple data types is powerful, the predictions are still in silico. The authors do not provide direct experimental evidence (e.g., CRISPR perturbation, reporter assays) to demonstrate that any of the nominated SNPs causally affect gene expression or contribute to disease pathogenesis. The validation examples provided (e.g., rs17421627) are from previous studies, not from this work.
    - **Why it matters** Overstating the findings as "causal" could mislead the field and lead to a misinterpretation of the results. The study's true value lies in its ability to generate high-confidence hypotheses for future experimental validation. The claims should be framed as "prioritizing" or "nominating" variants for functional testing, not as establishing causality.
    - **Resolution test** The authors should revise the language throughout the manuscript (title, abstract, results, discussion) to accurately reflect the predictive nature of their findings. They should explicitly state that the nominated variants and target genes require experimental validation to confirm causality. The title could be revised to "prioritize" or "functionally annotate" instead of "nominate causal."
    - **Concern ID** R1-M2
    - **Severity** Major
    - **Blocking** No
    - **Axis** Technical Soundness
    - **Claim pointer** The deep learning models are used to predict the effect of SNPs on chromatin accessibility and transcription factor binding. The authors state that "high-effect" SNPs are those with a significant predicted change in accessibility.
    - **Evidence pointer** Methods section "Deep learning model training" and "SNP scoring with BPNet"; Results section "Integration of the single-cell multiome with base-resolution deep learning nominates functional mechanisms for disease-associated SNPs"; Figure 6.
    - **Concern** The manuscript does not provide a direct evaluation of the BPNet model's performance on held-out experimental data. While the model architecture is based on a published and validated framework (BPNet), the authors do not show that their cell-type-specific models accurately predict the effects of known functional variants or that the "high-effect" calls have a low false-positive rate. The comparison to random SNPs is a useful control, but it does not validate the absolute accuracy of the predictions.
    - **Why it matters** The deep learning predictions are a central pillar of the variant prioritization strategy. If the models are not well-calibrated or have a high false-positive rate, the list of "high-effect" SNPs may be unreliable. A demonstration of model performance, such as an AUC on a set of known functional variants or a comparison to alternative prediction methods, would significantly strengthen confidence in the results.
    - **Resolution test** The authors should include a validation section for the deep learning models. This could involve (1) benchmarking their models against other variant effect predictors (e.g., Enformer, DeepSEA) on a set of experimentally validated functional variants, or (2) performing a "dropout" experiment where a known functional variant is removed from training and the model's ability to predict its effect is assessed. At a minimum, they should report the AUROC or a similar metric for their "high-effect" classification on a held-out test set.
    - **Concern ID** R1-M3
    - **Severity** Major
    - **Blocking** No
    - **Axis** Technical Soundness
    - **Claim pointer** The authors use HiChIP and eQTL data to "validate" their SNP-target gene predictions. They state that these data "clarified" or "refined" target gene predictions.
    - **Evidence pointer** Results section "Integration of the single-cell multiome with HiChIP and eQTL data validates SNP-target gene predictions"; Figure 5.
    - **Concern** The use of the term "validate" is misleading. HiChIP and eQTL data are used as additional lines of evidence to support the predictions, but they are not a gold standard for validation. HiChIP loops can be cell-type-specific and may not always connect a variant to its functional target gene. eQTL data, especially from bulk tissue, can be confounded by cell-type composition and linkage disequilibrium. The authors do not demonstrate that the HiChIP and eQTL data are independent of the scATAC-seq data or that they provide a higher-confidence set of predictions.
    - **Why it matters** The claim of "validation" overstates the strength of the evidence. The HiChIP and eQTL data are better described as "supporting" or "corroborating" evidence. Using the term "validation" implies a level of experimental proof that is not provided.
    - **Resolution test** The authors should rephrase the section title and text to use more accurate language, such as "Integration of the single-cell multiome with HiChIP and eQTL data supports and refines SNP-target gene predictions." They should also discuss the limitations of these data types as validation tools.

- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria**
    - **Originality:** The work is highly original. While single-cell atlases of the retina and deep learning models for variant effect prediction exist, the specific combination of a joint single-cell multiome atlas of the *adult* human retina with HiChIP, eQTL, and base-resolution deep learning for the purpose of interpreting eye disease GWAS is novel.
    - **Scientific importance:** The importance is high. The manuscript provides a publicly available resource (the atlas) and a generalizable analytical framework that will be of significant value to the vision research community and the broader field of functional genomics. The prioritized variants and target genes have the potential to accelerate research into the mechanisms of complex eye diseases.
    - **Interdisciplinary readership:** The work is interdisciplinary, bridging single-cell genomics, computational biology, statistical genetics, and ophthalmology. The findings will be of interest to researchers in all these fields.
    - **Technical soundness:** The experimental and computational methods are generally sound and well-described. The main weakness is the lack of a direct validation of the deep learning model's predictive performance, which is a key component of the analysis.
    - **Readability for nonspecialists:** The manuscript is well-written and the logic is clear. The authors do a good job of explaining the complex analytical pipeline. However, some sections, particularly the deep learning methods, may be challenging for readers without a strong computational background. The use of terms like "causal" and "validates" without proper qualification could also mislead nonspecialist readers.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The manuscript presents a valuable resource and a powerful analytical framework. The major claims need to be toned down to accurately reflect the predictive nature of the work, and the deep learning model's performance needs to be evaluated. Once these issues are addressed, the manuscript would be a strong contribution to the field.
