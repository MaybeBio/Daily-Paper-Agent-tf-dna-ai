## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text including abstract, introduction, results, discussion, materials and methods, and figure legends as provided.
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific content, methodological rigor, validity of conclusions, and alignment with Nature-style criteria. Editorial and formatting issues are noted only where they affect scientific clarity.
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors use a reconstructed ancestral steroid receptor (AncSR2) and engineered M75 variants to show that ligand-induced shifts in conformational ensembles, generated by accelerated molecular dynamics simulations, correlate with transcriptional activity. They propose this approach as a predictive platform for ligand design.
- **Visible evidence base** Abstract, full text of introduction, results, discussion, materials and methods, figure legends (as embedded in text), and supplementary figure references. No raw data files, simulation trajectories, or uncropped gels were provided.
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Raw MD simulation data, convergence metrics, force field validation details, uncropped Western blots, full HDX-MS peptide-level statistics, and source data for all figures were not provided. These are required to fully assess technical rigor.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript presents a compelling and timely hypothesis: that conformational ensembles from enhanced-sampling MD simulations can predict transcriptional outcomes for nuclear receptor ligands. The use of an ancestral receptor with engineered variants is a creative approach to dissect structure-function relationships. However, the central claim that "conformational ensembles accurately describe ligand responses" rests on correlative evidence that is currently underpowered and lacks rigorous statistical validation. Several technical aspects, particularly the MD simulation setup, clustering methodology, and the interpretation of HDX-MS data, require clarification and strengthening before the case is fully established. The work is of interest to the nuclear receptor and computational biophysics communities, but the current evidence does not yet support the broad predictive claims made in the abstract and discussion.

- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in nuclear receptor biology, structural biology, and computational chemistry would find this work relevant. The idea that MD-generated ensembles can serve as a functional readout for ligand efficacy is attractive for drug discovery programs targeting nuclear receptors. The engineering of AncSR2 variants also provides a useful platform for studying receptor evolution and ligand specificity. The methodological approach, if validated, could be extended to other ligand-regulated transcription factors.

- **Major strengths**
  1. The study addresses a significant and unresolved question: how ligand-induced conformational changes relate to transcriptional output.
  2. The use of an ancestral receptor with a series of point mutants provides a systematic and elegant way to vary functional outcomes.
  3. The combination of cellular assays, biophysical experiments (HDX-MS, binding), and computational modeling is multidisciplinary and ambitious.
  4. The correlation between MD-predicted ensemble shifts and functional data is a novel and potentially powerful concept.
  5. The manuscript is generally well-written and the figures are clearly organized.

- **Major Concerns**

- **Concern ID** R1-M1
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** Yes
- **Axis** Technical soundness of MD simulations
- **Claim pointer** The authors claim that "conformational ensembles accurately describe ligand responses based on observed population shifts" and that MD simulations "predict transcriptional responses in novel ligands."
- **Evidence pointer** Materials and methods, "Molecular dynamics simulations" and "Accelerated MD" sections; Figure 6.
- **Concern** The MD simulation protocol has several potential issues that are not adequately addressed. First, the ligands for DHT, hydrocortisone, aldosterone, and estradiol were constructed by "manually modifying the steroidal core of progesterone." This is a major approximation. The stereochemistry and conformational preferences of these ligands differ, and manual modification without rigorous parameterization and validation could introduce significant errors. Second, the force field used (parm99-bsc0) is outdated for protein simulations, and no mention is made of force field parameters for the ligands (e.g., GAFF vs. specific parameters). Third, the simulation length (500 ns per trajectory) may be insufficient to achieve converged ensembles, especially for a system as large as a nuclear receptor LBD. No convergence metrics (e.g., RMSD plateau, cluster population stability over time) are provided. Fourth, the choice of starting structures (from a single crystal structure, PDB 4FN9) may bias the sampling. Multiple independent starting structures or replica simulations would strengthen the analysis.
- **Why it matters** The central conclusion of the paper depends entirely on the reliability of the MD-generated ensembles. If the simulations are not converged or the ligand parameters are inaccurate, the observed population shifts may be artifacts, and the correlation with functional data could be coincidental. Without addressing these issues, the predictive claim is not supported.
- **Resolution test** Provide evidence of simulation convergence (e.g., time evolution of cluster populations, RMSD stability). Justify the ligand construction method and provide validation (e.g., comparison with known binding modes or experimental structures). Use a more modern force field (e.g., ff14SB or ff19SB) and ensure ligand parameters are properly derived and validated. Consider running multiple independent simulations or using enhanced sampling methods with established convergence criteria.

- **Concern ID** R1-M2
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** Yes
- **Axis** Statistical rigor of correlation
- **Claim pointer** The authors state that "population shifts, assessed by clustering ligand-bound conformations with unliganded ensembles, correlated with functional properties of ligands" and that "changes in ensemble populations were predictive of strong, weak or no agonist activity."
- **Evidence pointer** Figure 6F–H; Results section "Clustering demonstrates that ligands selectively shift conformational states in AncSR2 variants."
- **Concern** The correlation between overlap fraction and functional activity (EC50, fold activation) is presented visually, but no statistical analysis is provided. The number of data points is small (5 ligands for WT and 4-5 variants), and the correlation appears to be driven by a few extreme points. No confidence intervals, correlation coefficients, or significance tests are reported. The claim of "predictive" power is not justified without a proper statistical framework, such as cross-validation or a larger dataset.
- **Why it matters** The predictive claim is the main novelty of the paper. Without statistical validation, the observed correlation could be due to chance, especially given the small sample size and the qualitative nature of the clustering analysis. The paper would be significantly strengthened by a quantitative, statistically robust demonstration of predictive power.
- **Resolution test** Perform a formal statistical analysis of the correlation (e.g., Spearman rank correlation with p-values). If possible, use a leave-one-out cross-validation approach to test whether the overlap fraction can predict activity for held-out ligand-receptor pairs. Report effect sizes and confidence intervals.

- **Concern ID** R1-M3
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** No
- **Axis** Interpretation of HDX-MS data
- **Claim pointer** The authors state that HDX-MS studies "support the model in which the M75L mutation shifts the ensemble conformation to a ligand-bound state, allowing the receptor to be less dynamically responsive to the addition of ligand."
- **Evidence pointer** Results section "Ligand binding and overall AncSR2 dynamics are impacted by M75 mutations"; Figure 4.
- **Concern** The HDX-MS data are used to infer that the M75L mutant adopts a "partially-active state." However, the interpretation is largely qualitative. The authors report increased deuterium uptake in the M75L mutant compared to WT, but do not provide a detailed analysis of which specific regions are affected and how this relates to known functional domains (e.g., AF-2 surface, coactivator binding site). The claim that the mutant is "less dynamically responsive" to ligand is based on a comparison of ΔHDX between apo and ligand-bound states, but the statistical significance of these differences is not reported. Furthermore, the HDX-MS experiments were only performed on the M75L mutant, not on the other variants, limiting the generalizability of the conclusion.
- **Why it matters** The HDX-MS data are used to provide a mechanistic explanation for the constitutive activity of M75L. If the interpretation is not robust, the mechanistic model is weakened. A more detailed and quantitative analysis would strengthen the link between dynamics and function.
- **Resolution test** Provide a detailed residue-level or peptide-level analysis of the HDX-MS data, highlighting regions with significant changes. Report statistical significance for the ΔHDX comparisons. Consider performing HDX-MS on additional variants (e.g., M75A, M75F) to see if the observed dynamic changes correlate with their functional profiles.

- **Concern ID** R1-M4
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** No
- **Axis** Generalizability of the approach
- **Claim pointer** The authors conclude that "this work demonstrates that an in silico approach can be used for describing ligand-specific conformational changes in NR ensembles" and that the platform "will allow structural characterization of physiologically-relevant conformational ensembles, as well as provide the ability to design and predict transcriptional responses in novel ligands."
- **Evidence pointer** Discussion section.
- **Concern** The study is limited to a single receptor (AncSR2) and a small set of ligands. The authors do not test whether the correlation between ensemble shifts and activity holds for other nuclear receptors or for a more diverse set of ligands (e.g., antagonists, partial agonists, inverse agonists). The claim of a general "platform" is therefore premature. The authors also do not discuss potential limitations of the approach, such as the sensitivity of the results to simulation parameters or the difficulty of capturing long-timescale conformational changes.
- **Why it matters** The broader impact of the work depends on its generalizability. If the approach only works for this specific receptor-ligand system, its utility as a predictive tool is limited. The authors should temper their claims and discuss the conditions under which the approach is likely to be valid.
- **Resolution test** Acknowledge the limited scope of the current study and explicitly state that further validation on other receptors and ligand classes is needed. If possible, include preliminary data on a second receptor or a broader ligand panel to demonstrate generalizability.

- **Concern ID** R1-m1
- **Severity** Minor
- **Axis** Clarity of methods
- **Affected element** Materials and methods, "Ligand binding and competition assay"
- **Evidence pointer** Section text.
- **Issue** The description of the binding assay is confusing. The text mentions "dexamethasone-fluorescein (11-DOC-FAM)" but dexamethasone and 11-deoxycorticosterone are different compounds. This appears to be a typographical error. Additionally, the synthesis of the fluorescein-labeled 11-DOC is described in detail, but the validation of the probe (e.g., specificity, affinity compared to unlabeled 11-DOC) is not fully described.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that "conformational ensembles accurately describe ligand responses" is not fully supported due to the lack of statistical validation and potential issues with MD simulation methodology.
- The claim that the approach can "predict transcriptional responses in novel ligands" is premature given the limited dataset and lack of prospective validation.
- The mechanistic interpretation of the M75L constitutive activity based on HDX-MS is plausible but not rigorously established.
- The generalizability of the platform to other nuclear receptors is asserted but not demonstrated.
- The statement that "M75I and M75L reveal very similar patterns to one another" in clustering (Figure 6A) is not quantified and may be misleading given their very different functional phenotypes (inactive vs. constitutively active).
