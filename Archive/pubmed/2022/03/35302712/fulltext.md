# Functional Disruption of Gli1-DNA Recognition via a Cobalt(III) Complex


## Abstract
  The aberrant activation of the Gli family of zinc finger transcription factors (ZFTFs) is associated with several types of human cancer, including medulloblastoma and basal cell carcinoma. We have reported the use of cobalt(III) Schiff-base complexes (Co(III)-sb) as potent inhibitors of ZFTFs in vivo. These complexes inhibit transcription by displacing the zinc finger domain’s structural Zn(II) ion, destabilizing the alpha helix necessary for DNA recognition. Here, we describe the use of Co(III)-sb complexes for the selective inhibition of Gli1. Spectroscopic and computational studies of the Gli1 DNA binding domain found that Co(III)-sb displaced Zn(II) through direct coordination with the His residues of the Cys2His2 Zn(II) binding site. As a result, there is a dose-dependent degradation of the alpha-helix content in the DNA binding domain of Gli1 and corresponding inhibition of consensus sequence recognition. We conclude that this strategy is well suited for the development of new and potent inhibitors of Gli1.


## Introduction
  The aberrant overexpression of the Gli family of zinc finger transcription factors (ZFTFs) leads to the overactivation of the Hedgehog (Hh) pathway and is implicated in the growth and metastasis of several tumor types. These include basal cell carcinoma (BCC), medulloblastoma, and pancreatic adenocarcinoma[1]. Due to its central role in cell proliferation, patterning, and migration, inhibiting the activity of the Hh pathway has become a means of treating these types of tumors[1a, 1e, 2]. However, while there are a small number of FDA-approved treatments for these particular types of cancers, very few strategies are designed to target Hh overactivation[3].

  Previous research has targeted proteins involved early in the Hh signaling pathway but has had limited success in the clinic due to developed drug resistance[1a, 1a, 4]. This is particularly true in treating BCC where the only approved Hh-specific anti-cancer treatment is Vismodegib – an inhibitor of the early Hh signaling protein, Smothened (Smo) [1c, 1e, 2a]. However, drug resistance to Visomidegib is frequently seen by several gain-of-function mutations in the Smo protein itself[1c]. A potentially more effective approach to developing inhibitors of the Hh activation has been targeting the ZFTF Gli1, which is the terminal step in the Hh pathway[1a, 2b, 4]. Targeting Gli1 may avoid the difficulty of mutation-driven drug resistance from targeting upstream signaling proteins.

  In contrast, the rational design of therapeutics that target transcription factors has proven particularly challenging due to the lack of structural “hot spots” typically utilized in drug development[5]. For example, the GANT family of small molecule antagonists are the only known direct inhibitors of Gli1. Still, they are limited by a lack of stability and specificity in vivo, hindering potency [1a, 2b].

  We have previously reported the use of DNA-targeted cobalt(III) Schiff-base complexes for in vivo inhibition of Snail1 and cubitus interruptus (Ci) ZFTFs[6]. These studies used the consensus sequences identified for these transcription factors conjugated to Co(III)-sb as targeting groups and then injected into X. Laevis or Drosophila embryos (Snail1 and Ci, respectively). Treatment with our Co(III)-sb complexes resulted in embryo phenotypes indicative of the inhibition of Snail1 and Ci transcription. Inhibition is hypothesized to occur through the displacement of the native Zn(II) ion from the Cys2His2 metal binding site common to many ββα zinc finger motifs[2c]. Once the Co(III)-sb complex is bound to a His, the coordination geometry of the metal binding site is converted from tetrahedral to octahedral. As a result, the alpha-helix secondary structure required for DNA recognition is distorted[6b, 7].

  Zinc finger model systems consisting of small peptide domains were synthesized to confirm that Co(III)-sb displaces Zn(II) from Cys2His2 binding sites through direct coordination to the imidazole side chain of His residues[7]. Furthermore, this coordination was shown to attenuate the alpha-helix content of these model peptide derivatives. These promising results encouraged us to exploit this strategy to create an inhibitor of Gli1-DNA recognition by this mechanism (Scheme 1). In addition to our research focusing on the inhibition of zinc finger proteins with Co(III)-sb complexes, two other examples of inorganic complexes consisting of Au(I/III) and Co(III) have been utilized in a similar fashion[8].

  The Schiff-base ligand that binds Co(III) provides significant advantages over other Gli inhibitors due to its modularity, irreversible binding, and increased potency.[6b] We have utilized this ligand as a scaffold to conjugate various targeting ligands such as DNA, peptides, and small molecules[6a, 9]. Within this architecture, it is possible to tune both specificity and bioavailability while retaining the potent inhibitory mechanism of Co(III)-His coordination. Our previous work has demonstrated that the dative bond between the imidazole nitrogen of the His sidechain is irreversible under biological conditions (Scheme 1)[6b, 9-10] As a result, this approach produces a far more potent inhibitor than other known small molecule Gli1 inhibitors (such as GANT-61). GANT-61 relies on reversible non-covalent interactions to bind Gli1[11]. Measured IC50 values for GANT-61 with recombinantly expressed Gli1 are typically on the order of a ten-fold molar excess with respect to Gli1 concentration[11].

  Here, we report a combined experimental and computational approach to investigate the effect of Co(III)-sb coordination on the secondary structure and DNA recognition of the Gli1 DNA binding domain. These studies are based on the only available crystallographic model of Gli1 bound to its DNA consensus sequence (PDBID: 2GLI)[12]. Molecular Dynamics (MD) was utilized to predict the effect of Co(III)-sb binding on the alpha-helical content of both the isolated first zinc finger (Gli1-ZF1) of Gli1, as well as the complete five finger DNA binding domain. Our MD studies begin with Co(III)-sb already coordinated to the Zn(II) binding pocket in a manner described in (Scheme 1) and our previously published work[7, 9, 13]. Next, the Co(III)-sb complex and its coordinating His residues were parameterized using density functional theory (DFT)[13]. These simulations predicted Co(III)-sb reduces alpha helix content and weakens the interactions between Gli1 and its consensus sequence. These findings were corroborated with experimental studies of Gli1 secondary structure and DNA binding assays.


## Results

### Molecular Dynamics simulations show the effect of Co(III)-sb coordination on Gli1-ZF1
  To efficiently and rigorously model the effect of Co(III)-sb coordination on Gli1 secondary structure, we first established appropriate simulation parameters. These include simulation equilibration time, selection of thermostat-barostat model, and Co(III)-sb binding modes. Due to its low molecular weight and reduced computational demand, the first zinc finger motif of the Gli1 DNA binding domain was used to optimize these parameters (Gli1-ZF1, three kDa). The selection of equilibration time and thermostat-barostat are described in the Supplemental Information. Because of the high degree of structural homology between each zinc finger motif, we believe these optimized parameters are sufficient to model the effect of Co(III)-sb coordination on the entire DNA binding domain.

  A benefit to using the Gli1-ZF1 sequence as a model system is because it has sufficient solubility for the spectroscopic experiments required to experimentally test a fundamental assumption of our molecular dynamics systems: that the Co(III)-sb complex binds Gli1 zinc finger motifs by direct coordination with His residues in the Cys2His2 Zn(II) binding site (Scheme 1). Unlike the recombinantly expressed complete binding domain, Gli1-ZF1 is soluble up to 1 mM. This concentration is sufficient for 2D-Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (2D-NMR) and mass spectrometry (MS) binding studies, as shown in Figure 2B-C. The MD and spectroscopic experiments with Gli1-ZF1 provide a critical foundation for developing and evaluating the larger MD systems of the complete Gli1 DNA binding domain.

  MD simulations were conducted on four central systems using the Gli1-ZF1 fragment shown in Figure 1: (1) A Zn(II) bound system as the untreated control (Figure 1A), (2) a His-bidentate bound Co(III)-sb with both His residues in the Zn(II) binding site (Figure 1B), (3) a monodentate bound Co(III)-sb with only a single His residue (Figure 1C), and (4) an apo structure (Figure 1D) were constructed using our derived Co(III)-sb parameters[13]. The his-bidentate system (Figure 1B) represents our hypothesis that Co(III)-sb is coordinated in an axially bidentate fashion within the Zn(II) binding site. Each system’s secondary structure content was determined with the DSSP algorithm[14].

  The His-bidentate bound Co(III)-sb showed the most significant decrease in alpha-helix content compared to the Zn(II) structure as demonstrated by DSSP plots (alpha-helix content in blue, Figure 1A-D). Statistics from triplicate simulations for each system show that while the bidentate Co(III)-sb and Zn(II) are significantly different, while the apo and monodentate Co(III)-sb simulations show minimal difference from the native Zn(II) (Figure 1E). This is likely due to added strain that the His-bidentate coordination places on the peptide’s backbone [6b, 7]. Both Co(III)-sb models showed an increase in random coil structure and general protein disorder. The significant increase in backbone RMSD for the bidentate Co(III)-sb model compared to the Zn(II) model corroborates the general degradation of the helical content to a disordered random coil (Figure 1F).


### Spectroscopic analysis of Co(III)-sb binding to Gli1-ZF1 and its effect on secondary structure
  Gli1-ZF1 (Figure 2D) was synthesized using solid-phase peptide synthesis and characterized by electrospray mass spectrometry (ESI-MS), 2D-Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (2D-NMR), and circular dichroism (CD) studies (Figure 2A-C). All spectroscopy experiments were conducted in a 10x molar excess of Zn(II) ions to test competitive coordination of Co(III)-sb with respect to Zn(II). In addition, ESI-MS was used to probe the coordination of Co(III)-sb to Gli1-ZF1 (Figure 2C). The 3+, 4+, and 5+ m/z of the Gli1-ZF1/Co(III)-sb complex were observed at 1238, 929, and 747 m/z, respectively. In addition, the 4+ and 5+ states of the apoprotein were also observed.

  1H 2D-TOCSY (total correlation spectroscopy) experiments were conducted, and a ~0.5–0.7 ppm upfield shift in the aromatic imidazole protons was observed upon Co(III)-sb coordination (H2, H5 Figure 2E) which is consistent with our group’s previous 2D-TOCSY experiments with small model zinc fingers.[7] The integrals from the shifted and residual imidazole hydrogen peaks were at a ratio of 2.2:1, which suggests that two His residues were involved in Co(III)-sb binding. These results indicate that Co(III)-sb is binding to Gli1-ZF1 via the imidazole nitrogen of the His residues and is consistent with our previously published results[7]

  CD analysis was used to experimentally measure the alpha-helix content of Gli1-ZF1 in the Zn(II) bound, apo, and Co(III)-sb treated samples (Figure 2A). The apo structure shows only a single strong feature at ~205 nm indicative of a random coil[15] The Zn(II) sample was treated with ten molar equivalents of Zn(II) ions and showed strong alpha helix features with large negative peaks at 222 nm and 216 nm. This waveform is consistent with literature values for ββα structural motifs.[5, 6d, 15a, 16] The same Zn(II) treated sample was dosed with only 1 molar equivalent of Co(III)-sb, which resulted in a significant decrease in the alpha-helical spectral features. The feature at 222 nm was reduced significantly, and the feature at 216 nm shifted to ~210 nm. These two perturbations indicate an alpha helix to random coil transition predicted in the MD simulations (Figure 1) and our previously published work[6b, 6d, 7]. Combined with the MD results, these findings suggest that Co(III)-sb can readily displace endogenous Zn(II) ions and degrade the alpha-helix content of Gli1-ZF1. These results demonstrate that Co(III)-sb complexes are viable inhibitors of Gli1 DNA binding through a similar mechanism as proposed in our previous results (Scheme 1).[6a, 6b, 7, 17]


### Molecular Dynamics Simulations of the complete DNA binding domain of Gli1
  Employing the same strategy of Zn displacement from the protein by the Co(III)-sb complex, we conducted MD simulations using the complete Gli1 DNA binding domain from 2GLI (Figure 3). We discovered that when the Co(III)-sb is coordinated to all five Zn(II) binding sites, the alpha-helical content of the protein decreases from approximately 33% to 7% of the total sequence secondary structure. Importantly, helical attenuation is “dose-dependent,” where for each Zn(II) displaced with Co(III)-sb, the alpha helix content decreased proportionally. (Figure 3 A-C, SI Figure 1). Figure 3 shows three representative models: (1). five Zn(II) bound, (2). five Co(III)-sb bound, and (3). a hybrid system where a Co(III)-sb coordinates the ZF4 and ZF5 metal binding sites and native Zn(II) coordination at ZF1-ZF3.

  This trend of a dose-dependent decrease in helical content was observed in systems with one, three, and five Co(III)-sb complexes bound (Figure S1). Importantly, we found the effect of Co(III)-sb coordination on helical content was isolated to the particular zinc finger motif to which it was bound, without any allosteric or secondary impact on the other zinc finger domains. This conclusion is supported by the results shown in Figure S2. The 2Co(III)/3Zn(II) model was repeated with the Co(III)-sb occupying distant zinc fingers (ZF1, ZF5) instead of adjacent zinc fingers (ZF4, ZF5) as in Figure 3B. The helical content and backbone RMSD for these two systems are within error of each other. These computational results were carried out on the entire DNA binding domain suggest that the Co(III)-sb complex disrupts the helical content required for DNA recognition.


### Molecular Dynamics Simulations of the Gli1-DNA interaction
  To further investigate the effect of Co(III)-sb on Gli1-DNA recognition, we conducted MD simulations on the Gli1/DNA complex from the 2GLI structure (Figure 3). 2GLI is the only known crystal structure that elucidates the residue contacts between the Gli1 zinc finger helices and the DNA consensus sequence. Therefore, it represents a crucial model system to investigate residue level interactions in the Gli1/DNA interaction. Thus, this analysis provides insights into how the structural changes induced by the Co(III)-sb complex’s binding interfere with the interactions between the zinc finger domains and the consensus DNA sequence.

  The simulations revealed that when the Co(III)-sb complex binds to the fifth zinc finger, the zinc finger-DNA distance increased substantially (Figure 4, ZF5). ZF5 has the most residue contacts with DNA and is believed to be critical for DNA recognition.[12] (Figure 4A) Three of the closest residue-base pair interactions in ZF5 (Lys 250, Ser 247, Asp 244) are significantly perturbed by the structural transition induced by Co(III)-sb coordination. The increases in the residue-to-base pair distance suggest a weakening of the interaction strength between ZF5 and the portion of the Gli1 consensus sequence that it binds to. Van der Waals interaction energy between ZF5 and the DNA decreased from 33 kJ/mol to 22 kJ/mol averaged over the last 100 ns of the MD trajectories. These results suggest that the structural perturbation caused by Co(III)-sb coordination to the Zn(II) binding site can decrease the strength of the interactions between Gli1 and its DNA consensus sequence.


### Recombinant expression and spectroscopic studies of Gli1 DNA binding domain
  The 20 kDa DNA binding domain of Gli1 (GliZF) was recombinantly expressed in E.coli and purified by Ni-NTA FPLC chromatography (Figure 5A). ZFTF domains are known to be particularly challenging to express and purify due to low solubility and their propensity to form inclusion bodies[15b, 18]. We successfully expressed and purified the domain using a 6xHisSUMO fusion tag joined with a thrombin cleavage linker.

  The tag was cleaved and removed in the final FPLC purification (Figure S4, S5). The final purified protein had limited solubility (< 0.5 mg/mL) and required a minimum of 300 mM NaCl to remain stable in the solution. This hampered spectroscopic characterization via ESI-MS and NMR, which require high concentrations and low ionic strength sample matrices. However, CD spectroscopy and DNA binding assays do not have these same limitations for experimental corroboration of our MD simulation predictions

  CD spectroscopy was conducted on GliZF to investigate the effect of Co(III)-sb on its secondary structure. We found that in the presence of 10 molar equivalents of Zn(II), GliZF exhibited robust alpha helix features similar to other multi-domain ZFTFs[5, 15a, 15b, 18e]. These features are the negative intensity peaks at 222 nm and 208 nm and the positive peak between 196–200 nm (Figure 5B)[15a, 15b, 18d, 18e]. However, these features were significantly reduced in a dose-dependent response upon treatment with Co(III)-sb. The negative peak at 222 nm is reduced in intensity, and the peak at 215 nm was reduced and shifted to 208 nm. This is indicative of a random coil secondary structure. In addition, the positive peak at ~200 nm decreased in intensity with an increasing dose of Co(III)-sb. Just as in the Gli1-ZF1 system, these changes suggest that Co(III)-sb induces an alpha helix to random coil transition in GliZF. The dose-dependent response is consistent with GliZF having multiple zinc finger domains. However, the fact that this dose-dependent response was observed in the presence of a ten molar equivalent of Zn(II) highlights the unique ability of Co(III)-sb to displace Zn(II) ions from the native binding site in the protein.

  Additionally, the molar equivalent doses of Co(III)-sb were calculated with respect to GliZF protein concentration and not the total number of Zn(II) binding sites available. This was done for clarity when comparing Co(III)-sb to other Gli inhibitors where minimum inhibitory concentrations are typically reported with respect to total protein concentration[2b, 11]. Considering that there are five unique metal binding sites per GliZF protein, this convention over-reports the minimum inhibitory concentration for Co(III)-sb.

  For example, a 1 molar equivalent Co(III)-sb dose can be considered 0.2 molar equivalents with respect to Zn(II) binding sites. This claim is based on the assumption that Co(III)-sb has no preference towards any individual zinc finger domain within this particular construct. This assumption is supported by the similar solvent accessible surface area (SASA) for each zinc finger domain calculated from the MD simulations (Figure S8). Overall, the CD data corroborate our MD simulations and demonstrate that Co(III)-sb can displace endogenous Zn(II) ions and significantly reduce the alpha-helix content of the entire Gli1 DNA binding domain.


### DNA binding assays studying the effect of Co(III)-sb on Gli1 consensus sequence interactions
  Combining our experimental and computational results suggests that the destabilization of alpha-helix features induced by Co(III)-sb coordination can inhibit Gli1-DNA recognition. We tested this hypothesis using two different DNA binding assays. The first was an electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA)[6d, 15b, 16b, 19]. GliZF samples were incubated with a series of Co(III)-sb and Gli1 consensus sequence DNA (GliCS, SI Table 1). We found that in the presence of 10x molar excess of Zn(II), Co(III)-sb strongly inhibited GliZF-DNA recognition in a dose-dependent manner. Interestingly, even a 1.0 molar equivalent of Co(III)-sb (with respect to GliZF concentration) induced an average decrease of 50% DNA binding (Figure 6A). Using ten equivalents of Co(III)-sb caused complete inhibition of DNA binding. As in the CD experiments, molar equivalencies were calculated based on the protein concentration of GliZF, not the total concentration of Zn(II) active sites. This implies that only 0.2 equivalents (or the displacement of a single zinc atom) are required to inhibit Gli1 DNA binding by 50%.

  Biolayer Interferometry (BLItz) experiments were conducted to validate the DNA binding inhibition observed in the EMSA assays and obtain a more quantitative measure of the dissociation constant (Kd) between GliZF and the DNA consensus sequence (Figure 7). Immobilized GliCS (GliCS-biotin, SI Table 1) was treated with GliZF that had been treated with Co(III)-sb. We observed a dose-dependent inhibition of GliZF-DNA binding. The observed Kd increased by a factor of 10, from 74 nM to 850 nM, with 1.0 eq of Co(III)-sb treatment. The 2.5, 5, and 10 eq samples yielded Kd values greater than 1 µM, implying negligible binding[20]. The binding specificity of GliZF for its consensus sequence was confirmed with experiments conducted using a nonsense DNA sequence of the same length as GliCS-biotin (SI Table 1). Taken together, the EMSA and interferometry measurements support our claims that the structural transition induced by Co(III)-sb coordination significantly inhibits the Gli1 DNA binding domain from recognizing the DNA consensus sequence.


## Discussion
  The combined experimental and computational studies reveal that Gli1 is susceptible to Co(III)-sb coordination which decreases the alpha-helix content of the protein and inhibits its ability to recognize the Gli consensus sequence (Scheme 1). This structural perturbation is corroborated by our MD simulations and spectroscopic studies of recombinant Gli1. Furthermore, in light of the data from DNA binding assays, we found that the degradation of helical content ultimately inhibits DNA binding to the ZFTF.

  The results are dose-dependent with respect to the Co(III)-sb concentration and where significant inhibition of DNA binding occurred at low molar equivalencies. With the addition of just 0.2 equivalents of the complex (with respect to the total concentration of Zn(II) bindings sites), a 50% decrease in binding affinity was observed. This suggests that all five Zn(II) ions do not need to be displaced to inhibit DNA binding. When compared to GANT-61 with IC50 values of >10x molar excess of GANT-61:Gli1.[2b, 11]

  The primary limitation of the current study is that the buffer conditions and low solubility of recombinantly expressed GliZF precluded high-resolution native MS and NMR analysis of the five-finger DNA binding domain[21]. As a result, we cannot experimentally determine if there is any preferred regiospecificity or stoichiometry for the Co(III)-sb interaction with the five-finger species. However, as previously discussed with respect to calculated molar equivalencies, it is reasonable to assume that Co(III)-sb coordination is equally favorable at each zinc finger motif given their comparable predicted SASA values and homologous ββα secondary structure (see Figure S8). Therefore, it is likely that MS and NMR analysis of this particular GliZF construct would yield a broad distribution of Co(III)-sb bound species that severely limit structural characterization of these GliZF/Co(III)-sb adducts[15a, 21a, 22] The approach we have taken to extrapolate the NMR and MS characterization of the Co(III)-sb/Gli1-ZF1 interaction to the complete GliZF construct is warranted.

  The results of the experimental and computational measurements described here clearly show that the Co(III)-sb complexes are highly effective inhibitors of Gli1 DNA recognition. Our results show that very low molar equivalents of Co(III)-sb reduced helical content and significantly inhibited DNA binding.

  Previously, we have used the consensus sequence of ZFTF to target Co(III)-sb[6a, 6e, 17]. This remains a viable approach for targeting Co(III)-sb to Gli1. Additionally, the computational models we developed allow for the future screening of small molecule targeting ligands to specifically target Co(III)-sb to the fourth or fifth zinc fingers in the Gli1 DNA binding domain (Figure 4, ZF4, ZF5). These regions have the most residue contacts with the consensus sequence in the 2GLI crystal structure and thus are likely the best targets to inhibit DNA binding with a single unit of Co(III)-sb[12].


## Conclusions
  We have shown that a strategy of using octahedral Co(III)-sb complexes as a new class of irreversible inhibitors of ZFTF domains has significant advantages over traditional approaches. Using molecular dynamics simulations, spectroscopic analysis, and in vitro DNA binding studies, we have demonstrated that Co(III)-sb can displace endogenous Zn(II) from the metal binding site of the ββα zinc finger domains of Gli1. MD simulations predict that Co(III)-sb binding in this manner induces an alpha helix to random coil transition, which decreases the strength of the Gli1-DNA interactions (Scheme 1). This structural perturbation is corroborated by spectroscopic analysis and showed that Gli-DNA binding to its consensus sequence is inhibited by Co(III)-sb in a dose-dependent manner. Because the overexpression of Gli1 is implicated in the proliferation of a variety of cancers, new ways of inhibiting Gli1 are required. Furthermore, we have shown that our Co(III)-sb complexes are selectively binding to the consensus DNA sequence necessary for Hh pathway activation. As a result, we believe this class of inorganic complexes are promising inhibitors of aberrant Hh activation and warrant further study and development.
