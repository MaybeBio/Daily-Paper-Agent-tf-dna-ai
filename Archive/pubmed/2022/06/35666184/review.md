## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific claims, methodology, and conclusions as presented in the provided manuscript text
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors train gradient-boosted tree models to predict which genes will respond to transcription factor (TF) perturbations using features measured in unperturbed cells, including TF binding locations, histone modifications, chromatin accessibility, dinucleotide frequencies, and gene expression level and variation. They report that TF binding data are useful for prediction in yeast but have little utility in human cells, where preperturbation gene expression level and variation dominate predictive performance. They conclude that some genes are poised to respond to TF perturbations and others are resistant.
- **Visible evidence base** Full text including Abstract, Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion, Data availability, Funding, and Conflicts of interest sections. Figures and tables are referenced but not provided. Supplementary materials are referenced but not provided.
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Figures 1-6, Supplementary Figures 1-13, Supplementary Files 1-5, and Table 1 are referenced but not included. These are essential for evaluating the validity of the reported results, including model performance metrics, SHAP value analyses, and statistical comparisons.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript addresses an important and timely question in regulatory genomics: whether machine learning models can predict transcriptional responses to TF perturbations using only data from unperturbed cells. The authors present a systematic comparison across yeast and three human cell lines, with a clear and logical analytical framework. The finding that TF binding data are predictive in yeast but not in human cells, while gene expression features dominate in humans, is potentially significant. However, the manuscript has several major weaknesses. The most critical issue is that the central claim regarding the utility of TF binding data in human cells is based on a negative result that may be confounded by the quality and nature of the ChIP-seq data used. The authors do not adequately address alternative explanations, such as the possibility that the ENCODE ChIP-seq data are too noisy or that the perturbation response measurements are too indirect. Additionally, the manuscript lacks a rigorous comparison with existing methods or baselines, and the practical utility of the models is not demonstrated. The writing is generally clear, but the manuscript would benefit from a more critical discussion of the limitations and implications of the findings. Overall, the work is of interest to the systems biology community, but the current evidence does not fully establish the authors' conclusions.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in regulatory genomics, systems biology, and computational biology would be interested in this work. The finding that gene expression level and variation are strong predictors of TF perturbation responses, while ChIP-seq binding data are not, has implications for how the field interprets binding data and prioritizes functional experiments. The methodological framework for using SHAP values to interpret model predictions is also of general interest.
- **Major strengths** 1. The manuscript addresses a well-defined and important problem: predicting transcriptional responses to TF perturbations without using data from perturbed cells. 2. The analytical framework is clear and systematic, with a logical progression from model training to feature importance analysis. 3. The use of SHAP values to interpret model predictions is appropriate and provides insights into feature contributions. 4. The comparison across multiple organisms and cell lines (yeast, K562, H1, HEK293) strengthens the generalizability of the findings. 5. The authors are transparent about the limitations of their models and the challenges in predicting responses.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **Concern ID** R1-M1
  - **Severity** Major
  umber **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Validity of central claim
  - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that "existing transcription factor location data (ChIP-seq) from human cells have very little detectable utility for predicting which genes will respond to perturbation of a transcription factor."
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section, Figure 3, Supplementary Files 3-5
  - **Concern** The central negative claim about ChIP-seq utility in human cells is based on a single dataset type (ENCODE ChIP-seq) and a specific analytical approach. The authors do not adequately address whether the lack of predictive utility reflects a true biological phenomenon or limitations of the data. For example, the ENCODE ChIP-seq data may have variable quality across TFs, and the peak-calling approach may not capture functionally relevant binding events. Additionally, the perturbation response data in human cells come from knockdown/knockout experiments with variable efficiency and timing, which may not be directly comparable to the yeast overexpression data. The authors should provide a more nuanced discussion of these potential confounders and ideally perform additional analyses to rule them out.
  - **Why it matters** The central claim of the paper is that ChIP-seq data are not useful for predicting TF perturbation responses in human cells. If this claim is not robust to data quality issues or analytical choices, the main conclusion of the paper is undermined.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should perform additional analyses to address potential confounders. For example, they could stratify the analysis by ChIP-seq data quality, use alternative peak-calling methods, or compare results across different perturbation types. They should also provide a more detailed characterization of the perturbation response data, including the distribution of effect sizes and the overlap between responsive genes and ChIP-seq targets.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M2
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Technical soundness of model evaluation
  - **Claim pointer** The authors report that models achieve "respectable" accuracy, with AUPRC values "more than twice the random expectation" for most TFs.
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section, Figure 1e, Figure 3c, Supplementary Files 1-5
  - **Concern** The evaluation of model performance relies heavily on AUPRC, but the manuscript does not provide a clear baseline for comparison. The "random expectation" is defined as the fraction of responsive genes, but this is a weak baseline. The authors do not compare their models to simpler alternatives, such as a model that always predicts the majority class or a model based solely on gene expression level. Without such comparisons, it is difficult to assess whether the reported AUPRC values represent meaningful predictive performance. Additionally, the authors do not report confidence intervals for AUPRC values, making it impossible to assess the statistical significance of differences between models.
  - **Why it matters** The manuscript's conclusions about the relative utility of different feature types depend on the accuracy of the model evaluation. If the models are not compared to appropriate baselines, the reported performance may be misleading.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should include comparisons to simple baseline models, such as a model using only gene expression level or a model that predicts the majority class. They should also report confidence intervals for AUPRC values and perform statistical tests to assess the significance of differences between models.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M3
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Generalizability of findings
  - **Claim pointer** The authors state that "some genes are poised to respond to transcription factor perturbations and others are resistant," implying a general property of genes.
  - **Evidence pointer** Abstract, Discussion
  - **Concern** The finding that gene expression level and variation predict responsiveness is based on a limited set of TFs and cell types. It is unclear whether this "poised" state is a stable property of genes across different cellular contexts and perturbation types. The authors do not test whether the predictive power of expression features generalizes to other cell types or to perturbations that are not represented in their training data. The claim of a general "poised" state is therefore not fully supported by the evidence.
  - **Why it matters** The authors' interpretation of their findings has implications for understanding the determinants of transcriptional responsiveness. If the "poised" state is not a general property, the conclusions may be limited to the specific experimental systems studied.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should test the generalizability of their findings by training models on one cell type and testing on another, or by using a held-out set of TFs that were not used in training. They should also discuss the potential limitations of their findings in terms of the specific cell types and perturbation types studied.
- **Minor Comments**
  - **Concern ID** R1-m1
  - **Severity** Minor
  - **Axis** Clarity of methods
  - **Affected element** Methods section, "TF-perturbation response data"
  - **Evidence pointer** Methods section, location not provided
  - **Issue** The description of the human perturbation data is incomplete. The authors state that they used "all RNA-seq expression profiles after gene perturbation from the ENCODE database" but do not specify the types of perturbations (e.g., knockdown vs. knockout) or the number of replicates per TF. This information is important for assessing the quality and consistency of the response data.
  - **Required correction** Provide a more detailed description of the perturbation data, including the types of perturbations, the number of replicates, and the criteria for including a TF in the analysis.
  - **Concern ID** R1-m2
  - **Severity** Minor
  - **Axis** Interpretation of SHAP values
  - **Affected element** Results section, "HMs downstream of the TSS are more predictive of responsiveness than upstream HMs"
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section, Figure 6
  - **Issue** The authors report that downstream H3K4me3 decreases the probability of response, which is counterintuitive given that H3K4me3 is typically associated with active promoters. The authors do not provide a biological explanation for this finding.
  - **Required correction** Provide a brief discussion of the potential biological interpretation of the negative association between downstream H3K4me3 and responsiveness, or acknowledge that this finding is unexpected and requires further investigation.
  - **Concern ID** R1-m3
  - **Severity** Minor
  - **Axis** Reproducibility
  - **Affected element** Data availability section
  - **Evidence pointer** Data availability section, location not provided
  - **Issue** The authors state that code is available on GitHub, but they do not provide a version number or a DOI. This makes it difficult for readers to access the exact version of the code used in the analysis.
  - **Required correction** Provide a versioned release of the code with a DOI, or at least a specific commit hash, to ensure reproducibility.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** 1. The lack of comparison to simple baseline models (R1-M2). 2. The lack of confidence intervals and statistical tests for model performance (R1-M2). 3. The incomplete description of the human perturbation data (R1-m1). 4. The lack of a versioned code release (R1-m3).
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** Originality: The manuscript addresses a novel question, as most prior work has focused on predicting static expression levels rather than responses to perturbations. The finding that expression features dominate over binding data in human cells is original and potentially important. Scientific importance: The work has implications for understanding the determinants of transcriptional responsiveness and for interpreting ChIP-seq data. However, the importance is somewhat limited by the negative nature of the central finding and the lack of a clear path to improved prediction. Interdisciplinary readership: The work is likely to appeal to researchers in computational biology, genomics, and systems biology. The use of machine learning and SHAP values may also attract readers from the broader machine learning community. Technical soundness: The analytical framework is sound, but the evaluation is incomplete, as noted in R1-M2. The lack of baseline comparisons and statistical rigor weakens the technical foundation. Readability for nonspecialists: The manuscript is generally well-written and accessible, with clear explanations of the methods and results. However, some sections, such as the description of SHAP values, may be challenging for readers without a background in machine learning.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The manuscript addresses an important question and presents a systematic analysis, but the central claims are not fully established due to the lack of rigorous model evaluation and the potential confounders in the human data. The authors should address the major concerns outlined above before the case is established.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that "existing transcription factor location data (ChIP-seq) from human cells have very little detectable utility" is not fully supported due to potential confounders in the data quality and perturbation response measurements (R1-M1).
- The claim that "some genes are poised to respond to transcription factor perturbations and others are resistant" is presented as a general property but is only demonstrated in a limited set of cell types and TFs (R1-M3).
- The reported model performance (AUPRC values) is not compared to simple baselines, making it difficult to assess the practical utility of the models (R1-M2).
- The statement that "the predictive power of histone marks is much less than that of gene expression level and expression variation" is based on SHAP value analyses, but the magnitude of the difference is not quantified or statistically tested.
