# Biological Activity
of Two Anticancer Pt Complexes
with a Cyclohexylglycine Ligand against a Colon Cancer Cell Line:
Theoretical and Experimental Study


## Abstract
  

  Because of their extraordinary ability to disrupt the natural structure of nucleic acids, metal complexes could be used in cancer therapy. In this study, cyclohexylglycine (HL) as a ligand and two new Pt complexes, [Pt(NH3)2(L)]NO3 (1) and [Pt(bipy)(L)]NO3 (2), were synthesized and characterized by elemental analysis, LC-MS, UV–vis spectrometry, FT-IR, 1H NMR spectroscopy, 13C NMR spectroscopy, 195Pt NMR spectroscopy, HPLC analysis, and single-crystal X-ray diffraction. Complex 2 crystallized in the orthorhombic Pbca space group, and density functional theory (DFT) was used to describe its structural parameters were described in detail. These complexes can be classified as oral medications and drug-like molecules based on a comparison of their absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion assessment. Quantum chemical descriptors (QCDs) were determined using DFT calculations to predict the tendency of DNA to approach these complexes. During the determination of the function of the metallodrug in DNA binding, the fluorescence data indicated that static quenching took place for all ligands and complexes with higher DNA binding affinity. CD and isothermal absorption studies indicate the presence of electrostatic and groove binding for the amine derivative and that DNA binds with the bipy moiety via groove binding. Furthermore, the interaction modes were determined using molecular docking to investigate the binding of these compounds with the target DNA molecule. According to docking investigations, binding energies of −5.7, −11.56, and −10.00 kcal/mol for HL and complexes 1 and 2, respectively, indicate partially electrostatic and groove binding. The anticancer activities of the Pt(II) complexes were tested against the HCT116 human colon cancer cell line, with IC50 values of 35.51 and 51.33 μM for 1 and 2, respectively, after 72 h. These values show that the inhibitory effect of complex 1 was better than those of 2 and carboplatin (IC50 = 51.94 μM).


## Introduction
  Cancer has been the top cause of death worldwide in recent years, and cisplatin is the main effective anticancer Pt drug in chemotherapy.1,2 Although cisplatin is still the most widely used chemotherapeutic medicine in the world, severe toxic side effects such as nephrotoxicity, emetogenicity, and neurotoxicity limit the doses at which may be prescribed to treat cancer. In recent research, considerable emphasis has been placed on metal complexes that are more potent and less hazardous than existing clinical platinum-based anticancer drugs.3−5 The beneficial and appropriate change of cisplatin’s leaving group, namely, chloride ligands, protects the chemical and limits its biodegradation before it reaches the target cells. To decrease the negative effects, amino acid compounds can be used as bidentate ligands for anticancer complexes, leading to high nutrition, enhanced selectivity and solubility, and potential transportation through the cell membrane.6 Among amino acid compounds, glycine is a biological agent and a good choice for chelation in platinum drugs to increase stability until the drug reaches the target cell. Because of the presence of glycine and its derivatives in Pt complexes with amine or N,N-donating ligands and a positive charge, noncovalent DNA interactions are more likely than covalent DNA interactions.7−9 As these kinds of compounds are more soluble in aqueous media than cisplatin, they could be employed as oral cancer treatments.10 Some complexes with the linear derivative of the glycine ligand exhibit good bioactivities and anticancer properties.11−16

  For this purpose, we synthesized and characterized a HL ligand and two platinum complexes, namely, [Pt(NH3)2(L)]NO3 (1) and [Pt(bipy)(L)]NO3 (2), by switching from the linear branch of the amino group to the cycloaliphatic branch of glycine (HL) (see Scheme 1). Experimental analyses were performed using FT-IR, CHN elemental analysis, UV–Vis, 1H NMR, 13C NMR, 195Pt NMR, and LC-MS. The structures were validated using DFT and molecular docking calculations. ADME was used to forecast and investigate Pt-drugs as anticancer drug candidates. Electronic absorption, CD, and fluorescence spectroscopies were applied to study the interaction of these molecules with CT-DNA. Using an MTT test, the cytotoxic activities of the synthesized complexes and Pt drugs against the HCT116 (human colon cancer) cell line were investigated.


## Results and Discussion

### Structure Validation Based on Spectral Data
  The HL IR spectrum is broad like those of other glycine derivatives, ranging from 2000 to 3500 cm–1. Figure 1 shows the FT-IR spectra of HL as free glycine and related Pt compounds. The carboxylate groups of HL and complexes 1 and 2 can be ascribed to the broad bands that emerge from the OH stretching vibration at 3439–3494 cm–1.17,18 The stretching vibrations of CH and NH in free HL and the two complexes were assigned in the ranges of 3335–2723, 3197–2869, and 3089–2889 cm–1, respectively. νas(COO–) and νs(COO–) for HL are responsible for the strong bands (1761 and 1418 cm–1). The redshift of the carboxylate group’s asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations in complexes 1 and 2 in the ranges of 1644–1686 and 1383–1384 cm–1, respectively, implies that HL coordinates the metal center using the carboxylate group as oxygen donor. Infrared spectra were used to distinguish between the unidentate (1708 cm–1) and bidentate (1610 cm–1) glycine coordination modes of glycine to the metal center. The glycine group, which can be called a bidentate (chelating) group, was observed at 1644 cm–1 for 1 and 1686 cm–1 for 2(17,19) The C–N stretching vibrations of HL, 1, and 2 were found at 1218, 1170, and 1204 cm–1, respectively. To assign the NO3– group as a counterion, the weak bands around ∼1050, ∼825, and ∼700 cm–1 were evaluated. Finally, for complexes 1 and 2, ν(Pt–O) and ν(Pt–N) were assigned to ∼465 and ∼550 cm–1, respectively.17

  A freq-DFT computational study was performed, and all geometries were optimized to validate the proposed structure using the UB3LYP/6311G(d,p)(LANL2DZ) method and the Gaussian 09 rev. D.01 program package20 and GaussView 6.0.1621 with the Opt-Freq keyword.12 The experimental and theoretical spectra of HL and complexes 1 and 2 are shown in Figure 1, and data are listed in Table S1 in the Supporting Information. Fittings are at an acceptable level, especially in the stretching bands of the carboxylate groups (the symmetric and asymmetric frequencies).

  The electronic absorption of all compounds (1 mM) was detected at room temperature (Figure S1 in the Supporting Information). It is worth noting that the complex’s stability was investigated. For a few days, the absorption was measured at the λmax of each complex. No shift was observed, indicating that the two complexes were stable in water and buffer solutions. #### 1H NMR Spectra
  The 1H NMR data are listed along with their numbering scheme in Tables S2–S4 for complexes 1 and 2 and the HL ligand. The carboxyl proton has a broad singlet peak in the free HL spectrum at a chemical shift of 13.68 ppm. According to refs (18−20), this signal was nonexistent in the spectra of complexes 1 and 2, implying that the carboxylate group had been deprotonated and that the carboxyl group’s oxygen atom was involved in coordination with the platinum center. The signal at 9.27 ppm for the HL ligand is attributed to the NH proton. For complexes 1 and 2, this signal can be found at 7.15 and 7.65 ppm, respectively, showing that the signal shifted upfield after the coordination of the nitrogen atom to Pt(II) in the complexes. This demonstrates that no proton displacement occurs when the NH group is coordinated with the Pt(II) ion. While the methylene signal in the amino acid moiety was a singlet at 3.89 ppm in the spectrum of the free HL ligand, assigned to the α-proton (H2), it was revealed that the two α-carbon protons arewere split into two different series in the Pt(II) complexes, becoming a doublet of a doublet in the range of 3.46 (J = 6.00 and 15.00 Hz) and 3.89 (J = 6.40 and 18.00 Hz) ppm for 1 and 2, respectively.22 Additionally, the ν(COO) bands at wavenumbers of 1644 and 1686 cm–1, which can be seen in the IR spectra of 1 and 2, respectively, confirmed that −NH2 and −COO were chelated to platinum. The bipy protons between 7.89 and 8.84 ppm in the 1H NMR spectrum of complex 2 are shifted upfield compared to those in the spectrum of [Pt(bipy)Cl2]. This is interpreted as stronger glycine binding to Pt(II) compared to chloride ion binding.23,24 Further evidence that an amine was also present in complex 1 came from the observation of the formation of a broad amino hydrogen NH3 peak. The 1H NMR spectra are shown in Figures S4–S6 in the Supporting Information.

### X-ray Crystallography Investigation
  The crystallographic data for complex 2 are presented in Table 1. Additionally, valence angles, selected bond lengths, and hydrogen bond geometries are provided in the Supporting Information in Tables S5 and S6. Complex 2 crystallizes in the orthorhombic space group Pbca. The molecular structure of compound 2 with selected atoms labeled is shown in Figure 2. The asymmetric unit is comprised of the cationic complex [Pt(bipy)(L)]+ with nitrate as the counterion, where Pt(II) (τ4 = 0.067) has a square-planar coordination geometry.

  The parameter τ4 is defined by , where α and β are the largest angles subtended at the metal center. τ4 is 1 for the ideal tetrahedral geometry and 0 for the ideal square planar geometry.25 The equatorial plane is occupied by N1 and N2 from the bipy ligand in addition to N3 and O1 from the HL ligand’s amino acid group. From the point of view of supramolecular chemistry, the cationic complex of 2 is connected and produces zigzag chains through hydrogen bonding (C2–H2···O2, 2.38 Å) along the a-axis (see Figure 3). These chains are linked to each other via hydrogen bonding (N3–H3A···O4, 2.56(7) Å; N3–H3A···O5, 2.31(5) Å; C4–H4···O3, 2.43 Å; and C18–H18A···O5, 2.50 Å) between the pyridine ring of bipy, the amino acid group and cyclohexyl ring of HL, and the nitrate ion, causing a motif explained by the graph-set notation R12(4) and thereby generating two-dimensional sheets (Figure 4). It is interesting that the structure displays bifurcated N–H···O interactions in which the nitrogen atom of HL’s amino group functions as a hydrogen bond donor with two separate oxygen acceptors while O5 from the nitrate ion acts as a bifurcated acceptor for two distinct donors from HL (N3 and C18) (see Figure 4). Furthermore, hydrogen bonds and π···π (with a centroid···centroid separation of 3.787 Å) and anion···π interactions26,27 (with a centroid···O separation of 3.796 Å) between the nitrate ion and the amino group of HL generate the third dimension along the b-axis (see Figure 5). The presence of the nitrate anion is particularly significant in the expansion and stability of this supramolecular network, as shown by the structure of this complex. The three-dimensional structure of complex 2 expanded via diverse interactions by connecting all the above-mentioned chains, as shown by Figure S16 in the Supporting Information.


### Drug-Likeness Analysis
and ADME Prediction
  A computer evaluation study was used to determine the ADME parameters of the produced HL ligand and complexes 1 and 2. Table 2 (Figure 6 and Figure S17 in the Supporting Information) shows the ADME property prediction gate. The ADME factors of all complexes were determined using Qikprop ver. 3.5 (Schrödinger, LLC). This analysis was used to calculate the molecular weight (MW), the number of hydrogen bond acceptors (n-ON), the number of hydrogen bond donors (n-OHNH), the projected octanol–water partition coefficient (log Po/w), and the topological polar surface area (TPSA). The ADME attributes of any pharmaceutical or produced molecule can be determined using the properties given above. Furthermore, all compounds had acceptable MW (157–509 g/mol), consensus log P (−0.24 to 1.45), and TPSA (43–47 Å2) values. Surprisingly, all produced drugs pass the drug-like rules, specifically Lipinski’s rule and Veber’s rule,28,29 and meet the desired drug-likeness criteria.30 The BOILED-Egg presentation in the SwissADME web tool can be used to measure the brain or gastric absorption of medication. As shown in Figure S17 in the Supporting Information, all synthesized compounds are in the yellow section, indicating that they can passively permeate through the BBB. The HL ligand, 1, and carboplatin (red dots), which are not actively effluxed by PGP–, are nonsubstrates, whereas other compounds, namely, cisplatin, oxaliplatin, and 2, are PGP+ and blue dots. As a result, we predict that [Pt(NH3)2(L)]NO3 may be a contender for an oral Pt drug. Table 2 displays the ADME property prediction results.

  The SwissADME prediction shows the lipophilicity of HL and 2. HL and 2 with consensus log Po/w values of +0.22 and +1.45, respectively, are more skin-permeant, while oxaliplatin, carboplatin, cisplatin, and 1 with negative log Po/w values are less skin-permeant. Positive lipophilicity means that 2 is an oral medication, while 1, like oxaliplatin, carboplatin, and cisplatin, is a hydrophilic and injectable medication. Additionally, the good solubility of the complexes in water facilitates drug design, absorption, and active ingredient delivery in reasonable pharmaceutical dosage; in particular, 2 can be considered a drug candidate like clinical Pt drugs.

  The bioavailability radar displays some drug-likeness factors in the pink area (acceptable area) for all compounds. To show the drug-likeness activity, size (150 ≤ Mw ≤ 500), lipophilicity (−0.7 to +5.0), polarity (20–130 Å), insolubility (S ≤ 6), flexibility (≤9), and unsaturation (amount of sp3-hybridized carbon ≥0.25) were considered, as these are the most important parameters for drug candidates (Figure 6). All compounds are soluble in water. Therefore, it is a good idea to design new drugs, such as the mentioned oral Pt drug, similar to injectable clinical Pt drugs.


### DFT Investigation
  DFT was used to optimize the ligand and complex geometry. Figure 7 depicts the MEP maps for all compounds, revealing that the O atoms of the carboxylate group, as well as the N atoms of the amino and pyridyl rings, can participate as donor and acceptor electrons in hydrogen bonds and some other DNA interactions. The Egap energy between the HOMO and the LUMO reveals the bioactivities and DNA binding affinity patterns of these compounds.31Figure 7 shows contour graphs of the energies for HL and each complex. Red and blue represent negative and positive regions, respectively. For HL, the electron density of the LUMO is focused on the glycine section, while the surface of the HOMO is involved in all ligand orbitals. For complex 1, the HOMO and LUMO electron densities are primarily concentrated on HL and the metal ion. In compound 2, the HOMO is concentrated on the chelating ring of HL and the metal ion, whereas the LUMO is located on the rings of the complex’s bipy ligand.

  The energy levels (eV) of the MOs were also calculated in their ground states in an aqueous solution for HL and its Pt(II) complexes (Table S7 in the Supporting Information). The computed quantum chemical parameters of the compounds stated above were also calculated and are given in Table 3. Moreover, numerous theoretical investigations have found that when the EHOMO of a biomolecule is high and the ELUMO of a small molecule is low, the interaction between the biomolecule and the small molecule can be considerable.32 Study of the HOMO and HOMO + 1 orbitals in DNA revealed that these orbitals are frequently focused on base-pair orbitals, whereas HOMO + n (n = 2 or 3) orbitals are located on the phosphate groups.32,33 According to the information above, binding via intercalation is more likely for complex 2 because the LUMO of this compound is located on the bipy rings. Furthermore, because of the orientation of HOMOs and LUMOs of complex 2 and DNA, electronic charge transfer occurs from DNA (phosphate groups) to complex 2 (LUMO orbital) (see Figure 8). Additionally, a list of bond lengths (Å) and angles (°) was obtained from the optimized geometry of complex 2. The data are comparable and in good agreement with the values from the single-crystal X-ray diffraction analysis and are presented in Table S5 in the Supporting Information. Following that, QCDs were calculated as an excellent method of evaluating molecular activity. Table 3 shows the obtained parameters at the theoretical level.

  The reactivity tendency is significantly influenced by the energy difference between frontier molecular orbitals. As Egap values decrease, the biological reactivity increases. Chemical hardness and softness are additional factors. The HSAB (hard–soft-acid–base) approximation can be used to talk about a molecule’s coordination tendencies toward the right structure. Cells, enzymes, and other biological structures are soft. As a result, soft molecules have higher biological reactivity than hard molecules. ΔNmax and global softness are the other variables. ΔNmax is associated with the compound’s charges. As ΔNmax values increase, a compound’s biological activity increases as well.30 Furthermore, an increase in the value of global softness indicates an increase in the biological activity of the compound. Additionally, the ranking of biological activity for each compound varies depending on additional factors such as absolute electronegativity, chemical potential, electrophilicity, and nucleophilicity indices. Low absolute electronegativity values or high chemical potential values imply that the electrons on the molecule are delocalized, making it simple for the molecule to provide the necessary electrons for the proper structure. Finally, both electrophilicity and nucleophilicity indices influence the biological reactivity. Compared to the other compounds, complex 2 has a high electrophilic index of 17.383 eV and a low nucleophilic index of 0.057 eV–1. Additionally, it can be deduced from compound 2’s MEP map that 2 can form advantageous associations with species rich in electrons, such as the phosphate group oxygens in DNA’s backbone and the oxygen and nitrogen atoms in DNA base pairs. In exchange, the HL ligand with the highest nucleophilic index of 3.029 eV–1 has a strong propensity to connect with electron-deficient species. Taking into account its MEP map and the presence of carboxylate and amine sites that are conducive to electrons, it may also be deduced that this ligand has the appropriate capacity to link with a metal such as platinum(II).

  According to data in Table S3 in the Supporting Information and the above explanations, the biological activity rankings of HL and its Pt(II) complexes are as follows: 2 > cisplatin > 1 > HL. However, QCDs never yield accurate outcomes because the acceptor and receptor secondary may chemically interact.


### Docking Simulation Data
  To accomplish this study, the HL ligand, 1, and 2 were docked on B-DNA from PDB ID 1BNA at the lowest relative binding energy for docked structures out of 2000 docking solutions. In aspects of the structure of the docked complex placed in G-C sites and DNA, grooves are stabilized by van der Waals and hydrogen bonding. Hydrogen bonds, and electrostatic energy, in particular, are effective for the interaction between compound and DNA, which is primarily a groove-binding and electrostatic interaction (see Table 4). Figure 9 shows an optimized docked structure and hydrogen bonds for HL, complexes 1 and 2 at N8–H9···O2(G22) (2.073 Å), N2–H8···OP2(T7) (1.94 Å), and N2–H6···OP2(C21) (1.90 Å), respectively. The relative binding energies of the docked structures of HL, 1, and 2 were −5.71, −11.56, and −10.00 kcal/mol, respectively. Table 4 and Table S8 in the Supporting Information show the additional parameters, such as the folding energies of both complexes.

  Based on the data in Table 4 and Figure 9, the relative electrostatic energies of the docked structures of HL, 1, and 2 are −5.74, −6.85, and −3.35 kcal/mol, respectively. The results show that the interactions of compounds HL ligand and 1 occur via the amino acid moiety and hydrogen bonding and that the electrostatic interaction gives the compounds the capability for biological activity, while 2 interacts with DNA via the bipy section and is more likely to exhibit groove binding. To investigate this possibility, a docking simulation of the optimized structure of Hoechst 33258, a minor groove binder, was performed, the results of which are reported in Table 4. According to the information in Table 4, the binding energies of the two platinum complexes are within a reasonable range when compared to the reference molecule Hoechst 33258, which has a binding energy of −14.26 kcal/mol. Since electronegative A–T sequences are narrower than G–C sections and demonstrate better fitting for small molecule docking, they generally offer superior van der Waals interactions.34,35 Additionally, in Figure S18 in the Supporting Information, these two complexes were shown alongside Hoechst 33258 to demonstrate how these substances interact with DNA through molecular docking. Hoechst 33258 and complex 2 are found in AT-rich areas of the DNA helix, as shown in great detail in Figure S18 in the Supporting Information. These complexes were found to bind preferentially in the AT-rich region of the minor groove, especially complex 2. The results shown in Table 4 demonstrate that Pt(II) complexes are capable of electrostatic interaction as well as minor groove binding. These results are in line with those of our experimental studies using fluorescence measurements of competitive binding, which showed that complex 2 exhibited substantially competitive binding compared with the minor groove binder Hoechst 33258.


### Metallodrug Function during DNA Interaction as a Target
  The curves of DNA’s interactions with added complexes 1 and 2 at 27 and 37 °C are displayed in Figure 10. At a glance, it can be seen that the diagrams are sigmoidal and demonstrate ascending DNA absorption via complex titration, showing the capability of 1 and 2 for conformational DNA denaturation due to increased absorption and the exposure of purine and pyrimidine groups. Data from the UV–vis assay for DNA binding show a change in DNA absorption during complex titration at 258 nm. Thus, this matter is related to the modification of chromophores in the DNA configuration.36 Plots show midpoint dosages ([L]1/2) at 0.39 and 0.33 mM for 1 and 0.04 mM for 2 at 27 and 37 °C, respectively; data are listed in Table 5. The low dosage means that complex 2 is more capable than 1, so 2 will need to bind and interact with DNA like [Pt(bipy)(But-gly)]NO3–DNA, [Pt(bipy)(Hex-gly)]NO3–DNA,37 and [Pt(NH3)2(amylgly)]NO3–DNA38 interactions. Thermodynamic parameters (K and ΔG°) were obtained based on the PACE method36 using equations K = (AN −AObs)/(AObs – AD) and ΔG° = −RTln K at 27 and 37 °C, where AN, AD, and AObs are the absorbance amounts of the native and denatured conformations of DNA and the observed absorbance in the transition region of denaturation diagrams, respectively (Figure 10); the data are summarized in Table 5. Plots of ΔG° versus the complex concentration for each system at 27 and 37 °C are shown in Figure S19 in the Supporting Information. DNA interaction becomes spontaneous with complex titration due to the decrease in ΔG°. Considering the formula ΔG° = ΔGH2O° – m[complex] and the ΔG° versus [complex] plot, where the slope displays the sensitivity of the DNA denaturant (m) and the intercept corresponds to ΔGH2O°, the DNA stability energy without any complexity can be evaluated, and the results are presented in Table 5. These data show the greater capability 2, potentially because of the presence of bipy in this complex. It should be mentioned that temperature is an impressive agent in DNA denaturation studies in the range of 27–37 °C, and the amount ΔGH2O° for each system decreased due to a spontaneous and exothermic process. The molar enthalpy of DNA unfolding Pt(II) complexes titration in the range of 27–37 °C was obtained using the Gibbs–Helmholtz equation.39 The straight line of ΔH° values versus [complex] is descending. ΔHH2O° in the absence of complex can be calculated (see Figure S20 in the Supporting Information and Table 5).

  In the end, ΔSH2O°, the molar entropy of DNA unfolding denaturation in the absence of complex, can be obtained using the equation ΔGH2O° = ΔHH2O° – TΔSH2O°. Due to the enhancement of DNA disorder, positive values ΔSH2O° were determined for each system, as shown in Table 5.


#### Competitive Fluorescent
Measurements
  The fluorescence emission of CT-DNA incubated with EB, the HL ligand, and the two complexes titrated at 25 °C is shown in Figure 11 and Figure S21 in the Supporting Information. Fluorescence emissions were quenched without any shift, which can illustrate the complex–DNA formation. Using the Stern–Volmer equation (F0/F = 1 + Ksv[Q] = 1 + kqτ0[Q]), the mechanism of quenching was obtained.40F0 and F are the fluorescence intensities of DNA–EB and each ligand and complex interaction as a quencher, respectively. [Q] is the concentration of the quencher, i.e., the concentration of the HL ligand or the Pt(II) complexes. The biomolecular quenching constant, kq, can be calculated using the equation kq = Ksv/τ0, where τ0 is the unquenched lifetime of the fluorophore, which is around 10–8 s, and Ksv is the Stern–Volmer quenching constant acquired from the linear F0/F versus [Q] plot. The Ksv values were calculated to be 1.69 × 103, 2.46 × 103, 1 × 103, and 2.74 × 103 M–1 for 1, 2, HL, and bipy, respectively.

  The KSV values show the good potential quenching of 2 and the lower affinity for HL and 1 in CT-DNA binding. Additionally, the kq values indicate a dynamic or static quenching mechanism. The quenching mechanism is probably static quenching for all compounds (see Table 6).41 The parameters were calculated by using the equation (log(F0 – F/F) = logKb + nlog[Q]). The plot of log(F0/F) versus log[Q] is shown in Figure S22 in the Supporting Information, and the Kb and n values are listed in Table 6. The Kb values for the HL, bipy, 1, and 2 systems were 0.43 × 103, 0.55 × 103, 1.71 × 103, and 2.39 × 103 M–1, respectively (like the UV titration data, the greatest Kb is that of 2). These values are lower than those of classical intercalators (e.g., ethidium bromide or [Ru(phen)DPPZ]),42 since electrostatic approach and groove binding are more likely interactions for complexes and weak interactions for ligand systems.43

  To further elucidate the Pt(II) complexes’ manner of binding to CT-DNA, a competitive binding experiment with Hoechst 33258 (Ho) was carried out. An artificial N-methyl piperazine derivative known as Hoechst 33258 binds to AT-rich sequences in B-DNA at the margin of four or five contiguous base pairs in the minor groove.44,45 As Ho is bonded to CT-DNA, Figure 11 displays the typical emission spectrum of Ho (Figure 11 c and d). As can be observed in Figure 11c, there was hardly any change in the fluorescence intensity of the DNA-Ho complex as the concentration of 1 increased (approximately 9.12% of its starting value). Given that complex 1 was unable to remove Hoechst from the DNA helix, it likely binds to DNA in a manner not compatible with the minor groove. On the other hand, a significant reduction in the fluorescence intensity of the DNA-Ho system resulted from the addition of complex 2 to the system, with a concentration-dependent effect (approximately 49.02% of its starting value).

  The quenching data were plotted using the Stern–Volmer equation, and the resulting linear function and the quenching constant are shown in Table 6. As a result of the fluorescence quenching of EB and the minor groove binder Ho, it is clear that complexes 1 and 2 are connected to DNA through electrostatic and minor groove binding mechanisms, respectively. The Ksv values of these displacement agents differ, which is consistent with the results. Comparing the Ksv values of the DNA–Ho–complex systems for complexes 1 and 2 (2.31 × 103 and 21.78 × 103 M–1, respectively) with the Ksv values of the DNA–EB and DNA–Ho systems for complex 2 (2.46 × 103 and 21.78 × 103 M–1, respectively) for the quenching fluorescence intensity, one can say that complex 2 was more effective as a groove binder. Based on the shapes of the examined complexes (Scheme 1), the outcomes of the theoretical studies on molecular docking, and the DNA binding experiments, the best type of interaction for complex 1 is proposed to be electrostatic and that for complex 2 is proposed to be minor groove binding to the CT-DNA double helix.


#### CD Study
  In the CD study, the DNA spectrum was altered due to the change in the DNA conformation caused by the addition of the Pt(II) complexes.46 These two bands are extremely sensitive throughout every interaction of DNA with a medication. Figure 12 shows the CD spectra of B-DNA for interactions with both complexes in addition to free HL and bipy ligands. Schematic molecular models of the complexes are shown on the right side of Figure 12. The decrease in intensity across both positive and negative bands, with no red or blue shift for 1 and a slight blue shift of the positive band centered at around 275 nm for 2, and the weak interaction of the free ligands of HL and bipy47 can be observed, clearly showing that drug binding to B-DNA creates conformational changes, such as the opening of the helix, and suggest that the electrostatic approach and groove binding may be merged.48−51


### In Vitro Cytotoxicity Study
  The growth inhibitory activities of HL and the two synthesized complexes were tested against the human model colon cancer cell line HCT116 after 48 and 72 h of incubation by treating the cells with concentration of all compounds ranging from 0 to 100 μM at 37 °C. The IC50 value of HL was ≥700 μM, which shows was no side effect on the mentioned cell line. The IC50 values of each compound were calculated based on the results of Figure 13, and the results are reported in Table 7. Furthermore, the cytotoxicities of the complexes were evaluated and compared to those of major chemotherapeutic drugs (cisplatin, carboplatin, and oxaliplatin) (see Table 7). Furthermore, to inhibit their cytotoxic abilities, the IC50 value of each complex were gathered and assessed in combination, as shown in Figure 14. Complex 1 with a lower IC50 value (35.51 μM) is more effective at inhibiting cancer cell lines after 72 h of incubation. When utilized as a ligand, aliphatic glycine was found to have a significant impact on cytotoxicity against colorectal cancer cells.

  In accordance with the simulation data, the cytotoxicy data show the electrostatic mode of the DNA–complex 1 interaction, which exhibited good inhibitory potential with an IC50 value and 35.5 μM, and interaction between the bipy moiety in 2 and CT-DNA base pairs (the highest binding constant and the groove-binding mode), which demonstrated cytotoxicity with an IC50 value of 51.3 μM. It was clear that amine ligands with more hydrogen-bonding capabilities have an influence on anticancer activity against human colon cancer cell lines, causing the lower IC50 values of complex 1 than 2. Additionally, DNA–complex molecular docking data in Table 4 show more negative electrostatic energy for complex 1 (−6.85 kcal/mol) than for 2 (−3.35 kcal/mol). Therefore, the mechanism of interaction of chemical compounds with DNA, i.e., the noncovalent interactions, can affect the anticancer activity of Pt-candidate drugs.


## Conclusion
  In summary, the HL ligand and its two complexes [Pt(NH3)2(L)](NO3) (1), [Pt(bipy)(L)](NO3) (2) were synthesized and characterized. Because of their high stability and solubility in water and buffer, these cationic compounds could aid in the improvement of the anticancer efficacy of drugs by including a leaving group and increasing their solubility. The N and O atoms of (R)-glycine (five-membered chelate ring) act as bidentate ligands to the metal center. Compound 2’s structure was validated by single-crystal X-ray diffraction studies, which showed that complex 2 crystallized in the orthorhombic Pbca space group. The platinum(II) ion is coordinated in a square pyramidal environment. The current study shows that this complex cooperativity between various weak and strong hydrogen bonds and a range of relatively weak π···π and anion···π interactions is necessary for the construction of the supramolecular framework. SwissADME prediction data showed positive lipophilicity values for HL and 2 (oral medication) and negative values for 1, similar to clinical Pt drugs (injectable medication). UV–vis, fluorescence, and CD spectroscopies were used to study changes in the configuration of DNA. Thermodynamic and binding parameters for the two complexes (ΔHH2O° = 306 and 106.7 kJmol–1 and ΔSH2O° = 0.92 and 0.32 kJ/mol·K for 1 and 2, respectively) revealed that both complexes denatured DNA exothermically and spontaneously; however, complex 1 was more potent for DNA denaturation than complex 2, and HL had a weak interaction with DNA. Furthermore, 2 is capable of denaturing DNA in lower quantities than 1. For both compounds, static quenching was observed in the fluorescence study, with Kb values lower than those of typical intercalators. It can be assumed that these complexes bind to DNA during nonintercalation, such as an electrostatic approach and groove binding. The competitive fluorescence emission of these complexes with reference Hoechst 33258 was also studied to investigate the mechanism of how these complexes bind to DNA. The emission spectra produced from this titration made it abundantly evident that complex 2 may bind to DNA via minor groove binding such as that to Hoechst. DFT investigations are also utilized to calculate the activities of the compounds (QCDs). Complex 2 was discovered to be more reactive than complex 1. According to MEP and FMOs the O atom of the carboxylate group, as well as the N atoms of the amino and pyridyl rings, can operate as donor and acceptor electrons in a hydrogen bond. Additionally, Hoechst molecular docking was carried out, and the lowest binding energy of this substance was evaluated in comparison to the two complexes. This study demonstrates that the optimum binding mechanisms are for 2 to bind to the DNA minor groove and for 1 to attach to phosphate groups. Experimental DNA tests and results from DFT and molecular docking investigations also concur with these results. IC50 data show that these synthesized compounds are toxic to the HCT116 cell line after 72 h of incubation (35.51 μM for 1 and 51.33 μM for 2). Complex 1 appears to be more inhibitory and better at passing through the cell membrane than complex 2.

#### Synthesis of Cyclohexylglycine

### X-ray Structure Determination and Structure Refinement
  A STOE IPDS2T diffractometer was used to perfom single-crystal X-ray diffraction with graphite monochromated MoK radiation. A polarizing microscope was used to select a single crystal, which was then put on glass fibers for data collection. Least-squares refinement was used to produce cell constants and orientation matrices for all reflections. Diffraction data were obtained in the form of a series of 1° ω-scans and merged using the STOE X-AREA software package.57 The data were subjected to Lorentz and polarization adjustments as well as a numerical absorption correction using X-RED and X-SHAPE.58,59 The structure was solved using direct approaches and subsequent differences. Fourier maps are used to refine all non-H atoms on F2 using full-matrix least-squares with anisotropic displacement parameters by evaluating the STOE X-STEP32 structure.60,61 The atomic scattering factors were derived from the International Tables for X-ray Crystallography.62,63

Experimental data can be supplemented with computational models and the Bioavailability Radar. ### Theoretical Calculation
  DFT was used for the geometry optimizations and energy computations of HL and complexes 1 and 2 using the LANL2DZ basis set for the Pt metal atom and the uB3LYP functional and 6-311G basis set for H, C, N, and O atoms. GausView 6.0 was used to create the input files for the HL and Pt derivatives20,21 and molecular electrostatic potential (MEP) maps and to determine the electrostatic potential charges. Furthermore, several quantum chemical parameters were estimated,32,65 namely, the energy gap between the LUMO and the HOMO (Egap = ELUMO – EHOMO), the ionization energy (I = −EHOMO), the electron affinity (A = −ELUMO), the absolute hardness (η = −1/2(ELUMO – EHOMO)), the absolute electronegativity (χ = −1/2(ELUMO + EHOMO)), the absolute softness (σ = 1/η), the chemical potential (CP = −χ), the global softness (S = 1/2η), the electrophilicity index (ω = CP2/2η), additional electronic charges (ΔNmax = −(CP/η)), and the nucleophilicity index (N = (1/ω)).


### Molecular Simulation
  Computational molecular docking is an intriguing way to understand biomolecular drug interactions with the main target, which here is DNA (PDB ID 1BNA (5′-D(*CP*GP*CP*GP*AP*AP*TP*TP*CP*GP*CP*G)-3′). This simulation could be used to investigate the free and binding modes and the energies of the drug–receptor complex.66 AutoDock 4.2 (AutoDock 4.2 rel. 4.2.5.1(c), 1989–2012) and AutoDockTools 1.5.6 with AutoGrid 4.2.5 were used to perform docking simulation of each Pt complex–DNA complex.67 The complexes’ structures were optimized with the Gaussian 09 software at the theoretical level of UB3LYP with the 6-311 G basis set for H, C, N, and O atoms and the LANL2DZ basis set for the platinum metal. Before docking, all water molecules were removed as a solvent, then the charges, Gasteiger partial charges, and hydrogen were added. For all compounds, the grid dimensions were preserved at 126 × 126 × 126 Å points in the x-, y-, and z-direction, respectively, with a grid-point spacing of 0.375. To find the best conformer, the Lamarckian genetic algorithm was applied. Finally, the AutoDock, Chimera, and Discovery studio 4.5 tools were used to generate the molecular docking figures.


### DNA–Pt Complex Interaction
  The titration of isothermal Pt complexes with CT-DNA was carried out in a Tris-HCl buffer (containing 20 mM sodium chloride with pH 7.4) medium. A mixture of 5 mg/mL DNA in Tris-HCl buffer was stirred very slowly for three days at 4 °C, whereas the stock solutions of 1 and 0.5 mM of each complex were made in buffer and water.68 The change in the absorption of DNA was used to monitor drug complex addition at two temperatures of 27 and 37 °C, then binding and thermodynamic parameters were primarily determined using the PACE method.69

  Competitive fluorescence measurements were used to study the binding forces between DNA as a biomacromolecule target and other small molecules such as metallodrugs.41 Both complexes are similarly nonfluorescent; therefore, the fluorescent emission of EB was detected as a fluorescent probe. In water, EB emits a weak fluorescent signal, but after binding to DNA its emission intensity increases by around 30×.36 The maximal wavelength for EB was determined to be 471 nm, and emission monitoring (slit width was 10 nm) was performed between 540 and 700 nm, with fluorescence peaks at 580 nm. At room temperature, a solution of (0.12 mM) CT-DNA incubated with (0.002 mM) EB was titrated with 0–0.31 and 0–0.2 mM of 1 and 2, respectively.

  Similar procedures to those used for the EB displacement assay were used for the Hoechst 33258 displacement assay.45 By titrating the mixture with increasing concentrations of both complexes from 0 to 0.045 mM, the Hoechst-DNA complex consisting of (0.003 mM) Hoechst and (0.03 mM) Ct-DNA was excited at 343 nm. The fluorescence emission spectra were recorded between 350 and 650 nm. The aforementioned tests were all carried out in a Tris-HCl buffer (containing 20 mM sodium chloride with pH 7.4) medium.

  In a DNA conformation study performed using circular dichroism, DNA form variations were followed using drug titration.70 Base stacking changes in DNA were observed at 275 nm (the positive band), and helicity sensitivity was monitored at 245 nm (the negative band). The ellipticity structural DNA change of a (0.12 mM) CT-DNA solution was measured from 200 to 320 nm at 25 °C isomg HL (0–0.36 mM) and bipy (0–0.24 mM) ligands and [Pt(NH3)2(L)]NO3 (0–0.44 mM) and [Pt(bipy)(L)]NO3 (0–0.13 mM) complexes in a Tris-HCl buffer (containing 20 mM sodium chloride with pH 7.4) medium.


### In Vitro Study
  HCT116, a human colorectal cancer cell line, was provided by the Pasteur Institute of Iran. The cells were grown in a DMEM medium (Sigma) supplemented with 2 mM l-glutamine, 5 g/mL streptomycin, penicillin, and 10% heat-inactivated fetal calf serum at 37 °C in a 5:95 CO2/air environment. Cytotoxicities of both complexes and clinical Pt drugs were studied in a 96-well plate (1 × 104 cell/mL) with overnight adherence according to the MTT assay. After that, HCT116 cell lines were incubated for 24, 48, and 72 h with different doses of complexes 1 and 2 (0–100 μM). Four hours before the termination of incubation, 20 μL of a 5 mg MTT/mL PBS solution was added to the cultured media, then the insoluble formazan was formed. Later, using a multiwell scanning spectrometer, the optical density (OD) of the dissolved insoluble formazan generated was measured in a solution of 10% SDS and 50% DMF (left for 2 h at 37 °C in darkness) at 570 nm using an ELISA reader (Hitech, Austria).
