# Metabolic activation of tachysterol3 to biologically
active hydroxyderivatives that act on VDR, AhR, LXRs and PPARγ
receptors


## Abstract
  CYP11A1 and CYP27A1 hydroxylate tachysterol3, a photoproduct of previtamin D3, producing 20S-hydroxytachysterol3 [20S(OH)T3] and 25(OH)T3, respectively. Both metabolites were detected in the human epidermis and serum. Tachysterol3 was also detected in human serum at a concentration of 7.3±2.5 ng/mL. 20S(OH)T3 and 25(OH)T3 inhibited the proliferation of epidermal keratinocytes and dermal fibroblasts and stimulated the expression of differentiation and anti-oxidative genes in keratinocytes in a similar manner to 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D3 [1,25(OH)2D3]. They acted on the vitamin D receptor (VDR) as demonstrated by image flow cytometry and the translocation of VDR coupled GFP from the cytoplasm to the nucleus of melanoma cells, as well as by the stimulation of CYP24A1 expression. Functional studies using a human aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) reporter assay system revealed marked activation of AhR by 20S(OH)T3, a smaller effect by 25(OH)T3, and a minimal effect for their precursor, tachysterol3. Tachysterol3 hydroxyderivatives showed high affinity binding to the ligand binding domain (LBD) of the liver X receptor (LXR) α and β, and the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ) in LanthaScreen TR-FRET coactivator assays. Molecular docking using crystal structures of the LBDs of VDR, AhR, LXRs and PPARγ revealed high docking scores for 20S(OH)T3 and 25(OH)T3, comparable to their natural ligands. The scores for the non-genomic binding site of the VDR were very low indicating a lack of interaction with tachysterol3 ligands. Our identification of endogenous production of 20S(OH)T3 and 25(OH)T3 that are biologically active and interact with VDR, AhR, LXRs and PPARγ, provides a new understanding of the biologic function of tachysterol3.


## Introduction
  Absorption of ultraviolet B (UVB) radiation by the B ring of 7-dehydrocholesterol (7-DHC) leads to its transformation to previtamin D3. Once produced in the plasma membrane of the keratinocyte it rapidly undergoes a nonenzymatic temperature dependent isomerization to vitamin D3. Previtamin D3 can also absorb UVB radiation which results in its isomerization to tachysterol3 (T3) and lumisterol3 (L3) (1). While the role of vitamin D3 in regulating calcemic and noncalcemic biological actions are well established (2-5), the role of other photo products of 7-DHC and pre-vitamin D3 is less well understood.

  In the canonical pathway, vitamin D3 is activated to its biologically active form by sequential hydroxylations to 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 [25(OH)D3] by CYP2R1 or CYP27A1 in the liver (6, 7) and skin (3-5, 8, 9) and subsequent hydroxylation at C-1α by CYP27B1 (2, 3, 5, 6, 9). However, new non-canonical pathways of vitamin D activation by CYP11A1, with the initial major product being 20S-hydroxyvitamin D3 [20S(OH)D3], were discovered generating new products that were detectable in human epidermis, placenta and serum, and in the pig adrenal gland (10-17) and honey (18). These hydroxyderivatives show potent biological activity in several systems, with some being non-calcemic (16, 19-36). Their mechanism of action not only includes activation of the vitamin D receptor (VDR) (37-40), but also agonism on the aryl hydrocarbon (AhR) (41) and liver X (LXRα and β) receptors (42), and inverse agonism on retinoic acid orphan receptors (ROR α and γ) (27).

  We have also shown that 7-DHC can be hydroxylated by CYP11A1 to biologically active products (43-45). These, including 20S-hydroxy-7-DHC [20S(OH)7-DHC], are detectable in the human epidermis and serum (16). Similarly, CYP27A1 can hydroxylate 7-DHC to 25-hydroxy-7-DHC [25(OH)7-DHC] (46). After exposure to the UVB, these 5,7-dienes can be transformed to their previtamin D derivatives which in turn can be photolyzed to their corresponding lumisterol and tachysterol isomers (21, 43). Recent studies clearly show that lumisterol is not only metabolized to different products by CYP11A1 and CYP27A1 (28, 47), but that these metabolites are detectable in the human body and are biologically active, acting on the non-genomic site of the VDR and as inverse agonists of RORα and γ (48), as well as agonists on LXR α and β (42). This raises the question of whether similar actions are applicable for tachysterols.

  Therefore, we decided to test whether tachysterol3, which is produced in the epidermis (1, 49-51), is also circulating in the serum and whether it is metabolized by CP11A1 and CYP27A1. In addition, we have tested the phenotypic activity of the two major metabolites, 20(OH)T3 and 25(OH)T3, on skin cells and defined potential nuclear receptors for them using functional assays and in silico molecular modeling.


## Materials and Methods

### Materials
  Human foreskins from African American (AA) or Caucasian (C) subjects were used to establish primary cultures of keratinocytes and dermal fibroblasts. Their use was approved by the IRB at the University of Alabama Birmingham (UAB) as not the subject to FDA regulation and not Human Subject Research (IRB protocol N150915001)(48, 52).

  The use of human skin for extraction of secosteroids was approved by the UAB IRB as an exempt protocol #4 (Dr. A. Slominski, PI). The use of previously extracted samples of human serum for which collection was previously approved by the UTHSC IRB protocol #7526(16), was also approved by the local UAB IRB protocol E150427002. The samples were deidentified as already described (16).

  1α,25(OH)2D3, 7-DHC and pregnenolone acetate were from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Other cell and molecular biology supplies included keratinocyte media (Lonza Walkersville Inc., Walkersville, MD) supplemented with keratinocyte growth factors (KGF) (Lonza), DMEM medium containing antibiotics and charcoal-treated fetal bovine serum (ctFBS) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-5-(3-carboxymethoxyphenyl)-2-(4-sulfophenyl)-2H-tetrazolium) (MTS) (Promega, Madison, WI), Syber green Master Mix (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA), the Human AhR Reporter Assay System (Indigo Biosciences, State College, PA, USA), NucBlue™ Live ReadyProbes™ Reagent (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). LanthaScreen™ TR-FRET Coactivator Assay Kits for LXRα, LXRβ and PPARγ were purchased from Life Technologies Corporation (Grand Island, NY, USA).

  2-Hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin (HP-β-CD) was from Cerestar (Hammond, IN). NADPH was from Sigma-Aldrich (Sydney, Australia). T3 was either prepared from the 7-DHC as described before (53) or was purchased from Toronto Research Chemicals (Toronto, Canada) for enzymatic assays and further purified before use on a Grace Alltima C18 column (25 cm × 0.46 cm, 5 μm particle size) using a gradient of methanol in water (64 - 100% methanol) for 15 min followed by 100% methanol for 45 min at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min.

  25(OH)T3 was prepared and purified as described previously (53). 20S(OH)T3 was synthesized as reported previously with some modifications (54) as described in the supplementary file (SI). Briefly, 20S(OH)T3 was synthesized starting from commercially available pregnenolone acetate (1), which was subjected to allylic bromination using N-bromosuccinimide followed by dehydrobromination to give 7-dehydropregnenolone (2) (Fig. S1). Compound 2 was then allowed to react with 4-methylpentylmagnesium bromide to provide 20S(OH)-7-DHC (3). Compound 3 (1 mg/mL in methanol in a quartz cuvette) was exposed to UVB (312 nm) for 10 min, the solution was transferred into a brown vial and incubated at room temperature excluding light for 48 h. The reaction mixture was then purified using reversed phase high performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC). Specifically, a two step purification was performed. First, the sample was subjected to HPLC (1260 Infinity II, Agilent, Santa Clara, CA) using a Zorbax C18 column (9.4 × 250 mm, 5 μm) (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) with 95% methanol in water isocratically at flow rate of 1.5 mL/min. A Waters C18 column (Waters, Milford, MA, USA) (250 × 4.6mm, 5 μm) was used for second purification using a gradient of 40→100% acetonitrile in water at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min for 15 min, followed by 100% acetonitrile isocratically for 25 min at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. The identity of the compounds was confirmed by UVR spectra, high-resolution mass spectra (MS) and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) analyses (54) (Fig. S2).

  Human (55) and bovine (14) CYP11A1 were expressed in E. coli and purified as before. Human CYP27A1 with a six-histidine tag added to the C-terminus was expressed in E. coli and purified by Ni affinity- and octyl Sepharose-chromatography, as described previously (56). Human adrenodoxin reductase and human adrenodoxin were also expressed in E. coli and purified as described before (14, 55).


### Methods

#### Liquid chromatography and mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analyses.
  Q-TOF LC-MS was used for detection of 20S(OH)T3, 25(OH)T3 and tachysterol3 in serum and epidermis. Extraction of serum and epidermis was performed using protocols described previously (16). Briefly, serum was extracted with 90% methanol followed by centrifugation to remove protein and supernatant was dried and kept in −80 °C for further experiments. Epidermis was homogenized in PBS and 75% acetonitrile was added and the homogenization repeated. After centrifuging, the supernatant was dried and kept in −80 °C for further experiments. A Xevo G2 XS LC-MS system with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 × 50 mm, 1.7 μm) (Waters, Milford, MA, USA) or a Zorbax RRHT Eclipse Plus C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was used to detect T3 and its metabolites. Elution was carried out with a gradient of 20→60% methanol in water for 3 min, 20→60% methanol for 0.2 min and 60→97% methanol for 1.6 min, at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min, for the ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column. For the Zorbax RRHT Eclipse Plus C18 column, a gradient of 20→60% methanol in water for 12 min, 60→97% methanol in water for 0.8 min and 97% methanol isocratically for 0.4 min at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min, were used. Pre-purification of extracted hydroxytachysterols of interest was performed using a Waters C18 column (Waters, Milford, MA, USA) with a gradient of acetonitrile in water (40→100%) for 15 min and 100% acetonitrile for 30 min, at flow rate of 0.5 mL/min, followed by 100% acetonitrile for 40 min at flow rate of 1.5 mL/min. Mass analysis conditions were set as described previously (18).

  Concentrations of tachysterol3 were directly measured by LC-MS using a Zorbax RRHT Eclipse Plus C18 column, as described above. The concentrations of tachysterol3 were calculated from standard curves constructed using the corresponding standard.


#### Measurement of metabolites of CYP11A1 and CYP27A1 action on
tachysterol3
  Tachysterol3 (20 μM) dissolved in 0.9% w/v 2-hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin (13) was incubated at 37 °C with bovine or human CYP11A1 (0.5 – 1.0 μM), or human CYP27A1 (1.0 μM), in buffer comprising 20 mM Hepes, pH 7.4, 100 mM NaCl, 0.1 mM EDTA and 0.1 mM dithiothreitol. The incubation mixture also contained adrenodoxin (15 μM), adrenodoxin reductase (0.4 μM), glucose-6-phosphate (2 mM), glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (2 U/mL), NAPDH (50 μM) and the antioxidant butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT, 0.1 μg/mL). The final incubation volume was 0.4 mL. After incubation for the times indicated in Figure Legends, reactions were stopped by the addition of 2.5 mL of ice-cold dichloromethane then 0.6 mL water and a further 40 μg of BHT were added. The bottom organic phase was retained and the aqueous phase extracted twice more with 2.5 mL dichloromethane. The combined extracts were dried under a stream of N2 gas and dissolved in 300 μL 64% methanol. Samples were analyzed by reversed phase HPLC on a 25 × 0.46 cm C18 Grace Alltima column using a gradient of 64→100% methanol for 40 min followed by 100% methanol for 40 min, at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. Metabolites were detected with a UV detector at 280 nm.


#### Cell proliferation assays
  Primary human epidermal keratinocytes or dermal fibroblasts from AA or C subjects (passage #3) were plated onto 96-well plates at a confluence of about 70% and further treated with graded concentrations of 1α,25(OH)2D3, 20S(OH)T3, 25(OH)T3 or T3 and incubated, respectively, in serum free keratinocyte media with keratinocyte growth factors (KGF) or in DMEM containing 10% ctFBS (20, 48, 52). Ethanol (0.1%) served as a vehicle control. After 24 h or 48 h the mitochondrial activity of viable cells was measured using the MTS assay (20, 48, 52).


#### Real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)
  Human keratinocytes were grown as described above and cells were harvested for RNA isolation (RNAeasy Micro kit, Qiagen) and cDNA synthesis (The High Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit with RNase Inhibitor, The Applied Biosystems) following the manufacturers’ protocols. QPCR assays were run using Cyber green as previously described (52). Approximately, 750 ng of cDNA was used for PCR. GAPDH and cyclophilin were used as internal controls. Primer sequences are in supplemental Table 1, amplifying condition have been described previously (22, 23).


#### Protein expression assays
  Human epidermal keratinocytes from the third passage were plated into 96-well plate and after reaching 60% confluency they were treated with 10−7 M of either 1,25(OH)2D3, tachysterol3, 20(OH)T3 or 25(OH)T3 versus ethanol control for 24 h. Cells were fixed and stained with anti-catalase (Santa Cruz Biotechnology), anti involucrin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) conjugated with Alexa Fluor 488, anti-CK10 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology), anti Mn-SOD (Millipore Corp) or anti-SOD1 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology), at dilutions of 1:100, 1:300, 1:100, 1:50 and 1:50, respectively, as described previously (22, 23, 48, 57). For secondary antibodies, anti-goat (for CAT and SOD1), anti-mouse (for CK10) both with Alexa Fluor 488, or anti-rabbit with Alexa Fluor 660 (for MnSOD) were used, all at a dilution of 1:200. Images were taken at 10 x magnification using Cytation 5 reader. Nuclei were stained with blue DAPI. Fluorescence intensity was measured using the Cytation 5 reader and calculated using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health).


#### Nuclear receptor assays
  To study the ligand-induced VDR translocation from cytoplasm to the nucleus, we used SKMEL-188 melanoma cells transduced with pLenti-CMV-VDR-EGFP-pgk-puro, following protocols described previously (40, 48, 58). Briefly, cells were grown on 96 well plates using Ham’s F10 media containing 5% ctFBS until 70% confluent and then treated with the compounds for 1.5 h, followed by treatment of NucBlue™ Live ReadyProbes™ Reagent using the manufacturer’s protocol. The ratios of cell number with a nuclear GFP to the total cell number stained with DAPI were determined using Cytation 5 (Biotek, Winooski, VT, USA).

  Image flow cytometry was used for the studies on the ligand induced translocation of VDR to the nucleus in human keratinocytes, as described previously (42, 59). Briefly, HaCaT keratinocytes were treated with 0.1% ethanol (negative control), 10−7 M 1α,25(OH)2D3 (positive control), 20S(OH)T3 or T3 and after 6 h cells were harvested, fixed with paraformaldehyde and permeabilized in methanol-containing buffer (60, 61). The permeabilized samples were incubated with Hoechst dye (r37165, ThermoFisher) and antibodies against the VDR (sc-13133, AF 488, SantaCruz) and subjected to analysis by imaging flow cytometry using ImageStream II as described previously (42, 62). Data were analyzed using IDEAS software (Amnis, Seattle, WA, USA). Ratios of nuclear vs cytoplasmic localization of VDR were determined following analysis of 1,000 to 3,000 individual cells. Individual images were captured using an Amnis ImageStreamX Mk II Imaging Flow Cytometer (Luminex Corporation) and IDEAS software version 6.2.

  The binding of tachysterol compounds to the AhR was measured using the Human AhR Reporter Assay System (Indigo Biosciences, State College, PA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol, as described previously (41). Briefly, the cells including the luciferase reporter gene linked to an AhR-responsive promoter were treated with the compounds for 23 h and the luminescence signal was measured using Cytation 5 after treatment with the detection substrate provided in the kit system.

  The binding of tachysterol compounds to the LXRα, LXRβ and PPARγ was measured using commercially available LanthaScreen™ TR-FRET Coactivator Assay Kits (Life Technologies Corporation, Grand Island, NY, USA). Briefly, each LBD was added to the compounds to be tested followed by the addition of the antibody (Tb-anti-GST) and a mixture of peptides (Fluorescein-TRAP220/DRIP-2). After incubation of the reaction mixture at room temperature for 2 h or overnight, the emission signal at 520 nm was divided by the emission signal at 495 nm for TR-FRET ratio using Synergy neo2 (BioTek, Winooski, VT).


### Molecular modeling
  The crystal structures of the genomic and non-genomic LBDs of the VDR, AhR, PPARγ and of RORα and γ were used for docking experiments and modeling as previously described (41, 42, 48). Crystal structures for VDR (PDBID: 1DB1) (63), RORα (PDBID 1S0X)(64), RORγ (PDBID: 3L0J) (65), PPAR γ (PDBID:3TY0) (66), LXRα (PDBID: 5AVI) (67) and LXRβ(PDBID: 5HJP) (68) and a human AhR homology model from our previous study (41) were used for the docking studies. Openbabel (69) software was used to convert the ligands in PDB format to PDBQT format. Receptors were loaded into AutoDock Tools (70) software for preparation: polar hydrogen atoms were added; water molecules, ion molecules, and bound ATP were removed; and lastly, Kollman Charges were added. Prepared receptors were output in PDBQT format for molecular docking in Autodock Vina (71). To evaluate the receptor-ligand interaction, 3D and 2D interactions between vitamin D3 derivatives with each studied receptor were performed using PyMol (72) software for 3D interactions and Maestro (73) for a 2D interaction map.


### Statistics
  Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prizm statistical software (GraphPad Software Inc.) and are presented as means ± standard deviation of the mean (SD) of at least three experiments (n ≥ 3). The significance of non-treated controls in comparison to the compound-treated groups was evaluated by the student t-test, or by one-way ANOVA test when various doses were compared. The significance was: *p<0.05; **p<0.01; ***p<0.001 in student t-test, and #p<0.05; ##p<0.01; ###p<0.001 for Dunnett’s multi comparison post-hoc test with one-way ANOVA.


## Results

### Metabolism of tachysterol3 by CYP11A1 and CYP27A1:
  First, we tested whether bovine CYP11A1 can metabolize tachysterol3 in vitro. Incubation of tachysterol3 with bovine CYP11A1 for 20 min resulted in the appearance of 4 products that were not present in the control incubation with adrenodoxin omitted (Fig. 1A). The major product identified was 20S(OH)T3 based on its identical retention time (50.9 min) to standard. This product had also the same retention time as standard 20S(OH)T3 when products were analyzed by HPLC using an acetonitrile gradient, rather than a methanol gradient (not shown), further confirming the identification. The other three products remain to be identified due to the lack of availability of standards but are likely to be mono-oxygenated derivatives of tachysterol3 based on their retention times being close to that of 20S(OH)T3. Likely sites of hydroxylation are C22, C23, C24 or C17 since hydroxylation at one or more of these carbons have been observed for other CYP11A1 substrates, including vitamin D3 and lumisterol3 (6, 47). The antioxidant BHT was required during the incubation and extractions of tachysterol3, without it much of the tachysterol3 and its metabolites disappeared, presumably degrading to products lacking 280 nm absorbance. The rate of tachysterol3 metabolism by bovine CYP11A1 slowed over the duration of the incubation with little or no increase in the amount of 20S(OH)T3 (RT50.9) or the products RT49.2 and RT49.6 after 10 min, with only RT52.0 increasing from 10 min to 20 min of the incubation (Fig. 1B). The initial rate of tachysterol3 metabolism by bovine CYP11A1 (approximately 3 mol/min/mol CYP11A1) is similar to that reported by us previously for lumisterol3 (4.8 mol/min/mol CYP11A1), but slower than that reported for cholesterol (6.1 mol/min/mol) under fairly similar conditions (28).

  Human CYP11A1 also converted tachysterol3 to 20S(OH)T3 and the same 3 minor products as bovine CYP11A1, but the proportions were different with 20S(OH)T3 predominating for the human enzyme (Fig. 1C). The time course for metabolism of tachysterol3 by human CYP11A1 shows that 20S(OH)T3 is by far the major product at all times analyzed (Fig. 1D). Only two of the three minor products (RT49.2 and RT49.7) were made in sufficient amounts to quantitate and these shared essentially identical time courses, superimposed on each other in Fig. 1D. At 20 min of incubation, 20S(OH)T3 represented 85% of the total products made by the human enzyme while for the bovine enzyme it was 47%. The initial linear rate of 20S(OH)D3 formation by human CYP11A1 (0.06 mol/mol CYP11A1/min) was much lower than for the bovine enzyme (1.0 mol/mol CYP11A1/min) but the rate was maintained for a substantially longer time period (Fig. 1B and D).

  Incubation of human CYP27A1 with tachysterol3 resulted in three products that were not present in the control where adrenodoxin was omitted (Fig. 1E). The major product was identified as 25(OH)T3 based on its identical HPLC retention time to standard with the methanol gradient used in Fig. 1E. This was confirmed by reverse-phase HPLC using an acetonitrile gradient for separation where the retention times of the major product and standard 25(OH)T3 were also the same (not shown). This identification is also consistent with C-25 being the preferred hydroxylation site of CYP27A1 on vitamin D3 (56). The other two products remain to be identified as suitable standards were not available. However, one of these is likely to be 27(OH)T3 as C-27 is a preferred site of hydroxylation of cholesterol, 7-DHC and lumisterol3 by CYP27A1(47, 74-76). The time course for the metabolism of T3 by CYP27A1 revealed that the production of 25(OH)T3 and product RT50.9 was approximately linear over the 30 min incubation time period. Product RT52.5 could not be detected at the 5-min time point suggesting that it might be a secondary metabolite derived from one of the other two products. The rate of tachysterol3 conversion to 25(OH)T3 was 0.13 min−1 under the conditions of the assay. For comparison, we have reported turnover numbers (kcat) for metabolism of cholesterol and vitamin D3 by CYP27A1 of 9.8 and 2.1 min−1, respectively, but these measurements were made under different conditions to those used in the present study (56). CYP27A1 exhibits a much higher kcat for lumisterol3, 76 min−1 as measured in phospholipid vesicles (47). It was not possible to measure tachysterol3 metabolism in phospholipid vesicles as considerable T3 degradation occurred during the sonication required for vesicle preparation.


### Detection of T3, 20S(OH)T3 and 25(OH)T3 in
human epidermis and serum:
  Figure 2 shows the presence of tachysterol3, 20S(OH)T3 and 25(OH)T3 in the human epidermis (Fig. 2A) and human serum (Fig. 2B). The parent compound, tachysterol3, was also found to be present in the epidermis and at a high enough concentration to be reliably measured at 25.1±5.2 ng/mg protein, about 30 times lower than the concentration reported for 7DHC (227.3 ±59.4 ng/mg protein)(48), but higher than that of lumisterol3 (2.5±1.1 ng/mg protein) (48) and vitamin D3 (0.2±0.05 ng/mg protein) (16). While the detection of tachysterol3 in the epidermis was expected (1, 50), its presence in the serum was demonstrated for the first time. Its concentration was 7.3±2.5 ng/ml (n=4) which is ~3 times lower than that of 7-DHC and lumisterol3 (48) and ~3.5 higher than that of vitamin D3 (16). The endogenous production of 20S(OH)T3 and 25(OH)T3 in the epidermis and their circulation in the serum is shown for the first time. Although we could not quantify concentrations of these hydroxytachysterols in a reliable manner because their detection required a pre-purification chromatography step prior to LC/MS, they were detected in 2 independent, combined samples of the epidermis (1 and 2 individuals) and serum (2 and 11 individuals).


### Phenotypic activity of tachysterols on skin cells:
  Since high concentrations of 25(OH)T3 and tachysterol3 showed an inhibitory effect on proliferation of epidermal keratinocytes, as reported by Holick and coworkers (53), we tested the effects of 20S(OH)T3 on proliferation of human dermal fibroblasts and keratinocytes (Fig. 3) and the induction of the expression of genes and proteins involved in keratinocyte differentiation and antioxidative programs (Fig. 4). Fig. 3A shows inhibition of proliferation of dermal fibroblasts from African American (AA) subjects by 20S(OH)T3 in comparison to 1α,25(OH)2D3 and T3 after treatment for 24 h and 48 h. Similar inhibitory effects were observed for 20S(OH)T3 and 25(OH)T3 on epidermal keratinocyte proliferation (Fig. 3B). These secosteroids also inhibited proliferation of dermal fibroblasts from Caucasian subjects (Fig. 3C). In general, 1α,25(OH)2D3 showed greater potency in comparison to 20S(OH)T3, 25(OH)T3 and T3. Figures 3D and E show stimulation of the expression of genes involved in keratinocyte differentiation (KRT1, KRT10, FLG, IVL, LOR, TGM1) and antioxidative pathways (CAT, SOD1, SOD2) by 20S(OH)T3, 1α,25(OH)2D3 and T3. Figure 3F shows stimulation of the expression of CK10, INV, CAT, SOD1 and MnSOD proteins after 24 h treatment with the secosteroids.


### Hydroxytachysterols interactions with the VDR:
  It was previously reported that 25(OH)T3 and to lesser degree tachysterol3 could bind to the VDR (53). In the present study, we show that both 20S(OH)T3 and 25(OH)T3 stimulated the expression of CYP24A1 to a level approximately 10 times less than that induced by the positive control, 1,25(OH)2D3 (Fig. 4A). They also increased nuclear accumulation of VDR-GFP in melanoma cells (Fig. 4B). This was supported by the increase in cytoplasm-to-nucleus translocation of VDR in keratinocytes treated with 10−7 M of tachysterol3 or 20S(OH)T3 (Fig. 4C). Treatment with 1α,25(OH)2D3 served as a positive control and 0.1% ethanol (vehicle) as the negative control.

  Fig. 4D shows the interactions of 20S(OH)T3 and 25(OH)T3 within the binding pocket of the genomic (G) VDR ligand binding domain (LBD) in comparison to that of 1α,25(OH)2D3. The binding position and orientation for 20S(OH)T3 and 25(OH)T3 are similar to those for binding of 1α,25(OH)2D3 to the VDR LBD (Fig. 4E). The binding in the binding pocket of the G VDR LBD with 20S(OH)T3 or 25(OH)T3 is mainly through hydrophobic and polar interactions. Hydrogen bonds are formed between the hydroxyl group on C25 of 25(OH)T3 and His397, and between the hydroxyl group on C3 of 25(OH)T3 and Ser278 (Fig. 4E). Hydrophobic residues Leu233, Val234, Val300, Ile271, Tyr143, Tyr147 and Trp286, and polar residues of Ser237, Ser278, Ser275, His305 and His397 are directly involved in the binding with both hydroxytachysterols (Fig. 4E). Their docking scores for the G-site of the VDR were comparable to recognized ligands such as 1α,25(OH)2D3, and most recently described 1α,20S(OH)2D3 (38). Very low docking scores for the hydroxytachysterols on the A site (non-genomic binding site) of the VDR indicate a lack of interaction with this site (Table 1).


### Hydroxytachysterol interactions with the AhR:
  Dose-dependent interactions of 20S(OH)T3, 25(OH)T3, and tachysterol3 with AhR, LXRs and PPARγ are shown in Figure 5A-C. We used a human AhR Reporter Assay System to analyze the effect of tachysterol3 and its hydroxyderivatives on AhR-mediated transactivation. Figure 5A shows a marked activation of AhR activity by 20S(OH)T3 with weaker but significant activation by 25(OH)T3 or its natural ligand, indole acetic acid (IAA). A weak stimulation was observed by tachysterol3. These functional assays indicate that T3 hydroxyderivatives as well as T3 act as agonists on the AhR.

  To further evaluate the interactions with AhR, we performed molecular docking using a homology model of the LBD of human AhR from our previous study (41). Table 1 shows docking scores of tachysterol3 analogs and these were slightly higher than those of the natural ligand for AhR, indole acetic acid (IAA). These analyses are similar to the docking performed by a commercial company, OTAVA LTD (Vaughan, ON L4K 0C3, Canada), using Glide in Extra Precision (XP) implemented in Schrodinger (version 2016-1). These scores were −12.48, −12.24, −12.78, −12.82 and −12.89, for 20S(OH)T3, 25(OH)T3, tachysterol3, 22R(OH)T3 and 22S(OH)T3, respectively, being similar to the score of −12.44 for 6-formylindolo[3,2-b]carbazol, a high affinity endogenous ligand for the AhR (77).

  Figures 5D and E shows docked poses for 20S(OH)T3 and 25(OH)T3 in comparison to 1α,25(OH)2D3 and IAA binding with AhR. All molecules are docked in the same binding pocket (Fig. 5D). The binding between AhR and hydroxytachysterols is mainly through hydrophobic interaction with Ile325, Leu353, Cys333, Ala367, Met348, Phe295, Tyr322, Leu315, Cys300, Leu308, Tyr310 and Phe287. For 25(OH)T3, a hydrogen bond is formed between the hydroxyl group on C3 and Ser365 (Fig. 5E).


### Hydroxytachysterol interactions with the LXRs:
  Previously we have shown that hydroxyderivatives of 7-DHC, D3 and lumisterol3 act on LXRα and β (42). In addition, our previous molecular modeling using Schrodinger software has shown relatively high Glide XP docking scores for T3, 20S(OH)T3 and 25(OH)T3 and other theoretically predicted mono- and dihydroxy-T3 derivatives docked into the LXRα and LXRβ crystal structures with PDBID 5AVI, 3IPQ, 5HJP, 1PQC and 1UPV (see supplemental file of the paper (42)). These scores were similar to those of 7-DHC, lumisterol3 and vitamin D3 analogs. In this study using LanthaScreen TR-FRET LXRα and β coactivator assays we show dose-dependent binding of 20S(OH)T3 and 25(OH)T3 to the LBD of the LXRα and β with corresponding EC50 values in the range of 10−8−10−7 M (Fig. 6A, B). This is further supported by their interactions with the LBD of LXRα and β shown in Fig. 6 C-F. The docking scores for tachysterol3 analogs were similar to their natural ligands (hydroxycholesterol derivatives) (Table 1). For both LXRα and LXRβ, 20S(OH)T3 and 25(OH)T3 have similar binding postures to 1α,25(OH)2D3 but in reverse orientation (Fig. 6C, D). A hydrogen bond is formed between the hydroxyl group on C3 of 20S(OH)T3 and Ala329 on LXRα. Hydrophobic residues Phe335, Trp443, Leu428, Leu435, Phe254, Leu331, Ala329, Met298, Ile295, Leu260 and Ala261, as well as polar residues Ser264, Thr258, Thr302, Gln424 and His421 in LXRα, are directly involved in the binding with both hydroxytachysterols (Fig. 6E). In LXRβ, hydrophobic residues Ile309, Met312, Leu313, Phe349, Phe329, Leu330, Ile277, Ala275, Phe340, Leu274, Trp457, Phe271, Leu449 and Leu345 as well as polar residues Ser242, Ser278 and Thr316 are directly involved in the binding with both 20S(OH)T3 and 25(OH)T3 (Fig. 6F).


### Interactions with the PPARγ:
  We show dose-dependent binding of 20S(OH)T3 and 25(OH)T3 to the LBD of the PPARγ using a LanthaScreen TR-FRET coactivator assay (Fig. 7A). IC50 values for 20S(OH)T3 and 25(OH)T3 were around 10−7 and 10−6 M, respectively, with low binding for the parent compound, tachysterol3 (IC50 = 5 x 10−4 M). Docking scores of tachysterol3 metabolites were similar to the natural ligand of the PPARγ, rosiglitazone, and were markedly higher than those shown for 1α,25(OH)2D3, 25(OH)cholesterol and 20(OH)cholesterol, which are not ligands for the PPARγ (Table 1). These analyses are similar to the docking performed commercially by OTAVA LTD (Vaughan, ON L4K 0C3, Canada) using Glide in Extra Precision (XP) implemented in Schrodinger (version 2016-1). These scores were −11.96, −11.87, −10.09, for 20S(OH)T3, 25(OH)T3, and tachysterol3. Binding interaction for 20S(OH)T3 and 25(OH)T3 in comparison to 1α,25(OH)2D3 and rosiglitazone with PPARγ are shown in Fig. 7B and C. All four molecules are docked in the same binding pocket with slightly different docking postures (Fig. 7B). For both 20S(OH)T3 and 25(OH)T3, hydrophobic residues including Ile326, Met329, Leu330, Leu333, Ile341, Ile281, Cys285, Ala292 and Phe226 are directly involved in binding with PPARγ (Fig. 6C); a hydrogen bond is formed between the hydroxyl group on C3 of 25(OH)T3 and Ile281.

## Discussion
  In this study we show for the first time that human and bovine CYP11A1, and human CYP27A1 hydroxylate tachysterol3 producing 20S(OH)T3 and 25(OH)T3, respectively, and that these metabolites are detectable in the human epidermis and serum. Detection of the former was possible due to establishing a route of photochemical synthesis of 20S(OH)T3 from 20S(OH)-7-DHC. This discovery represents a significant advancement in our knowledge on metabolic transformation of photoproducts of 7-DHC (6, 78). While it is appreciated that activation of the vitamin D3 prohormone starts by hydroxylation at C25 in the classical pathway, and predominantly at C20 in a non-canonical pathway initiated by CYP11A1 (6, 15, 78, 79), it has only recently been established that lumisterol3 can also be metabolically activated by CYP11A1 and CYP27A1 (42, 47, 48, 80). The possibility that tachysterol3 can be enzymatically hydroxylated was not considered previously and the detection of 20S(OH)T3 and 25(OH)T3 in the epidermis and serum indicates its enzymatic hydroxylation in vivo in organs expressing CYP11A1 and CYP27A1. Relatively high serum concentrations of tachysterol3 (7.3±2.5 ng/ml), higher than that of that of vitamin D3, but lower than its precursor 7-DHC (16, 42), indicate that it can be hydroxylated at the systemic level after delivery to adrenals, liver, gonads or other organs having sufficient expression of CYP11A1 and/or CYP27A1, including skin. However, it remains to be determined whether tachysterol3 and it hydroxyderivatives bind to the vitamin D binding protein (DBP), as has been described for vitamin D3 and its hydroxyderivatives.

  20S(OH)T3 and 25(OH)T3 showed biological activity towards skin cells as demonstrated by inhibition of cell proliferation both in keratinocytes and fibroblasts and stimulation of the expression of differentiation and anti-oxidative genes in keratinocytes. The modulatory effects exerted by tachysterol3 are likely secondary to its metabolic transformation to 20S(OH)T3 and 25(OH)T3, and other yet to be defined tachysterol3-hydroxymetabolites produced by CYP27A1 and CYP11A1, which are expressed in keratinocytes and fibroblasts (43, 81, 82). The above phenotypic observations are consistent with inhibition of cell proliferation in skin cells and the induction of the keratinocyte differentiation program and anti-oxidative responses by CYP11A1-derived vitamin D3 and lumisterol3 hydroxyderivatives (21-23, 30, 48, 57). Thus, locally produced T3 can undergo metabolic activation to hydroxytachysterols which can regulate skin biological actions including epidermal barrier function and photoprotective activities. In the case of vitamin D deficiency, oral supplementation will have the desired effect of increasing serum 25(OH)D3 levels, but will not elevate the other photoproducts of UVB-action on the epidermis, lumisterol3 and tachysterol3, which also lead to active hydroxymetabolites. The similarity in net phenotypic effects between tachysterol3 and vitamin D3 derivatives is not surprising given that their major actions appear to be mediated by the same nuclear receptors, see below, which regulate skin cell proliferation keratinocyte differentiation and anti-oxidative programs.

  Based on the secosteroidal configuration, activation of the VDR by the 25(OH)T3 and tachysterol3 was predictable (53) and the function of 25(OH)T3 as an alternative ligand on the VDR (53) was confirmed in our study. The function of 20S(OH)T3 as an agonist on the VDR was documented by image flow cytometry, the induction of the VDR translocation to the nucleus from the cytoplasm, stimulation of the expression of CYP24 and molecular modeling. The docking scores for the G site were comparable to the classical VDR ligands, 1α,25(OH)2D3 (5, 83) and 1α,20S(OH)2D3 (38), while being very low for the A site. This observation is consistent with our previous molecular analyses of CYP11A1-derived vitamin D3 hydroxymetabolites using Schrödinger software which showed good interaction with the G site but not with the A site (28, 37). The former was confirmed by the co-crystallization of the VDR LBD with either 20S(OH)D3 or 1α,20S(OH)2D3 (38, 39). Thus, 20S(OH)T3 and 25(OH)T3 can interact with G binding site of the VDR but not with its A binding site, which is similar to CYP11A1-derived vitamin D3-hydroxymetabolites. In summary, CYP11A1 not only generates 20S(OH)D3 which with its downstream metabolites act as VDR agonists (28, 38-40, 58), but it also generates a similar side-chain hydroxyderivative, 20S(OH)T3, as a novel VDR agonist.

  Both 20S(OH)T3 and 25(OH)T3 and to lesser degree their precursor tachysterol3, can interact with additional nuclear receptors besides the VDR including AhR, LXRα and β and PPARγ. This was shown by functional studies with further confirmation by molecular modeling. This represents an important novel finding since the dogma is that secosteroids act predominantly on the VDR (83, 84). With respect to the AhR, we have previously shown that structurally similar CYP11A1-derived vitamin D3 hydroxymetabolites act as agonists on the AhR (41, 85). For LXRα and β, both vitamin D3- and lumisterol3-hydroxyderivatives have been recently identified as the ligands for these receptors (42, 85). Also, our previous modeling using Schrodinger software has predicted that tachystrol3-hydroxyderivatives can interact with the LBD of LXRα and β. The current functional and modeling data further substantiate this prediction. Of note is the unexpected and novel discovery that PPARγ is a target for tachysterol3-hydroxyderivatives, demonstrated in functional assays and supplemented by molecular modeling where these secosteroids gave similar dockings scores to the natural ligand. Finally, our molecular modeling also indicates that 20S(OH)T3, 25(OH)T3 and tachysterol3 have similar docking scores and binding poses to natural ligands for RORα and γ. This suggests that they can also act on RORs as previously was shown for vitamin D3- or lumisterol3-hydroxyderivatives with similar structures (27, 37, 48). In summary, identification of the AhR, LXRα and β, PPARγ, and likely RORα and γ as nuclear receptors targets for tachysterol3-hydroxyderivatives represents a significant advancement in our understanding of the mechanism of the phenotypic activities of these compounds, which will be mediated in a context and a cell-type dependent fashion.

  In summary, we have identified 20S(OH)T3 and 25(OH)T3 in the human epidermis and serum, identified enzymes responsible for their production, presented phenotypic effects in skin cells, and identified VDR, AhR and LXRs, and possibly RORs, as their genomic receptor targets. Thus CYP11A1, aside from its role in steroidogenesis, does not only metabolize vitamin D3, 7-DHC and lumisterol3 to biologically active metabolites (6, 28, 45, 48, 79, 82), but also activates tachysterol3. The same function can be assigned for CYP27A1, already known to act on lumisterol3 (47), D3 (3, 81) and 7-DHC (46, 74, 75). We believe that these findings open new areas for studies on the role of active forms of tachysterol3 not only in the skin but also in other target organs that possess VDR, AhR, LXRs and PPARγ receptors.
