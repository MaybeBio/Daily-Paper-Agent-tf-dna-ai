## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text (abstract, introduction, results and discussion, materials and methods, data availability, funding, acknowledgements, footnotes)
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific claims, methodological soundness, reproducibility, and interpretation of results as presented in the manuscript
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors present a protocol combining Structural Interaction Fingerprints (SIFts) generated by their previously published software fingeRNAt with machine learning (ML) methods to predict binding of small molecule ligands to six RNA targets. They report that SIFt-based ML models outperform classic general-purpose scoring functions in virtual screening, and that SHAP-based explainable AI can provide insights into molecular recognition determinants, using HIV-1 TAR RNA as a case study.
- **Visible evidence base** Results and discussion section with three subsections; figures referenced (Fig 1, Fig 2, Fig 3, Fig 4, Fig 5); materials and methods section; data availability statement
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Figures themselves are not provided; exact numerical results (e.g., AUROC values per model per target) are only partially reported in text; statistical test details (e.g., exact p-values) are referenced but not shown; hyperparameter settings and model configurations are not fully specified; dataset composition details (e.g., number of ligands per target) are not fully enumerated in the visible text

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** The manuscript addresses a relevant and timely problem: the application of interaction fingerprints and ML to RNA-ligand binding prediction, an area with limited prior work compared to protein-ligand systems. The authors build on their previously published fingeRNAt software and present a benchmark dataset that could be useful to the community. The main claims, however, are only partially supported by the evidence presented. The performance comparison with classic scoring functions is reported as not statistically significant, which weakens the central claim of superiority. The XAI analysis is illustrative but limited to a single target and a single docking pose per ligand, raising questions about generalizability. The manuscript would benefit from more rigorous statistical analysis, additional validation, and clearer reporting of dataset and model details.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in computational drug discovery, particularly those focused on RNA as a therapeutic target; developers of virtual screening methods and scoring functions; computational chemists and bioinformaticians interested in interaction fingerprints and explainable AI applied to biomolecular systems. The benchmark dataset and the open-source code may be of practical value to these communities.
- **Major strengths** The problem is relevant and underexplored; the use of SIFts for RNA-ligand systems is a reasonable extension of prior protein-focused work; the authors provide a benchmark dataset and code, which supports reproducibility; the application of SHAP to interpret RNA-ligand binding models is a useful demonstration; the manuscript is generally clearly written and accessible.
- **Major Concerns** See detailed items below.
- **Minor Comments** See detailed items below.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** R1-M1 (statistical significance of performance comparison), R1-M2 (limited validation of XAI findings), R1-M3 (incomplete reporting of dataset and model details)
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** Originality: moderate. The application of SIFts and ML to RNA-ligand binding is not entirely new, given the authors' prior work on fingeRNAt, but the specific combination with ML and XAI for RNA targets has some novelty. Scientific importance: moderate. RNA is an emerging therapeutic target, and improved virtual screening methods for RNA would be valuable; however, the demonstrated advantage over existing methods is not statistically robust. Interdisciplinary readership: the topic bridges computational chemistry, bioinformatics, and RNA biology, which could attract a broad audience, but the technical depth may limit accessibility. Technical soundness: the methodology is generally sound but has gaps in statistical rigor and validation. Readability for nonspecialists: the manuscript is reasonably accessible, though some ML and docking concepts may be challenging for a general audience.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The core idea is promising and the resources provided are useful, but the central claims require stronger statistical support and additional validation before the case is fully established.

### Major Concerns

- **Concern ID** R1-M1
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** Yes
- **Axis** Statistical rigor
- **Claim pointer** The authors claim that "SIFt-based models outperform the classic, general-purpose scoring functions in virtual screening" (abstract and results section).
- **Evidence pointer** Results and Discussion, section "SIFts, in combination with ML methods, outperform classic scoring functions in ligand binding predictions"; Fig 2
- **Concern** The authors state that the performance difference between SIFt-based methods and classic scoring functions is "not statistically significant" (location not provided, but implied in the text). This directly undermines the central claim of superiority. Without statistical significance, the claim of outperformance is not supported.
- **Why it matters** The primary contribution of the manuscript is the demonstration that SIFt-based ML models are better than existing scoring functions. If this difference is not statistically significant, the practical value of the method is unclear.
- **Resolution test** Provide a detailed statistical analysis, including confidence intervals and appropriate hypothesis tests (e.g., paired tests across targets), and report exact p-values. If significance is not achieved, temper the claim accordingly.

- **Concern ID** R1-M2
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** Yes
- **Axis** Validation of XAI findings
- **Claim pointer** The authors claim that SHAP analysis "allowed us to understand the decision-making process and decipher molecular recognition processes" and that identified interactions are "in agreement with the experimental data deposited in the literature."
- **Evidence pointer** Results and Discussion, section "Explainable ML models"; Fig 3; Fig 4
- **Concern** The XAI analysis is performed on a single RNA target (HIV-1 TAR) and uses only the top-scored docking pose for each ligand. This is a limited basis for drawing general conclusions about molecular recognition. Furthermore, the agreement with literature is qualitative and not systematically validated.
- **Why it matters** The claim that XAI can decipher molecular recognition processes is broad and potentially impactful. If the analysis is based on a narrow and potentially unrepresentative set of conditions, the conclusions may not generalize.
- **Resolution test** Extend the XAI analysis to multiple targets and multiple docking poses per ligand. Provide a more systematic comparison with experimental data, including quantitative metrics where possible.

- **Concern ID** R1-M3
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** Yes
- **Axis** Reproducibility and completeness of reporting
- **Claim pointer** The authors state that "the training dataset and the code required for performing all the described analyses can be found at https://github.com/filipsPL/fingernat-ml."
- **Evidence pointer** Data Availability section; Materials and Methods
- **Concern** The manuscript does not provide sufficient detail on the composition of the datasets (e.g., number of ligands per target, distribution of binders vs. non-binders), the exact hyperparameters used for each ML model, or the specific settings for the docking and fingerprint calculations. This limits reproducibility.
- **Why it matters** For a methods-oriented manuscript, reproducibility is essential. Without complete details, other researchers cannot fully replicate or build upon the work.
- **Resolution test** Provide a comprehensive description of dataset composition, model hyperparameters, and all computational settings, either in the main text or as supplementary material.

### Minor Comments

- **Concern ID** R1-m1
- **Severity** Minor
- **Axis** Clarity of reporting
- **Affected element** Performance metrics
- **Evidence pointer** Results and Discussion, section "CatBoost, Extra Trees, and LightGBM are the most effective ML methods in predicting ligand binding"; Fig 1
- **Issue** The text reports average and median AUROC values but does not provide a full breakdown of performance per target and per model configuration. This makes it difficult to assess variability and identify specific strengths or weaknesses.
- **Required correction** Include a table or supplementary figure showing AUROC values for each model, target, and configuration.

- **Concern ID** R1-m2
- **Severity** Minor
- **Axis** Interpretation of results
- **Affected element** Discussion of target-specific performance
- **Evidence pointer** Results and Discussion, section "SIFts, in combination with ML methods, outperform classic scoring functions in ligand binding predictions"
- **Issue** The authors note that performance varies by target but do not discuss potential reasons for this variability (e.g., structural differences, dataset size, ligand diversity). This would strengthen the interpretation.
- **Required correction** Add a brief discussion of factors that may contribute to target-specific performance differences.

- **Concern ID** R1-m3
- **Severity** Minor
- **Axis** Presentation of XAI results
- **Affected element** Fig 3 and Fig 4
- **Evidence pointer** Results and Discussion, section "Explainable ML models"
- **Issue** The figures are referenced but not provided in the manuscript text. The description of SHAP values and feature importance is clear but would benefit from visual confirmation.
- **Required correction** Ensure figures are included and clearly labeled, with appropriate legends explaining all elements.

- **Concern ID** R1-m4
- **Severity** Minor
- **Axis** Terminology
- **Affected element** Use of "local scoring functions"
- **Evidence pointer** Results and Discussion, section "SIFts, in combination with ML methods, outperform classic scoring functions in ligand binding predictions"
- **Issue** The term "local scoring functions" is used to describe SIFt-based ML models, but this term is not defined or commonly used in the field. It may confuse readers.
- **Required correction** Define the term clearly or use more standard terminology, such as "target-specific scoring functions."

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that SIFt-based models "outperform" classic scoring functions is not supported by statistically significant evidence, as acknowledged in the text.
- The claim that SHAP analysis "deciphers molecular recognition processes" is based on a single target and a single pose per ligand, which is insufficient to support such a broad conclusion.
- The claim that the benchmark dataset "can be readily used by the scientific community" is plausible but cannot be fully assessed without verifying the dataset's completeness and quality, which is not possible from the manuscript alone.
- The statement that this is "the first described protocol for predicting ligand activity towards RNA receptors with molecular docking and explainable SIFt- and ML-based scoring techniques" is a strong claim that would require a thorough literature review to verify; the manuscript does not provide such evidence.
