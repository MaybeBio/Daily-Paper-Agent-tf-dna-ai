## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text (abstract, introduction, results, discussion, methods) from bioRxiv preprint
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific content, methodology, claims, and evidence as presented in the provided text; no external data or supplementary files were assessed beyond what is described
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors identify thousands of DNA variants showing reproducible allele-biased transcription factor (TF) binding across human brain regions using graph-genome-based ChIP-seq analysis, link these to eQTLs, and propose mechanistic insights into gene regulation in the brain
- **Visible evidence base** Main text figures (Figures 1-5), tables (Tables 1-3), and supplemental figures/tables as referenced; methods section; no raw data or code were independently verified
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Supplementary tables (1-4), supplemental figures (1-12), full ChIP-seq and RNA-seq datasets, code repository, and detailed quality control metrics were not available for direct inspection; this limits verification of several quantitative claims

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript presents a substantial and potentially valuable resource for understanding allele-specific TF binding in human brain tissue. The use of graph genomes to reduce reference allele bias is methodologically sound and well-motivated. The scale of the dataset (93 TFs, 1,004 ChIP-seq experiments, multiple brain regions) is impressive. However, several key claims require stronger statistical support, and the biological insights, while interesting, are largely descriptive. The link to eQTLs is a strength, but the mechanistic depth is limited by the correlational nature of the analyses. The manuscript would benefit from clearer presentation of effect sizes, more rigorous multiple-testing corrections, and validation of key findings in independent datasets or experimental perturbations.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in regulatory genomics, neurogenetics, and psychiatric genetics would find this resource valuable. The dataset provides a large catalog of allele-biased TF binding events in brain tissue, which could inform interpretation of GWAS and eQTL studies. The methodological demonstration of graph-genome mapping for allele-specific analysis is also relevant to the broader genomics community. Clinicians or researchers focused on specific neuropsychiatric or neurodegenerative diseases may find the FBXO7 and SYN3 example particularly relevant.
- **Major strengths** 1) Large-scale, systematic analysis of allele-biased TF binding in human brain tissue, a significant advance over previous studies in cell lines or limited tissue types. 2) Rigorous methodological approach using graph genomes to mitigate reference allele bias, with clear demonstration of improvement over linear mapping. 3) Integration with eQTL data provides a direct link between TF binding variation and gene expression, enhancing biological interpretability. 4) The reproducibility assessment across donors and brain regions is thoughtful and adds confidence to the findings. 5) The resource is publicly available, which will facilitate further research.
- **Major Concerns** 
  - **R1-M1** (Blocking: Yes) The statistical framework for identifying allele-biased binding is not fully described, and the use of a nominal p-value threshold without correction for multiple testing across millions of variants and 93 TFs raises concerns about false positives. The authors state that 7,828 and 9,570 variants are significant at p<=0.001 in each donor, but given the number of tests performed, the expected number of false positives could be substantial. The manuscript does not report false discovery rates or provide any correction for multiple comparisons.
  - **R1-M2** (Blocking: Yes) The claim that "allele-biased binding is prevalent in functional regions important for neuronal differentiation" is based on enrichment analysis, but the statistical test used (binomial test) and the control set are not clearly described. The authors state that 73.3% of TF-allele bias falls within or near cCREs, but the expected proportion given the genomic distribution of heterozygous variants is not provided. Without a proper background model, this enrichment claim is not well-supported.
  - **R1-M3** (Blocking: No) The correlation between allele-biased TF binding and eQTL effects (Figure 4A) is reported as Spearman's rho = 0.43, but the analysis appears to be limited to a subset of variants (those with both ChIP-seq and RNA-seq bias). The authors do not address potential confounding factors, such as linkage disequilibrium between nearby variants or tissue-specific effects. The causal direction (TF binding affecting expression vs. expression affecting TF binding) is not established.
  - **R1-M4** (Blocking: No) The analysis of rare variants (Figure 3B-C) is interesting but limited by small sample sizes. The authors report 791 variants with DAF <= 0.001, but the number of individuals contributing to these rare allele observations is not stated. The conclusion that rare alleles "more frequently led to reduced TF binding" is based on a modest number of observations and would benefit from confidence intervals or replication.
  - **R1-M5** (Blocking: No) The manuscript does not provide a clear assessment of the biological significance of the identified allele-biased binding events. While the authors highlight two examples (RPS14 and FBXO7), the overall impact on gene regulation is not quantified. For instance, what fraction of eQTLs can be explained by allele-biased TF binding? How many of the identified variants are in strong LD with known GWAS hits for brain-related traits?
- **Minor Comments** 
  - **R1-m1** Axis: Clarity. The methods section for "Identification of Allele Bias" is brief and lacks details on how reads were assigned to haplotypes when multiple variants were present in a single read. The custom R code is mentioned but not described in sufficient detail for replication.
  - **R1-m2** Axis: Presentation. Figure 2A-B would benefit from error bars or confidence intervals to convey uncertainty in the reproducibility estimates. The current presentation of point estimates without uncertainty is misleading.
  - **R1-m3** Axis: Completeness. The authors state that "approximately half are biased for at least one of these datasets" (histone marks or POL2RA), but do not provide a breakdown by histone mark or by TF. This would be informative for understanding the specificity of the effects.
  - **R1-m4** Axis: Interpretation. The discussion of the FBXO7 example (Figure 5) is speculative regarding the role of CTCF and cohesion in distal regulation. The authors should be more cautious in their interpretation, as the Hi-C data are from a different cell type (iCell GlutaNeurons) and may not reflect the brain regions studied.
  - **R1-m5** Axis: Reproducibility. The authors do not report the number of reads per variant or the distribution of read depths across variants. This information is critical for assessing the reliability of the allele-bias calls, particularly for low-depth variants.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** R1-M1 (multiple testing correction), R1-M2 (proper enrichment background model), and R1-M3 (confounding and causality in eQTL link) are the primary technical issues. Additionally, the lack of detailed methods for read assignment and the absence of read-depth reporting (R1-m1, R1-m5) undermine reproducibility.
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** 
  - Originality: High. The scale and tissue-specific focus of this allele-biased TF binding analysis are novel, and the use of graph genomes in this context is innovative.
  - Scientific importance: Moderate to high. The resource has clear utility for the field, but the biological insights are largely descriptive and do not yet provide deep mechanistic understanding.
  - Interdisciplinary readership: Moderate. The work will appeal primarily to genomics and neurogenetics specialists; the broader neuroscience community may find the eQTL links relevant but the technical focus may limit accessibility.
  - Technical soundness: Moderate. The graph-genome approach is well-executed, but the statistical framework for identifying allele bias and the enrichment analyses have significant gaps that need addressing.
  - Readability for nonspecialists: Moderate. The manuscript is generally well-written, but the methods and results sections assume familiarity with ChIP-seq, eQTL, and graph-genome concepts. The examples (Figures 4-5) help illustrate the findings but could be more accessible.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The manuscript presents a valuable resource and a sound methodological approach, but the statistical rigor and biological depth need improvement before the claims are fully established. The authors should address the multiple testing issue, provide a proper enrichment analysis, and strengthen the eQTL link with additional analyses.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that "thousands of DNA variants show reproducible bias" is not supported by a clear multiple testing correction; the number of false positives is unknown.
- The enrichment of allele-biased variants in cCREs is not properly benchmarked against a null model.
- The correlation between allele-biased TF binding and eQTL effects (rho = 0.43) is presented without addressing potential confounders or establishing causality.
- The conclusion that rare alleles "more frequently led to reduced TF binding" is based on a small number of observations and lacks statistical confidence intervals.
- The statement that "approximately half are biased for at least one of these datasets" (histone marks or POL2RA) is vague and not quantified by histone mark or TF.
- The biological significance of the findings is not quantified; the fraction of eQTLs explained by allele-biased TF binding is not reported.
