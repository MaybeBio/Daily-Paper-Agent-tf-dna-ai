## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text (bioRxiv preprint, 2023)
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific content, methodology, data interpretation, and claims as presented in the provided text. No supplementary figures or tables were available for direct inspection.
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors use AlphaFold2-predicted structures of human DRG1/DFRP1 and DRG2/DFRP2 complexes to identify interface residues governing paralog-specific binding. They report that five mutations in DRG1 (L155E, I260R, Y262A, V264K, D280Y) can switch its binding specificity from DFRP1 to DFRP2, with Y262 and D280 being most critical. They also report that DFRP2 binding does not stimulate DRG1 GTPase activity, whereas DFRP1 does.
- **Visible evidence base** Main text figures (Figures 1-8), supplementary figure references (S1-S7), supplementary tables (S1-S2), methods section, and sequence alignment descriptions. No raw data, uncropped blots, or structural coordinates were provided.
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Supplementary figures and tables (S1-S7, S1-S2) were not available for review. Structural model coordinates and confidence metrics (pLDDT, PAE) were not provided. Raw GTPase assay data and statistical analysis details were not included. Uncropped western blot images were not available.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript addresses a well-defined and interesting question in protein-protein interaction specificity using a combination of computational prediction and biochemical validation. The finding that a small number of mutations can rewire DRG1 binding specificity is potentially valuable. However, the evidence presented is largely qualitative, with limited quantitative binding data, and several claims regarding structural mechanisms are inferred from models without experimental validation. The GTPase assay data are presented in a preliminary manner, and the lack of statistical analysis weakens the conclusions. The manuscript would benefit from more rigorous quantitative analysis and additional controls.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers studying GTPase biology, particularly the DRG/DFRP family, and those interested in the molecular determinants of protein-protein interaction specificity in paralogous systems. The work may also be of interest to structural biologists using AlphaFold for hypothesis generation, and to those studying the evolution of binding specificity.
- **Major strengths** The study addresses a clear and important question about how paralogous proteins achieve binding specificity. The use of AlphaFold models to guide mutagenesis is a modern and potentially powerful approach. The identification of a small set of residues that can switch specificity is a notable finding. The inclusion of functional assays (GTPase activity) adds a layer of biological relevance.
- **Major Concerns**
  - R1-M1: The quantitative basis for binding specificity claims is weak. The manuscript relies on qualitative western blot assessments of co-immunoprecipitation without providing quantitative measurements of binding affinity or stoichiometry. The claim that "as few as five mutations" switch specificity is not supported by quantitative binding data.
  - R1-M2: The structural interpretation is based entirely on AlphaFold models without experimental validation. The authors do not provide confidence metrics (pLDDT, PAE) for the predicted interfaces, and the models are not validated by mutagenesis of predicted interface residues beyond the few tested. The claim that Y262 and D280 are "most important" is based on qualitative observations.
  - R1-M3: The GTPase assay data are presented without statistical analysis. The authors report differences in activity but do not provide error bars, replicates, or statistical tests. The conclusion that DFRP2 cannot stimulate DRG1 activity is based on a single representative experiment.
  - R1-M4: The manuscript does not address the potential role of the DFRP C-terminal regions in binding specificity. The authors note that DFRP1 has a C-terminal region that wraps around DRG1, but they do not test whether this region contributes to specificity. The truncation experiment (DFRP1 1-295) is only used to test binding, not specificity.
  - R1-M5: The evolutionary interpretation in the Discussion is speculative and not directly supported by the data. The claim that the identified residues "shed light on evolutionary constraints" is not backed by phylogenetic or selection analyses.
- **Minor Comments**
  - R1-m1: The manuscript would benefit from a clearer description of the AlphaFold model quality, including pLDDT scores for the interface regions.
  - R1-m2: The authors should provide a more detailed description of the "Find Contacts" parameters used to define interface residues.
  - R1-m3: The western blot figures are not quantified. Densitometry should be performed to support the qualitative claims.
  - R1-m4: The authors should clarify whether the DRG1 LIYVD mutant was tested for binding to DFRP2 in the presence of competing DFRP1, as this would more directly assess specificity.
  - R1-m5: The manuscript should include a discussion of the potential limitations of using AlphaFold models for predicting binding specificity.
  - R1-m6: The authors should provide a more detailed description of the GTPase assay, including the concentration of GTP used and the linearity of the assay.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** R1-M1, R1-M2, R1-M3. The core claims of binding specificity switching and functional specificity are not quantitatively established from the provided evidence.
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** Originality: The question of how paralogous DRG proteins achieve binding specificity is somewhat novel, but the approach (AlphaFold-guided mutagenesis) is increasingly common. Scientific importance: The DRG/DFRP system is relatively niche, and the broader significance of the findings is not fully articulated. Interdisciplinary readership: The work is primarily of interest to structural biologists and biochemists; the broader cell biology or disease relevance is not developed. Technical soundness: The experimental approach is sound in principle, but the lack of quantitative data and statistical analysis is a significant weakness. Readability for nonspecialists: The manuscript is generally well-written and accessible, but the significance of the findings for a general audience is not clearly established.
- **Recommendation posture** Currently not established from the provided evidence. The manuscript addresses an interesting question and presents a plausible hypothesis, but the quantitative evidence is insufficient to support the central claims. Major revisions, including quantitative binding assays, statistical analysis of GTPase data, and validation of structural models, would be required.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that "as few as five mutations in DRG1 are able to switch its binding from DFRP1 to DFRP2" is not quantitatively supported.
- The claim that Y262 and D280 are "most important" for specifying DFRP1 over DFRP2 is based on qualitative observations without statistical comparison.
- The claim that DFRP2 binding cannot stimulate DRG1 GTPase activity is based on a single experiment without statistical analysis.
- The evolutionary interpretation in the Discussion is speculative and not supported by data presented in the manuscript.
- The statement that the predicted structural models are "likely accurate" is not fully supported, as confidence metrics are not provided and only a limited number of interface residues were tested.
