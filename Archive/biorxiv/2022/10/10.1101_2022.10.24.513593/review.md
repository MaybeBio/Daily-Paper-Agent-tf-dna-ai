## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text (abstract, introduction, results, discussion, methods, data/code availability statements)
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific and technical evaluation of the EUGENe toolkit as presented, including its design, implementation, and the three use case demonstrations
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors present EUGENe, a Python toolkit designed to support FAIR deep learning analyses of regulatory sequences. The toolkit is built around two core objects, SeqData and BaseModel, and provides modular functionality for data extraction/transformation/loading, model instantiation/training, and model evaluation/interpretation. The authors demonstrate the toolkit's utility and extensibility through three use cases: plant promoter activity prediction, RNA binding protein specificity prediction, and transcription factor binding classification.
- **Visible evidence base** Full manuscript text including methods sections for all three use cases, figure descriptions, supplementary table references, and code/data availability statements
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Actual code implementation, supplementary tables (1-10), supplementary figures, documentation pages, and the associated GitHub repository were not provided for direct inspection. Performance metrics are described in text but detailed numerical results are not fully reported in the manuscript body.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript describes a timely and potentially valuable software contribution to the genomics deep learning community. The authors correctly identify a gap in the availability of comprehensive, FAIR-compliant toolkits for regulatory sequence analysis with deep learning. The design philosophy, modeled after the successful Scanpy ecosystem, is sound and the modular architecture appears well-conceived. The three use cases demonstrate genuine versatility across different data types, model architectures, and prediction tasks. However, the manuscript as presented has several significant weaknesses. The performance comparisons are incomplete and lack statistical rigor in places. The novelty of the toolkit relative to existing tools is not clearly articulated. The extensibility claims, while plausible, are not demonstrated in a way that would convince a skeptical reader. The manuscript would benefit from a more critical discussion of limitations and a clearer statement of what specific problems EUGENe solves that existing tools do not. The writing is generally clear but some sections, particularly the use case descriptions, are dense and would benefit from restructuring for readability.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Computational biologists and bioinformaticians working on cis-regulatory element function, particularly those applying deep learning to genomic sequences. Researchers developing or using similar toolkits (e.g., Selene, Janggu, Kipoi) would be interested in the comparative features and design choices. The broader genomics community interested in adopting deep learning methods but lacking the engineering expertise to build custom pipelines would find the toolkit's simplicity and documentation appealing. The plant genomics community specifically would be interested in the promoter activity prediction use case.
- **Major strengths** The manuscript addresses a real and pressing need for standardized, FAIR-compliant deep learning toolkits in regulatory genomics. The design is thoughtful, drawing on the successful Scanpy model, and the two-object architecture (SeqData and BaseModel) provides a good balance of abstraction and control. The three use cases are well-chosen to demonstrate versatility across different data modalities (DNA, RNA), prediction tasks (regression, classification), and model architectures (custom, SOTA, imported). The inclusion of interpretation methods (filter visualization, feature attribution, in silico experiments) as core functionality is a significant strength, as interpretability is often an afterthought in such toolkits. The emphasis on documentation, tutorials, and reproducibility is commendable.
- **Major Concerns**
    - **Concern ID** R1-M1
    - **Severity** Major
    - **Blocking** Yes
    - **Axis** Technical soundness
    - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that EUGENe enables users to "achieve high predictive performance" across the three use cases, and that the toolkit's built-in models perform comparably to or better than the custom architectures they were benchmarked against.
    - **Evidence pointer** Results section, Figure 3b, Figure 4b-c, Figure 5b-c, Supplementary Tables 3, 4, 6, 7, 9, 10
    - **Concern** The performance comparisons presented are incomplete and not sufficiently rigorous. For the plant promoter use case, the authors state that "Hybrids and CNNs outperforming Jores21CNNs when evaluated by variance explained (R2)" for leaf models, but the opposite trend was observed for protoplast models. However, no statistical analysis is presented to determine whether these differences are significant. The comparison is further complicated by the fact that the Jores21CNN was initialized with motif-derived filters while the built-in models were not, making the comparison potentially unfair. For the RNA binding use case, the authors report that their models show "comparable performance to Kipoi and DeepBind models" but do not provide a direct statistical comparison. The multitask model comparison is particularly problematic, as the authors trained their multitask model on 233 RBPs while the DeepBind multitask model was trained on a different set. For the JunD use case, the authors report that their best model achieves an auPRC of 0.87, but do not compare this to the auPRC reported by Kopp et al. for their model. Without these direct comparisons and appropriate statistical tests, the claim of "high predictive performance" is not fully supported.
    - **Why it matters** The primary value proposition of a toolkit like EUGENe is that it allows users to train models that perform at least as well as custom implementations. If the performance claims are not rigorously demonstrated, potential users cannot make informed decisions about whether to adopt the toolkit. The lack of statistical rigor also undermines the scientific validity of the use case demonstrations.
    - **Resolution test** The authors should provide a comprehensive performance comparison table for each use case, including all relevant metrics (R2, AUC, auPRC, correlation coefficients) with confidence intervals and appropriate statistical tests (e.g., paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests across random initializations). For the plant promoter use case, the comparison should be made with matched initialization schemes (i.e., either all models initialized with motifs or none). For the RNA binding use case, the multitask model should be compared to a multitask DeepBind model trained on the same set of RBPs. For the JunD use case, the authors should directly compare their auPRC to that reported by Kopp et al. under the same evaluation conditions.
    - **Concern ID** R1-M2
    - **Severity** Major
    - **Blocking** Yes
    - **Axis** Originality
    - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that EUGENe represents a "springboard toward a collaborative ecosystem for DL applications in genomics research" and that it addresses the "fragmented nature of the set of tools, methods and data that exist across the field."
    - **Evidence pointer** Introduction, Discussion
    - **Concern** The manuscript does not provide a clear and systematic comparison of EUGENe with existing tools such as Selene, Janggu, Kipoi, and ENNGene. While the authors mention these tools in the introduction, they do not provide a detailed feature-by-feature comparison that would allow readers to understand what specific gaps EUGENe fills. The claim of being a "springboard toward a collaborative ecosystem" is aspirational but not supported by evidence of community adoption or contribution mechanisms beyond standard open-source practices. The authors also do not discuss how EUGENe's design differs from or improves upon the Scanpy model it is explicitly modeled after.
    - **Why it matters** In a crowded field of existing tools, a new toolkit must clearly articulate its unique value proposition. Without a systematic comparison, it is difficult for potential users to understand why they should choose EUGENe over existing alternatives. The claim of fostering a collaborative ecosystem is a strong one that requires evidence of mechanisms for community contribution and a roadmap for governance.
    - **Resolution test** The authors should include a table comparing EUGENe's features (e.g., data formats supported, model architectures available, interpretation methods, documentation, community contribution mechanisms) with those of Selene, Janggu, Kipoi, and ENNGene. They should also discuss specific design decisions that differentiate EUGENe from these tools and from Scanpy. If the collaborative ecosystem claim is to be maintained, the authors should describe specific mechanisms for community contribution (e.g., contribution guidelines, plugin architecture, governance model).
    - **Concern ID** R1-M3
    - **Severity** Major
    - **Blocking** No
    - **Axis** Technical soundness
    - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that EUGENe's interpretation suite can be used to "identify key features of the cis-regulatory code" and that the in silico evolution experiments demonstrate the toolkit's utility for this purpose.
    - **Evidence pointer** Results section, Figure 3e-g, Figure 4d-f, Figure 5d-f, Supplementary Figures 1, 3, 4, 5
    - **Concern** The interpretation results are presented descriptively without quantitative validation. For example, the authors state that in silico evolution of plant promoters led to "an 142% average increase in predicted activity" but do not provide confidence intervals or statistical tests. More importantly, the biological validity of the interpretation results is not assessed. For instance, the authors do not compare the motifs identified by their interpretation methods to known motifs or to results from the original publications. The claim that the toolkit can identify "key features of the cis-regulatory code" requires validation against known biology.
    - **Why it matters** Interpretation methods are only useful if they produce biologically meaningful results. Without validation against known motifs or experimental data, the interpretation results could be artifacts of the model or the interpretation method. This is particularly important for a toolkit that aims to be a community resource, as users will rely on the interpretation methods to generate biological hypotheses.
    - **Resolution test** The authors should validate their interpretation results against known biology. For the plant promoter use case, they could compare the motifs identified by their filter visualization and feature attribution methods to known plant transcription factor binding motifs. For the RNA binding use case, they could compare their identified motifs to the known binding preferences of the RBPs. For the JunD use case, they could compare their identified motifs to the known JunD binding motif. They should also provide confidence intervals for their quantitative claims (e.g., the 142% increase in predicted activity).
    - **Concern ID** R1-M4
    - **Severity** Major
    - **Blocking** No
    - **Axis** Technical soundness
    - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that EUGENe is "simple" and that users can "develop workflows on new or existing datasets with two customizable Python objects."
    - **Evidence pointer** Abstract, Results section, Methods section
    - **Concern** The simplicity claim is not supported by the manuscript. The use case descriptions are dense and require significant background knowledge to follow. The methods sections are highly technical and do not provide a clear step-by-step guide for a new user. The authors do not provide any usability metrics (e.g., time to complete a typical workflow, number of lines of code required) or user studies. The claim of simplicity is subjective and would benefit from concrete evidence.
    - **Why it matters** The simplicity of a toolkit is a key factor in its adoption. If the toolkit is not genuinely simple to use, the claim of lowering the barrier to entry for deep learning in genomics is not supported. The manuscript should provide evidence that a typical user can achieve the demonstrated results with reasonable effort.
    - **Resolution test** The authors should provide a more accessible walkthrough of a typical workflow, perhaps using a simple example dataset. They could also provide code snippets that demonstrate the minimal amount of code required to train a model and generate interpretations. If possible, they could include a usability study or at least a qualitative assessment of the learning curve based on user feedback.
- **Minor Comments**
    - **Concern ID** R1-m1
    - **Severity** Minor
    - **Axis** Readability
    - **Affected element** Results section, use case descriptions
    - **Evidence pointer** Results section, Figures 3-5
    - **Issue** The use case descriptions are dense and difficult to follow, particularly for readers not familiar with the specific datasets or model architectures. The figures are complex and not always well-referenced in the text.
    - **Required correction** The authors should restructure the use case descriptions to provide a clearer narrative: (1) what question is being asked, (2) what data is used, (3) what models are trained, (4) what results are obtained, and (5) what conclusions can be drawn. The figures should be simplified or better annotated to guide the reader.
    - **Concern ID** R1-m2
    - **Severity** Minor
    - **Axis** Reproducibility
    - **Affected element** Methods section, hyperparameter specifications
    - **Evidence pointer** Methods section, Supplementary Tables 3, 6, 9
    - **Issue** The hyperparameter specifications are provided in supplementary tables, but the manuscript does not describe how these hyperparameters were chosen. It is unclear whether they were optimized for each task or used as defaults.
    - **Required correction** The authors should briefly describe the hyperparameter selection process in the methods section, including whether any hyperparameter optimization was performed and, if so, what method was used.
    - **Concern ID** R1-m3
    - **Severity** Minor
    - **Axis** Technical soundness
    - **Affected element** Methods section, RNA binding data analysis
    - **Evidence pointer** Methods section, RNA binding data analysis
    - **Issue** The description of the RNA binding data preprocessing is incomplete. The authors state that they "capped at the 99.95 percentile" and "Z-scored" the data, but do not describe how missing values were handled for the single task models.
    - **Required correction** The authors should describe how missing values were handled in the single task models, as this could affect the performance comparison.
    - **Concern ID** R1-m4
    - **Severity** Minor
    - **Axis** Readability
    - **Affected element** Discussion
    - **Evidence pointer** Discussion
    - **Issue** The discussion is largely forward-looking and does not critically assess the limitations of the current version of EUGENe. For example, the authors do not discuss the memory limitations of SeqData that they mention in the discussion.
    - **Required correction** The authors should include a more balanced discussion of limitations, including the memory issue, the lack of built-in hyperparameter optimization, and the need for more extensive benchmarking against existing tools.
    - **Concern ID** R1-m5
    - **Severity** Minor
    - **Axis** Reproducibility
    - **Affected element** Code availability
    - **Evidence pointer** Code availability section
    - **Issue** The manuscript states that the code is available on GitHub and PyPI, but does not provide a version number or a DOI for the specific version used in the manuscript.
    - **Required correction** The authors should provide a version number and a DOI (e.g., via Zenodo) for the specific version of the code used in the manuscript to ensure reproducibility.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** The performance comparisons need to be made more rigorous with appropriate statistical tests and matched experimental conditions. The interpretation results need to be validated against known biology. The novelty of the toolkit relative to existing tools needs to be clearly established through a systematic comparison.
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** 
    - **Originality** The concept of a comprehensive, FAIR-compliant deep learning toolkit for regulatory genomics is not entirely novel, as tools like Selene and Janggu exist. However, the specific design choices, particularly the two-object architecture and the integration of interpretation methods, may offer a new combination of features. The originality claim is weakened by the lack of a systematic comparison with existing tools.
    - **Scientific importance** The need for standardized, accessible deep learning tools in genomics is significant, and a well-designed toolkit could have substantial impact on the field. The three use cases address important problems in regulatory genomics. However, the scientific importance of the toolkit itself, as opposed to the use cases, is not fully established.
    - **Interdisciplinary readership** The toolkit is relevant to computational biologists, bioinformaticians, and genomics researchers. The manuscript is written at a level that is accessible to these groups, but may be challenging for researchers without a strong computational background. The use cases span plant biology, RNA biology, and transcription factor biology, which broadens the potential readership.
    - **Technical soundness** The technical design of the toolkit appears sound, but the performance claims are not rigorously supported. The lack of statistical analysis and the incomplete comparisons are significant weaknesses. The interpretation methods are standard but their application is not validated.
    - **Readability for nonspecialists** The manuscript is generally well-written but the use case descriptions are dense and may be difficult for nonspecialists to follow. The abstract and introduction are accessible, but the results section requires significant background knowledge.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The manuscript describes a potentially valuable toolkit, but the current evidence does not fully establish its performance, novelty, or utility. The authors should address the major concerns regarding performance comparison rigor, interpretation validation, and systematic comparison with existing tools before the case for the toolkit's value is fully made.
