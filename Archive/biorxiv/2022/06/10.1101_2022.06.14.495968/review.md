## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** The abstract presents a potentially interesting structural and functional study linking SALL4 zinc-finger DNA recognition to Okihiro syndrome mutations. The central claims are plausible and the work could be of value to the developmental biology and transcriptional regulation communities. However, the abstract alone does not provide sufficient evidence to evaluate the technical soundness of the structural determination, the robustness of the binding data, or the strength of the genomic association analyses. The claim that the mutations are "likely pathogenic" is not fully supported by the evidence described in the abstract, as reduced binding and altered genomic association are suggestive but not definitive proof of pathogenicity.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Developmental biologists studying limb development and stem cell identity, researchers working on zinc-finger DNA recognition mechanisms, clinicians and geneticists investigating Okihiro syndrome and SALL4-related disorders, and structural biologists interested in protein-DNA interactions. The link to thalidomide teratogenicity may also attract interest from the pharmacology and toxicology communities.
- **Major strengths** The study addresses a clinically relevant question with a structural approach, which is likely to provide mechanistic insight. The observation that SALL4 uses small hydrophobic and polar side chains for flexible AT-rich recognition is a potentially novel contribution to the understanding of zinc-finger DNA-binding specificity. The inclusion of patient-derived mutations and the attempt to link structural findings to functional and genomic consequences strengthens the translational relevance.

### Major Concerns

- **Concern ID** R1-M1  
- **Severity** Major  
- **Blocking** Yes  
- **Axis** Structural evidence  
- **Claim pointer** The authors claim to present a crystal structure of SALL4 C-terminal zinc fingers with an AT-rich DNA sequence, showing that small hydrophobic and polar side chains provide flexible recognition in the major groove.  
- **Evidence pointer** Abstract only; no structural figures, resolution, refinement statistics, or validation metrics are provided  
- **Concern** The abstract states that a crystal structure was determined, but no details are given regarding resolution, data quality, or how the structure supports the proposed recognition mechanism. Without these details, the structural claim cannot be evaluated.  
- **Why it matters** The structural model is the foundation of the proposed mechanism. If the structure is of insufficient quality or the interpretation of side-chain interactions is not well supported, the central mechanistic claim would be weakened.  
- **Resolution test** Provide the structural figures, crystallographic statistics, and a clear description of how the side-chain interactions were identified and validated. Show electron density maps for the key residues involved in DNA recognition.

- **Concern ID** R1-M2  
- **Severity** Major  
- **Blocking** Yes  
- **Axis** Functional evidence  
- **Claim pointer** The authors claim that patient-derived missense mutations within the C-terminal zinc fingers reduced overall binding to DNA but did not alter the preference for AT-rich sequences.  
- **Evidence pointer** Abstract only; no binding assay data, mutation list, or quantitative results are provided  
- **Concern** The abstract reports reduced overall DNA binding for patient mutations, but no experimental details are given. It is unclear which assays were used, how binding was quantified, what controls were included, and whether the reduction was statistically significant. The claim that AT-rich preference was unchanged also requires quantitative comparison across multiple sequences.  
- **Why it matters** The functional distinction between reduced overall binding and preserved sequence preference is central to the proposed mechanism. If the assays are not robust or the interpretation is not statistically grounded, the conclusion that mutations affect binding without altering specificity would be unsupported.  
- **Resolution test** Provide detailed binding assay methods, including protein constructs, DNA sequences, assay formats, replicates, and statistical analysis. Show that the mutations reduce binding affinity or occupancy while maintaining relative preference across AT-rich versus non-AT-rich sequences.

- **Concern ID** R1-M3  
- **Severity** Major  
- **Blocking** Yes  
- **Axis** Genomic and pathogenic evidence  
- **Claim pointer** The authors claim that patient mutations altered association of SALL4 with AT-rich genomic sites, providing evidence that these mutations are likely pathogenic.  
- **Evidence pointer** Abstract only; no genomic data, cell types, or analysis methods are described  
- **Concern** The abstract states that mutations altered genomic association, but no details are provided on the experimental system, the type of genomic assay used, the number of sites affected, or the magnitude of the changes. The leap from altered genomic association to "likely pathogenic" is not justified without additional evidence, such as correlation with known disease phenotypes or functional consequences in relevant cellular models.  
- **Why it matters** The pathogenicity claim is the clinically relevant conclusion of the study. If the genomic data are not robust or the link to disease mechanism is not established, the clinical significance of the findings would be overstated.  
- **Resolution test** Provide genomic assay details, including cell type, method, replicates, and analysis pipeline. Show that the mutations cause significant changes in SALL4 occupancy at AT-rich sites and connect these changes to known Okihiro syndrome phenotypes or relevant functional readouts.
