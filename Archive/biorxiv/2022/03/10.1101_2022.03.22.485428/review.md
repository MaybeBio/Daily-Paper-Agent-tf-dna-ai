## Reviewer

- **Overall assessment** The manuscript addresses a timely and intriguing question about the functional divergence of archaeal histones in hypersaline environments. The conceptual framing is strong, and the comparative approach across domains of life is ambitious and potentially impactful. However, the provided text is insufficient to evaluate the technical rigor of the experiments, the statistical support for the claims, or the robustness of the conclusions. The central claims, while plausible, are not verifiable from the supplied material. The manuscript would benefit from a clearer articulation of methods and quantitative results, but the scientific premise is compelling and warrants further consideration.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in archaeal biology, chromatin structure and evolution, transcriptional regulation, and comparative genomics would find these results relevant. The work challenges assumptions about histone conservation and function, and could inform broader questions about the origins of eukaryotic chromatin. The halophilic focus also appeals to those studying extremophile adaptation and gene regulation under environmental stress.
- **Major strengths** The comparative design across TFs, NAPs, and histones is conceptually novel and broad. The focus on a non-essential histone in a model halophile provides a clear entry point for functional divergence. The claim that halophilic histones exhibit a mosaic of TF-like and chromatin-like features is interesting and potentially transformative for the field.
- **Major Concerns** The absence of any quantitative data, figure descriptions, or methodological detail makes it impossible to assess the validity of the binding assays, the computational comparisons, or the phenotyping results. The claim of conservation across halophiles is stated but not supported by any comparative dataset in the text. The distinction between TF-like and histone-like binding is asserted but not rigorously defined or tested.
- **Minor Comments** The importance statement is well written and accessible. The text would benefit from explicit definitions of terms such as "sparse binding" and "dinucleotide periodicity" in the context of the methods used. The phrase "mosaic of functions" is evocative but requires operational criteria to be meaningful.

- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** Originality is high, as the work proposes a novel functional role for archaeal histones. Scientific importance is potentially substantial for evolutionary biology and chromatin research. Interdisciplinary readership is plausible, spanning microbiology, genomics, and molecular evolution. Technical soundness cannot be evaluated from the provided text, which is a critical deficiency. Readability for nonspecialists is good in the abstract and importance section, but the main text lacks sufficient context for non-experts to follow the evidence.

### Major Concerns

- **Concern ID** R1-M1
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** Yes
- **Axis** Technical soundness
- **Claim pointer** The authors claim that halophilic histones bind the genome sparsely, similar to TFs, based on protein-DNA binding assays and computational analysis
- **Evidence pointer** Methods and results sections, location not provided
- **Concern** The text states that binding assays and computational analysis were used, but no details are given on the assay type, data processing, thresholds for binding detection, or statistical comparisons. Without these, the claim of "sparse" binding cannot be evaluated or reproduced.
- **Why it matters** The central distinction between halophilic histones and canonical histones rests on the quantitative interpretation of binding patterns. If the assay conditions or analysis thresholds are not described, the claim is unverifiable.
- **Resolution test** Provide a detailed methods section including assay protocols, binding definitions, normalization procedures, and statistical tests, along with representative figures showing binding distributions.

- **Concern ID** R1-M2
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** Yes
- **Axis** Technical soundness
- **Claim pointer** The authors claim that halophilic histone occupancy is not depleted at gene start sites and that halophilic genomes lack dinucleotide periodicity
- **Evidence pointer** Results section, location not provided
- **Concern** These claims require genome-wide occupancy data and sequence analysis, but no data, genome versions, or analysis pipelines are described. The absence of any quantitative support makes these assertions unsupported.
- **Why it matters** These features are used to differentiate halophilic histones from eukaryotic histones, and are central to the conclusion that halophilic histones do not form chromatin. Without evidence, the conclusion is not established.
- **Resolution test** Include occupancy profiles around gene start sites, periodicity analyses with appropriate controls, and statistical comparisons to known histone-binding patterns.

- **Concern ID** R1-M3
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** Yes
- **Axis** Support for claims
- **Claim pointer** The authors claim that unique sequence preferences for histone binding are detected in halophiles
- **Evidence pointer** Results section, location not provided
- **Concern** No sequence motif analysis, enrichment scores, or comparison to other DNA-binding proteins is described. The claim of uniqueness cannot be assessed without a defined sequence preference and a comparative baseline.
- **Why it matters** The identification of unique binding preferences is a key novel finding, but without data it remains an assertion rather than a demonstrated result.
- **Resolution test** Provide motif discovery results, enrichment statistics, and comparisons to known TF and histone binding motifs.

- **Concern ID** R1-M4
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** Yes
- **Axis** Support for claims
- **Claim pointer** The authors claim that the non-essentiality and genome-wide binding features are conserved across halophiles
- **Evidence pointer** Results and discussion sections, location not provided
- **Concern** The text mentions "halophiles" broadly, but no comparative data from multiple halophilic species is presented. The claim of conservation is not supported by any cross-species analysis in the provided text.
- **Why it matters** The generalizability of the findings is a major part of the paper's significance. Without comparative data, the claim is speculative.
- **Resolution test** Include data from at least two additional halophilic species, with binding assays and genomic analyses, to demonstrate conservation.
