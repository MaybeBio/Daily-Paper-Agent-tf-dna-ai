## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text, including abstract, introduction, methods, results, discussion, and supplemental data list
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific validity, methodological soundness, reproducibility, and significance of the claims as presented in the provided manuscript
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors present a machine learning framework, FamilyCode, that predicts the effect of missense mutations in transcription factor (TF) DNA binding domains on DNA binding specificity, using a reference-free tetrahedral representation of base preference and family-level training data from wild-type TFs. The method is validated on bHLH and homeodomain families using HT-SELEX and PBM data, with experimental confirmation for selected bHLH mutants.
- **Visible evidence base** Abstract, full introduction, full methods section, full results section, full discussion, supplemental data list, author contributions, data availability statement
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Figures and figure legends, supplemental data files (S1–S12), detailed model configuration files, raw sequencing data, and code repository contents were not provided for direct inspection

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript presents a conceptually interesting and potentially useful framework for predicting the quantitative effects of TF mutations on DNA binding specificity. The tetrahedral representation of base preference is a novel and elegant idea that addresses known issues with raw ΔΔG values. The validation on two TF families with multiple data types is commendable. However, several methodological details are insufficiently described, the statistical framework for evaluating prediction accuracy is not fully justified, and the comparison with existing methods is incomplete. The claim that the approach is "biophysically interpretable" requires more explicit demonstration. The manuscript would benefit from clearer articulation of the novelty relative to prior family-level analyses and a more rigorous treatment of prediction uncertainty.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in transcriptional regulation, computational biology, and protein-DNA interactions would find this work relevant. Clinically oriented groups studying the functional impact of TF mutations in developmental disorders or cancer would also be interested, as the method offers a way to prioritize variants for experimental testing. The methodological framework may appeal to machine learning practitioners working on sequence-function relationships.
- **Major strengths** 1) The tetrahedral representation is a thoughtful solution to the problem of comparing base preferences across TFs with different consensus motifs. 2) The use of family-level data from wild-type TFs to predict mutant effects is a practical and scalable strategy. 3) The inclusion of two TF families and two experimental platforms strengthens the generalizability claims. 4) The experimental validation with SELEX-seq and EMSA for bHLH mutants is a valuable addition. 5) The comparison with rCLAMPS and DeepPBS provides useful context for the method's performance.
- **Major Concerns** 
  - **R1-M1** (Severity: Major, Blocking: Yes) The statistical framework for evaluating prediction accuracy is not adequately described. The authors report R² and RMSD values but do not specify the exact definition of R² used (e.g., Pearson correlation squared, fraction of variance explained), the number of data points, or the confidence intervals. The comparison between FamilyCode and rCLAMPS using a paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test on 92 R² values is mentioned, but the distribution of these R² values and the effect size are not reported. Without this information, the reader cannot assess whether the reported performance differences are meaningful.
  - **R1-M2** (Severity: Major, Blocking: Yes) The methods section lacks critical details about the FamilyCode model itself. The iterative feature selection procedure is described only briefly, and the exact form of the regression model (e.g., linear, regularized, interaction terms) is not specified. The handling of amino acids not present in the training data at a given position is described, but the justification for the BLOSUM62-based weighting scheme is not provided. The manuscript would benefit from a formal mathematical description of the model and its training procedure.
  - **R1-M3** (Severity: Major, Blocking: No) The claim that the method is "biophysically interpretable" is not fully substantiated. While the tetrahedral representation is geometrically interpretable, the manuscript does not demonstrate that the learned coefficients correspond to specific biophysical mechanisms (e.g., hydrogen bonding, steric effects). The authors mention structural rationalization for some findings, but this is presented as post hoc interpretation rather than a predictive feature of the model.
  - **R1-M4** (Severity: Major, Blocking: No) The comparison with existing methods is incomplete. The authors compare with rCLAMPS and DeepPBS, but do not compare with simpler baselines such as position weight matrix averaging or phylogenetic-based approaches. The claim that FamilyCode outperforms closest-paralog and similarity regression is based on a single cross-validation setup, and the statistical significance of these differences is not fully reported.
  - **R1-M5** (Severity: Major, Blocking: No) The handling of heterodimeric bHLH factors is not addressed. Many bHLH TFs function as heterodimers, and the manuscript does not clarify whether the models are for homodimers only or how heterodimeric partners are accounted for. This is a significant limitation given the biological importance of heterodimerization in this family.
- **Minor Comments** 
  - **R1-m1** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Clarity, Affected Element: Methods section, Evidence Pointer: "Collecting HT-SELEX data for training bHLH binding models") The description of data collection states that 121 datasets covering 62 distinct bHLH proteins were obtained, but the final training set contains 52 proteins. The filtering criteria are described, but the number of proteins removed at each step is not reported. A flow diagram or table showing the data attrition would improve transparency.
  - **R1-m2** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Reproducibility, Affected Element: Methods section, Evidence Pointer: "Constructing binding free energy models using ProBound") The JSON configuration file is referenced as Supplemental Data S5, but the key parameters (e.g., model order, regularization, convergence criteria) are not summarized in the main text. A brief description of the model settings would help readers understand the modeling choices.
  - **R1-m3** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Statistical rigor, Affected Element: Results section, Evidence Pointer: "A collection of SELEX-derived DNA binding specificity models for wild-type bHLH factors") The statement that 96 out of 147 experiments yielded models matching the E-box consensus is presented without a statistical threshold. The authors should specify how "matching" was defined and whether this was a post hoc criterion.
  - **R1-m4** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Clarity, Affected Element: Results section, Evidence Pointer: "Mapping the DNA binding specificity determinants for homeodomains") The description of the homeodomain analysis is brief, and the number of TFs, data sources, and quality filters are not described in the same detail as for bHLH. A more balanced presentation would help the reader evaluate the generalizability claims.
  - **R1-m5** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Interpretation, Affected Element: Discussion section, Evidence Pointer: "An important determinant of the quality of our predictions") The authors state that prediction accuracy depends on the number of TFs in the training set, but they do not provide a quantitative analysis of this relationship. A figure showing prediction accuracy as a function of training set size would strengthen this claim.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** R1-M1 (statistical reporting), R1-M2 (model specification), R1-M3 (biophysical interpretability), R1-M5 (heterodimer handling)
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** 
  - Originality: The tetrahedral representation and the family-level prediction strategy are novel contributions. The idea of predicting mutant effects from wild-type family data is not entirely new, but the specific implementation is original.
  - Scientific importance: The ability to predict the quantitative effects of TF mutations on DNA binding specificity has clear relevance for understanding disease mechanisms and for variant interpretation. The work addresses a real need.
  - Interdisciplinary readership: The work bridges computational biology, molecular biology, and genomics. The methods are likely to be of interest to a broad audience, but the presentation is heavily computational and may be challenging for experimental readers.
  - Technical soundness: The core idea is sound, but the manuscript lacks sufficient methodological detail and statistical rigor to fully assess the validity of the claims. The experimental validation is limited to a small number of mutants.
  - Readability for nonspecialists: The abstract is clear, but the methods and results sections assume substantial familiarity with machine learning and biophysical modeling. The manuscript would benefit from a more accessible explanation of the tetrahedral representation and its advantages.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The core concept is promising and the preliminary results are encouraging, but the manuscript requires substantial revisions to address the statistical reporting, model specification, and methodological clarity issues before the claims can be fully evaluated.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that FamilyCode "accurately predicts" mutation effects is not fully supported by the reported statistics, as the R² and RMSD values are presented without confidence intervals or a clear definition of the evaluation metric.
- The statement that the approach is "biophysically interpretable" is not demonstrated beyond geometric interpretability of the tetrahedral representation.
- The claim that the method outperforms closest-paralog and similarity regression is based on a single cross-validation setup without reported statistical significance for all comparisons.
- The generalizability of the approach to other TF families is asserted but not demonstrated beyond the two families studied.
- The statement that "large-scale ChIP-seq data sets from projects such as ENCODE might also be leveraged" is speculative and not supported by any analysis in this manuscript.
