## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text (abstract, methods, results, discussion) as provided
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific validity, methodological soundness, claims versus evidence, and suitability for a generalist high-impact journal
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors present GenomicLinks, a deep learning model combining CNNs and an LSTM, trained on maize Hi-ChIP data, that predicts 3D chromatin interactions from DNA sequence. They report high classification performance, identify bHLH and AP2/EREBP transcription factor binding motifs as predictive of loop stability via in silico mutagenesis, and validate predicted interaction losses using single-cell co-accessibility data from diverse maize genotypes.
- **Visible evidence base** Abstract, Materials and Methods (Hi-ChIP data collection, data preprocessing, model architecture, in silico mutagenesis, validation with genetic variation and single-cell data), Results (model performance, TF motif analysis, natural variant effects, single-cell validation), Discussion, Acknowledgements, Footnotes
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Figures 1-5 referenced but not provided; Supplementary Information (SI materials, SI Table 1) referenced but not provided; GitHub repository mentioned but not assessed; detailed model architecture summary and training logs referenced but not included; exact statistical test details for some comparisons not fully specified

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** The manuscript addresses an important and timely question: whether deep learning can identify sequence features predictive of 3D chromatin interactions in a plant genome. The authors have assembled a substantial dataset and developed a model with reported performance metrics that are competitive with animal systems. The in silico mutagenesis approach and the attempt to validate predictions with natural genetic variation and single-cell data are commendable and represent a rigorous framework. However, several technical aspects require clarification and additional analysis before the central claims can be considered fully established. The reliance on a single Hi-ChIP dataset, the potential for confounding in the negative set construction, the lack of independent replication, and the incomplete reporting of validation statistics are notable concerns. The manuscript is well-written and accessible, but the evidence as presented does not yet fully support the strength of the conclusions drawn.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Plant molecular biologists and geneticists studying 3D genome organization, gene regulation, and chromatin architecture. Researchers working on cis-regulatory element function and their role in agronomic traits. Computational biologists and machine learning practitioners interested in applying deep learning to genomics, particularly those working on sequence-to-function prediction. The open-source web tool may also be of interest to the broader maize research community for prioritizing variants in non-coding regions.
- **Major strengths** The study addresses a significant gap in plant genomics, where deep learning models for 3D chromatin prediction are lacking. The model architecture is thoughtfully designed, integrating CNNs and LSTM in a manner consistent with successful animal models. The use of in silico mutagenesis to identify specific TF motif classes is a powerful and appropriate application of the model. The attempt to validate predictions using both natural genetic variants and independent single-cell co-accessibility data is a strong feature that adds biological credibility. The manuscript is clearly written and the methods are described in sufficient detail for replication.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **Concern ID** R1-M1
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Data sufficiency and generalizability
  - **Claim pointer** The model is trained on a single Hi-ChIP dataset from one study (Ricci et al., 2019), yet the authors claim GenomicLinks can predict 3D chromatin interactions in maize with high accuracy and imply general utility.
  - **Evidence pointer** Materials and Methods, "Hi-ChIP data collection"; Results, "GenomicLinks displays favorable classification performance"
  - **Concern** The model is trained and tested on data derived from a single Hi-ChIP experiment. This raises concerns about overfitting to dataset-specific biases (e.g., antibody efficiency, library preparation, sequencing depth) rather than learning general principles of chromatin loop formation. The reported performance metrics (Accuracy 0.848, AUROC 0.925) may not reflect performance on independent datasets generated by different protocols or in different cell types/tissues. The authors do not provide any external validation on an independent Hi-C or Hi-ChIP dataset from maize.
  - **Why it matters** Without independent validation, the generalizability of the model is unknown. A model that performs well only on the training data distribution has limited utility for the broader research community, which is a stated goal of the work. The claim of "high accuracy" is only meaningful if it extends beyond the specific dataset used.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should validate GenomicLinks on an independent, publicly available maize Hi-C or Hi-ChIP dataset (e.g., from a different tissue or genotype). If such data are not available, they should clearly state this limitation and temper the generalizability claims. Alternatively, cross-validation within the dataset should be supplemented with a hold-out chromosome approach to assess robustness.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M2
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Negative set construction and confounding
  - **Claim pointer** The negative training set was constructed by permuting anchor positions from positive interactions and shifting them 5-25 bp, with the rationale that this forces the model to learn sequence features determining interaction potential rather than 3D architecture.
  - **Evidence pointer** Materials and Methods, "Data preprocessing"
  - **Concern** The negative set construction is a critical methodological choice. Permuting anchors from positive interactions and shifting them by only 5-25 bp creates negative examples that are highly similar in sequence composition and genomic location to positives. This could lead to the model learning subtle sequence differences that distinguish these near-identical sequences, rather than features that distinguish interacting from non-interacting regions genome-wide. The authors argue this avoids learning euchromatin vs. heterochromatin differences, but the approach may introduce a different bias: the model may learn to detect the specific sequence context of the shifted region rather than general loop-forming potential. The lack of comparison with alternative negative set strategies (e.g., random genomic regions matched for distance and GC content) weakens the claim that the chosen approach is optimal.
  - **Why it matters** The validity of all downstream analyses (TF motif identification, variant effect prediction) depends on the model having learned meaningful sequence features. If the negative set is not representative of true non-interacting regions, the model's predictions and the biological conclusions drawn from them may be artifacts of the training data construction.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should provide a comparison of model performance using alternative negative set strategies (e.g., random non-interacting regions, distance-matched regions). They should also demonstrate that the model's predictions on held-out chromosomes are robust and that the identified TF motifs are not simply reflecting sequence composition differences between the positive and negative sets.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M3
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Validation statistics and interpretation
  - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that single-cell co-accessibility data validate predicted interaction losses, citing a pooled genotype test with χ² = 7.89, df = 1, P = 5.0e03, and a Fisher's Exact Test with P = 1.05e-02.
  - **Evidence pointer** Results, "Single-cell co-accessibility validates predicted interaction losses"
  - **Concern** The reported p-value for the pooled genotype test (P = 5.0e03) appears to be a typographical error, as this value is not a valid probability (p-values range from 0 to 1). This is likely P = 5.0e-03. More importantly, the statistical approach is not fully described. It is unclear how the pooled test was conducted, whether genotypes were treated as fixed or random effects, how the co-accessibility values were normalized, and whether multiple testing corrections were applied across the many RAPs tested. The claim that B97 is an exception is noted but not explored. The effect sizes are not reported, making it difficult to assess the biological significance of the observed decreases.
  - **Why it matters** The validation with single-cell data is a key piece of evidence supporting the biological relevance of the model predictions. If the statistical analysis is flawed or incompletely reported, the strength of this validation is diminished. The apparent typographical error in the p-value also raises concerns about the overall care in reporting.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should provide a complete description of the statistical methods used, including the exact test, the model, the handling of multiple comparisons, and the effect sizes. The p-value typo should be corrected. They should also discuss the B97 exception and whether it reflects biological variability or a technical artifact.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M4
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Interpretability and causal inference
  - **Claim pointer** The authors state that removal of TF motifs from loop anchors leads to reduced interaction probabilities, and that natural variants in these motifs lead to predicted losses in 3D chromatin contacts.
  - **Evidence pointer** Results, "In silico predictions of TF mediated 3D chromatin contacts" and "Natural mutations in TFBS lead to predicted losses in 3D chromatin contacts"
  - **Concern** The in silico mutagenesis approach demonstrates correlation between motif presence and model predictions, but it does not establish causation. The model may be using the TF motifs as proxies for other sequence features (e.g., GC content, nucleosome occupancy, or other regulatory elements) that are the true drivers of the predictions. The authors do not perform any additional analyses to disentangle these possibilities, such as testing whether random sequence substitutions of similar size have similar effects, or whether the identified motifs are enriched in experimentally validated loop anchors. The claim that "removal of these motifs leads to reduced interaction probabilities" is a statement about the model's behavior, not necessarily about biology.
  - **Why it matters** The central biological claim of the paper is that specific TF motifs mediate chromatin loop formation. If the model's predictions are driven by correlated but non-causal features, the biological conclusions would be misleading. The authors need to provide additional evidence that the identified motifs are functionally relevant.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should perform control in silico mutagenesis experiments with random sequence substitutions matched for size and composition. They should also compare the identified motifs with known functional elements in maize (e.g., from ENCODE or other functional genomics data). Ideally, they would validate a subset of predictions using an independent experimental approach, such as CRISPR-based perturbation of specific TF binding sites.
- **Minor Comments**
  - **Concern ID** R1-m1
  - **Severity** Minor
  - **Axis** Clarity and completeness
  - **Affected element** Model architecture description
  - **Evidence pointer** Materials and Methods, "Model architecture and training process"
  - **Issue** The description of the model architecture is incomplete. The authors mention "dual CNN architecture" and "one bidirectional LSTM layer" but do not specify the number of layers in each CNN, the activation functions, the pooling strategies, the dropout rates, or the total number of trainable parameters. The reference to "jlab github" is not sufficient for a self-contained methods section.
  - **Required correction** Provide a complete architectural description, including a table or figure summarizing layer types, sizes, and hyperparameters. Include the total number of parameters and the computational resources required for training.
  - **Concern ID** R1-m2
  - **Severity** Minor
  - **Axis** Statistical reporting
  - **Affected element** TF class frequency analysis
  - **Evidence pointer** Results, "In silico predictions of TF mediated 3D chromatin contacts"
  - **Issue** The authors report a chi-square test (χ² = 87.21, df = 6, P = 1.85e-23) for the distribution of TF classes in RAPs versus all loops. However, it is unclear which TF classes were included in the test, how the expected frequencies were calculated, and whether the test was performed on the number of motifs or the number of anchor pairs. The degrees of freedom (df = 6) suggest 7 categories, but the text only mentions AP2/EREBP and bHLH.
  - **Required correction** Specify the exact categories included in the chi-square test, the method for calculating expected frequencies, and whether the analysis was performed at the motif level or the anchor pair level.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that GenomicLinks can be used to "test the impact of specific genomic variants on chromatin looping" is not fully supported, as the model's predictions for variant effects are only validated in a limited manner (single-cell co-accessibility for a subset of RAPs) and the causal relationship between TF motif disruption and loop loss is not experimentally demonstrated.
- The claim that "removal of these motifs from loop anchors leads to reduced interaction probabilities" is a statement about model behavior, not a demonstrated biological fact. The authors do not provide experimental evidence that these motifs are functionally required for loop formation.
- The statement that "GenomicLinks learned very different DNA sequence features predictive of chromatin looping" compared to animal models is based on a single comparison with DeepMILO and is not systematically demonstrated.
- The generalizability of the model to other maize tissues, developmental stages, or genotypes beyond the training data is not established.
- The statistical validation using single-cell co-accessibility data is incompletely reported, and the apparent p-value typo (P = 5.0e03) undermines confidence in the reported results.
- The claim that the model can identify "putative molecular mechanisms" underlying loop formation is speculative, as the model is purely correlational and the interpretability analyses are limited.
