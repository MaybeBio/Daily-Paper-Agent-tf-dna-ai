## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text including abstract, introduction, results, discussion, materials and methods, and supplementary information sections as provided.
- **Assessment boundary** Technical and scientific evaluation of the DARSI framework, its predictive performance claims, binding site identification methodology, and validation against existing databases. No assessment of formatting, editorial fit, or publication policy matters.
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors present DARSI, a convolutional neural network that predicts discretized gene expression levels from raw regulatory DNA sequences using MPRA data from 95 E. coli operons. They report an average predictive accuracy of 79.8% and claim the model identifies transcription factor binding sites at single-base pair resolution via saliency mapping, recovering 88.4% of previously annotated sites and predicting 73 novel sites.
- **Visible evidence base** Abstract, full introduction, results sections for predictive accuracy and saliency mapping, discussion, materials and methods for dataset description, RNA-seq processing, one-hot encoding, RNA count labeling, saliency map generation, and binding site identification. Supplementary information sections on architecture optimization, backpropagation example, and comparison with mutual information approaches. References to Figures 1-5, Tables S1-S2, and Figures S1-S4.
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Actual figures and tables are not provided. The GitHub repository contents are referenced but not accessible for verification. The supplementary table listing all binding site predictions is not included. The mutual information comparison plots for all 95 operons are not shown. The specific architecture details in Table S2 are not visible. The algorithm pseudocode for binning and saliency map generation is referenced but not shown.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** The manuscript presents a potentially useful application of deep convolutional neural networks to MPRA data for both gene expression prediction and binding site identification. The core idea of using saliency maps to extract regulatory features from a trained predictive model is not entirely novel but is applied here in a systematic manner to a substantial bacterial dataset. The reported predictive accuracy of approximately 80% across 95 operons is encouraging. However, the manuscript has several significant weaknesses that prevent the case from being fully established. The validation of predicted novel binding sites is entirely absent, with no experimental or orthogonal computational confirmation. The comparison to mutual information approaches is presented only qualitatively for a few examples. The treatment of the independence assumption, which motivates the work, is not rigorously demonstrated to be overcome by the DARSI approach. The statistical treatment of accuracy and F1 scores lacks measures of uncertainty across the 95 models beyond standard deviation. The manuscript would benefit from a more rigorous demonstration that the saliency peaks correspond to functional binding sites rather than artifacts of the model or the discretization procedure.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in gene regulation, computational biology, and genomics would be interested in this work. Specifically, those working on MPRA data analysis, transcription factor binding site prediction, and the application of deep learning to regulatory genomics would find the DARSI framework potentially useful. The claim of identifying novel binding sites that are missed by traditional methods could be of interest to experimentalists seeking candidate regulatory elements for validation. The broader claim of applicability to eukaryotic systems, though not demonstrated here, may attract interest from those studying more complex regulatory landscapes.
- **Major strengths** The manuscript addresses a real and important problem, namely the incomplete annotation of transcription factor binding sites in sequenced genomes. The use of a convolutional neural network that can capture non-local interactions between nucleotides is a reasonable approach that goes beyond simple independence assumptions. The systematic application to 95 operons with consistent methodology is a strength. The authors provide a clear description of the data processing pipeline and make code and data available through a GitHub repository. The use of saliency maps to generate testable predictions is a practical and useful output. The benchmarking against curated databases for known binding sites provides some level of validation for the approach.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **Concern ID** R1-M1
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Validation of novel predictions
  - **Claim pointer** The manuscript claims DARSI predicted 73 novel binding sites across the operons, implying these are biologically meaningful and not false positives.
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section "Uncovering Binding Sites Using Saliency Maps", Figure 5A, supplementary table referenced but not provided
  - **Concern** The 73 novel predicted binding sites are presented as a key output of the work, but there is no validation of these predictions whatsoever. No experimental confirmation, no cross-referencing with other prediction tools, no analysis of sequence conservation, no assessment of whether these sites fall in regions with any prior evidence of regulatory function. The manuscript does not even provide a qualitative assessment of a subset of these predictions. Without any form of validation, these novel predictions remain unsubstantiated claims. The authors acknowledge that future experiments are needed, but the current manuscript does not provide any evidence that these predictions are more likely to be true than random genomic regions.
  - **Why it matters** The identification of novel binding sites is a central claim of the manuscript and a primary motivation for the work. If these predictions cannot be shown to have any validity, the practical utility of DARSI for genomic annotation is not established. The claim of discovering new regulatory elements is a strong one that requires supporting evidence beyond the model's internal logic.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should provide some form of validation for at least a subset of the novel predictions. This could include comparison with independent computational predictions, analysis of sequence conservation across related species, enrichment of known transcription factor binding motifs within the predicted regions, or experimental validation of a few sites. Alternatively, the claims should be substantially softened to present these as hypotheses requiring testing rather than as identified binding sites.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M2
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Comparison to existing methods
  - **Claim pointer** The manuscript implies DARSI provides advantages over traditional mutual information approaches for identifying binding sites from MPRA data, particularly in capturing non-independent contributions of nucleotides.
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section "Uncovering Binding Sites Using Saliency Maps", Discussion section, Supplementary Figure S4
  - **Concern** The comparison between DARSI and mutual information approaches is presented only for a few illustrative examples in the supplementary material. The manuscript does not provide a systematic, quantitative comparison across all 95 operons. It is unclear how often DARSI identifies sites that mutual information misses, how often mutual information identifies sites that DARSI misses, and whether the overall recovery rates of known sites differ significantly between the two methods. The claim that DARSI can account for correlations between distant bases is plausible given the architecture, but the manuscript does not demonstrate that this capability leads to improved binding site identification in practice.
  - **Why it matters** The motivation for developing DARSI is to overcome limitations of existing methods that assume independence between base pairs. To establish that DARSI is a meaningful advance, the authors need to show that it performs at least as well as existing methods on known sites and that it can identify additional sites that are missed by these methods. Without a systematic comparison, the added value of DARSI over simpler approaches is not demonstrated.
  - **Resolution test** Provide a quantitative comparison of DARSI and mutual information across all 95 operons, including metrics such as the number of known sites recovered by each method, the number of novel sites predicted by each method, and the overlap between the two sets of predictions. This analysis should be presented in the main text or as a comprehensive supplementary figure.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M3
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Statistical rigor of performance metrics
  - **Claim pointer** The manuscript reports an average predictive accuracy of 79.8% across 95 operons and F1 scores of 0.76, 0.77, and 0.80 for the zero, low, and high expression bins respectively.
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section "DARSI Can Predict Gene Expression from Raw Sequence", Figure 3A and 3C
  - **Concern** The performance metrics are reported as averages with standard deviations, but the manuscript does not provide information on the distribution of accuracies across the 95 operons. It is unclear whether all operons perform similarly or whether some operons have much lower accuracy that is masked by the average. The F1 scores are reported with standard deviations of approximately 0.1, which suggests considerable variability across operons. The manuscript does not discuss which types of operons or regulatory architectures lead to better or worse performance. Additionally, the choice of three discrete expression bins is a simplification that may not capture the full range of expression levels, and the manuscript does not discuss how this discretization affects the interpretation of the results.
  - **Why it matters** Understanding the variability in model performance across different operons is important for assessing the generalizability of the approach. If the model performs poorly on certain types of regulatory architectures, this limits its utility. The discretization of expression levels into three bins is a significant simplification that may affect both the predictive accuracy and the ability to identify binding sites, and this should be discussed more thoroughly.
  - **Resolution test** Provide a more detailed analysis of model performance, including the distribution of accuracies across operons, identification of any operons with notably poor performance, and a discussion of potential reasons for variability. Discuss the implications of the three-bin discretization and consider whether alternative discretization schemes would affect the conclusions.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M4
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Interpretation of saliency maps
  - **Claim pointer** The manuscript interprets saliency peaks as indicating transcription factor binding sites, with positive values suggesting activator binding and negative values suggesting repressor binding.
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section "Uncovering Binding Sites Using Saliency Maps", Materials and Methods section "Binding Site Identification"
  - **Concern** The interpretation of saliency values as directly indicating activator or repressor binding sites is not well justified. The sign of the saliency value reflects the direction of the gradient of the loss function with respect to the input, which does not necessarily correspond to a biological mechanism of activation or repression. A mutation that increases expression could be due to disruption of a repressor binding site, but it could also be due to other effects such as changes in RNA stability or cryptic promoter activity. The manuscript does not address these alternative interpretations. Additionally, the thresholding procedure for identifying binding sites (one standard deviation above the mean, minimum length of 10 bp) is presented without justification for these specific choices.
  - **Why it matters** The biological interpretation of the saliency maps is central to the claim that DARSI can identify binding sites. If the sign of the saliency values does not reliably indicate activation versus repression, the biological conclusions drawn from the analysis are not supported. The choice of thresholds for defining binding sites can significantly affect the results, and these choices should be justified.
  - **Resolution test** Provide a more careful discussion of the interpretation of saliency values, including potential alternative explanations for the observed patterns. Justify the choice of thresholds for defining binding sites, possibly by showing how the results change with different threshold values. Consider validating the sign interpretation on a subset of known activator and repressor binding sites.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M5
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Generalizability of the approach
  - **Claim pointer** The manuscript suggests that DARSI is broadly applicable to regulatory sequences across bacterial and eukaryotic systems.
  - **Evidence pointer** Discussion section
  - **Concern** The DARSI framework is demonstrated only on E. coli data from a single growth condition (LB medium). The authors claim broad applicability to eukaryotic systems, but this is not supported by any data. Eukaryotic regulatory regions are typically much larger and more complex than the 160 bp bacterial regions used here, with chromatin structure, enhancer-promoter interactions, and other layers of regulation that are not captured by the current model. The claim of broad applicability is speculative and not justified by the results presented.
  - **Why it matters** The potential impact of the work depends on its generalizability. If the approach is limited to bacterial systems with relatively simple regulatory architectures, its utility is more restricted. The authors should either provide evidence for generalizability or temper their claims about applicability to other systems.
  - **Resolution test** Either provide preliminary results from a different organism or system to demonstrate generalizability, or revise the discussion to present the broader applicability as a future direction rather than a current capability.

- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** The lack of any validation for the 73 novel binding site predictions is a critical gap. The comparison to mutual information methods is insufficient to demonstrate superiority or complementarity. The statistical treatment of performance metrics is incomplete. The interpretation of saliency map signs as indicating activation versus repression requires stronger justification. The claim of broad applicability to eukaryotic systems is not supported by evidence.
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** Originality: The application of convolutional neural networks to MPRA data for binding site identification is not entirely novel, as similar approaches have been used in other contexts. However, the specific combination of prediction and saliency-based interpretation applied to a large bacterial dataset has some original elements. Scientific importance: The problem of incomplete regulatory annotation is important, and tools that can improve binding site identification have potential value. However, the lack of validation for novel predictions limits the current importance of the findings. Interdisciplinary readership: The work is primarily of interest to computational biologists and genomics researchers. The methods and results are presented in a way that is accessible to this audience, though some details of the deep learning methodology may be challenging for non-specialists. Technical soundness: The technical approach is generally sound, but the validation of the binding site predictions is inadequate, and the comparison to existing methods is incomplete. Readability for nonspecialists: The manuscript is generally well-written and the concepts are explained clearly, though some sections on the deep learning methodology may be difficult for readers without a computational background.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The core approach has potential, but the manuscript requires substantial additional work to establish the validity of the novel binding site predictions and to demonstrate the advantages of DARSI over existing methods. The authors should provide validation for at least a subset of the novel predictions, conduct a systematic comparison with mutual information approaches, and address the other concerns raised above.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that DARSI identified 73 novel binding sites is unsupported without any form of validation.
- The claim that DARSI accounts for correlations between distant bases in a way that improves binding site identification is not demonstrated by the data presented.
- The claim of broad applicability to eukaryotic systems is unsupported by any evidence.
- The interpretation of saliency map signs as indicating activator versus repressor binding sites is not adequately justified.
- The statement that DARSI achieves an average accuracy of approximately 80% is supported by the reported data, but the variability across operons and the implications of the three-bin discretization are not fully addressed.
