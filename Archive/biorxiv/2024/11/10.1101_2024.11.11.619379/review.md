## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text (abstract, introduction, results, discussion, methods, and supplementary figure legends as provided)
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific content, methodological soundness, claims versus evidence, and resource utility; not editorial or formatting issues
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors present a large-scale cross-platform benchmarking of ten DNA motif discovery tools applied to Codebook data (4,237 experiments, five platforms, 394 human TFs), with human curation yielding an approved set (1,460 datasets, 236 TFs). They report that no single tool is universally superior, that Dimont dominates top-ranked motifs, that basic motif features (length, IC, GC) are poor predictors of performance, and that a random forest of PWMs (Archipelago) improves genomic binding site prediction over single PWMs. They provide the Codebook Motif Explorer (MEX) as an interactive resource.
- **Visible evidence base** Abstract, full introduction, full results, full discussion, full methods, and supplementary figure legends (SF1-SF11) as provided; no main-text figures or tables were included in the supplied material
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Main-text figures (Figures 1-5) and tables (ST1-ST4) are referenced but not provided; no supplementary tables were included; no code or data availability URLs were verified; no statistical details or exact p-values are given in the text

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript addresses an important gap in the field: systematic, cross-platform benchmarking of motif discovery tools on a large, diverse dataset of understudied human TFs. The scale of the effort (4,237 experiments, 394 TFs, ten tools) is impressive and the resulting resource (MEX) is likely to be valuable. However, the manuscript as presented has several major weaknesses. The central claims about tool performance and the utility of the Archipelago model are not fully supported by the evidence provided, primarily because the main figures and tables are missing from the supplied material. The benchmarking methodology, while detailed, has potential circularity issues (e.g., using the same tools for discovery and evaluation) and the human curation step, while reasonable, introduces subjectivity that is not fully quantified. The finding that Dimont dominates is interesting but the explanation (discriminative objective) is speculative. The Archipelago results, while promising, are presented with limited statistical rigor and the cross-platform asymmetry is not fully explained. The manuscript is well-written and the methods are described in sufficient detail for replication, but the conclusions are stronger than the visible evidence warrants.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Computational biologists and bioinformaticians working on TF binding site prediction, motif discovery, and regulatory genomics. Developers of motif discovery tools will be interested in the comparative performance data. Experimental biologists studying transcriptional regulation, particularly of understudied TFs, will find the MEX resource useful for obtaining reliable motifs. The benchmarking framework and the Archipelago model may also interest researchers developing machine learning approaches for genomic sequence analysis.
- **Major strengths** 1) Large-scale, multi-platform, multi-tool benchmarking on a diverse set of TFs, including many understudied proteins, is a significant and timely contribution. 2) The human curation step, while subjective, adds a layer of quality control that is often missing in automated benchmarking studies. 3) The MEX resource provides a practical, interactive platform for the community. 4) The Archipelago model, combining multiple PWMs in a random forest, is a sensible and potentially powerful approach. 5) The methods are described in detail, facilitating replication and extension.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **Concern ID** R1-M1
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Evidence sufficiency
  - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that "Dimont excels across the board" and that "nearly half of the top-ranking motifs in the final collection were generated by a single tool, Dimont."
  - **Evidence pointer** Figure 1B, 1C, 1D; Supplementary Figures SF1C, SF2, SF3; location not provided for exact numbers
  - **Concern** The central claim of Dimont's dominance is based on figures that are not provided in the supplied material. Without seeing the actual distributions, effect sizes, and statistical comparisons, it is impossible to assess whether the dominance is robust or an artifact of the specific benchmarking setup (e.g., the choice of metrics, the weighting of different platforms, or the human curation process). The text mentions "nearly half" but does not provide confidence intervals or tests of significance.
  - **Why it matters** This is the primary finding of the paper. If the claim is not robustly supported, the paper's main conclusion is undermined.
  - **Resolution test** Provide the main figures with clear statistical annotations (e.g., confidence intervals, pairwise comparisons). Include a sensitivity analysis showing that the ranking is stable across different metric choices, platform weightings, and curation thresholds.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M2
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Methodological soundness
  - **Claim pointer** The authors state that "we applied ten different DNA motif discovery tools to generate PWMs from the 'Codebook' data" and that the benchmarking is "cross-platform."
  - **Evidence pointer** Methods, "Workflow overview" and "Motif discovery tools and data tool-specific data processing"; location not provided
  - **Concern** The benchmarking methodology has a potential circularity issue. The same tools used for motif discovery are also used for evaluation (via the benchmarking protocols). While this is common practice, it can bias results towards tools that produce motifs that score well on the specific evaluation metrics used. The authors do not discuss this limitation. Additionally, the "cross-platform" claim is weakened by the fact that some tools (e.g., gkmSVM) were only applied to a subset of data types, and the comparison is not always apples-to-apples (e.g., different tools may use different background models or motif representations).
  - **Why it matters** If the evaluation is circular, the ranking of tools may not reflect true performance on independent data, and the "cross-platform" conclusions may be overstated.
  - **Resolution test** Include a discussion of the circularity issue and its potential impact. Perform a validation on an independent dataset (e.g., ChIP-seq data from ENCODE not used in training) to confirm the ranking. Clarify which tools were applied to which data types and how the comparisons were made fair.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M3
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Statistical rigor
  - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that "the basic motif properties such as nucleotide composition and information content offered little help in detecting such pitfalls" and that "there was no correlation between motif length and performance" on artificial sequences.
  - **Evidence pointer** Results, "Basic motif features..."; Supplementary Figures SF6, SF7; location not provided
  - **Concern** The text reports correlation coefficients (e.g., Pearson ρ of 0.05 to 0.2) but does not provide p-values or confidence intervals. The claim that these features "offered little help" is qualitative and not backed by a formal statistical test. The analysis appears to be purely correlational, and the possibility of non-linear relationships or interactions between features is not explored.
  - **Why it matters** The conclusion that motif features are not useful for predicting performance is important for practical guidance. Without proper statistical support, this claim is not convincing.
  - **Resolution test** Provide p-values and confidence intervals for all reported correlations. Consider using a multivariate model to test for interactions. Report the variance explained by these features.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M4
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Interpretability and generalizability
  - **Claim pointer** The authors state that "by combining multiple PWMs in decision trees, we demonstrate how our setup can be readily adapted to train and test binding specificities more complex than PWMs" and that Archipelago "consistently outperformed individual PWMs."
  - **Evidence pointer** Results, "A random forest of PWMs..."; Figure 4; Supplementary Figures SF8, SF9; location not provided
  - **Concern** The Archipelago model is presented as a key advance, but the details are sparse. The text mentions a random forest with "top 20 MEX PWMs" as features, but does not specify the hyperparameters beyond a few values, the number of trees, or the feature importance. The claim of "consistent outperformance" is not quantified (e.g., average improvement, range across TFs). The cross-platform asymmetry (ChIP-to-GHT works, GHT-to-ChIP does not) is mentioned but not explained mechanistically.
  - **Why it matters** The Archipelago model is a significant part of the paper's contribution. Without sufficient detail and statistical support, its utility and generalizability are unclear.
  - **Resolution test** Provide full model details, including hyperparameter tuning, feature importance, and a breakdown of performance gains by TF family. Explain the cross-platform asymmetry with a concrete hypothesis and test it.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that "Dimont excels across the board" is not supported by the visible evidence (figures missing).
- The claim that "nearly half of the top-ranking motifs" were from Dimont is not quantified with confidence intervals or statistical tests.
- The claim that "basic motif properties... offered little help" is not supported by formal statistical testing (no p-values or effect sizes).
- The claim that Archipelago "consistently outperformed individual PWMs" is not quantified (no average improvement, range, or statistical significance).
- The claim that "most of the popular motif discovery tools detect valid motifs" is based on the approved set, which was curated using the same tools, creating a potential circularity.
- The generalizability of the findings to TFs not in the approved set (158 TFs were not approved) is unclear and not discussed.
- The statement that "the basic motif properties such as nucleotide composition and information content offered little help in detecting such pitfalls" is a negative claim that is difficult to prove and requires a power analysis to be convincing.
