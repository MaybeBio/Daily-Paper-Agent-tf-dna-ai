## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text, including abstract, introduction, results, discussion, methods, figure legends, and supplementary figure legends as provided.
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific content, experimental design, data interpretation, and internal consistency of the claims as presented in the provided text. No external data or unpublished materials were consulted.
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors propose that intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) within the transcriptional coactivator CBP regulate its function by controlling the properties of its nuclear condensates. They identify distinct roles for specific C-terminal IDRs, showing that IDR6 restricts and IDR7 promotes condensate formation. They further demonstrate that these IDRs modulate the response of CBP condensates to changes in global lysine acetylation, and that disruption of these IDRs alters CBP chromatin localization, histone acetylation patterns, and gene expression.
- **Visible evidence base** The provided text includes descriptions of imaging experiments (confocal, lattice light sheet, FRAP), biochemical assays (western blot, in vitro HAT assays), and high-throughput sequencing (ChIP-seq, CUT&RUN, RNA-seq). Figure legends are provided, but the figures themselves are not.
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** The actual figures, supplementary figures, and tables are not provided. This significantly limits the ability to assess the quality of the data, the rigor of the image analysis, and the statistical significance of the reported differences. The specific numerical values for many quantitative claims (e.g., fold changes, p-values, effect sizes) are not available in the text.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript addresses a timely and important question in gene regulation: how do the large intrinsically disordered regions of the essential coactivator CBP contribute to its function? The central hypothesis, that distinct IDRs with different sequence features act as opposing regulators of condensate behavior to fine-tune CBP activity, is conceptually interesting and builds logically on prior work. The authors present a substantial body of work, combining cell biology, biophysics, and genomics. However, the current version of the manuscript, as presented in this text-only form, has several major issues that prevent me from being able to support its publication. The most significant concerns are the lack of a clear mechanistic model that integrates the observations, the potential for overexpression artifacts to confound the interpretation of the key experiments, and the absence of key data (e.g., quantitative validation of the sequencing results, statistical details) needed to evaluate the robustness of the claims. The link between the observed condensate phenotypes and the downstream effects on chromatin and gene expression is correlative and requires a more direct test.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in the fields of transcriptional regulation, chromatin biology, and the emerging area of biomolecular condensates would find this work of interest. The study provides a framework for understanding how IDRs within a single, large coactivator can have opposing functions, which is relevant to the broader question of how multivalent proteins are regulated. The connection between IDR-driven condensate properties and the response to a key post-translational modification (acetylation) is a conceptually important advance that would appeal to a wide audience.
- **Major strengths**
    1.  The study tackles a significant and unresolved question about the functional role of IDRs in a key transcriptional coactivator.
    2.  The systematic approach of deleting individual IDRs and assessing their impact on condensate properties, chromatin localization, and gene expression is logical and comprehensive.
    3.  The identification of opposing functions for the adjacent IDR6 and IDR7 is a novel and interesting finding.
    4.  The work connects the biophysical properties of condensates to downstream functional outputs (histone acetylation, gene expression), which is a key goal in the field.
- **Major Concerns**
    - **Concern ID** R1-M1
    - **Severity** Major
    - **Blocking** Yes
    - **Axis** Experimental Design and Interpretation
    - **Claim pointer** The manuscript claims that the effects of IDR deletions on condensate properties, chromatin localization, and gene expression are due to the specific functions of these IDRs in regulating CBP's intrinsic behavior.
    - **Evidence pointer** Results sections "CBP-IDRs regulate the number of CBP condensates", "CBP-IDRs regulate the size and intensity of CBP condensates", "CBP IDRs regulate endogenous CBP condensates", "CBP IDRs regulate CBP-dependent acetylation", "CBP IDRs shape CBP binding to chromatin", "CBP IDRs shape histone acetylation", "CBP-IDRs affect gene expression".
    - **Concern** The vast majority of the functional experiments are performed using transient overexpression of CBP-GFP constructs. The authors acknowledge this in the limitations but do not adequately address the central problem: at high expression levels, the behavior of these constructs may be dominated by non-specific, concentration-dependent effects (e.g., ectopic phase separation) rather than the specific regulatory functions of the IDRs. The claim that the observed phenotypes are due to the "specific" functions of the IDRs is not well-supported without experiments at or near endogenous expression levels, or without a demonstration that the overexpressed proteins are properly regulated (e.g., by acetylation) in a manner that mirrors the endogenous protein. The experiments with the inducible cell lines are a step in the right direction, but the text does not clearly state if the expression levels in these lines are truly near-endogenous.
    - **Why it matters** If the observed effects are largely a consequence of overexpression, the conclusions about the specific regulatory roles of IDR6 and IDR7 would be significantly weakened. The study's central claim is that these IDRs are part of a regulatory mechanism, and this claim requires experiments that minimize the risk of overexpression artifacts.
    - **Resolution test** The authors should provide a direct comparison of the expression levels of the overexpressed CBP-GFP constructs to endogenous CBP. Ideally, key experiments (e.g., condensate number, H3K27ac levels) should be repeated in the inducible cell lines with expression titrated to match endogenous levels. Alternatively, they could use a CRISPR-based approach to tag the endogenous locus.
    - **Concern ID** R1-M2
    - **Severity** Major
    - **Blocking** Yes
    - **Axis** Mechanistic Insight
    - **Claim pointer** The manuscript claims that "CBP-IDRs cooperate to control different aspects of CBP behaviour in the nucleus" and that the balance between IDR6 and IDR7 is functionally important.
    - **Evidence pointer** Results sections "CBP-IDRs regulate the number of CBP condensates", "CBP-IDRs regulate the size and intensity of CBP condensates", "CBP IDRs regulate endogenous CBP condensates", "Regulation of condensates by acetylation of CBP AL relies on CBP-IDR integrity".
    - **Concern** The manuscript describes the phenotypes of IDR deletions but does not provide a clear mechanistic model for how these IDRs exert their effects. For example, is the inhibitory effect of IDR6 due to a specific intramolecular interaction, a change in net charge, or an effect on a binding partner? The text speculates on this but does not provide direct evidence. The claim that the effects are due to "cooperation" is inferred from the phenotypes of single and double deletions, but a more direct test of cooperation (e.g., a physical interaction or a functional rescue experiment) is missing. The model presented in the discussion is largely a summary of the observations rather than a testable mechanistic hypothesis.
    - **Why it matters** For a Nature-style paper, a descriptive account of phenotypes is not sufficient. The work needs to provide a deeper mechanistic understanding of how the sequence features of these IDRs translate into their regulatory functions. Without this, the study remains largely phenomenological.
    - **Resolution test** The authors should perform experiments to test a specific mechanistic hypothesis. For example, they could use NMR or crosslinking to test for intramolecular interactions between IDR6 and other domains. They could create chimeric constructs where the IDR6 sequence is replaced with a sequence of similar composition but different patterning to test the role of specific sequence features. They could also test whether the effect of IDR6 is dependent on a specific binding partner.
    - **Concern ID** R1-M3
    - **Severity** Major
    - **Blocking** Yes
    - **Axis** Data Presentation and Validation
    - **Claim pointer** The manuscript claims that "CBP-IDRs affect gene expression" and that these changes are linked to altered CBP chromatin localization and histone acetylation.
    - **Evidence pointer** Results sections "CBP IDRs shape CBP binding to chromatin", "CBP IDRs shape histone acetylation", "CBP-IDRs affect gene expression".
    - **Concern** The RNA-seq, ChIP-seq, and CUT&RUN experiments are described, but the text lacks critical details and validation. For example, the number of differentially expressed genes is stated, but there is no mention of the magnitude of the changes, the false discovery rate thresholds used, or any validation by RT-qPCR. The ChIP-seq and CUT&RUN data are described in terms of "enrichment" and "depletion," but the text does not provide a clear quantitative measure of these changes or the statistical significance. The claim that changes in gene expression are a direct consequence of altered CBP localization and acetylation is correlative. The authors do not test whether the genes with altered expression are the same genes that show altered CBP binding or H3K27ac.
    - **Why it matters** The genomic data is a key part of the paper, as it aims to connect the biophysical observations to a functional outcome. Without a rigorous and quantitative presentation of this data, the conclusions drawn from it are not fully supported. The lack of validation and the purely correlative link between the datasets weaken the overall impact of the study.
    - **Resolution test** The authors should provide a more detailed analysis of their sequencing data, including the number of significant genes/peaks, the effect sizes, and the statistical thresholds. They should validate a subset of the key findings (e.g., changes in gene expression, H3K27ac at specific loci) by independent methods. To strengthen the link between CBP binding, acetylation, and gene expression, they should perform a formal integration analysis (e.g., a correlation analysis or a test for enrichment of differentially expressed genes near differentially acetylated regions).

- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria**
    - **Originality:** The work is original in its focus on the opposing functions of specific IDRs within a single coactivator. While the role of IDRs in condensates is known, the idea that adjacent IDRs with different sequence features act as a regulatory "balance" is a novel concept.
    - **Scientific importance:** The question of how IDRs regulate the function of a key transcriptional coactivator is of high importance. The potential link between condensate properties and the response to a major post-translational modification (acetylation) is a significant and timely topic.
    - **Interdisciplinary readership:** The topic is of interest to molecular biologists, biochemists, and biophysicists. The work bridges cell biology and genomics. However, the presentation is currently too specialized for a broad Nature readership.
    - **Technical soundness:** The technical soundness cannot be fully assessed without the figures and raw data. The described methods are generally appropriate, but the lack of quantitative details in the text is a major concern. The potential for overexpression artifacts is a significant technical weakness.
    - **Readability for nonspecialists:** The manuscript is not currently readable for a nonspecialist. The abstract and introduction are dense, and the results section is a long list of observations without a clear narrative.
- **Recommendation posture** Currently not established from the provided evidence. The manuscript addresses an important question and contains interesting observations, but the major concerns regarding experimental design (overexpression), lack of mechanistic insight, and incomplete data presentation prevent me from supporting the case. The authors need to provide a more rigorous and mechanistically deeper analysis to make a compelling case for publication in a high-impact journal.
