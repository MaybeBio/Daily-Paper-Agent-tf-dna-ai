## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text (abstract, results, discussion, methods, data/code availability statements)
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific claims, methodology, and evidence as presented in the provided text; no supplementary files, figures, or tables were available for direct inspection
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors introduce PLABench, a leakage-controlled benchmarking framework for protein–ligand binding affinity prediction, and use it to evaluate nine deep learning models across four datasets. They report that massively pretrained structure-based models achieve the best aggregate performance but show target-dependent variability, that structural fidelity does not consistently improve predictions, and that sequence-based models can outperform structure-based ones on some targets. They conclude that training scale and structural input alone do not guarantee generalization.
- **Visible evidence base** Text descriptions of results, tables, and figures; specific numerical values are reported for some claims (e.g., training set sizes, ligand counts, correlation values), but the underlying data, figures, and supplementary notes were not provided
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** All figures (Figs. 1–4), all tables (Tables 1–4), Supplementary Notes 1–3, Supplementary Tables 1–4, and the Zenodo data deposit; without these, quantitative claims and statistical analyses cannot be independently verified

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** The manuscript addresses a timely and important problem in computational drug discovery: the lack of standardized, leakage-controlled benchmarks for protein–ligand affinity prediction. The proposed framework, PLABench, is thoughtfully designed and addresses real limitations in existing evaluations. The finding that structural fidelity does not consistently improve prediction accuracy is potentially significant. However, the manuscript as presented has several major issues that prevent full assessment: the key results are described only in text without access to the actual figures and tables, the statistical rigor of some comparisons is unclear, and the causal attribution of performance differences to training set scale is not adequately supported. The work is promising but requires substantial revision and access to the full data to establish its claims.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers developing deep learning methods for protein–ligand binding affinity prediction, computational chemists and structural biologists working on drug discovery, and method developers in the broader protein function prediction community. The benchmark framework and the finding that structural accuracy does not guarantee improved affinity prediction are relevant to anyone designing or evaluating structure-based prediction methods.
- **Major strengths** The framework addresses a genuine and widely recognized problem of data leakage in affinity prediction benchmarks. The inclusion of both sequence- and structure-based methods under standardized conditions is a valuable contribution. The use of CASP16 blind targets and a leakage-controlled ChEMBL35 set is methodologically sound. The finding that sequence-based models can outperform structure-based ones on some targets challenges prevailing assumptions and is likely to stimulate further research.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **R1-M1** (Severity: Major, Blocking: Yes) The central quantitative claims cannot be verified from the provided material. All results are presented as text descriptions of figures and tables that were not included in the submission. Without access to Figs. 1–4, Tables 1–4, and the supplementary materials, it is impossible to assess the validity of the reported performance metrics, the statistical significance of differences between models, or the robustness of the conclusions. This is a fundamental limitation that must be resolved before the case is established.
  - **R1-M2** (Severity: Major, Blocking: Yes) The claim that training set scale contributes to the performance advantage of Boltz2 and FLOWR.ROOT is not adequately supported. The authors state that these models were trained on approximately 60-fold and 125-fold larger datasets than Graph_RG, respectively, and suggest this may explain their superior performance. However, the models also differ in architecture, training objectives, and data composition, and the authors acknowledge this confound. No controlled experiment isolating the effect of scale is presented. The claim is therefore speculative and should be reframed as a hypothesis rather than a finding.
  - **R1-M3** (Severity: Major, Blocking: No) The statistical analysis of model comparisons is not described in sufficient detail. The manuscript reports performance metrics for multiple models across multiple datasets, but it is unclear whether differences between models are statistically significant, what error bars or confidence intervals are used, and whether multiple testing corrections were applied. The text mentions "paired bootstrap 95% confidence intervals" in one specific context (hERG multimer analysis), but the general approach to statistical inference is not described. This is essential for interpreting the comparative claims.
  - **R1-M4** (Severity: Major, Blocking: No) The generalizability of the findings is limited by the small number of targets in the CASP16 dataset (two targets) and the specific composition of the ChEMBL35 set. The authors acknowledge this limitation for the multimer analysis, but the broader conclusions about model generalization are drawn from a relatively narrow set of targets. The manuscript would benefit from a more explicit discussion of how the target composition of the benchmark affects the generalizability of the conclusions.
- **Minor Comments**
  - **R1-m1** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Clarity) The description of the "guilt-by-association (GBA) Mixup" data augmentation strategy used by MixingDTA is too brief. A more detailed explanation of this method and its potential impact on the results would help readers assess the fairness of the comparison.
  - **R1-m2** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Reproducibility) The manuscript states that sequence-based models were retrained on a "common PDBbind training corpus" but does not specify the exact data split, hyperparameters, or training duration. Additional details on the training protocol would improve reproducibility.
  - **R1-m3** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Presentation) The Discussion section would benefit from a more explicit statement of the practical implications of the findings for drug discovery. The authors mention that the ensemble strategy improves performance on "high-value pharmacological target classes," but the practical significance of this improvement is not quantified.
  - **R1-m4** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Completeness) The manuscript mentions that "no overlap was detected for the remaining models" in the CASP16 cross-corpus audit, but it does not provide the details of this audit for all models. A summary table of the audit results would strengthen the leakage-control claims.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** R1-M1 (missing data), R1-M2 (unsupported causal claim), R1-M3 (insufficient statistical detail)
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** Originality: The work is original in its combination of leakage control, target-centric evaluation, and cross-paradigm comparison. The finding that structural fidelity does not consistently improve prediction is novel and potentially important. Scientific importance: The problem addressed is of high importance to the field, and the framework has the potential to become a standard benchmark. However, the importance of the specific findings is difficult to assess without the full data. Interdisciplinary readership: The topic is relevant to computational biology, medicinal chemistry, and machine learning, and the manuscript is generally accessible to these audiences. Technical soundness: The framework design is sound, but the lack of access to the data and the insufficient statistical detail prevent full assessment. Readability for nonspecialists: The manuscript is clearly written and the concepts are explained well, but the reliance on text descriptions of missing figures makes it difficult for any reader to follow the results.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The framework and the general approach are valuable, and the findings are potentially significant. However, the manuscript cannot be accepted in its current form because the key results are not verifiable from the provided material, and the causal claim about training set scale is not supported. The authors should provide the full data, figures, and tables, and revise the claims to be more cautious where the evidence is insufficient.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that training set scale contributes to the performance advantage of Boltz2 and FLOWR.ROOT is not supported by a controlled experiment and is confounded by architectural and data composition differences.
- The claim that "increasing structural fidelity from predicted to experimental conformations yields no consistent gains" is based on a single dataset (CASP16 L3000) with a reduced sample size (93 complexes) and may not generalize.
- The claim that the ensemble strategy "implicitly improves the predictive precision across high-value pharmacological target classes" is based on a single ensemble configuration and has not been validated prospectively.
- The overall performance comparisons between models are not supported by statistical significance testing as described in the text.
- The leakage-control claims for the ChEMBL35 dataset rely on a similarity threshold (Tanimoto ≥ 0.85) that is not justified in the text, and the sensitivity of the results to this threshold is not explored.
