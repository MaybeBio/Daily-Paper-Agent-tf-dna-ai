## Abstract

How transcription factors (TFs) selectively recognize their genomic targets among numerous sites containing similar DNA motifs remains an open question. Cooperative binding at neighboring motif sites is widely considered a key mechanism of TF specificity, yet its genome-wide contribution remains unclear. Machine learning models can now predict TF binding directly from DNA sequence, providing opportunities to investigate its underlying determinants. Here, we leveraged BPNet models trained on ∼2,000 ENCODE TF binding profiles to systematically assess motif cooperativity. Although detected at individual loci, cooperativity made a negligible contribution to genome-wide binding predictions. Nevertheless, the models captured differences in binding preferences between TFs with similar motif preferences, even within the same cellular environment. These findings highlight the need to critically assess prevailing assumptions and explore alternative mechanisms underlying selective TF binding.

A fundamental question in gene regulation is how transcription factors (TFs) select their genomic binding sites. DNA-binding domains (DBDs) recognize short DNA sequence motifs, yet TFs typically occupy only a fraction of the many genomic sites containing these motifs 1 , 2 ( Fig. 1A-B ). Moreover, TFs with similar or even identical motif preferences can occupy distinct sets of genomic sites. While DBD–motif recognition is relatively well understood, what determines the selective occupancy of individual motif sites remains poorly explained. Prevailing models attribute specificity primarily to chromatin accessibility and cooperative interactions with other TF 1,3-6 ( Fig. 1C ). Yet, the capacity of these models to explain TF binding preferences has not been systematically assessed at the genomic scale.

Recent advances in applying machine learning to functional genomic data offer a powerful approach to investigating TF binding specificity. Deep learning models such as BPNet 7 and AlphaGenome 8 can predict TF binding profiles from genomic DNA sequences. BPNet was recently applied across more than 2,000 ENCODE TF ChIP-seq experiments (ENCODE Project Consortium 9 ), demonstrating high accuracy in predicting binding profiles of hundreds of TFs ( Fig. 1D , Fig.S1). The models therefore capture sequence information relevant to genomic specificity, providing an opportunity to systematically evaluate the contributions of proposed specificity mechanisms.

We leveraged BPNet models trained on ENCODE data to examine the sequence determinants underlying their binding prediction 7 , 10 , 11 . The models’ interpretation framework identifies sequence regions contributing to predictions at individual binding peaks, termed seqlets , and clusters these into recurrent patterns summarized as contribution weight matrices (CWMs). These patterns often correspond to individual TF-binding motifs, as well as composite motifs representing combinations of binding sites that could reflect cooperative TF interactions ( Fig. 1E ). As a first test of motif cooperativity, we examined the prevalence of composite patterns across TF models 7 . Although detected, these patterns generally occurred in only a minority of binding peaks. Moreover, individual seqlets typically contained limited sequence information, approximately 12 bits, equivalent to a fully specified 6-bp sequence 12 ( Fig. 1 E-I ). Together, these findings suggest that neither composite motifs nor high-information-content sequence patterns contribute substantially to model-predicted binding specificity at the genomic scale.

Given the limited information content of individual seqlets, we next asked whether binding specificity emerges from cooperative interactions among multiple motifs within the same binding peak. We first focused on multiple occurrences of the motif bound by the profiled TF (direct motif), reasoning that occupancy at one site may enhance binding at neighboring sites 7 , 13 . To test this, we performed in silico perturbations, comparing the effects of mutating motifs individually or in combination ( Fig. 2A-C ). Under independent contributions, the effect of the combined mutations is expected to equal the sum of the individual effects, whereas a larger or smaller summed effect indicates cooperativity or redundancy, respectively 14 , 15 . Note that we used control-free predicted count scores, as BPNet predictions were often dominated by the free-DNA control ( Fig. 1D ).

Overall, cooperative interactions among direct motifs made a negligible contribution to model-predicted TF binding ( Fig. 2D-F ). Across peaks and TFs, motif contributions were largely additive. Cooperative effects were detected at individual peaks and were more prominent for certain TFs, including FOXA1 and FOXA2. However, their prevalence decreased sharply with increasing effect size, with effects exceeding twofold observed in fewer than 1% of peaks for nearly all TFs ( Fig. 2D-F ).

We next asked whether interactions between motifs of the profiled TF and those of other TFs (non-direct motifs) might play a more prominent role in predicted binding. To investigate this, we extended our in silico perturbation analysis to direct and non-direct motifs ( Fig. 3A-C ), comparing the effects of mutating either group individually or both together at each binding peak. Notably, some TFs showed greater dependence on non-direct motifs, although direct motifs dominated in most cases ( Fig. 3F-G ). We then assessed whether the contribution of one motif group depended on the presence of the other, as expected under cooperative binding. As with interactions among direct motifs, the combined effects were predominantly additive, with cooperative interactions detected only in a minority of cases ( Fig. 3D-E ). Thus, cooperativity between direct and non-direct motifs made a negligible contribution to genome-wide binding predictions.

Next, we asked how TFs with the same motif preferences bind in the same cells and chromatin context, and whether the models capture that. If binding preferences were determined primarily by canonical motif recognition and chromatin accessibility, TFs recognizing similar motifs within the same cellular environment would be expected to occupy similar genomic sites. To examine this, we systematically compared genome-wide binding profiles of TFs belonging to the same motif family and profiled in the same cell type across the ENCODE dataset. Notably, as exemplified by the FOX and SP/KLF families ( Fig. 4A-C ), TFs sharing similar motif preferences often exhibited substantially different genomic binding profiles (Fig. S2). These differences were accurately captured by the sequence-based models and persisted when mutating the major seqlets ( Fig. 4D-E ). Thus, canonical motif recognition and a shared chromatin environment are insufficient to fully explain TF genomic selectivity, pointing to additional sequence determinants captured by the models.

Together, our analysis reveals that sequence-based models accurately predict selective TF binding with limited reliance on motif cooperativity and that chromatin accessibility alone is insufficient to explain genomic preferences. Importantly, accurate prediction of TF binding profiles from DNA sequence does not necessarily imply that models capture the underlying biological mechanisms, as their learned representations may reflect alternative solutions, biases in ChIP-seq data, or other experimental confounders. Nevertheless, our findings, together with other studies questioning the central role of cooperativity in genomic targeting 15 - 17 raise the possibility that additional specificity mechanisms remain uncharacterized. For example, specificity may depend on weak, distributed DNA sequence signals extending beyond recognizable motifs 18 , 19 , potentially paralleling the distributed binding determinants identified within TF intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs), which are increasingly recognized as important contributors to genomic binding preference 20 - 24 . Distinguishing among these possibilities will require further experimental and computational investigation to establish the molecular basis of selective TF binding.
