## Abstract

Transcription factors have been shown to predominantly rely on independent contributions from each position of their target sequence motifs, even though sequence motifs can encode information through correlations among nucleotide positions. As a result, the position-weight matrix, a simple modeling approach that assumes independence among positions, has long since become the dominant model of TF-DNA binding. Why evolution would favor this comparatively simple strategy, known as single locus information ( SI ), over a correlation-based one remains unaddressed. Here, we use analytical derivations, numerical approaches and evolutionary simulations to show that SI is intrinsically more robust to mutation than correlated (multi-locus) encodings and is therefore indirectly favored under selection for functional transcriptional regulatory networks. At the same time, we show that SI smooths the affinity landscape, ensuring the evolvability of regulatory interactions. We find the same marked bias toward SI in the genetic code, suggesting that the preference for SI is not specific to transcriptional regulation, but may be a general property of biological codes that must reliably store information on DNA in the face of mutational noise.


## 1. Introduction

The binding of molecules to specific locations within nucleotide sequences underpins many fundamental cellular processes. Among these, transcriptional regulation plays a central role by controlling gene expression in response to endogenous and exogenous signals ( Ptashne 2005 ). Transcription factors (TFs) are specialized proteins that regulate the expression of target genes by binding to DNA within cognate regulatory regions, either facilitating or hindering the formation of an open promoter complex ( Orphanides and Reinberg 2002 ). The set of regulatory interactions between TFs and their target genes is commonly referred to as the transcriptional regulatory network (TRN). Most TFs bind to short (5-30 bp) contiguous sequence patterns, or motifs, which they must recognize within a much larger genome sequence. The modeling of TF-binding motifs generated from the alignment of experimentally validated target sites has been the focus of a substantial body of work in bioinformatics ( Osada et al. 2004 ). Early work on TF-binding motifs pioneered the use of position-specific frequency matrices (PSFM) to model TF-binding preferences and search for motif instances in genomic sequences ( Staden 1984 ). This approach rests on the assumption that each position of the motif contributes to TF-binding affinity independently of the others. Under this assumption, a binding-site score is computed as the sum of individual position contributions derived from the observed base frequencies at each position in the binding sites alignment ( Figure 1a-b and Figure 1e-f ).

Despite its simplicity, the PSFM formalism remains the dominant paradigm for modeling TF-DNA interactions. However, the assumption of independence is not mandated by biophysics. In principle, correlations between nucleotide positions can encode additional information about binding affinity, reflecting epistatic interactions among positions due to structural constraints, cooperative contacts, or other context-dependent effects. Motivated by this possibility, numerous studies have proposed motif models that incorporate dependencies between positions, ranging from pairwise to higher-order terms. While such models can capture correlations present in some datasets, their predictive performance has generally improved modestly relative to independence-based models, and in many cases not significantly ( Osada et al. 2004 ; Kulakovskiy et al. 2013 ; Omidi et al. 2017 ). Consistent with this, experimental evidence has confirmed that PSFM-based models provide a very good approximation to TF-binding affinity ( Benos et al. 2002 ; Zhao and Stormo 2011 ; Weirauch et al. 2013 ; Zuo and Stormo 2014 ).

These empirical findings raise a fundamental evolutionary question: if correlated encodings are a viable strategy to achieve binding specificity ( Le et al. 2018 ), why do TF-binding motifs predominantly rely on independent positions?

In practice, however, estimating higher-order correlations requires exponentially large sample sizes. Because TF-binding motifs typically comprise at most a few hundred sites, the analyses of correlation in these systems have been largely restricted to pairwise mutual information. Moreover, whenever present, the correlations contributing to TF-binding affinity are mostly between adjacent positions ( Zhao et al. 2009 , 2012 ; Le et al. 2018 ), and are commonly interpreted as arising from stacking interactions that generate local DNA shape features conducive to TF binding ( Zhou et al. 2015 ). For these reasons, in this work we restrict our informational analysis of TF-binding motifs to pairwise adjacent correlations (see Methods, Section 4.1 ).

In this work, we investigate whether the structure of the information-encoding strategy itself influences the mutational robustness and evolvability of TF-binding affinity. We compare motifs with identical total information that differ in how that information is encoded: only through independent positional contributions (pure SI ), only through correlations between positions (pure MI ) or through mixed strategies (varying percent SI ). By combining analytical results, numerical approaches, evolutionary simulations and landscape analysis, we ask whether independence could emerge as an evolutionarily favored encoding strategy under mutational noise. Finally, we extend the same informational framework beyond transcriptional regulation by examining the genetic code, asking whether a bias toward SI reflects a general principle that influences how biological information is encoded in DNA.


## 2. Results


## 2.1 Asymptotic information loss rates due to mutation

The ability to maintain encoded information in the face of mutation is a fundamental constraint on biological systems ( Masel and Trotter 2010 ; Rozhoňová et al. 2024 ). A possible explanation for the observed predominance of SI over MI in TF-binding motifs is therefore an intrinsic advantage at maintaining information when subject to mutations. To examine this possibility, we quantified the intrinsic sensitivity of these two encoding strategies to mutational perturbation. First, we derived analytical expressions for the asymptotic information loss due to random point mutations. We compared SI and MI motifs under identical initial total information content ( TI ). Specifically, we analyzed motifs of length L =2 bp (the minimal system in which MI can be defined) and constrained all motifs to have (1) equiprobable bound sequences and (2) an average of 1 bit per position (consistent with the typical information density of TF-binding motifs ( Stewart et al. 2012 ), yielding a total information of 2 bits in each motif). While MI is symmetrical over the two positions, SI can freely distribute information unequally among them, opening a range of possibilities for the distribution of the 2 bits over two positions. Therefore, we compared three encoding strategies: SI Case A , where information is distributed in the most unequal way (2 bits at one position and 0 at the other); SI Case B , where information is distributed as equally as possible (1 bit at each position); and the MI motif, which encodes the same total information (2 bits) via correlation among the two positions ( Figure 2 ).

## 2.2 Lower information loss leads to higher mutational robustness in SI -encoded TRNs

The analytical results presented above rely on assumptions introduced for mathematical tractability and define the information loss rate due to infinitesimal changes in an idealized motif. To test whether the predicted differences between SI and MI extend beyond these idealized cases, we next adopted a numerical approach based on randomly sampled motifs with a given total information content. We applied random mutations to samples of 100 2-bp motifs encoding 1 bit of information either via SI or via MI , and monitored information loss. We found that MI -based motifs lose information substantially faster than SI -based ones ( Figure 3 A ). The observed information loss rates after the first mutation event approximate a 2:3 ratio ( Figure 3 A - inset), in agreement with the analytical results for SI Case B. This alignment of experimental results with SI Case B reflects the fact that motifs with a roughly equal distribution of information across positions, as in SI Case B, vastly outnumber those with information concentrated in a single position ( Figure S1 ).

Extending this analysis to motifs encoding information through mixed contributions of SI and MI revealed that mutational robustness scales with the fraction of information encoded via SI , with motifs relying exclusively on SI showing the slowest information decay ( Figure S2 ). This pattern persists across different information densities ( Figure S3 ), and when generalizing from motifs of length two to motifs of arbitrary length ( Figure S4 ).

The loss of the information encoded in TF-binding sites is a challenge for transcriptional regulatory systems because it decreases the ability of a TF to discriminate between its target binding sites and all other genomic locations and, hence, maintain the integrity of the TRN. Treating the TF as a classifier that must distinguish its target sites from the other genomic locations based on binding affinity, we can assess TRN integrity using the area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) following a genome scan for TF-binding sites ( Kılıç and Erill 2016 ; Zhang et al. 2024 ). Therefore, we sampled 12-bp long motifs encoding information preferentially through either SI or MI , and we embedded each motif’s target binding sites in a random genome sequence. As we introduced random mutations, we monitored the decrease in AUPRC resulting from scanning the genome with the TF model defined by the original set of TF-binding sites. As expected, SI -based TRNs showed higher robustness to mutations, measured as a slower loss in classification performance as mutations accumulate ( Figure 3 B ), regardless of the length or the information density of the motif ( Figure S5 ; Figure S6 ).


## 2.3 Mutational robustness promotes the evolution of SI -based TRNs

The higher mutational robustness of SI -encoded networks suggests that they may have an evolutionary advantage under stabilizing selection, since the expected fitness of their offspring will be higher. To test this possibility, we devised evolutionary simulations for organisms encoding TF-binding models and their target sites on a randomly initialized genome background, using the TRN integrity (AUPRC) as a proxy for fitness. We applied this framework to conduct in silico competition experiments between evolved organisms that encode their TRN either preferentially through SI or preferentially through MI . Both sets of organisms were equally accurate at the beginning of the simulation (same fitness), but SI strategists systematically outcompeted MI strategists and reached fixation in the vast majority of the simulations ( Figure 4 A ), despite the small population size.

Competition experiments indicate that the impact of mutations accumulating over generations is greater in MI -based TRNs, leading to indirect selection for SI -based regulatory codes. Therefore, we hypothesized that transcriptional regulatory systems could evolve SI -based TF-binding affinity codes due to indirect selection for robustness, even in the absence of any direct fitness advantage, mutation biases or constraints favoring SI over MI . To test this hypothesis, we characterized the global evolutionary trajectories of freely evolving regulatory systems. We considered a discretized 2D phase space for TRNs, with low-to-high percent SI (see Methods 4.1) on one axis and low-to-high TRN functionality, quantified by the AUPRC, on the other. For each represented combination of percent SI and TRN functionality, we estimated the expected evolutionary change under selection for AUPRC, and represented it as a displacement vector. The resulting vector field demonstrates that, as DNA accumulates information and the TRN progresses toward functionality (greater AUPRC), the regulatory system experiences indirect selection toward higher SI , due to the ability of SI to preserve the already evolved regulatory interactions in the face of mutations ( Figure 4 B ).

The phase diagram shows a steady-state attractor at ~75% SI (marked with a star), with TRNs operating near perfect classification (AUPRC≈0.95). This attractor lies far from the mutational steady state in the absence of selection at ~25% SI (marked with an X), which instead favors MI simply because the space of motifs that encode information predominantly through MI is vastly larger than that of motifs relying primarily on SI . To quantify this representational bias toward MI , we analyzed the distribution of percent SI as a function of the total information ( TI ) in TF-binding motifs by uniformly sampling motifs encoding different amounts of TI and computing their percent SI . As shown in Figure 4 C and Figure S7 , SI -encoded motifs are extremely unlikely at low and intermediate information densities, and only become dominant at information densities that strictly require SI (i.e., which cannot be reached through MI alone), near the maximum density of 2 bits per position. As expected, the steady-state in the absence of selection ( X ) identified in Figure 4 B maps to the highest probability density region in Figure 4 C , whereas the steady-state attractor at selection-mutation balance (star) lies in a low probability density region, indicating that it can arise only through selection. Overall, these results demonstrate that, at the information densities typical of TF-binding motifs (~1 bit per position ( Stewart et al. 2012 )), predominantly SI -encoded sequence motifs are unlikely to arise by chance, and suggest that the evolution of SI -encoded regulatory networks is driven by indirect selection for mutational robustness, in the face of a representational bias that rather favors MI -rich motifs.

The specific value of the mutation-selection steady state reported in Figure 4B (~75% SI ) is the result of the simulation parameters used in this study, including a mutation rate set well above biologically realistic values for computational tractability, rather than a quantitative prediction of the equilibrium percent SI expected in any specific biological system. Different parameter choices will shift this attractor location without changing the qualitative direction of the effect. For example, for prokaryotic TF-binding motifs the relevant parameters are substantially different, including a much bigger population size (which increases selection efficacy) and a much lower mutation rate (which reduces the pull of mutational/representational biases). Consistent with this qualitative prediction, empirically measured percent SI values for experimentally-validated prokaryotic TF-binding motifs are substantially higher (~96%; Figure S8 ) than those observed in our simulations.


## 2.4 SI enhances the navigability of the affinity landscape

We provided evidence that SI -based TRNs can result from indirect selection for mutational robustness, a mechanism that is relevant when preserving already evolved regulatory interactions is advantageous, that is, under stabilizing selection. TRNs, however, can also be intermittently subject to directional selection in response to environmental changes, favoring small adjustments or even a complete network rewiring ( Galardini et al. 2015 ; Johnson 2017 ; Adhikari et al. 2021 ). Such processes may involve the loss, acquisition or fine-tuning of regulatory interactions and, therefore, require that the TF-binding affinity of certain sites be modulated, and not just preserved.

The evolvability of a TRN is hence predicated on how readily different affinity values can be reached through changes in the DNA sequences, with the possible mutational changes and their phenotypic effects defining a binding affinity landscape ( Aguilar-Rodríguez et al. 2017 ; Fragata et al. 2019 ). The predominance of SI in naturally occurring TF-binding motifs therefore raises the question of whether SI -based encoding, while conferring mutational robustness under stabilizing selection, is also compatible with efficient navigation of the affinity landscape under directional selection. To address this, we compared the navigability of TF-binding affinity landscapes associated with SI - and MI -encoded motifs using three complementary metrics. The fitness-distance correlation (FDC) measures the correlation between affinity and the sequence with maximum affinity. The genotype distance-phenotype distance correlation (GDPDC) measures the correlation between genotype and phenotype distances, across all possible pairs of genotypes. Lastly, the number of local optima is used as a proxy for the ruggedness of an adaptive landscape, with fewer local optima denoting a more navigable landscape.

Our results for 2-bp and 6-bp motifs show conclusively that SI -encoded motifs generate more navigable landscapes when compared to MI -encoded motifs ( Figure 5 ). FDC coefficients shift from positive to markedly negative as we increase the percentage of information encoded through SI ( Figure 5 B ), indicating that TF-binding affinity decreases with increasing genetic distance from the best binding sequence in SI -based motifs, but not in MI -based ones. Consistent with this finding, GDPDC coefficients increase with percent SI ( Figure 5 B), indicating the prevalence of available monotonic paths in the affinity landscapes of SI -based motifs, but not in MI -based ones. Moreover, the number of optima decreases as the percentage of SI increases ( Figure 5 B), indicating that SI -encoded motifs generate smoother affinity landscapes. Together, these results suggest that encoding information using SI not only increases the mutational robustness of embedded TRNs, but is also compatible with efficient tuning and navigation of TF-binding affinities, consistent with a high potential for TRN evolvability.


## 2.5 Mutational robustness correlates with percent SI in the genetic code

Our results indicate that encoding genomic information preferentially through SI increases mutational robustness while remaining compatible with, and potentially even facilitating, the tunability and evolvability of regulatory interactions. This advantage is due to intrinsic properties of SI and MI , rather than the molecular details of TF-DNA recognition. Therefore, we expect it to hold true for other biological codes outside the context of transcriptional regulatory systems. The genetic code is the most extensively characterized code used by organisms to encode information in DNA sequences. From the perspective of information theory, it can be formally conceptualized as a set of 21 sequence motifs encoding 21 messages: the 20 canonical amino acids and the stop signal ( Figure 6 A ). To explore the generality of SI -preference in the encoding of biological information, we analyzed the standard genetic code under the framework of percent SI . We quantified the percentage of SI used to encode each message, as well as the overall percent SI of the code (see Methods Section 4.1 ). To quantify mutational robustness, we calculate the probability of incorporating the correct amino acid after a codon undergoes a random point mutation (µ-robustness in ( Maeshiro and Kimura 1998 )). We compared the standard genetic code to 1,000 randomized codes that preserve its degeneracy structure, that is, the number of synonymous codons assigned to each message, while randomly permuting codon-message assignments. We found that the standard genetic code stands out not only in terms of mutational robustness, which is one of its well-known features ( Freeland and Hurst 1998 ; Maeshiro and Kimura 1998 ; Goodarzi et al. 2004 ), but also in terms of percent SI (97.39%). Unlike the standard genetic code, random codes display substantial levels of MI in most sequence motifs ( Figure 6 B-C ). The value of percent SI for the standard genetic code is consistent with the one estimated for experimentally-validated prokaryotic TF-binding motifs (96.37%, SEM=0.60 percentage points; Figure S8 ). Importantly, we also found that, as hypothesized, percent SI and code robustness are strongly correlated in randomized genetic codes ( Figure 6 B ).


## 3. Discussion


## 3.1 Robustness as a property of the coding structure

TF-binding motifs are known to encode most of the information through independent contributions from each nucleotide, with limited dependencies between them. This observation motivated us to investigate whether this encoding strategy could be an evolved feature that grants mutational robustness. We quantified robustness based on the expected loss of information due to mutation and found that SI yields lower information loss rates than MI , both in analytically tractable idealized cases and under general conditions evaluated numerically. This formulation of robustness differs conceptually from the classical definition based on neutral network theory, which quantifies the fraction of single-mutant neighbors of a given genotype that preserve its phenotype (thus estimating a phenotype loss probability). Robustness in the classical framework is hence a local property of a specific genotype-phenotype pair, and the phenotype is treated as a discrete trait that is either lost or retained (van Nimwegen et al. 1999 ; Wagner 2005 ). In contrast, by measuring expected information loss (in bits per mutation), robustness as defined here becomes a property of the coding structure itself, that is, of the rules governing the mapping of the 4 L possible genotypes into phenotypes. Our formulation does not require the phenotype to be discrete and applies equally to continuous traits such as TF-binding affinity. The key requirement for its application is the knowledge of the full genotype-phenotype map, which is rarely accessible. However, it is tractable for many TF-binding motifs, where the mapping from sequence to TF-binding affinity can be modeled at nucleotide resolution ( Mascolo et al. 2026 ), and for the genetic code, where the codon-amino acid mapping is known. These cases therefore provide a unique opportunity to analyze robustness at the level of the information-encoding structure.

Our approach can be formally reconciled with neutral network theory in the three idealized cases used in our analytical derivations ( Eq. 9 ), two using only SI (but distributed differently across positions) and a full- MI motif ( Figure 2 ). In these idealized cases, we require TF-binding sequences to be equiprobable. Under this simplifying condition, the phenotype becomes effectively binary: in these motifs, four 2-bp sequences bind the TF with equal probability, and the remaining twelve sequences do not bind it at all. Due to this discretization of the phenotype, the classic notion of robustness based on neutral networks can be applied to all three motifs. The resulting phenotype loss probabilities are different from the information loss rates we propose in this work, which depend on N and are expressed in bits per mutation . Nonetheless, phenotype loss probabilities recapitulate the 1:2 and 2:3 ratios we analytically derived for the asymptotic information loss rates ( Figure 7 ). Moreover, this binarized limit case provides an intuitive illustration of why fully SI -encoded motifs can differ in robustness: robustness depends not only on the percent SI , but also on how SI is distributed across positions.

More broadly, the perspective adopted in this work bridges bioinformatic descriptions of sequence motifs with evolutionary concepts such as neutral networks, showing that robust codes tend to be depleted in correlations among positions and enriched in SI . Somewhat counterintuitively, this hybrid approach also reveals that robustness can be further enhanced by concentrating SI in a few positions and hence reducing the mutational footprint of encoded information ( Hledík et al. 2022 ). Notably, our results are in agreement with the high SI percentages and highly unequal distributions of SI observed in both TF-binding motifs and the genetic code ( Figure 6 C , left panel; ( O’Neill and Erill 2016 )).


## 3.2 Robust codes generate smooth, weakly epistatic landscapes

The distinction between SI and MI is not merely a statistical description of sequence motifs, but an information-theoretic formulation of the long-studied problem of epistasis in molecular evolution. Indeed, epistatic interactions require MI -based codes, since SI entails purely additive (non-epistatic) landscapes (see Appendix D). Previous work ( Strelioff et al. 2010 ) demonstrated that in epistatic landscapes, epistatically interacting loci evolve MI , whereas in the absence of epistasis, sequences evolve SI -based motifs. But what happens if one allows molecular recognizers to coevolve with their target DNA sequences, so that the degree of epistasis is itself evolvable? Our results show that indirect selection for mutational robustness leads to SI -enriched (hence only weakly epistatic) sequence motifs, even in the absence of direct selection for SI , and despite a representational bias toward MI ( Figure 4 ). While the specific equilibrium values depend on many system-specific evolutionary and population-genetic parameters (such as mutation rates, population size and the selection coefficients), we show that a preference for SI spontaneously evolves when the phenotype must be preserved in the face of mutations ( Figure 4 B ).

This finding has implications beyond mutational robustness. Indeed, MI -based codes imply nonlinearities in the phenotype landscape (i.e. epistasis), including reciprocal sign epistasis , which is the type of interaction that can generate local optima and hinder navigability ( Poelwijk et al. 2011 ; Saona et al. 2022 ) ( Figure 5 ). In contrast, by avoiding epistasis, purely SI -based codes entail the smoothest possible landscape (a linear, or additive, landscape). In such landscapes, there is only one optimum, phenotypic and genotypic distances are strongly correlated, and beneficial mutations can be acquired in any order, thus maximizing the number of accessible paths to adaptation ( Figure 5 ). Thus, the mutational robustness conferred by SI , to fit populations during periods of stabilizing selection, does not come at the expense of evolvability, which is instead crucial when the population undergoes directional selection (e.g., right after an environmental change). This is consistent with substantial evidence that, in some cases, robustness can facilitate adaptation rather than impede it ( Masel and Trotter 2010 ; Draghi et al. 2010 ; Wagner 2007 ; Aldana et al. 2007 ).

The best example of a biological code that provides robustness and evolvability is the genetic code: it minimizes the effect of point mutations and mistranslations while facilitating adaptive exploration of protein sequence space ( Maeshiro and Kimura 1998 ; Schwersensky et al. 2020 ; Rozhoňová et al. 2024 ). In a classic study ( Maeshiro and Kimura 1998 ), Maeshiro and Kimura noted that the genetic code follows a specific encoding principle: synonymous codons are organized into 3D boxes within the “genetic cube” (i.e., the code viewed as a 3D table with the axes corresponding to the three codon positions). The only exceptions to this rule are the Leu, Arg, Ser and Stop codons. While their original formulation is difficult to parse and specific to the genetic code ( L =3, 21 messages), our work provides a simple and general interpretation. In pure- SI motifs of length L , codewords encoding the same message necessarily form L -dimensional hyper-rectangles within the code table (see Figure 7 C and Appendix C). The geometric organization identified by Maeshiro and Kimura essentially corresponds to SI encoding in the special case of the genetic code ( L =3, 21 messages). Our work thus reveals that the genetic code closely follows a rather simple encoding strategy, the same as our most robust idealized motif (“ SI motif, Case A”): encoding information solely as SI and packing it in a minimal set of highly conserved positions, making nucleotide contributions independent and unequal . The only four messages that depart from this strategy and involve correlations among positions are precisely the four exceptions identified by Maeshiro and Kimura (see Fig. Figure 6 C , Figure S10 ).


## 3.3 Additivity as an evolved feature of biological codes

Classical models of TF-DNA interactions assume that TF-binding site positions provide independent contributions to the overall binding affinity. The scientific community has also explored higher-order models that incorporate correlations between positions, but they do not generally improve TF-binding site predictions substantially ( Osada et al. 2004 ; Kulakovskiy et al. 2013 ; Omidi et al. 2017 ), and thus the PSFM remains the model of choice ( Stormo 2013 ). This indicates that TF-binding motifs do not encode a substantial amount of information via correlations between positions, which raises the question: why make so little use of a valid source of information?

One possible explanation is that it would be difficult for proteins to recognize inter-positional correlations. However, observed correlations, especially between adjacent bases, are often implied by sequence-dependent structural features of DNA, such as local curvature or bendability, that proteins can readily recognize ( Rohs et al. 2009 ; Velmurugu et al. 2018 ). Moreover, high-throughput experimental methods have been used to estimate global TF-DNA affinity landscapes with increasing accuracy and coverage ( Maerkl and Quake 2007 ; Zhao et al. 2009 ; Aguilar-Rodríguez et al. 2017 ; Le et al. 2018 ; Westmann et al. 2024 ). These studies have shown that contributions to site occupancy are predominantly additive for high-affinity sites, whereas they consistently report non-additive contributions for low-affinity sequences. These observations support our interpretation that additivity might be largely an evolved feature of robust encodings, rather than a biochemical constraint on TF-DNA interactions. Indeed, after analyzing the standard genetic code - the best-known genotype-phenotype map, where any alternative tRNA-amino acid assignment would be biochemically possible - we have found the same qualitative trends observed for TF-binding motifs.

These results point to a general evolutionary pressure favoring independence among sequence elements. Consistent with this view, growing evidence suggests that, as with TF-binding motifs, the genotype-phenotype-fitness maps of protein-coding sequences are also more linear than previously assumed. After accounting for nonlinear transformations of the measured phenotype (sometimes referred to as non-specific or global epistasis), additive maps can explain most of the observed phenotypic variance, with the remaining unexplained variance being mostly due to pairwise specific epistasis, rather than higher-order ( Park et al. 2024 ; Otwinowski et al. 2018 ; Grah et al. 2025 ; Weinreich et al. 2018 ; Carneiro and Hartl 2010 ; Metzger et al. 2024 ). Therefore, our framework rationalizes several recurring features of biological codes: nearly additive phenotypic effects, information-dense loci, and the coexistence of mutational robustness and evolvability. Importantly, this work recasts in information-theoretic terms several convergent insights that have independently emerged across traditionally distinct research communities, including bioinformatic approaches to sequence motifs and evolutionary genetic concepts such as robustness, evolvability, and epistasis. In doing so, it proposes a common framework for studying quantitatively how information encodings evolve in nucleic acid sequences.

## 4.2 Motif sampling

We apply two different algorithms to sample motifs with a required information content. When sampling from the full space of possible encodings (the general case), we apply an evolutionary algorithm (see Section 4.2.1 ). We also use this algorithm to generate motifs with specific values of SI and MI . When instead we work under the restricting assumption that selected (target) sequences are equiprobable, we can exactly enumerate all motifs and directly draw uniform samples (see Section 4.2.2 ).

For the general case, we employ an evolutionary algorithm to sample TF-binding motifs encoding a specific amount of SI and/or MI . Motifs are explicitly represented as a collection of n bs TF-binding sites of a given length ( l bs ). The evolutionary algorithm is initialized with a population of randomly initialized motifs and a requested number of bits of SI ( r SI ) and/or MI ( r MI ). The algorithm then iterates a replication-mutation-selection process, using the following error function as the objective function to be minimized: where α SI and α MI are control (0,1) values determining whether SI ( r PI ) and/or MI ( r MI ) are included as targets in the error function to be optimized.

At each iteration, each individual motif is first duplicated, and mutations are applied to its copy by randomly replacing nucleotides at each site position with a probability of 0.01 per position. The algorithm then compares the error e obj ( x ) of the parent and offspring motifs in terms of their deviation from the desired number of bits of SI ( r SI ) and/or MI ( r MI ), and the motif with the lower error proceeds to the next generation.

We principally employ α SI = 1, α MI = 0 and α SI = 0, α MI = 1 to generate SI- or MI -based motifs, respectively. However, we use α SI = 1, α MI = 1 to explicitly control for the fraction of both SI and MI in specific cases ( Figure S2 ).

Under the assumption that target sequences are equiprobable, we can exactly enumerate and sample motifs that have a required total information content ( TI ). This restrictive assumption, which was used in defining the three idealized motifs in Section 2.1 , effectively induces a binary phenotype in which n selected L -mers are bound with equal probability, while all the others are unbound. Under this assumption, TI = log (4 L / selected ). Motifs are then obtained by uniformly sampling over the possible subsets of recognized sequences. This approach was used to estimate the probability distributions in Fig. 4C , S7 and S8.


## 4.3 Mutational robustness analysis

To analyze information loss in motifs, we sample two sets of 100 motifs to encode information as either SI or MI , or a specific mixture of both in Figure S2 . We then iteratively apply mutations, randomly substituting in each motif a single base of the constituent n bs TF-binding sites at each iteration, and monitoring the average SI and MI in each motif set as iterations progress.

To analyze network integrity, we sample two sets of 100 motifs to encode information as either SI or MI . For each sampled motif, we generate a random genome sequence of length l G,int , and the n bs TF-binding sites that define the motif are then embedded into this sequence. The embedded TF-binding sites are used to instantiate a dinucleotide PWM (diPWM) model ( Kulakovskiy et al. 2013 ), which accounts for dependencies among adjacent nucleotides, and the diPWM model is used to calculate TF-binding affinity scores along the artificial genome sequence using a conventional sliding window approach. The embedded TF-binding sites are the true target sites, while all the other genomic positions are defined as non-target sites. The ability of the model to distinguish target sites from the rest of the genome sequence is captured by the area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC). We iteratively apply mutations, randomly substituting a single base of the genome at each iteration, and monitoring the average AUPRC for each motif set as iterations progress.

To explore the mutational robustness of TRNs, we simulate the coevolution of TF-binding motifs and their target sites within an artificial genome. Each organism in this co-evolutionary algorithm is defined by a genome that is represented as a DNA sequence. The locations for target sites are pre-designated, and the TF-binding model is stored in a pre-specified ‘gene’ region of the genome, where the diPWM model is represented as a set of TF-binding sites arranged column-wise Figure S 11 . ## 4.4 Affinity landscape analysis

In our study of the genotype-phenotype map, we associate each genotype x i with a phenotype f ( x i ), namely the TF-DNA binding affinity for the DNA sequence x i . The affinity is scored with the standard log-likelihood ratio approach ( Hertz and Stormo 1999 ). Importantly, the score is assigned using the true joint probability distribution of the L -mers: f ( x i ) = log 2 ( p ( x i )/ p 0 ( x i )), where p ( x i ) is the probability of a selected site being L -mer x i , while the genomic background probability for that L -mer is p 0 ( x i ). Only in the special case of pure SI motifs the joint distribution factorizes and this expression reduces exactly to the standard additive PSSM score, while for motifs that contain MI it correctly captures the epistatic structure that a PSSM approximation would erase. Genotype distances are simply defined as the Hamming distance ( Hamming 1950 ), i.e., the number of positions where the sequences differ. Two DNA sequences are adjacent to each other - or neighbors - if they are at a Hamming distance of 1 from each other (i.e., one sequence can be turned into the other by a single nucleotide substitution).

The fitness-distance correlation (FDC) is a widely used landscape metric to study evolvability, developed to estimate the navigability of fitness landscapes ( Sun et al. 2014 ; Zou et al. 2022 ). It measures the correlation between the fitness of a genotype and its distance (in genotype space) from the global optimum. Here we apply this metric to estimate the navigability of the affinity landscape, so f ( x ) is the TF binding affinity for sequence x , and not its fitness. ## Appendix C. SI -encoded messages represent rectangular subsets of the genotype table

There are 4 L possible L -mers. They can be organized into an L -dimensional genotype table with L axes, each with four possible entries (for the four possible nucleotides). Every cell in this table represents a distinct L -mer. Thus, the table can be represented as an L -dimensional (hyper)-rectangle, and the boundaries between rows and columns slice it in four parts along each dimension (see, as an example, the visualizations for the case L =2 in Figure 7 C , or see Figure S 10 for the case L =3).

When positions contribute independently of each other to molecular recognition (no correlations among positions), the probability distribution over the recognized codewords encoding a message follows a simple constraint: the probability that the recognized L -mer is sequence B simply factorizes as , where B i is the i -th element of B , and p i ( B i ) is the marginal probability of its occurrence at position i among recognized codewords encoding the message. If we now sort each axis of the table based on ascending or descending p i , one corner of the (hyper)-rectangle will contain a cell corresponding to an L -mer with the maximum probability, while the opposite corner the minimum probability, and probability monotonically increases/decreases in every direction.

## Appendix D. Pure- SI motifs imply additive (non-epistatic) landscapes

Two mutations are said to interact epistatically when the phenotypic effect of one mutation alone differs from its effect in the presence of the other mutation. In contrast, the absence of epistasis implies a purely additive interaction, such that where 0→1 indicates a mutation from the initial nucleotide (marked with 0) to a new nucleotide (marked as 1), and the e function is the effect of mutations (a measurable phenotype). The various categories in which epistatic interactions can be classified represent different ways of departing from this equality.

When a phenotype is genetically encoded via SI , the DNA sequence positions have independent (purely additive) effects. Under independence, the joint distribution over all the L -mers is simply the product of the marginals at each position, and the information encoded in DNA becomes strictly additive (see Eq. 4 in the main text). In biological codes, sequence motifs are typically decoded by molecular recognizers that differentially bind the possible L -mers. This allows the uncertainty on any phenotype that relies on recognizer-DNA binding to be decreased by up to TI ( σ ) bits (see Eq. A.3 ), which is an upper bound on phenotypic information gain due to the data processing inequality theorem.

Epistatic interactions among sequence positions are expected to result in correlations ( MI ) ( Strelioff et al. 2010 ). Instead, TF-binding sites are typically assumed to be approximately in a regime of independent effects from each nucleotide or with at most a few significant dinucleotide interactions (mostly from adjacent positions). This independent regime, captured by the conventional PSSM models, reduces the affinity score (based on the log-likelihood ratio; see Figure 1 f ) to a purely additive score. For example, if the e function in Eq. D.1 were to represent the classic PSSM score ( Figure 1 e-f ), the equality would always hold true. Thus, when TF-DNA interactions are estimated using this classical bioinformatic model, the underlying assumption is that there are no significant epistatic interactions among the positions of the sequence motif. Instead, in the presence of epistatic interactions, selection stores genetic information also through MI (or higher-order correlations) among positions ( Strelioff et al. 2010 ), breaking additivity (both sequence information contributions and binding energy contributions are no longer merely additive).


## Appendix E. The maximum number of optima for nucleotide dimers is four

Two optima in the landscape cannot be adjacent (only one mutation away from each other). Indeed, if they had both the maximum value, they would count as one (flat) optimum (see optimum definition in Methods, Section 4.4 ), while if they had different values, the lower one could not be an optimum, since it would have a neighbor with a higher value. Therefore, two optima always differ in at least two positions (for dimers, both positions must differ).

If four dimers are distinct optima, they must all have distinct nucleotides at position 1. With only four nucleotides available, this bounds the number of optima at four (position 2 gives the same bound). This bound is attained: in a maximal-correlation MI -motif (e.g., selected dimers AA, CC, GG, TT; see Appendix B.1), every neighbor of a selected dimer is non-selected, so all four selected dimers are optima.
