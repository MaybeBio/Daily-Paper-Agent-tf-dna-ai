## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript addresses a significant and timely question in transcription biology: how RNA polymerases navigate DNA-bound transcription factors within gene bodies. The experimental approach using optical tweezers is sophisticated and well-suited to the question. The authors present a compelling narrative that moves beyond simple passive waiting or mechanical eviction models toward a more nuanced mechanism involving RNAP-induced kinetic destabilization of the TF-DNA complex. The inclusion of CpG methylation as a modulator adds a potentially important regulatory dimension. However, the manuscript as provided has several critical gaps. The most significant concern is the lack of direct evidence for the proposed destabilization mechanism. The static experiments measure Egr-1 kinetics in the presence of a stalled RNAP, but the authors do not demonstrate that an actively translocating RNAP destabilizes the TF in the same way. The claim that RNAP "destabilizes" Egr-1 is inferred from kinetic measurements, but alternative explanations, such as RNAP-induced conformational changes in the DNA that indirectly affect Egr-1 binding, are not fully excluded. Additionally, the simulation model, while successful for the ZF321 orientation, explicitly fails to capture the ZF123 orientation data, and the authors attribute this to unmodeled effects on RNAP backtracking without providing a mechanistic basis. The force measurements are used to argue against mechanical displacement, but the comparison between a stalled RNAP rupture force and an actively translocating RNAP may not be directly applicable. The manuscript would be substantially strengthened by direct single-molecule observation of Egr-1 dissociation events during active transcription, rather than inference from passage times and static measurements. The writing is clear and the logical flow is strong, but the evidence as presented does not fully establish the central mechanistic claim.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers studying transcription elongation mechanisms, particularly those interested in how RNA polymerases interact with DNA-bound proteins. The work is also relevant to investigators studying transcription factor dynamics, gene body regulation, and the role of DNA methylation in transcription. The single-molecule methodology will interest biophysicists working on molecular motors and protein-DNA interactions. The findings have potential implications for understanding how gene body transcription factor binding sites might regulate elongation dynamics in a tunable manner.
- **Major strengths** The study addresses a fundamental and understudied question in transcription biology. The optical tweezers approach is technically demanding and well-executed, providing real-time measurements of RNAP passage through a defined protein-DNA complex. The authors carefully consider and test alternative models (passive waiting, mechanical eviction) before proposing their own. The inclusion of CpG methylation as a modulator of TF stability and its effect on passage is a novel and potentially important finding. The Monte Carlo simulation framework is a valuable tool for integrating kinetic measurements into a predictive model. The writing is clear, and the logical progression from observation to model is well-structured.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **Concern ID** R1-M1
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Evidence for central mechanism
  - **Claim pointer** "RNAP destabilizes the Egr-1–DNA complex over a short, structured interaction zone, increasing TF dissociation non-monotonically with distance."
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section, Figure 4, Figure S5, Figure S6
  - **Concern** The central claim that RNAP actively destabilizes the Egr-1-DNA complex is based on static measurements using stalled RNAP complexes at defined distances. The authors measure Egr-1 association and dissociation kinetics in the presence of a stalled RNAP and compare these to rates in the absence of RNAP. However, a stalled RNAP is not equivalent to an actively translocating RNAP. The conformational state of the stalled complex, its interactions with downstream DNA, and its potential to exert forces or induce DNA distortions may differ substantially from an actively translocating polymerase. The authors do not provide direct evidence that an actively translocating RNAP destabilizes Egr-1 in the same manner. The inference from static to dynamic conditions is a significant logical leap. The non-monotonic distance dependence is intriguing but is only demonstrated in the static system. It is possible that the observed kinetic changes are due to RNAP-induced DNA bending or distortion that affects Egr-1 binding indirectly, rather than a specific active destabilization mechanism. The authors should either provide direct evidence of destabilization during active transcription or temper their mechanistic claim.
  - **Why it matters** The central mechanistic conclusion of the paper rests on this claim. If the destabilization is only a property of stalled complexes and does not occur during active translocation, the proposed model for passage would be incorrect. The distinction between active destabilization and passive steric or conformational effects is critical for understanding the physical basis of the interaction.
  - **Resolution test** The authors could perform single-molecule FRET or similar experiments to directly observe Egr-1 dissociation during active transcription by RNAP. Alternatively, they could use a system where RNAP is translocating but stalled at a defined position downstream of the Egr-1 site, allowing measurement of Egr-1 kinetics while RNAP is engaged in active translocation but paused. If the destabilization is observed under these conditions, the claim would be strengthened. If not, the mechanism would need to be revised.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M2
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Model completeness and validation
  - **Claim pointer** "Monte Carlo simulations incorporating these kinetic changes recapitulate passage-time distributions."
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section, Figure 5, Figure S7, Methods section "Monte Carlo simulations of transcription"
  - **Concern** The simulation model successfully reproduces the passage-time distributions for the ZF321 orientation but explicitly fails to capture the ZF321 orientation data. The authors state that the model "did not fully account for the longest passage times observed in the ZF123 orientation" and attribute this to orientation-dependent effects on RNAP backtracking. However, no mechanistic basis for this orientation-dependent effect on backtracking is provided, and no additional modeling is presented to test this hypothesis. The model is therefore incomplete for one of the two orientations studied. The claim that the simulations "recapitulate passage-time distributions" is only partially true. The authors should either extend the model to account for the ZF123 orientation data or clearly state that the model is only applicable to one orientation. The lack of a complete model weakens the quantitative support for the proposed mechanism.
  - **Why it matters** The simulation is presented as a key validation of the proposed mechanism. If the model cannot account for the full range of experimental observations, its predictive power and the support it provides for the mechanism are limited. The orientation-dependent discrepancy suggests that important aspects of the interaction are not captured by the model.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should extend the simulation to include orientation-dependent effects on RNAP backtracking, perhaps by incorporating a distance- and orientation-dependent backtracking probability. They should then test whether this extended model can reproduce the ZF123 orientation data. If it cannot, the model's limitations should be explicitly acknowledged, and the claim of recapitulation should be qualified.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M3
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Force comparison validity
  - **Claim pointer** "RNAP-generated forces are insufficient to mechanically displace a bound Egr-1."
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section, Figure 3, Figure S4
  - **Concern** The authors compare the stall force of an actively translocating RNAP with the rupture force of Egr-1 from DNA, measured by unzipping. They conclude that since the RNAP stall force is lower than the Egr-1 rupture force, RNAP cannot mechanically displace Egr-1. However, the comparison may not be directly valid. The stall force is the maximum force an RNAP can generate against a load, but the force required to displace a protein from DNA may depend on the direction and geometry of the applied force. The unzipping geometry applies force perpendicular to the DNA helix, whereas an elongating RNAP applies force along the DNA axis. The force required to disrupt a protein-DNA interaction can be highly direction-dependent. The authors do not address this potential discrepancy. Additionally, the stall force measurement is performed on a stalled RNAP, which may not represent the force generation capability of an actively translocating RNAP encountering an obstacle. The conclusion that mechanical eviction is impossible may be premature.
  - **Why it matters** The argument against the mechanical eviction model is a key part of the logical progression toward the proposed destabilization mechanism. If the force comparison is not valid, the mechanical eviction model may not be fully excluded, and the need for an alternative mechanism is less compelling.
  - **Resolution test** The authors could measure the force required to disrupt the Egr-1-DNA complex when force is applied along the DNA axis, mimicking the geometry of an elongating RNAP. They could also measure the force generated by an RNAP as it encounters a bound Egr-1, rather than using the stall force from a different experimental configuration. If the force generated during the encounter exceeds the Egr-1 rupture force under the same geometry, the mechanical eviction model would need to be reconsidered.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M4
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Generalizability of findings
  - **Claim pointer** "These results reveal DNA-bound TFs as tunable barriers that locally shape transcription elongation."
  - **Evidence pointer** Discussion section
  - **Concern** The study uses a single transcription factor (Egr-1) and a single RNAP (E. coli RNAP) in a simplified in vitro system. The authors generalize their findings to "DNA-bound TFs" broadly, but it is unclear whether the mechanism described is specific to Egr-1 or applies to other transcription factors with different DNA-binding domains, affinities, and structures. The discussion mentions that Egr-1 is a "well-suited model" but does not provide evidence that the mechanism is general. The authors also note in the limitations that the system lacks accessory elongation factors, which are known to modulate RNAP backtracking and elongation. The claim of tunability is supported by the methylation experiments, but the broader claim that this represents a general mechanism for TF-RNAP encounters is not fully supported by the data.
  - **Why it matters** The broader significance of the work depends on whether the mechanism is general or specific to Egr-1. If the mechanism is specific, the impact on the field is more limited. The authors should be more cautious in their generalizations or provide additional evidence that the mechanism applies to other TFs.
  - **Resolution test** The authors could repeat key experiments with a different transcription factor, ideally one with a different DNA-binding domain or affinity. They could also test whether the methylation effect is observed with other TFs whose binding sites contain CpG dinucleotides. If similar results are obtained, the generalizability claim would be strengthened.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that RNAP "destabilizes" the Egr-1-DNA complex during active transcription is not directly supported by the data, as the kinetic measurements were performed with stalled RNAP complexes.
- The claim that RNAP-generated forces are insufficient for mechanical displacement relies on a comparison between stall force and rupture force measured under different geometries, which may not be valid.
- The generalization that "DNA-bound TFs" act as tunable barriers is based on a single TF (Egr-1) and may not apply to other transcription factors.
- The claim that CpG methylation "largely eliminates" the TF-dependent delay is based on a comparison to passage in the absence of TF, but the control for methylation effects on RNAP alone is not described in the provided text.
- The simulation model's failure to capture the ZF123 orientation data is acknowledged but not mechanistically explained, limiting the model's completeness.
- The biological relevance of the findings to in vivo transcription is speculative, as the system lacks chromatin, elongation factors, and other cellular components.
