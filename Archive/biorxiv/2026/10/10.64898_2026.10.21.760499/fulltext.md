## Abstract

MyD88 is a universal adaptor that connects to Toll-like receptors and interleukin-1 receptors through its Toll/IL-1R homology (TIR) domain and activates inflammatory signaling via its death domain (DD). However, how MyD88 TIR serves as the platform for these functions and if it has additional functions remain unclear. Here, we report cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of MyD88 TIR and its cancer-specific mutant L252P (previously L265P) mainly in ring and spiral forms, respectively. Both assemblies are formed by antiparallel double-filaments, using canonical TIR interfaces within each strand but unique surfaces between the strands, and position MyD88 DD at the same sides of the assemblies for self-oligomerization and pathway activation. Surprisingly, these WT or L252P filaments are bound to dsDNA using the concave sides of the rings and the spirals. In vitro assays and cellular studies establish that MyD88 directly binds dsDNA and promotes inflammatory cytokine expression, particularly pro-IL-1β, following dsDNA transfection or viral infection. Notably, these responses are unaffected by deficiency of UNC93B1, the essential chaperone required for trafficking endosomal nucleic acid-sensing TLRs. Structure-guide MyD88 mutant that disrupts dsDNA binding specifically compromises dsDNA-induced transcriptional program while maintaining TLR signaling. RNA-seq further reveals that MyD88 plays a crucial role in inducing gene expression upon dsDNA sensing and that about half of cGAS-dependent genes are also MyD88-dependent. Together, our structural, biochemical and genetic data support the findings that MyD88 directly engages with cytosolic dsDNA and cooperates with cGAS to promote the full innate immune transcriptional response.


## Main

Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and interleukin-1 receptors (IL-1Rs) are key players in the innate immune system. 1 , 2 TLRs recognize pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), while IL-1Rs detect cytokines from the IL-1 family. Despite differences in their extracellular domains, both receptors share a conserved cytosolic Toll/IL-1R homology (TIR) domain. Upon activation by PAMPs or cytokines, receptors dimerize or oligomerize, which brings the TIR domains into close proximity, creating a platform for recruiting adaptor proteins like TIRAP (also known as Mal), TRIF, TRAM, and myeloid differentiation primary response 88 (MyD88). 1 , 2 The adaptor protein MyD88 is particularly well known for its broad involvement in TLR signaling. 1 – 7 It is recruited in response to a wide array of activating signals for all TLRs except TLR3. 8 , 9 MyD88 also plays a crucial role in mediating the signaling pathways activated by cytokines IL-1, IL-18, and IL-33, which are recognized by the IL-1R family. 10

MyD88 consists of two distinct domains separated by a linker: an N-terminal death domain (DD) and a C-terminal TIR domain ( Fig. S1A ). The DD is essential for the recruitment of interleukin-1 receptor-associated kinase (IRAK) family members, particularly IRAK4, IRAK1 and IRAK2. 8 , 10 , 11 These IRAK kinases feature an N-terminal DD and a C-terminal serine/threonine kinase or pseudokinase domain. 10 , 11 Previous structural studies have elucidated the arrangement of the MyD88 DD −IRAK4 DD −IRAK2 DD complex revealing a helical structure termed the ‘Myddosome’ with a stoichiometry of 6:4:4. 12 This molecular arrangement brings the kinase domains into close proximity promoting their transphosphorylation and activation. 13 Once activated, these kinases recruit TRAF6 to the Myddosome, ultimately leading to activation of the nuclear factor κ-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) and the MAP kinase pathways and induction of a pro-inflammatory response. 8 , 9 , 14 , 15

Patients with autosomal recessive deficiency of the MyD88 gene due to deletion or missense mutations in both chromosomes are highly susceptible to pyogenic bacterial infections which can have an early onset in childhood. 16 By contrast somatic gain-of-function mutations have been detected in patients suffering from lymphomas. Notably, the L252P mutation (historically referred to as L265P) is the most frequent somatic mutation in lymphoid cancers, found in approximately 10-20% of patients with activated B-cell-like diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (ABC-DLBCL) and in 90% of patients diagnosed with Waldenström macroglobulinemia (WM). 16 – 19 The L252P mutation occurs in a β-sheet in the core of the TIR domain leading to spontaneous punctum formation, recruitment and activation of IRAK4, IRAK1 and IRAK2, and downstream pathway activation, ultimately promoting cell survival. 17 , 20 Understanding the effect of this oncogenic driver mutation, L252P, may be a good starting point for developing better therapeutics and improving patient outcomes in the future.

Several monomeric structures of MyD88 TIR have been solved by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) (PDB 2Z5V and 2JS7). 21 In addition, structures of MyD88 TIR by X-ray crystallography (PDB 4DOM and 4EO7) 22 and microcrystal electron diffraction (MicroED) (PDB 7BEQ and 7L6W) 23 revealed linear filament structures of MyD88 TIR in the crystal packing; these filament structures are similar to the basic TIR filament assembly observed in Mal 24 , AbTir 25 (TIR domain-containing protein from Acinetobacter baumannii ) and SPARTA 26 [short prokaryotic argonaute TIR-APAZ (analogue of Piwi–Argonaute-Zwille domain)]. However, it remains unclear whether these MyD88 filament structures observed from crystal packing represent the activated and signaling competent form of MyD88. Moreover, the structure of the oncogenic L252P mutant MyD88 has not yet been determined.

To address these knowledge gaps, we determined the cryo-EM structures of the wild-type (WT) and cancer-specific L252P mutant MyD88 TIR domains at resolutions of 3.6 Å and 3.4 Å, respectively. Our findings reveal that both WT and L252P TIR domains form double-filament assemblies arranged in an antiparallel fashion, but with WT organized into rings and L252P into spirals. Unexpectedly, the MyD88 TIR assemblies are bound with double-stranded DNA (dsDNA). Combined with biochemical assays, cellular experiments, and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), we revealed an unexpected DNA-binding activity of MyD88 and investigated its contribution to cytosolic DNA signaling.


## MyD88 TIR forms double-filament rings

To gain a deeper understanding of MyD88 TIR assembly, we sought to solve its structure using cryo-EM. We tested various construct boundaries and ultimately expressed the human MyD88 TIR domain (residues 155-296) fused N-terminally to an His-MBP-TEV tag in bacteria ( Figure S1B ). Gel filtration chromatography revealed a fraction of the protein eluted at the void volume with an elevated A 260 /A 280 ratio ( Figure S1C,D ). Using negative-stain EM, we observed ring-like structures with an average diameter of approximately 30 nm ( Figure S1E ).

Using this sample from the void peak, we prepared cryo-EM grids and collected a large dataset ( Figure S1F-I ). Because these rings are heterogeneous in size and shape, we picked segments of the rings for structure determination, like what we did for the structure determination of NINJ1 oligomers 27 . Two-dimensional (2D) classification of the ring segments revealed a repetitive pattern within the ring, with a fuzzy density on the convex side corresponding to the MBP tag and a thin density on the concave side ( Figure S1G ). An initial three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction revealed a double-ring assembly, and refinement with C2 symmetry resulted in a structure of this ring segment at a resolution of 3.6 Å ( Figures 1A and S1I,J , Table S1 and Video S1).

We built the nearly complete atomic model of MyD88 TIR spanning residues 161-296, revealing an arc structure of 16 subunits with two curved filaments running antiparallel to each other and a C2 symmetry axis perpendicular to the arc ( Figure 1B,C ). Sequential alignment of the hexadecameric model reveals a fully closed double-layered ring with each layer composed of 33 MyD88 TIR protomers and inner and outer diameters of 310 Å and 350 Å, respectively ( Figure 1D ). The TIR domain conformation is highly conserved with a Cα root-mean square deviation (RMSD) of 0.8 Å over 129 Cα atoms compared to the previously determined MyD88 MicroED crystal structure 23 (PDB ID: 7BEQ) ( Figure S2A ). However, in the crystal lattice of the latter, the packing-inferred straight filaments are arranged in a parallel, rather than antiparallel orientation, whereas TIR structures from other mammalian or lower species can adopt either arrangement 23 , 24 ( Figure S2B ). The antiparallel filament orientation in our MyD88 ring positions the N-termini of both rings where the MyD88 DDs are located on the same convex side to promote DD oligomerization and recruitment of downstream signaling kinases IRAK4 and IRAK2 ( Figures 1E and S2C ).

Within each filament of the ring, interactions between adjacent subunits occur via interaction of the BB loop (the loop connecting βB and αB) of one subunit with the EE surface (consisting of the βD and βE strands and the αE helix) ( Figure 1C ), which are consistent with those reported previously in the MyD88 TIR filaments observed in crystal packing. 23 The interstrand interactions in the MyD88 ring occur predominantly via the BC and CD interfaces consisting of the αB and αC helix as well as the CD loop ( Figures 1C and S4 ). By contrast, the antiparallel TIR domain assembly of human SARM1 (sterile alpha and Toll/interleukin-1 receptor motif-containing 1) engages interstrand interactions at different surfaces ( Figure S2B ).

The interstrand interactions in our MyD88 TIR structure are mainly hydrophobic interactions involving residues W205 of the αB helix, F235 and F239 of the αC helix, and I267 and F270 of the αD helix ( Figure S3 ). Moreover, the aliphatic side chains of K231 and K238 engage the neighbouring subunit and are part of the interstrand interface ( Figure S3 ). Interestingly, the S242 side chain projects into a partially hydrophobic pocket formed by F235 and F239 of the neighbouring subunit ( Figure S3 ). S242 has been reported as an inhibitory phosphorylation site for downstream NF-κB activation. 20 , 28 Based on our structure, introducing a large, negatively charged moiety at this position would create steric clashes and unfavourable interactions with the neighbouring subunit, likely destabilizing double-layered ring formation.


## The MyD88 TIR ring is bound with dsDNA

After completion of the model building, we could not assign any residues from either the MyD88 TIR or the purification tags to the thin density on the concave side of the ring assembly ( Figure 1A ). To further improve the resolution of this unidentified density, we conducted focused 3D classification using a soft mask covering this extra density ( Figure S4A ). The classification revealed considerable variability on this part of the structure, both in terms of its appearance and occupancy (Video S2). Despite this heterogeneity, we successfully isolated one class that exhibited an improved density with more distinct features ( Figure S4A ). Upon closer examination, the shape and dimensions of this density strongly resembled a helical shape that is consistent with the characteristic dimensions of double-stranded DNA ( Figure S4B ). This finding aligns with the high A 260 /A 280 absorbance ratio we observed in gel filtration chromatography ( Figure S1C ), which is indicative of the presence of nucleic acids. We also loaded and separated an aliquot used to prepare cryo-EM grids on an agarose gel and visualized a smear of nucleic acids; if they were dsDNA species, they mainly represented sizes of around ∼750 bp and larger, likely genomic fragments at the top of the gel ( Figure S4C ). Furthermore, our cryo-EM micrographs revealed the presence of thin filamentous structures that shared similar dimensions to the additional density observed in our reconstructions ( Figures S4D,E ). We manually picked these thin filamentous particles and solved their structure at ∼6 Å resolution ( Figure S4F ). The resulting map allowed us to unambiguously fit a model of double-stranded DNA, with clear evidence of the expected helical pitch ( Figure S4F ).

We fitted a 60 bp dsDNA model into the additional thin density observed in our MyD88 TIR reconstruction ( Figure 1F ), which aligns along the inner surface of the double-layered TIR ring. The concave surface of the antiparallel MyD88 TIR ring features positively charged patches primarily contributed by two lysine residues from each subunit, K261 and K262 ( Figure 1G ), whose side chains protrude like talons to engage and grip the dsDNA phosphate backbone ( Figure 1H,I ). Comparative analysis of surface charge distributions among human TIR domains revealed that MyD88 exhibits a uniquely positive surface charge, unlike other mammalian TIR domains that typically display negatively charged surfaces or concentrate positive charges at TIR–TIR interaction sites ( Figure S5 ). Based on the location of the dsDNA density, the lysine side chains appear positioned to interact exclusively with the phosphate backbone ( Figure S6A ). Thirty-two lysine residues could engage approximately 60 bp of dsDNA, corresponding to a spacing of ∼28 Å per four lysines. In the double-layered ring, regularly spaced positively charged patches along the filament lengths grip dsDNA from two sides. These intriguing data suggest the possibility of a specialized role of MyD88 in DNA recognition not shared by other TIR domains.

A closer inspection of our micrographs revealed extended stretches of dsDNA protruding from the lateral edges of the MyD88 TIR rings ( Figure S4D ), suggesting that DNA sequences exceeding ∼240 bp cannot be fully enclosed within a single ring. This observation implies that while each MyD88 TIR ring can accommodate ∼240 bp of dsDNA, any additional length could extend beyond the ring structure from either end ( Figure S4E ). Such a configuration may be particularly relevant during bacterial or viral infections, where longer DNA fragments are common. Moreover, it is tempting to speculate that multiple MyD88 TIR rings could assemble along a single dsDNA molecule, potentially modulating the immune response in a length-dependent manner.


## MyD88 TIR binds dsDNA in vitro

To gain deeper insights into the MyD88–dsDNA interaction, we performed electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSAs) to assess the binding affinities using dsDNA of varying lengths. MBP-MyD88 TIR , purified from the later, presumably monomeric size-exclusion fractions, was incubated at 37 °C with Cy3-labelled dsDNA fragments of 30, 45, 60, and 75 base pairs (bp) derived from Herpes Simplex Virus-1 (HSV-1) sequences, and the complexes were resolved by native PAGE. MBP-MyD88 TIR shifted dsDNA in a concentration-dependent manner ( Figures 2A and S6B ). The binding affinities for the different DNA lengths were broadly similar, all showing evidence of cooperativity as indicated by the fitted Hill coefficients ( Figures 2B,C ). Competition EMSAs using unlabeled DNA further confirmed the specificity of the interaction and excluded non-specific binding to the Cy3 fluorophore ( Figures 2D,E and S6C ).

To determine whether dsDNA binding is mediated by K261 and K262, as suggested by the structure, we introduced charge-reversal mutations in MBP-MyD88 TIR (K261E/K262E; hereafter referred to as K2E). The K2E mutation completely abrogated the MyD88–dsDNA interaction in the EMSA ( Figure 2F ), supporting the structural model of dsDNA engagement. Additionally, to rule out any influence of the MBP tag on the interaction, we performed fluorescence size-exclusion chromatography (FSEC) using His-tagged recombinant MyD88 TIR and Cy3-labelled HSV-60bp dsDNA. In the presence of MyD88 TIR , Cy3-HSV-60bp dsDNA eluted in earlier fractions, confirming the formation of a complex between MyD88 and dsDNA ( Figure 2G ). Finally, recombinant full-length His-tagged MyD88, despite low yield, formed similar ring structures under negative-stain EM ( Figure 2H ) and retained the ability to interact with IRAK4 ( Figure S6D ), supporting the functionality of MyD88 rings in absence of a large affinity tag.


## Cytosolic dsDNA activates MyD88-dependent cytokine expression

MyD88 is primarily known for its role as an adaptor protein for TLR- and IL-1R-dependent inflammatory gene expression. 4 These pathways involve activation of IRAK family kinases, which stimulates the E3 (ubiquitin ligase) activity of TRAF6, leading to ubiquitin-dependent activation of the NF-κB pathway. 29 Given the direct MyD88–dsDNA interaction, we asked whether dsDNA could trigger MyD88 activation to initiate NF-κB transcriptional regulation. To investigate these questions, we first examined the mRNA and the secreted protein levels of TNFα, a classical inflammatory gene. 30 , 31 We found that MyD88 is required to increase Tnf mRNA level and TNFα protein secretion in mouse immortalized bone marrow-derived macrophages (iBMDMs) upon transfection of HSV-60bp dsDNA ( Figures 3A,B ). By contrast, knockout (KO) of cGAS, the known cytosolic DNA sensor for activating type-I interferon gene 32 , 33 , minimally affected TNFα expression (< 2-fold), and pretreatment with the potent cGAS inhibitor G140 34 in MyD88 KO iBMDMs had no additional effects ( Figures 3A,B and S7A ). When we increased the dsDNA lengths to 2 kb and 7 kb, TNFα expression was further increased in comparison with HSV-60bp dsDNA, which was however equally dependent on cGAS and MyD88 ( Figure S7B ). Thus, dsDNA-induced expression of the inflammatory TNFα depends on both cGAS and MyD88.

We next tested dsDNA-induced pro-IL-1β expression. Transfection of HSV-1 dsDNA caused pro-IL-1β expression in a length-dependent manner from 30bp to 90bp ( Figure 3C ). MyD88 KO almost abolished this dsDNA-triggered pro-IL-1β expression ( Figure 3C ). cGAS KO diminished but did not abolish pro-IL-1β expression ( Figure 3D ). When the dsDNA lengths were increased to 2 and 7 kilo bp (kb), MyD88 KO abolished, while cGAS KO had no effect on, the dsDNA-triggered pro-IL-1β expression ( Figure 3E ). Both MyD88 KO and cGAS KO dampened IKKα/IKKβ phosphorylation upon DNA transfection ( Figures 3C,D ,F). Transfection with poly I:C, dsRNA of similar lengths (1.5 kb - 8 kb), did not activate expression of the NF-κB-dependent gene pro-IL-1β in WT, MyD88 KO, or cGAS KO iBMDMs ( Figure 3E ), suggesting that MyD88 does not sense dsRNA.

As controls, we tested IFN-β gene expression by mRNA and secretion levels in MyD88 KO or cGAS KO iBMDMs. As expected 35 , IFN-β mRNA and protein levels were completely abolished by cGAS KO upon transfection of two different lengths of HSV-1 dsDNA or kb long dsDNA ( Figures 3G,H and S7C ). Surprisingly, MyD88 KO also significantly dampened the levels of Ifnb1 mRNA and IFN-β secretion, and G140 pre-treatment further lowered Ifnb1 mRNA ( Figures 3G,H and S7C ). Consistent with a previously implicated role of MyD88 in inhibiting p-STING degradation, total STING and p-STING levels in MyD88 KO iBMDMs were both reduced upon transfection of HSV-1 dsDNA ( Figure 3C ). By contrast, in cGAS KO iBMDMs, there was abolished p-STING but no observable total STING reduction ( Figure 3D ). Together with the RNA-seq data (see below), our findings suggest that the reduced IFN-β levels in MyD88 KO cells may result from dual roles of MyD88 in promoting Ifnb1 transcription and preventing p-STING degradation.


## Inflammatory cytokine expression by HSV-1 infection is dependent on MyD88

To examine our finding in a more physiological setting, we carried out HSV-1 infection of primary BMDMs obtained from WT and MyD88 KO mice and analysed TNFα and IFN-β expression. HSV-1 infection induced TNFα expression in primary WT BMDMs, whereas this response was significantly reduced in MyD88 KO BMDMs, highlighting the importance of MyD88 in mediating the inflammatory cytokine response ( Figure 3I ). Inhibition of cGAS by the small molecule G140 further reduced HSV-1-induced TNFα expression in MyD88-deficient BMDMs, suggesting that cGAS and MyD88 function in parallel or converging pathways to promote TNFα expression upon HSV-1 infection ( Figure 3I ). By contrast, IFN-β expression by HSV-1 infection of BMDMs was not affected by the absence of MyD88 ( Figure 3J ), unlike transfection of short HSV-1 dsDNA in iBMDMs ( Figure 3H ). To further dissect the differential regulation of IFN-β versus NF-κB-dependent gene expression in response to HSV-1 infection, we examined well-established target genes of each pathway. MyD88 deficiency markedly impaired the transcription of NF-κB-dependent proinflammatory cytokine genes ( Il1b , Il6 , and Tnf ), whereas the expression of IRF3-dependent genes such as Cxcl10 and Ifnb remained unaffected ( Figure 3K ).

We also used the HSV-1 strain 7134, which lacks ICP0, a protein known to downregulate inflammatory response. 36 This strain can induce cytokine responses at a lower multiplicity of infection (MOI) and has been used to examine the role of cGAS–cGAMP signaling in antiviral defense. 35 With HSV-1 7134 at an MOI of 10 in primary WT and Myd88 KO BMDMs, TNFα and IFN-β were both robustly elevated to higher levels than with WT HSV-1 at MOI of 100 ( Figures S7D,E and 3I,J ). While TNFα activation was highly dependent on MyD88 but not cGAS ( Figure S7D ), IFN-β activation was independent of MyD88 ( Figure S7E ). These data supported that HSV-1-induced inflammatory cytokine expression, but not interferon expression, is highly dependent on MyD88.


## Unc93b1 deficiency does not dampen inflammatory cytokine expression

Because TLR9, which recognizes ssDNA entities with motifs containing the unmethylated CG sequence (CpG), uses MyD88 for its signaling 37 , we tested if TLR9 was responsible for the MyD88-dependent cytokine expression upon dsDNA transfection. We first used the TLR9 inhibitor ODN2088. While ODN2088 inhibited activation of TLR9 by the agonist ODN1826 ( Figure S7F ), it failed to inhibit dsDNA-induced MyD88 activation ( Figure S7G ). It is known that endosomal nucleic acid-sensing Toll-like receptors (TLRs), specifically TLR3 (dsRNA), TLR7-8 (ssRNA), and TLR9 rely on the membrane protein UNC93B1 for their trafficking, stability, and signaling. 38 – 40 Because of the availability of Unc93b1 KO RAW 264.7 monocytes/macrophages 41 ( Figure S7H ), we tested if dsDNA-induced inflammatory cytokine expression is dependent on UNC93B1. Production of TNF a and Il1b upon dsDNA transfection showed no difference in control versus Unc93b1 KO RAW 264.7 cells ( Figures 3L,M and S7I ). As a control, we also analysed Ifnb1 and Cxcl10 , two genes that are cGAS-dependent, and showed lack of change by Unc93b1 deficiency ( Figure S7I ). These results supported that dsDNA transfection-induced inflammatory cytokine production is not dependent on nucleic acid-sensing TLRs such as TLR9.


## DNA binding is required for MyD88’s response to cytosolic DNA but not in TLR signaling

To investigate the direct interaction between cytosolic DNA and MyD88 in cells, we transfected dsDNA into the previously generated iBMDMs expressing GFP-tagged endogenous MyD88. 31 DNA transfection induced the formation of punctate MyD88 structures, which were not observed in buffer-transfected control cells ( Figure 4A ). In agreement with our findings, MyD88 punctum structures colocalized with dsDNA ( Figure 4A ), implicating a direct role of MyD88 in sensing and responding to cytosolic DNA. We further used stimulated emission depletion microscopy (STED) to observe in more detail the micron-sized puncta in these cells. Similar to Myddosome puncta formed by TLR activation 31 , dsDNA-induced Myddosome formation involved barrel-shaped distribution of both DNA and MyD88 in these puncta ( Figure S8A ).

To demonstrate that the observed effects from MyD88-dependent cytosolic dsDNA sensing were driven specifically from the MyD88–DNA interaction, we generated Myd88 KO iBMDMs expressing the MyD88 K2E mutant with impaired DNA binding ( Figure 2F and S8B ). While WT MyD88 rescued dsDNA-induced Tnf and Il1b mRNA levels in Myd88 -deficient iBMDMs, the K2E mutant was unable to initiate cytokine expression in response to cytosolic dsDNA ( Figures 4B,C ), indicating that direct MyD88–DNA interaction is essential for mediating dsDNA-induced proinflammatory cytokine expression. We also imaged these iBMDMs using anti-dsDNA and anti-MyD88 antibodies. Upon dsDNA transfection, numerous puncta with colocalized dsDNA and MyD88 were observed in WT MyD88-reconstituted cells. By contrast, few puncta were seen in K2E-reconstituted cells after dsDNA transfection, which is visibly similar to buffer-transfected cells ( Figure 4D ), arguing that WT MyD88, but not K2E, caused DNA to aggregate. Quantification of the images confirmed the markedly reduced punctum formation when the K2E MyD88 was used ( Figure 4E ). By contrast, we did not observe any significant difference between WT and K2E mutant iBMDMs in pro-IL-1β expression upon TLR activation: Pam3CSK4 for TLR1/2, lipopolysaccharides (LPS) for TLR4, R848 for TLR7/8, and ODN1826 for TLR9 ( Figures 4F,G ). Thus, dsDNA-binding residues of MyD88 are selectively required for DNA sensing, effectively decoupling TLR- and DNA-induced MyD88 signaling.

To gain more mechanistic insights in the antiparallel orientation of the TIR filaments, we reconstituted Myd88 KO iBMDMs with MyD88 interstrand interface mutants. As expected, most of the interstrand mutations abolished the MyD88’s ability to trigger dsDNA-induced Il6 expression ( Figure S8C ). We further investigated the role of MyD88 in dsDNA-induced cell death. Our results indicated that the release of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) following transfection with different lengths of HSV-1 dsDNA was unaffected by Myd88 deficiency, while NLRP3 activation by LPS and nigericin was dependent on MyD88 due to the requirement for LPS priming ( Figure S8D ). These data argued that MyD88 does not play a role in dsDNA-mediated cell death.


## RNA-seq reveals a broad contribution of MyD88 in cytosolic dsDNA transcriptional response

To dissect which signaling pathways are regulated by MyD88 and to what extent they intersect with the cGAS-STING pathway, we performed bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on WT, Myd88 KO, cGas KO, and cGas/Myd88 dKO iBMDMs under buffer, HSV-60bp or poly dA:dT dsDNA transfection ( Figure S9A , Table S2, S3). Principal component analysis (PCA) of RNA species showed close clustering of the triplicates ( Figure S9B ). Evaluation of upregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) suggested that like cGas , Myd88 is a major contributor to gene expression in response to dsDNA with 643/1576 genes and 699/1314 genes dampened in Myd88 KO among the total of upregulated genes in WT by poly dA:dT transfection and HSV-60bp transfection, respectively ( Figure 5A ). Of the two RNA-seq datasets using poly dA:dT versus HSV-60bp, the shorter HSV-60bp transfection-induced gene expression appeared to be more dependent on MyD88, with more than half (699 genes) of upregulated genes (1314 genes) in WT by DNA transfection dampened in Myd88 KO ( Figure 5A ). cGas / Myd88 KO further dampened the dsDNA-induced gene upregulation ( Figure 5A ). Thus, although cGas was necessary for most dsDNA-responsive genes 35 , it is not sufficient for about half of the upregulated genes, as shown by the Myd88 KO phenotypes.

Plotting of log fold change (LFC) for upregulated DEGs in WT iBMDMs (dsDNA versus buffer transfection) against LFC in Myd88 KO iBMDMs highlighted genes whose upregulation was dampened by Myd88 deficiency ( Figures 5B,C ). Transcription factor analysis of dampened genes in Myd88 KO revealed overwhelming associations with RelA, Nfkb1, and Jun ( Figures 5D and S9C ), which are known transcription factors in the MyD88 pathway. Pathway analysis of the same downregulated genes revealed a dominant enrichment for TNFα signaling via NF-κB and inflammatory response ( Figure 5E ). For HSV-60bp dsDNA transfection, IFN- γ and IFN- a induction was also dependent, at least partly, on MyD88 ( Figure S9D ). A Venn diagram of the knockout data revealed that nearly half of all cGAS-dependent genes are co-dependent on MyD88 ( Figure 5F ). This subset includes Il1b and Tnf , which were consistently validated in this study, as well as many additional genes. These results argued for extensive collaboration between cGAS and MyD88 in DNA-sensing induced gene transcription.


## Structure of the cancer specific mutant L252P MyD88 TIR

Having established a biochemical and structural protocol for the WT MyD88 TIR domain, we extended this work by investigating the cancer-specific TIR domain mutant L252P. Gel filtration chromatography showed that majority of the mutant protein eluted broadly over a wide range of elution volumes, suggesting the presence of various oligomeric states ( Figure S10A ). In parallel, we observed a significant amount of protein eluting at the void volume, indicative of large assemblies ( Figures S10B,C ). Like for WT MyD88 TIR , the A 260 /A 280 absorbance ratio of the void volume fraction was elevated, pointing to the presence of co-purified nucleic acids. Negative-stain EM revealed long, spiral-like filaments for the void fraction sample ( Figure S10D ), unlike closed rings for WT MyD88 TIR . We then determined the L252P spiral segment structure at 3.4 Å resolution ( Figures 6A,B , S10E-H and Table S1). Like WT MyD88 TIR , the L252P cryo-EM map contains an additional thin density on the concave side of the spiral segment, which suggests a similar capability of the L252P mutant to bind dsDNA.

The overall oligomeric assembly of the mutant is similar as that of the WT TIR ring. The L252P TIR domain arranges in a double-layered filament with antiparallel orientation of the individual strands. We also placed a model of HSV-60bp dsDNA into the additional density on the concave side of the spiral ( Figure 6B ), which is the exact same interface used for interaction observed in the WT. When the maps of the L252P mutant are sequentially aligned, a spiral structure is observed with a width of ∼350 Å and a height for one complete turn of ∼430 Å ( Figure 6C ), highly reminiscent with the appearance on our cryo-EM grids ( Figure S10E ).

The overall conformation of the WT and L252P MyD88 TIR structures are highly similar, with an RMSD of 0.703 Å over 129 Cα atoms, indicating that differences may rest in detail ( Figure 6D ). A closer look revealed a shift of the CD loop (residues P245–R251) of L252P, which is displaced by approximately 6.4 Å from the TIR domain core of the WT MyD88 towards the interstrand interface ( Figure 6D-F and Video S3). Within the CD loop, the shift also causes a 180° flip or switch of the K250 and R251 side chains ( Figure 6E,F ). The flipped K250 side chain forms new hydrogen bonds with the backbone carbonyl oxygen atoms of I212 and E213 in the critical αB helix ( Figure 6D,F ). We propose that K250-mediated hydrogen bonds stabilize the αB helix and shifts the equilibrium towards oligomerization for the L252P mutant. A previous study 42 reporting molecular dynamics (MD) simulations on the L252P MyD88 TIR domain suggested that the L252P mutation promotes homo-oligomerization by reducing αC helix flexibility by a salt bridge formed by residues D225 and K258. However, we did not observe this interaction in either the WT or L252P structures. Instead, we propose that the L252P mutation restrains the conformational flexibility of the TIR domain by stabilizing the αB helix via K250, which might potentially also impact the BB loop conformation. Interestingly, the CD loop has been reported to undergo post-translational modification, specifically phosphorylation at the activating residue S244. 43 , 44 This may highlight the important role of the CD loop in allosterically controlling MyD88 TIR homo-oligomerization.

Overlay of the WT MyD88 TIR ring structure and the L252P mutant spiral structure uncovers subtle rearrangement of the subunit interfaces due to the different CD loop conformations, which drives the transition from a closed ring to a spiral-like structure ( Figure 6G ). When one subunit of the WT ring (P1) is structurally aligned with that of the L252P spiral, the next subunit (P2) within the same filament and P1’ and P2’ subunits in the opposing filament all twist slightly at the four-subunit junction as if a “gear” there has been rotated ( Figure 6H ). Thus, by modulating both intrastrand and interstrand interactions, the small shift in L252P, repeated across protomers in the filament, leads to the formation of a spring-like structure instead of the closed ring seen in the WT (Video S4).

We wondered if autoactivation of L252P is associated with DNA binding. To address this question, we imaged MyD88 KO iBMDMs reconstituted with L252P. Strikingly but consistent with autoactivation, L252P iBMDMs transfected with buffer already displayed spontaneous MyD88 puncta, which colocalized with cytosolic dsDNA ( Figure 6I ). Transfection with DNA further enhanced MyD88 and dsDNA puncta in L252P iBMDMs ( Figure 6I ). These data support, although not demonstrate, a role of endogenous cytosolic DNA (e.g. from DNA damage or even cell division) in L252P activation.


## Discussion

Our findings uncover a previously unrecognized role of MyD88, revealing that this canonical adaptor may engage directly with cytosolic dsDNA that activates innate immunity independent of TLR engagement or signaling. Our high-resolution cryo-EM structures of the WT MyD88 TIR ring and the L252P spiral both reveal bound dsDNA. A previous study 45 has reported that a plant TIR binds dsDNA to execute its function as a cAMP/cGMP synthetase. We demonstrated direct dsDNA binding of MyD88 TIR in vitro . Similarly, the mammalian TIR domain-containing protein SARM1 has been shown to directly bind dsDNA via its TIR domain, triggering its NAD + -degradation activity to promote cell death and chemotherapy-induced neuropathy. 46 The DNA-binding defective MyD88 mutant K2E is incapable of DNA transfection-induced inflammatory cytokine expression while still capable of mediating TLR activation. Similarly, residues mediating the assembly of the double-layered TIR ring are required for dsDNA-induced pro-inflammatory cytokine expression, establishing the importance of the observed structure ( Figure 7 ).

Our unbiased RNA-seq experiments provided strong genetic evidence that MyD88 is a major contributor to transcriptional regulation induced by cytosolic DNA, controlling upregulation of genes in many pathways related to immune activation and host defense. Quite surprisingly, the number of DEGs cooperatively regulated by MyD88 is comparable to that observed with cGAS, the established cytosolic DNA sensor. 32 , 33 Among all genes upregulated by dsDNA, most of them are co-regulated by MyD88 and cGAS, while the remaining are regulated by either MyD88 or cGAS. Generally, cGAS and MyD88 are most prominent for their roles in interferon pathway genes and inflammatory pathway genes, respectively. Given the co-dependence of most cytosolic DNA-induced genes on MyD88 and cGAS, it is tempting to speculate that stimulation of antitumor immunity requires activation of both cGAS-STING and MyD88 pathways. Conversely, suppressing autoimmunity might need to inhibit both cGAS-STING and MyD88 pathways.

MyD88 is ubiquitously expressed in different cell types and is evolutionarily ancient with conservation across a wide range of metazoans. It has been noted that cytosolic dsDNA can also activate NF- κ B, one of the most ancient and conserved immune pathways in animals, as well as the MAP kinase pathway, in normal and cancer cells but with unclear mechanisms. 33 While many more details need to be resolved, our data are consistent with an intertwined relationship between cGAS and MyD88.

Our experimental data implicate a mechanistic model for how cytosolic dsDNA present during bacterial or viral infections or under sterile cellular stress activates the MyD88 signaling pathway independent of TLR signals using double-layered ring structures ( Figure 7 ). We speculate that the kind of MyD88 assembly is also induced by TLR or IL-1R activation, which is supported by a recent study reporting analogous WTMyD88 TIR ring and cylindrical filament structures formed in vitro without dsDNA. 47 . In addition, we uncovered that Myddosome puncta formed by MyD88 and dsDNA are also barrel shaped, like the micron-sized puncta of Myddosome involved in TLR signaling. 31 We surmise that the much smaller MyD88 rings or spirals could be the building blocks for these larger barrels in TLR, IL-1R, and DNA-induced MyD88 pathways, likely by condensate formation or other mechanisms.

Our study also provided a structural rationale of how the L252P mutation, frequently observed in a subset of lymphoma patients, triggers autoactivation of the MyD88 signaling pathway ( Figure 7 ). Our data suggest that this autoactivation is likely dependent on dsDNA. Because lymphoma cells or other cancer cells may have more DNA damage or exhibit other features for cytosolic exposure to dsDNA, spontaneous NF-κB activation may occur in these cells, leading to survival signaling and cancer promotion. Collectively, our work highlights an unexpected role of MyD88 in dsDNA signaling and implicates the molecular architecture for MyD88-mediated pathway in TLR/IL-1R signaling, dsDNA sensing, and cancer.


## Methods


## Construct expression and purification

For structural studies, the human MyD88 TIR domain (155-296) was cloned into a pDB.His.MBP vector. His-MyD88 TIR domain and full length His-MyD88 were cloned into a pet28a vector. All mutations were introduced using a Q5 polymerase mutagenesis protocol. The construct was transformed into BL21-CodonPlus(DE3)-RIPL cells and plated on LB-Agar supplemented with kanamycin (30 µg/mL). A single colony was picked and grown overnight in Luria broth (LB) media supplemented with kanamycin as a pre-culture. For expression, LB media supplemented with kanamycin was inoculated with 1% pre-culture and grown to an OD 600 of 0.6–0.8. The culture was then incubated for 30 minutes at 4 °C before adding 1 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) to induce expression overnight at 18 °C. Bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation at 3,000 g for 20 minutes at 4 °C. The pellet was resuspended in Buffer A (40 mM HEPES pH 7.0, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM imidazole). Lysis was performed by sonication (1 second on, 5 seconds off, 3 minutes total on, 42% power, Branson) and subsequently centrifuged at 40,000 g for 45 minutes at 4°C. The soluble fraction was incubated with Ni-NTA resin and purified according to the manufacturer’s recommendation. Elution fractions containing MyD88 protein were concentrated using an Amicon Ultra 50 kDa MW cutoff and loaded on a Superose 6 Increase (Cytiva) size exclusion chromatography (SEC) column in SEC buffer (40 mM HEPES pH 7.0, 150 mM NaCl). Protein quality was assessed after each purification step by SDS-PAGE.


## Negative stain grid preparation and analysis

A 10 µL sample of the SEC void fraction containing MyD88 was loaded onto a glow-discharged negative stain grid (Formvar/Carbon Film 10/1 nm on Square 400 mesh) for 60 seconds, and excess protein was blotted onto filter paper. Grids were washed twice with Buffer A and stained with a 1% phosphotungstic Acid (PTA) solution for 30 seconds, blotted, and air-dried for a few minutes. The grids were imaged on a JEOL JEM1400 Transmission Electron Microscope operating at 120 keV.


## Cryo-EM grid preparation and data collection

The purified MyD88 TIR WT rings and L252P spirals from the void fractions were concentrated to 1 mg/mL and 2 mg/mL, respectively. The concentrated samples (4 µL) were loaded onto a glow-discharged Quantifoil (R 1.2/1.3 Au 400-mesh, Electron Microscopy Sciences), blotted for 3–5 seconds under 100% humidity at 4 °C, and plunged into liquid ethane with a Mark IV Vitrobot (ThermoFisher). All grids were screened before collection for ice thickness, particle quality, and distribution. WT samples were collected at the National Cancer Institute Cryo-EM facility, with a Titan Krios microscope (ThermoFisher) at an acceleration voltage of 300 keV equipped with a BioQuantum K3 Imaging Filter (slit width 20 eV), and a K3 direct electron detector (Gatan) operating in counting mode at 105,000× magnification (0.835Å pixel size). The L252P samples were collected at the S2C2 Stanford-SLAC Cryo-EM facility with a Titan Krios microscope (ThermoFisher) at an acceleration voltage of 300 keV equipped with a SelectrisX energy filter (slit width 20 eV), and a Falcon 4i direct electron detector (ThermoFisher) operating in counting mode at 105,000× magnification (0.95Å pixel size).


## Cryo-EM data processing

All data were processed using support for the infrastructure from SBGrid Consortium. 48 Both data sets for WT MyD88 TIR and L252P MyD88 TIR were processed similarly. The raw movies were imported into CryoSPARC 49 version 4.5.3. Beam-induced motion was corrected using Patch Motion, and contrast transfer function (CTF) parameters were estimated with PatchCTF. Images with an estimated CTF resolution worse than 6 Å were discarded, leaving 7,946 good images for the WT MyD88 TIR and 32,194 good images for the L252P MyD88 TIR .

For WT MyD88 TIR , particle coordinates were identified using a custom-trained Topaz 50 model, resulting in the extraction of 3,792,682 particles with a box size of 400x400 and Fourier-cropped to 100x100 pixels. After several rounds of 2D classification, 391,591 particles with features characteristic of the MyD88 TIR ring segment were selected for further processing. An initial model was generated from the selected particles, and two junk models were created from discarded particles. These models served as references for multiple rounds of heterogeneous refinement (k=3, number of final full iterations=20). After each round, particles in the good class were selected for further rounds of heterogenous refinement, continuing until no more junk classes remained.

A total of 132,657 particles were re-extracted with a 400x400 box size and sequentially refined using homogeneous and heterogeneous refinement with C2 symmetry applied (window dataset (real space) false , minimize over per particle scale true ), resulting in a 3.7 Å reconstruction. Following global and local CTF refinement, duplicate particle removal (separation distance < 20 Å), and 2D classification, 90,711 particles were further refined using non-uniform refinement to yield a 3.7 Å map with improved quality. Local refinement with a mask focused on the central octamer (2 layers of 4 subunits each) and C2 symmetry improved the resolution to 3.6 Å. To enhance the DNA density, 3D classification (k=30) with a DNA-focused mask produced a lower-resolution map with better DNA density. The final map was automatically sharpened in CryoSPARC using a soft mask and a B-factor of -105.8 Å 2 .

For the L252P MyD88 TIR dataset, 6,949,592 particles were initially extracted with a box size of 400x400 and Fourier-cropped to 100x100 pixels. After several rounds of 2D classification, 230,088 particles displaying features of the MyD88 TIR ring segment were selected for further processing through heterogeneous refinement and non-uniform refinement. Finally, 78,342 particles were refined locally with a soft mask around the central octamer and C2 symmetry applied, resulting in a 3.4 Å reconstruction. The final map was automatically sharpened in CryoSPARC using a soft mask and a B-factor of -100.5 Å 2 .

To process free DNA observed on the grid, we utilized the WT dataset. Initially, particle coordinates were determined using filament tracer. A total of 7,636,665 particles were extracted with a box size of 300x300 pixels, Fourier-cropped to 100x100 pixels, and subjected to multiple rounds of 2D classification (k=100). From these, 1,674,497 high-quality particles were selected for initial model generation. As described above, two junk models were generated from the discarded particles, serving as references for subsequent rounds of heterogeneous refinement. Subsequently, 109,455 particles were re-extracted with a box size of 300x300 pixels and refined using helical refinement, without applying any symmetry constraints. This iterative process resulted in a reconstruction with a resolution of approximately 6 Å. The final map was automatically sharpened in CryoSPARC using a soft mask and a B-factor of -100 Å 2 .


## Model building and figure preparation

To model the WT and L252P MyD88 TIR structures, the MicroED structure of MyD88 TIR (PDB ID: 7BEQ) 23 was fitted as rigid body into the respective map using ChimeraX. 51 A starting model for the dsDNA was generated by inputting a 60 bp herpes simplex virus 1 (HSV-60bp) reverse and forward strand DNA sequence into Alphafold3 52 , and fitted into the MyD88 TIR maps using ChimeraX. 51 The models were iteratively refined in iSOLDE 53 and Phenix 54 until convergence was reached. Model validity was assessed in Phenix using MolProbity 55 and EM Ringer score. 56 ChimeraX was used to generate structural representations.


## Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA)

DNA oligos were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT). Forward primers were modified by the addition of a Cy3 fluorescent probe. Forward and reverse primers were resuspended in 10 mM HEPES pH 7.0 and 50 mM NaCl, mixed to a 1:1 molar ratio and heated at 95 °C for 3 minutes in a heat block. The heat block was then left to cool down to room temperature allowing the formation of dsDNA.

Recombinant MBP-MyD88 TIR monomers at the indicated concentrations were incubated at 37 °C for 2 hours with 0.5 nM Cy3 labelled dsDNA in 40 mM HEPES pH 7.0, 150 mM NaCl, 0.2% BSA, and 5% glycerol. Bis-Tris 3-12% polyacrylamide electrophoresis gel (NativePAGE™) were pre-ran for 45 minutes at 4 °C in 0.5x running buffer, and the reactions were then loaded and ran for 75 minutes. Gels were quickly washed in running buffer and imaged by Cy3 fluorescence using a Typhoon™ laser scanner.


## Fluorescence size-exclusion chromatography (FSEC)

100nM of recombinant His-MyD88 TIR monomers were incubated at 37 °C for 1 hours with 20 nM Cy3 labelled dsDNA in 40 mM HEPES pH 7.0, 150 mM NaCl, and 5% glycerol. Sample were analysed by FSEC.


## Cell culture

Wild-type, cGAS knockout and MyD88 knockout immortalized bone marrow-derived macrophages and L929 cells were kindly provided by Dr. Jonathan Kagan. Endogenously GFP-tagged MyD88 expressing iBMDM cells were previously described 31 and also kindly provided by Dr. Jonathan Kagan. All cells were cultured in DMEM containing 10% heat-inactivated and sterile-filtered fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin and streptomycin, and supplements of 2 mM L-glutamine and 1 mM sodium pyruvate. All cell lines were grown under sterile and standard cell culture conditions (humidified atmosphere, 5% CO 2 ) and routinely tested for mycoplasma. All cells were passaged 1:10 every 2 days to prevent overcrowding and media acidification.


## Mice and bone-marrow-derived macrophage (BMDM) differentiation

MyD88 -/- knockout mice model was previously described. 57 C57BL/6J mice were used as a control. All mice were maintained in a pathogen-free animal facility at the Boston Children’s Hospital. For obtaining the bone marrow-derived macrophages, sex and age matched 6- to 12-week-old mice were selected for wild-type and MyD88 -/- mice. Primary bone marrow-derived macrophages from mice were cultivated 7 days in DMEM supplemented with 10%FBS, 30% L929-conditioned media, 1% penicillin and streptomycin and 1% non-essential amino acids. The differentiation medium was replenished every 2-3 days and differentiation was verified by double F4/80 and CD11b double staining by flow cytometry.


## Intracellular nucleic acid delivery

Wild-type, cGAS or MyD88 knockout iBMDMs were seeded into 6-well plates at 1.5x10 6 cells/well density 24 hours prior to the delivery of the respective nucleic acids. Indicated lengths of dsDNA or poly I:C (high molecular weight, cat.no. tlrl-pic-5) were prepared at 0.1 mg/mL concentration. Double-stranded DNA of 2000 and 7000 bp was amplified by PCR from a custom endotoxin-free pCDNA 3.1 vector. Cells were transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, cat.no. L3000001) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 2.5 µg of respective nucleic acid was diluted in 125 µl of OPTIMEM. For each reaction, 5 µl of P3000 reagent was then added to the mixture. In a separate tube, 7.5 µl of Lipofectamine 3000 was diluted in 125 µl of OPTIMEM. After 5 minutes incubation at room temperature, nucleic acid-containing mixture was added to the lipofectamine and incubated for 15 minutes at room temperature. Nucleic acid-lipofectamine complexes were then added to the cell media in a drop-wise manner.


## HSV-1 infection

On the final day of differentiation, primary BMDMs were seeded into 12 well plates at 1x10 6 cells/well density. Cells were infected with HSV-1 KOS strain (HSV_KOS) 58 at 100 MOI or with HSV-1 mutant 7134 strain (HSV_ΔICP0) 59 at 10 MOI in DMEM plain media for 18 hours,. Supernatants were collected for ELISA and the remaining cells harvested for subsequent RNA extraction.


## Cell stimulations

Cells were harvested 6 hours after nucleic acid delivery. The two-step activation of NLRP3 inflammasome was performed as previously described. 60 Briefly, iBMDMs were seeded to 6-well plate at 1.5 x 10 6 /well density 24 hour prior to the treatment. Cells were primed with 1 µg/mL of LPS (Invivogen, cat.no. tlrl-pb5lps) for 4 hours and stimulated with 20 µM of nigericin (Sigma-Aldrich, cat.no. N7143) for 30 minutes. For TLR activation, cells were stimulated with the following TLR1/2 (1 μg/ml Pam3CSK4), TLR7/8 (1 μg/ml, R848), TLR4 (0.1 μg/ml, LPS-B5 for 18 hours, or TLR9 (2 μM ODN1826) agonists.


## Lentiviral production and transduction to iBMDMs

293T cells were seeded into 10 cm plate in DMEM media containing 10% FBS without antibiotics at 70% density 20-24 hours prior the transfection. 8 µg of the indicated transfer vector pLV was diluted in 1 ml OPTIMEM together with 4 µg of packaging vector psPAX2 and envelope plasmid pMD2.G. 80 µl of Lipofectamine 2000 was then added to the mixture and incubated for 20 minutes at room temperature. The transfection mixture was the add to the 293T cell media in a drop-wise manner. Cell media was change at 6 hours post transfection and lentiviral supernatants were harvested 72 hours later. Supernatants were then sterile filtered using 0.45 µm PES filter and centrifuged at 1500 RPM for 5 minutes at room temperature to remove any cell debris. Supernatants were then mixed with a lentiviral concentrator (Lenti-X-Concentrator, Takara), at 1:3 ratio and incubated at 4 °C overnight. Lentiviruses were then concentrated using centrifugation at 1500xg for 45 minutes at 4 °C and lentivirus containing pellets were resuspended in 1 ml DMEM plain media.

Lentiviruses were immediately transduced to iBMDM cells using spinfection. Briefly, 0.75 x10^6 cells were seeded to each well of 12-well plate and 120 µl of concentrated virus was then added. Polybrene was then added to the mixture at 5 µg/ml concentration. Cells were spinfected at 932xg for 60 minutes at 37 °C and then placed in the incubator. 48 hours post transduction, 7.5 µg/ml puromycin was added to the cells to select the transduced population.


## CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout

CRISPR knockout was performed utilizing pLentiCRISPRv2 plasmid. 61 The gRNA containing plasmids were transfected into 70-80% confluent 293T cells with the following reaction mixture: 8 µg of the indicated gRNA-containing CRISPR vector was diluted in 1 ml OPTIMEM together with 4 µg of packaging vector psPAX2 and envelope plasmid pVSVG. 80 µl of Lipofectamine 2000 was then added to the mixture and incubated for 20 minutes at room temperature. The transfection mixture was the add to the 293T cell media in a drop-wise manner. Cell media was change at 6 hours post transfection and lentiviral supernatants were harvested 48 hours later. Supernatants were then sterile filtered using 0.45 µm PES filter and centrifuged at 1500 RPM for 5 minutes at room temperature to remove any cell debris. Supernatants were then mixed with a lentiviral concentrator (Lenti-X-Concentrator, Takara), at 1:3 ratio and incubated at 4 °C overnight. Lentiviruses were then concentrated using centrifugation at 1,500xg for 45 minutes at 4 °C and lentivirus containing pellets were resuspended in 1 ml DMEM plain media. Lentiviruses were immediately transduced to iBMDM cells using spinfection. Briefly, 0.75 x10^6 cells were seeded to each well of 12-well plate and 120 µl of concentrated virus was added. Polybrene was then added to the mixture at 5 µg/ml concentration. Cells were spinfected at 932xg for 60 minutes at 37 °C and then placed in the incubator.

48 hours post-transduction cells were selected with 7.5 µg/ml puromycin for 72 hours. Single cell clones were obtained using single-cell sorting. Genomic DNA was extracted from the cells and genomic area 400-500 bp spanning around the gRNA-target site was amplified via PCR, gel purified and sequenced. The sequencing reads were compared with the wild-type sequence using ICE CRISPR Analysis tool (Synthego) to determine the knockout containing clones. Subsequently, the knockout was verified by immunoblotting.


## Indirect immunofluorescent (IF) imaging

IF imaging was performed as previously described. 61 Briefly, cells were seeded into coverslips containing 6-well plates and stimulated as indicated. 2-3 hours post dsDNA transfection, cell media was aspirated and cells were washed twice with PBS. Cells were then fixed in 4%PFA for 15 minutes at room temperature. PFA was then washed away 3-times with PBS. Cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton_X_100 for 15 minutes at room temperature. Permeabilization buffer was then washed away 3 times with PBS washes and cells were blocked with 6%BSA in PBS for 1 hours at room temperature. Coverslips were embedded in dsDNA marker primary antibody at 1:100 dilution in 6%BSA in PBS solution at 4 °C for overnight. Coverslips were washed with PBS for 3 times and were embedded in anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 647 nm for 1 hour at room temperature in 6%BSA in PBS. Coverslips were then washed 3 times with PBS and samples were incubated 20 µM of Hoechst staining solution for 15 minutes at room temperature. Coverslips were then embedded in approximately 10 µl of Vectashield.

Immunofluorescence images were acquired using a spinning disk confocal microscope with a 63× oil immersion objective. Z-stacks (13–15 slices per field) were collected to capture the full depth of the cells. Maximum intensity projections were generated using ImageJ to visualize and analyse the data. To verify signal specificity and exclude the signal bleed through between channels, the IF staining procedure was in parallel performed under no primary or secondary antibody and no Hoechst conditions.


## Western blotting

Cell media was aspirated, and cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS. 150 µl RIPA buffer containing EDTA-free Protease Inhibitor (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. no. 11836170001) and PhosSTOP (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. no. 4906837001) cocktails were added to each well. Cells were then scraped with the lysis buffer and transferred into 1.5 mL tubes. Lysates were incubated at 4 °C for 20 minutes with end-to-end rotation and spun down at the highest setting in an Eppendorf centrifuge for 10 minutes. Supernatants were obtained as whole cell lysates.

The whole cell extracts were mixed with Laemmli Buffer and separated by electrophoresis using 4-20% gradient gels. Proteins were transferred and immobilized onto a nitrocellulose membrane using standard Towbin Buffer containing 20% methanol for 2 hours at constant 220 mA. For blocking, membranes were treated with 3% BSA in TBST for anti-IL-1β, and with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for all other antibodies. Membranes were incubated with the respective primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, washed three times (5 minutes each) with TBST, and incubated with the corresponding isotype specific HRP-conjugated secondary antibody in 1% non-fat dry milk containing TBST for 2 hours at room temperature. Membranes were then washed three times (5 minutes each) with TBST, and chemiluminescent signals were visualized using standard ECL solution.

## LDH release

Wild-type or MyD88 knockout iBMDMs were seeded into 6-well plates at 1.5x10 6 cells/well density 24 hours prior to the treatment. For each cell type, quadruplicate wells were seeded for determination of the maximum LDH content. Cells were treated or transfected with the respective nucleic acids as indicated. The maximal LDH release wells were treated with 0.1% Triton-X-100 at final concentration for 10 minutes before collecting the samples. After treatment, cell supernatants were collected and spun at 1,500 RPM for 5 minutes to eliminate the floating cells. Supernatants were diluted to 1:20 in LDH storage buffer containing 200 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.3), 10% Glycerol, 1% BSA. LDH levels were measured using LDH-Glo TM Cytotoxicity Assay kit (Promega, cat. no. J2380) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. Percentage cytotoxicity was calculated according to the following formula: Percent Cytotoxicity=100 x (Experimental LDH-Medium Background) / (Maximal LDH Release Control-Medium Background).


## Gene expression analysis

Wild-type, MyD88 or cGAS knockout iBMDMs were treated as indicated. Total RNA was isolated using TRizol TM Reagent (Invitrogen, cat. no. 15596026) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. In brief, cell supernatants were aspirated after indicated stimulations, and cells were washed twice with PBS. Cells were then directly harvested in 1 ml TRizol and incubated at room temperature for 2-3 minutes, followed by addition of 200 µl chloroform. After phase separation by centrifugation (12,000 g for 35-55 minutes at 4 °C), total RNA was precipitated from the aqueous layer with isopropanol. The RNA pellet was washed three times with 75% RNase-free ethanol, air-dried and resuspended in nuclease-free water.

For cDNA synthesis, a total of 1,000 ng RNA was used for each condition. High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, cat. no. 4368813) was used according to the manufacturer’s instruction to reverse transcribe the RNA into cDNA. The cDNA was subsequently diluted 1:20 in nuclease free water.

qRT-PCR was performed as described previously. 62 Primers were designed according to MIQE guidelines (Minimum Information for Publication of qRT-PCR Experiments) using exon-intron spanning primers with 62 °C melting temperature. The DNA folding tool from the mfold server was used to exclude primers forming secondary structures. Primer efficiencies were determined using three serial dilutions of cDNA and only primers with efficiencies around 98-100% were chosen for further analysis. To determine fold induction of target genes, two reference genes with an M value lower than 0.5 were used, Hrpt1 and Gapdh respectively. For the detection of the qRT-PCR reaction, GoTaq qPCR Master Mix, 2X (Promega, cat. no. A600A) was used. For a single reaction, 5 µl of GoTaq qPCR Master Mix was mixed with 1 µl forward and reverse primer pairs (0.3 µM final concentration), 2 µl of nuclease free water and 2 µl of cDNA template. Samples were prepared in quadruplicates and reaction was run using CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad) using the following setup: Stage 1: Activation at 50 °C for 2 minutes; Stage 2: pre-soak at 95 °C for 10 minutes; Stage 3: Denaturation at 95 °C for 15 seconds, Annealing at 60 °C for 1 minute; Stage-4: Melting Curve, 95°C for 15 seconds, 60 °C for 15 seconds, 95 °C for 15 seconds.
