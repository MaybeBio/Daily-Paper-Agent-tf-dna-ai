## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text including abstract, results, discussion, methods, and supplementary figure legends
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific validity, technical soundness, and claims as supported by the provided evidence
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors present DAPE-PD, a method that adapts the pretrained autoregressive genomic foundation model Evo 1 into a bidirectional masked diffusion model via efficient fine-tuning. They claim this approach generates de novo promoter sequences with high novelty and native-like compositional features, and that experimentally validated promoters were recovered in three non-model gut commensals using a small number of candidates selected through in silico prioritization.
- **Visible evidence base** Abstract, full results narrative, methods descriptions, figure legends (Figs 1-6, Extended Data Figs 1-10), supplementary table description
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Actual figures and data plots are not provided; only figure legends and narrative descriptions. Quantitative results (e.g., exact activity measurements, statistical tests, error bars) are described but not shown. Supplementary Table 1 contents are referenced but not included. Code availability and data availability statements are absent.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** The manuscript presents a conceptually interesting approach: adapting a large pretrained autoregressive genomic model into a bidirectional diffusion model for regulatory DNA generation. The core idea of repurposing foundation model representations for generative tasks without full retraining is timely and potentially valuable. However, the evidence as presented is insufficient to fully evaluate the central claims. Key quantitative data are described narratively but not shown, making it impossible to assess effect sizes, variability, or statistical significance. The experimental validation appears limited in scope, and several methodological details require clarification. The claim that diffusion-based generation is superior to AR approaches for regulatory DNA is plausible but not rigorously established from the provided evidence.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in synthetic biology, genome engineering, and computational biology would be interested. The work addresses a practical bottleneck: generating functional regulatory elements for non-model organisms. Those working on genomic foundation models and generative models for biological sequences would also find the adaptation strategy relevant. The potential application to engineering gut commensals for therapeutic purposes broadens the appeal to the microbiome engineering community.
- **Major Strengths** The conceptual framing is strong: identifying the mismatch between AR generation and the bidirectional nature of regulatory element function is a thoughtful motivation. The technical approach of adapting a large pretrained model via efficient fine-tuning rather than training from scratch is practical and resource-conscious. The inclusion of three non-model gut commensals addresses a genuine need. The prioritization pipeline that reduces experimental burden is a useful contribution.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **Concern ID** R1-M1
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Evidence sufficiency
  - **Claim pointer** The manuscript claims that DAPE-PD generated functional de novo promoters in three non-model gut commensals, with activity comparable to native promoters.
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section "Promoters generated by diffusion for non-model bacteria", Figure 5c, Extended Data Figs 5-7
  - **Concern** The quantitative data supporting functional promoter activity are not shown. The narrative states that promoters were "active" and that some "approached the strength" of native references, but no actual activity values, fold-changes, replicates, or statistical analyses are presented. Without seeing the data, the strength of the functional claim cannot be assessed.
  - **Why it matters** The central claim of the paper is that the method produces functional promoters in non-model organisms. If the activity levels are marginal or highly variable, the practical utility of the approach is substantially weakened. The current presentation does not allow the reader to judge this.
  - **Resolution test** Provide the actual activity measurements for all tested promoters, including native controls, with replicates and appropriate statistical tests. Show the distribution of activities across the tested set, not just selected examples.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M2
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Technical soundness
  - **Claim pointer** The manuscript claims that diffusion-based generation produces promoters with greater novelty than AR fine-tuning while retaining native-like composition.
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section "Diffusion increases promoter novelty while preserving host sequence grammar", Figures 2d, 2e, 3a, 3b
  - **Concern** The comparison between diffusion and AR approaches relies on metrics (query coverage, BLAST hits, GC content, 6-mer frequencies) that are described but not displayed. The claim of "substantially greater novelty" is based on a single comparison with specific AR training configurations. It is unclear whether the comparison is fair: were the AR models trained with equivalent compute, data, and hyperparameter tuning? The diffusion model was trained for 3 epochs while AR models may have had different training durations. Without seeing the actual distributions and statistical comparisons, the superiority claim is not established.
  - **Why it matters** The novelty-composition tradeoff is a key claimed advantage of the diffusion approach. If the comparison is confounded by training differences, the conclusion may not hold. This is central to the paper's contribution.
  - **Resolution test** Show the full distributions of the metrics for all generation strategies, with statistical comparisons. Clarify training protocols for all models to ensure comparability. Consider additional metrics such as diversity, motif conservation, and predicted activity.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M3
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Technical soundness
  - **Claim pointer** The manuscript claims that the model learned "spatial grammar" and that the emergence of a Shine-Dalgarno motif in generated sequences indicates learning of regulatory syntax.
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section "Promoters generated by diffusion for non-model bacteria", Figure 6c, Extended Data Fig 8c
  - **Concern** The claim that the model learned spatial grammar is inferred from the presence of a Shine-Dalgarno motif in the top 25 active promoters. However, the Shine-Dalgarno sequence is a ribosome binding site, not a promoter element per se. Its presence in sequences generated to be promoters may reflect the training data composition (intergenic regions include 5' UTRs) rather than learned promoter grammar. The distinction between promoter elements and translation initiation signals is not addressed. Additionally, the motif was found in only a subset of active promoters, and the analysis does not establish whether this motif is causally related to activity.
  - **Why it matters** The claim that the model learned regulatory grammar is a strong interpretive statement. If the evidence is based on a motif that is not a promoter element, the interpretation is weakened. The paper should clarify what specific promoter features were learned and how they relate to function.
  - **Resolution test** Perform motif analysis specifically for known promoter elements (e.g., -10, -35 boxes, UP elements) and relate their presence to activity. Discuss the role of Shine-Dalgarno sequences in the context of the training data. Consider mutagenesis experiments to test the causal role of identified motifs.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M4
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Reproducibility
  - **Claim pointer** The manuscript claims that the method requires only publicly available genomic sequences and can generate and prioritize promoter candidates without large functional training datasets.
  - **Evidence pointer** Methods sections "Intergenic Region Mining Pipeline" and "Filtering Pipeline for Promoters Generated for Non-model Bacteria"
  - **Concern** The filtering pipeline relies on TXpredict, a tool for predicting gene expression levels. The manuscript does not provide details on TXpredict's accuracy for the specific organisms tested, nor does it validate that the predicted expression levels correlate with actual promoter strength. The use of predicted rather than measured expression as a proxy for promoter strength introduces potential systematic biases that are not discussed.
  - **Why it matters** The prioritization pipeline is a key practical contribution. If the underlying prediction tool is unreliable for these organisms, the pipeline may not generalize. The claim that the method works without functional data needs to be supported by evidence that the prediction step is accurate.
  - **Resolution test** Validate TXpredict predictions against the experimental data obtained in this study. Discuss known limitations of expression prediction tools for non-model organisms. Consider whether the pipeline could be simplified or made more robust.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M5
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Technical soundness
  - **Claim pointer** The manuscript claims that AlphaFold3 modeling predicted canonical sigma factor engagement and transcription bubble formation for generated promoters.
  - **Evidence pointer** Results sections "Diffusion increases promoter novelty while preserving host sequence grammar" and "Promoters generated by diffusion for non-model bacteria", Figures 3f, 5d, Extended Data Figs 6c, 7
  - **Concern** AlphaFold3 is a structure prediction tool, not a molecular dynamics or docking tool. Its ability to predict protein-DNA interactions, particularly the formation of a transcription bubble, is not established. The manuscript does not describe the AlphaFold3 methodology used (e.g., input construction, confidence metrics, interpretation criteria). The claim that AlphaFold3 predicted a "transcription bubble" is surprising and requires substantial methodological justification.
  - **Why it matters** Structural predictions are used to support the mechanistic interpretation of promoter function. If the methodology is not appropriate or the interpretation is overreaching, the mechanistic claims are weakened. This could mislead readers about the reliability of the structural predictions.
  - **Resolution test** Provide detailed AlphaFold3 methodology, including input construction, output interpretation, and confidence metrics. Justify the use of AlphaFold3 for this application. Consider whether the structural predictions add meaningful evidence beyond the functional data.

- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** Originality: The concept of adapting a pretrained AR genomic foundation model into a diffusion model is novel and potentially significant. The application to non-model gut commensals is a useful extension. Scientific importance: If the claims are substantiated, the work could provide a practical tool for regulatory element engineering in organisms lacking genetic parts. However, the current evidence does not fully establish the importance. Interdisciplinary readership: The work bridges machine learning, synthetic biology, and microbiology, which could attract a broad audience. Technical soundness: The core technical approach is reasonable, but several methodological details and the lack of shown data prevent full assessment. Readability for nonspecialists: The manuscript is generally well-written and accessible, though some sections assume familiarity with both diffusion models and bacterial promoter architecture.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The core idea is promising and the experimental design is thoughtful, but the evidence as presented is insufficient to fully establish the central claims. The authors should provide the actual data, clarify the comparison methodology, and address the interpretive concerns before the case is made.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that diffusion-based generation is superior to AR approaches for regulatory DNA is not fully supported by the shown evidence; the comparison may be confounded by training differences.
- The claim that the model learned "spatial grammar" is inferred from limited motif analysis and is not rigorously established.
- The claim that AlphaFold3 predicted transcription bubble formation is not supported by described methodology; AlphaFold3 is not designed for this type of prediction.
- The claim that the prioritization pipeline reduces experimental burden is plausible but the effectiveness of the TXpredict-based filtering is not validated against the experimental results.
- The claim that generated promoters have "substantially greater novelty" is based on metrics that are described but not displayed.
- The functional activity of promoters in non-model organisms is described narratively without quantitative data, making the strength of the claim unassessable.
