## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript addresses a fundamental and long-standing question in regulatory genomics: why most TF binding events do not lead to measurable changes in gene expression upon TF perturbation. The authors introduce a new dataset generated by Transposon Calling Cards, a method orthogonal to ChIP, and propose a statistical framework to identify functional synergy between TFs. The question is important and the conceptual contribution is potentially significant. However, the manuscript as presented has several critical gaps. The central claim that functional synergy is "surprisingly common" rests on a statistical modeling pipeline that is described at a level of detail insufficient to assess its validity. The lack of access to the actual figures, tables, and supplemental files makes it impossible to evaluate the magnitude of the reported effects, the quality of the data, or the robustness of the conclusions. The manuscript would benefit from a clearer articulation of the biological mechanisms proposed and a more careful treatment of alternative explanations.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in regulatory genomics, systems biology, and transcriptional regulation would be interested. The work challenges the standard interpretation of TF binding data and proposes a new framework for understanding context-dependent TF function. The TCC dataset itself, if validated, would be a valuable community resource. The concept of functional synergy, if substantiated, could influence how TF regulatory networks are constructed and interpreted.
- **Major strengths** The question addressed is of central importance to the field. The use of TCC as an orthogonal method to ChIP is a thoughtful approach to address potential technical artifacts. The conceptual distinction between cooperative binding and functional synergy is clear and useful. The idea of modeling interaction terms between TFs to predict perturbation responses is a reasonable and potentially powerful approach. The authors are appropriately cautious in their discussion of alternative explanations and limitations.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **Concern ID** R1-M1
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Technical soundness
  - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that their three-stage pipeline identifies "high-confidence instances of functional synergy" and that such synergies are "surprisingly common."
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section, "A stringent pipeline for identifying robust functional synergies"; Figure 2; Supplemental Files S5-S7
  - **Concern** The description of the statistical pipeline is insufficient to evaluate its validity. The authors state that they used L1 regularized linear regression (LASSO) with bootstrap resampling and iterative elimination of terms retained in <50% of bootstrap samples. However, critical details are missing: how was the regularization parameter chosen in each bootstrap? How were the 1,000 bootstrap samples generated (e.g., sampling genes with replacement, but what about the correlation structure between genes)? How were the confidence intervals constructed exactly, and what is the justification for the 99% and 95% thresholds? Most importantly, the manuscript does not report the false discovery rate or any multiple testing correction across the large number of interaction terms tested (97 TFs × 96 potential modifiers per TF). Without this information, the claim that 45% of TFs have at least one modifier after Stage 3 could be entirely consistent with chance.
  - **Why it matters** The central conclusion of the paper rests on the statistical validity of this pipeline. If the pipeline is not rigorously described and the error rates are not controlled, the claim that functional synergy is common cannot be evaluated. This is a blocking issue.
  - **Resolution test** The authors must provide a complete description of the statistical pipeline, including details on regularization parameter selection, bootstrap procedures, confidence interval construction, and multiple testing correction. They should report the expected number of false positive interactions under a null model (e.g., by permuting the response data) and demonstrate that their observed number of interactions significantly exceeds this null expectation.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M2
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Evidence quality
  - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that "TCC tends to agree with the perturbation response data better than the ChIP data does" and that "it remains the case that TCC data on 66 of these TFs (56%) does not show a significant overlap between bound and responsive genes."
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section, "A new, genome-wide dataset on TF binding locations"; Figure 1C-E
  - **Concern** The comparison between TCC and ChIP is central to the argument that non-functional binding is not a ChIP artifact. However, the manuscript does not provide the actual numbers, the criteria for "significant overlap," or the details of the DTO procedure. The statement that the median fraction of bound genes that are responsive is 20% (based on DTO thresholds) is presented without the distribution or confidence intervals. Furthermore, the comparison is made to "older ChIP data" (reference 36), which may not be directly comparable in terms of growth conditions, strain background, or analysis methods. The authors also state that they discarded replicates that did not show significant overlap with perturbation data, which could introduce a selection bias.
  - **Why it matters** The claim that most binding is non-functional even with an orthogonal method is a key negative result. If the comparison is not rigorously quantified or if the replicate filtering introduces bias, the conclusion is weakened. This is a blocking issue.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should provide a direct, quantitative comparison between TCC and ChIP data on the same set of TFs and conditions, including the number of bound genes, the overlap statistics, and the response rates with confidence intervals. They should also demonstrate that the replicate filtering does not substantially alter the conclusions.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M3
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Interpretation
  - **Claim pointer** The authors state that "the interaction terms were significant even when the main effects of the mTFs were included in the model," and use this to argue that the interactions are not merely proxies for indirect effects.
  - **Evidence pointer** Discussion section, "Several possible explanations for our findings can be ruled out"
  - **Concern** The argument that including main effects rules out indirect effects is not fully convincing. An indirect effect mediated by the mTF could still manifest as an interaction if the mTF's effect on the target gene is nonlinear or if the mTF's expression is itself affected by the perturbation of the pTF. The authors do not test for the latter possibility. Furthermore, the manuscript does not report the magnitude of the interaction coefficients or the improvement in predictive accuracy, making it difficult to assess the biological significance of the effects.
  - **Why it matters** The interpretation of the interaction terms as evidence for functional synergy depends on ruling out alternative explanations. If indirect effects are not fully excluded, the biological interpretation is uncertain.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should perform additional analyses to rule out indirect effects, such as examining whether the expression of the mTF changes upon perturbation of the pTF, or using a mediation analysis framework. They should also report the effect sizes and the improvement in predictive accuracy for specific examples.

  - **Concern ID** R1-m3
  - **Severity** Minor
  - **Axis** Clarity
  - **Affected element** Results, "Data transformations and univariate regression models"
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section, paragraph beginning "Next, we set out to model the relationship..."
  - **Issue** The description of the data transformations is confusing. The authors state that they tried enrichment, -log P, and rank-based transformations, but the rationale for the final choice of the log rank transformation is not fully explained. The statement that "the log rank transformation emphasizes strong signals... and compresses weak signals" is reasonable, but the authors do not show how this transformation affects the distribution of the data or the model fit.
  - **Required correction** Provide a figure or table showing the distribution of the raw and transformed variables, and a clearer justification for the chosen transformation.

A brief discussion of the potential biological basis for this observation would strengthen the paper.
  - **Required correction** Add a sentence or two in the Discussion speculating on why these specific TFs might be more frequent modifiers (e.g., their roles in stress response or general transcription).

If the claims are substantiated, the work would have significant impact. Interdisciplinary readership: The work is primarily of interest to molecular biologists and computational biologists; the broader interdisciplinary appeal is moderate.
