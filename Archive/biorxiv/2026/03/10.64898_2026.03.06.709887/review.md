## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text (abstract, introduction, materials and methods, results, discussion, conclusion) as provided. Supplementary figures and tables are referenced but not supplied.
- **Assessment boundary** Assessment is limited to the scientific claims, methodology, and interpretation as presented in the provided text. No external validation of data or code was performed.
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors claim that various types of DNA damage (ribonucleotide, 8-oxoguanine, abasic site, nick, gap) induce measurable, long-range conformational changes in DNA duplex structure and dynamics, detectable by single-molecule FRET, and that these changes may serve as a non-protein-based signal for repair protein recruitment.
- **Visible evidence base** Descriptions of smFRET measurements, statistical analyses (Kruskal-Wallis, Dunn's test), photophysical characterisation, accessible volume modelling, rigid body docking, and burst variance analysis. Quantitative results are reported in the text for bending and twist angles. Supplementary figures and tables are referenced but not available for inspection.
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Supplementary Figures 1-47, Supplementary Tables 1-14, raw data files, and analysis notebooks are referenced but not provided. This limits verification of statistical analyses, BVA results, and modelling outputs.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** The manuscript addresses an interesting and potentially important question: whether DNA damage itself, independent of protein binding, produces structural changes that could serve as a recognition signal. The experimental approach using smFRET with multiple dye positions is thoughtful and the scale of data collection is substantial. However, the manuscript as presented has significant gaps in methodological detail and quantitative reporting that prevent full evaluation of the claims. The central conclusion that damage-induced conformational changes propagate long-range and could act as a damage signal is plausible but not fully established from the evidence provided. The distinction between local and long-range effects is not clearly quantified, and the link to biological function remains speculative.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in DNA repair, particularly those studying base excision repair and damage recognition mechanisms. Also of interest to biophysicists using single-molecule techniques to study nucleic acid structure and dynamics, and to computational biologists modelling DNA flexibility. The potential implication that DNA structure itself contributes to damage sensing could be of broad interest to the DNA damage and genome stability community.
- **Major strengths** The study uses a comprehensive set of damage types relevant to BER, including subtle modifications like ribonucleotide incorporation. The use of nine dye pairs per construct is thorough and allows position-dependent analysis. The inclusion of photophysical characterisation and attempts to account for dye behaviour are commendable. The automated analysis pipeline and public availability of data and code are positive features. The bending and twist quantification provides a more detailed structural readout than simple FRET efficiency changes.
- **Major Concerns** 
  - R1-M1: The claim of "long range changes" is not quantitatively supported. The manuscript does not define what constitutes "long range" in this context, nor does it provide a systematic analysis of how structural changes decay with distance from the lesion site. The dye positions are described as proximal, intermediate, and distal, but the actual distances are not given, and no trend analysis across positions is presented.
  - R1-M2: The conversion of FRET efficiencies to distances and subsequent modelling relies on assumptions that are not fully validated. The authors acknowledge discrepancies between predicted and measured FRET efficiencies in control duplexes but do not adequately address how this affects the interpretation of damage-induced changes. The use of scalars normalised to control constructs may propagate errors.
  - R1-M3: The statistical analysis is described but the results are not presented in sufficient detail. The manuscript states which comparisons were significant but does not report effect sizes, confidence intervals, or the magnitude of FRET efficiency changes. Without these, the biological relevance of the observed differences cannot be assessed.
  - R1-M4: The link between the observed structural changes and the proposed biological function (damage detection) is speculative. No experiments or quantitative arguments are provided to show that the magnitude of conformational changes observed would be sufficient to influence protein binding or search efficiency. The discussion overreaches the data.
  - R1-M5: The bending and twist analysis is presented as a key result, but the methodology for extracting these parameters from FRET data is not described in sufficient detail. The relationship between dye position changes and actual DNA bending/twisting is not straightforward and requires validation, which is not provided.
- **Minor Comments** 
  - R1-m1: The abstract states "six different types of DNA damage" but the introduction mentions "six different types" while the methods describe six constructs. The text later refers to "seven DNA duplexes" in the abstract. This inconsistency should be resolved.
  - R1-m2: The introduction cites references 1-10 but the reference list is not provided. This makes it impossible to evaluate the context and prior work.
  - R1-m3: The methods section for sample preparation states "six different duplex constructs" but the abstract mentions seven. Clarification is needed.
  - R1-m4: The description of the "smfBox" instrument and its validation is minimal. Given that one of the authors is affiliated with a company that commercialises this instrument, additional details on calibration and potential biases would be appropriate.
  - R1-m5: The conflict of interest statement is present but the potential impact of the commercial affiliation on the choice of instrumentation and analysis methods is not discussed.
  - R1-m6: The discussion of NMR studies (references 71-72, 90-96) is used to support interpretations, but the specific comparisons between the present FRET results and those structural studies are not made explicit.
  - R1-m7: The conclusion states "a single oxygen atom addition" as the smallest change, but the manuscript does not discuss whether the sensitivity demonstrated here would extend to other subtle modifications not tested.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** The lack of quantitative distance information for dye positions, insufficient detail on the modelling pipeline, absence of effect sizes and confidence intervals for statistical comparisons, and the unvalidated assumption that FRET-derived distance changes directly reflect DNA bending and twisting. The manuscript also does not provide a clear definition or quantitative criterion for "long range" changes.
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** 
  - Originality: The idea that DNA damage itself creates a structural signal independent of protein binding is not entirely new, but the systematic experimental approach across multiple damage types and positions is a valuable contribution. The work is original in its scope and methodology.
  - Scientific importance: The question of how repair proteins find damage is fundamental. If the claims are fully supported, the work could have significant implications for understanding damage recognition. However, the current evidence does not yet establish the biological relevance of the observed changes.
  - Interdisciplinary readership: The topic bridges DNA repair biology, biophysics, and structural biology. The manuscript is written in a way that is largely accessible, but the methods section assumes familiarity with smFRET and modelling approaches.
  - Technical soundness: The experimental design is generally sound, but the lack of detailed quantitative reporting and validation of the modelling pipeline weakens the technical case. The statistical approach is appropriate but underreported.
  - Readability for nonspecialists: The introduction and discussion are clear and accessible. The results section is more technical and would benefit from clearer presentation of quantitative findings and their significance.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The core idea and experimental approach are promising, but the manuscript currently does not provide sufficient quantitative evidence to fully support the claims of long-range structural changes and their potential role in damage detection. Major revisions are required to address the concerns raised.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that damage-induced conformational changes propagate to "distal" regions is not quantitatively supported without distance information and trend analysis.
- The assertion that observed structural changes "could act as a signal for repair protein recruitment" is speculative and not tested experimentally.
- The statement that "all modifications altered FRET efficiencies compared to undamaged DNA" is made without reporting the magnitude or consistency of these changes across all positions.
- The bending and twist angle values are reported as single numbers without uncertainty estimates or validation against independent structural methods.
- The claim that the automated analysis pipeline enables "rigorous statistical comparisons" is not fully verifiable without access to the supplementary materials and code.
