## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text, including abstract, introduction, methods, results, discussion, and supplementary figure references.
- **Assessment boundary** Technical and scientific evaluation of the DNAsight framework, its validation, and the biological findings reported. Assessment is limited to the provided manuscript text; figures and supplementary materials were not directly available for inspection.
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors present DNAsight, a modular, ML-based framework for automated segmentation and quantitative analysis of AFM images of DNA and chromatin. The framework integrates DNA tracing, base-pair calibration, and modules for quantifying compaction, looping, nucleosome spacing, and protein clustering. The authors demonstrate its application to diverse chromatin-associated proteins, reporting protein-specific organizational signatures, and benchmark its segmentation accuracy against manual annotations and TopoStats.
- **Visible evidence base** Methods sections for experimental procedures, DNAsight architecture, training, and quantification; Results sections describing segmentation accuracy, geometric quantification, loop detection, and nucleosome analysis; Discussion of limitations and interpretability; Data availability statement.
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Figures and supplementary figures (referenced as Figure 1-5, S1-S18) were not provided. Detailed statistical outputs, model architecture specifics (e.g., layer counts, hyperparameters), and the full dataset characteristics are not fully described. The manuscript states it is a revised version responding to prior reviewer comments, but the original reviews are not available.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** The manuscript presents a timely and potentially valuable contribution to the field of AFM-based chromatin analysis. The modular design and integration of ML-based segmentation with multiple quantification modules address a recognized bottleneck in the field. The validation approach, including benchmarking against TopoStats and comparison to manual annotations, is appropriate. However, the manuscript's impact is currently limited by several factors: the biological conclusions are presented as suggestive rather than definitive, the technical validation lacks depth in certain areas (e.g., robustness across a wider range of imaging conditions, error analysis), and the absence of figures limits a full assessment of the presented results. The framework's generalizability and the strength of the biological findings require further substantiation.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in chromatin biology, biophysics, and single-molecule imaging would be interested. Specifically, groups using AFM to study DNA-protein interactions, chromatin fiber structure, and the mechanisms of architectural proteins (e.g., cohesin, CTCF, GAF) would find the automated, quantitative pipeline valuable. The framework's potential for high-throughput analysis and its modularity could appeal to labs seeking standardized, reproducible analysis methods. The benchmarking against existing tools (TopoStats) is relevant to the computational biology and image analysis community.
- **Major strengths**
    1.  Addresses a clear and important need for standardized, scalable, and automated analysis tools in AFM chromatin imaging.
    2.  The modular architecture (segmentation and quantification modules) is well-conceived and likely to be adaptable to various experimental systems.
    3.  The use of continuous proximity maps and a weighted loss function is a thoughtful approach to the class imbalance problem in DNA segmentation.
    4.  The validation strategy, including comparison to manual annotations and benchmarking against an existing tool (TopoStats), is a strength.
    5.  The application to multiple biological systems (IHF, cohesin-CTCF-PDS5A, GAF, nucleosomes) demonstrates the framework's versatility.
- **Major Concerns**
    - **R1-M1**
        - **Severity** Major
        - **Blocking** Yes
        - **Axis** Technical soundness and validation
        - **Claim pointer** "DNAsight achieved 76% of skeleton points within 1.5 pixels of the manual trace on the 10% test dataset... indicating that the automated backbone localizes the DNA contour with accuracy comparable to the spatial resolution of the AFM image and the precision of manual annotation."
        - **Evidence pointer** Results section "Automated DNA Tracing Achieves Human-Level Precision...", Figure 2C-E, Figure S1, S5
        - **Concern** The claim of "human-level precision" is based on a single metric (76% of points within 1.5 pixels) on a single test set. The comparison to inter-annotator variability is mentioned but not quantified in the text. The benchmark against TopoStats is presented as a single set of mean deviation values (0.58, 0.60, 0.68 vs 2.05, 1.54, 1.77 pixels) for three images. This is insufficient to establish robust equivalence or superiority. The manuscript lacks a more comprehensive error analysis, such as the distribution of errors, performance on complex topologies (e.g., supercoiled DNA, dense protein-DNA complexes), and sensitivity to image quality parameters beyond SNR.
        - **Why it matters** The core value proposition of DNAsight is its ability to replace manual, subjective analysis with automated, accurate segmentation. Without a more rigorous and statistically robust validation of its accuracy across diverse and challenging conditions, the claim of "human-level precision" is not fully supported. This is critical for the framework's adoption and for the reliability of all downstream quantitative analyses.
        - **Resolution test** Provide a more detailed error analysis, including error distributions (e.g., median, percentiles), performance stratified by image complexity (e.g., DNA length, degree of supercoiling, presence of protein clusters), and a statistical comparison of DNAsight's error to the inter-annotator variability. Include a larger benchmark dataset for the TopoStats comparison, with statistical tests to support claims of improved performance.
    - **R1-M2**
        - **Severity** Major
        - **Blocking** Yes
        - **Axis** Scientific importance and support for biological conclusions
        - **Claim pointer** "Applied across diverse chromatin-associated proteins, DNAsight reveals protein-specific organizational signatures, including topology-dependent compaction by integration host factor (IHF), condition-dependent changes in loop-like DNA structures in cohesin-CTCF-precocious dissociation of sisters 5A (PDS5A) reactions, and promoter-driven multimerization of GAGA factor (GAF) clusters."
        - **Evidence pointer** Results sections "Quantification of Supercoiling and Protein-Induced Changes in DNA Spatial Organization", "Automated Detection of DNA Loop-Like Structures Reveals Condition-Dependent Enrichment of CTCF-Proximal Loops", "Quantifying Protein-DNA Assemblies and Nucleosome Architecture", Figures 3, 4, 5
        - **Concern** The biological findings are presented as "reveals" and "signatures," but the manuscript text itself repeatedly cautions that the results are "suggestive rather than definitive" (e.g., for the cohesin-CTCF-PDS5A loop data). The statistical framework for comparing conditions (e.g., IHF vs. no IHF, +PDS5A vs. -PDS5A) is not described in the provided text. The number of independent replicates, the number of molecules analyzed per condition, and the statistical tests used are not reported. Without this information, the robustness and significance of the claimed "protein-specific organizational signatures" cannot be evaluated.
        - **Why it matters** The biological applications are intended to demonstrate the utility of the DNAsight framework. If the biological conclusions are not rigorously supported by the data and statistical analysis, the framework's value as a tool for generating biological insight is diminished. The manuscript needs to clearly distinguish between demonstrating the framework's capabilities and making robust biological claims.
        - **Resolution test** Clearly describe the experimental design, including the number of independent replicates and the total number of molecules analyzed per condition. Report the specific statistical tests used for each comparison (e.g., Mann-Whitney U, permutation tests) and provide effect sizes and confidence intervals. For the key biological claims, provide a more nuanced interpretation that explicitly separates framework demonstration from established biological findings.
    - **R1-M3**
        - **Severity** Major
        - **Blocking** No
        - **Axis** Technical soundness and generalizability
        - **Claim pointer** "The framework operates robustly across diverse imaging conditions, including variations in pixel size, SNR, DNA length, and substrate type, enabling analysis of both linear and plasmid DNA without per-image parameter tuning."
        - **Evidence pointer** Results sections, Figures S3, S4, S6, S7
        - **Concern** The claim of robustness "across diverse imaging conditions" is not fully supported by the evidence described in the text. The manuscript mentions a separate model trained on 90% of data and evaluated on 10%, and mentions generalization to a previously published dataset (Figure S3/S4). However, the specific range of pixel sizes, SNRs, and substrate types tested is not systematically described. The statement that the model was "trained on images spanning approximately 1–5 nm/pixel" suggests a limited range. The robustness to different substrates (e.g., mica vs. other surfaces) and buffer conditions is not detailed.
        - **Why it matters** A key selling point of DNAsight is its generalizability, which would allow labs with different instruments and protocols to use it without extensive retraining or parameter tuning. The current evidence is insufficient to support this broad claim, which could lead to poor performance when the tool is applied to datasets outside the tested range.
        - **Resolution test** Provide a systematic characterization of the model's performance across the stated range of imaging conditions. Include a table or figure showing the error metrics as a function of pixel size, SNR, and DNA length. Clearly state the range of conditions tested and any observed limitations. If the model is not robust to certain conditions, state this explicitly and provide guidance on when retraining or parameter adjustment is necessary.
- **Minor Comments**
    - **R1-m1**
        - **Severity** Minor
        - **Axis** Clarity and reproducibility
        - **Affected element** Methods section "DNAsight"
        - **Evidence pointer** Methods section, "Model architecture", "Training procedure"
        - **Issue** The description of the U-Net architecture and training procedure is high-level. Key hyperparameters (e.g., number of layers, filter sizes, learning rate schedule, batch size, number of training epochs) are not specified. The exact definition of the "customized WMSE loss function" is not fully provided (the equation is referenced but not shown in the text).
        - **Required correction** Provide a complete description of the model architecture, training hyperparameters, and the exact loss function. This is essential for reproducibility and for other researchers to adapt or retrain the model on their own data.
    - **R1-m2**
        - **Severity** Minor
        - **Axis** Statistical reporting
        - **Affected element** Results sections, Figures 3, 4, 5
        - **Evidence pointer** Results sections, Figures 3E, 4E-F, 5D, 5G, 5J, 5M
        - **Issue** The manuscript reports p-values for some comparisons (e.g., Mann-Whitney U test for IHF compaction) but does not consistently report effect sizes, confidence intervals, or the number of molecules (n) for each distribution. The text mentions "N = 43 molecules" for one calibration example, but this level of detail is not provided for other analyses.
        - **Required correction** For all quantitative comparisons, report the number of independent replicates and the number of molecules analyzed. Include effect sizes and confidence intervals alongside p-values. This will allow readers to assess the magnitude and precision of the reported effects.
    - **R1-m3**
        - **Severity** Minor
        - **Axis** Interpretation and framing
        - **Affected element** Discussion section
        - **Evidence pointer** Discussion section, paragraphs on limitations
        - **Issue** The Discussion appropriately acknowledges limitations, but some statements are vague. For example, "some features are represented as reduced topological descriptors rather than complete structural reconstructions" is a useful caveat, but the practical implications for interpreting the results could be more explicit.
        - **Required correction** Strengthen the Discussion by providing concrete examples of how the reduced descriptors might affect interpretation. For instance, discuss how the underestimation of contour length in supercoiled DNA might impact the calculated compaction values, or how the inability to distinguish true loops from overlaps affects the interpretation of the cohesin data.
    - **R1-m4**
        - **Severity** Minor
        - **Axis** Presentation and accessibility
        - **Affected element** Abstract, Introduction
        - **Evidence pointer** Abstract, Introduction
        - **Issue** The abstract and introduction are well-written but dense with terminology. For a non-specialist reader, terms like "proximity maps," "skeletonization," and "topology-dependent compaction" may be barriers to understanding the core contributions.
        - **Required correction** Consider adding a brief, plain-language explanation of the key concepts in the introduction or abstract. For example, a single sentence explaining that the framework "traces the path of DNA molecules in images and measures their shape and interactions" would improve accessibility.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established**
    - R1-M1: Insufficient validation of segmentation accuracy to support the "human-level precision" claim.
    - R1-M2: Lack of detailed statistical reporting for biological comparisons, preventing assessment of the robustness of the claimed "protein-specific organizational signatures."
    - R1-M3: The claim of robustness across diverse imaging conditions is not systematically demonstrated.
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria**
    - **Originality** The work is original in its specific integration of ML-based segmentation with a modular suite of quantification tools tailored for AFM chromatin analysis. While individual components (e.g., U-Net segmentation, geometric analysis) are not novel, their combination and application to this specific problem represents a new contribution.
    - **Scientific importance** The importance is moderate to high. The tool addresses a real bottleneck in a growing field. However, the scientific importance of the biological findings presented is currently limited by their suggestive nature. The framework's importance will be determined by its adoption and the quality of the biological insights it enables in future studies.
    - **Interdisciplinary readership** The work is likely to appeal to a niche but significant interdisciplinary audience at the interface of biophysics, image analysis, and chromatin biology. The methods are technical, and the biological applications are specific, which may limit broader appeal across all of biology.
    - **Technical soundness** The technical approach is sound in principle, but the validation is currently insufficient to fully establish its robustness and accuracy. The lack of detailed statistical reporting and the limited scope of the benchmark are the main weaknesses.
    - **Readability for nonspecialists** The manuscript is clearly written for a specialist audience. The abstract and introduction are accessible to researchers in the field but may be challenging for those outside it. The methods are detailed but assume familiarity with AFM and image analysis concepts.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The manuscript presents a valuable and timely tool. The core concept is strong, and the modular design is a significant advantage. However, the current evidence does not fully support the claims of "human-level precision" and robust generalizability. The biological findings, while interesting, are not yet rigorously established. Addressing the major concerns, particularly the validation and statistical reporting, is essential before the case for the framework's broad utility and the specific biological conclusions is fully made.
