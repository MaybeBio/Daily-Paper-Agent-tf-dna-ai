## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text (abstract, introduction, methods, results, discussion, supporting information descriptions) as provided
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific content, methodological soundness, interpretation of results, and alignment with stated claims; limited to material supplied
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors present microsecond-resolved single-molecule FRET studies of T4 bacteriophage gp32 assembly on oligo(dT) n -dsDNA constructs of varying length and polarity, proposing a five-state kinetic model in which the gp32 C-terminal domain (CTD) orients monomers at ss-dsDNA junctions in a polarity-dependent manner, thereby facilitating nucleation of cooperative dimers and informing the regulatory role of gp32 in DNA replication.
- **Visible evidence base** Main text sections I–IV, Table 1 (referenced), Figures 1–5 (referenced), and supporting information descriptions (Figs. S1–S4, Tables S1–S8, equations S1–S8)

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript addresses an important and timely question in the field of DNA replication: how the intrinsically disordered CTD of gp32 contributes to polarity-dependent nucleation of cooperative filaments at ss-dsDNA junctions. The experimental approach, using microsecond-resolved smFRET with kinetic network modeling, represents a significant technical advance over prior work from the same group. The five-state model and the proposed role of the CTD in orienting monomers are plausible and potentially impactful. However, the manuscript as presented has several critical weaknesses. The results section is largely descriptive, with key quantitative data relegated to the supporting information. The assignment of macrostates to specific structural conformations (e.g., S2 vs S3 vs S4) appears to rely heavily on inference rather than direct structural evidence. The proposed model for CTD-mediated orientation, while interesting, is speculative and not directly tested. The comparison with prior work, particularly the discrepancies with gel electrophoresis studies, is discussed but not resolved. Most importantly, the biological significance of the findings, while asserted, is not convincingly demonstrated. The manuscript would benefit from a more rigorous presentation of the data, direct testing of the proposed model, and a clearer articulation of how these in vitro observations on short oligo(dT) constructs translate to the complex in vivo replication context.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers studying single-stranded DNA binding proteins, DNA replication mechanisms, protein-nucleic acid interactions, and the role of intrinsically disordered regions in protein function. The methodological advances in microsecond-resolved smFRET and kinetic network modeling would also interest biophysicists and single-molecule spectroscopists. The findings may have broader relevance to understanding how disordered protein domains facilitate target search and recognition processes in genome biology.
- **Major strengths** 1) The use of microsecond-resolved smFRET represents a significant technical improvement, enabling detection of transient intermediates not accessible in previous millisecond-resolution studies. 2) The systematic variation of construct length and polarity allows for a comprehensive mapping of the assembly landscape. 3) The kinetic network modeling approach, with explicit testing of hundreds of configurations, provides a rigorous framework for interpreting complex single-molecule data. 4) The proposed model for CTD-mediated orientation at junctions is mechanistically appealing and generates testable predictions. 5) The discussion of discrepancies with prior gel electrophoresis studies is honest and addresses an important methodological issue in the field.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **Concern ID** R1-M1
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Technical soundness
  - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that their data are "best modeled using a five-state network scheme" and that this is "supported by the fact that all the two-point TCFs are well described as a sum of four single exponential decay components."
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section, Fig. S1, Table S1 (location not provided)
  - **Concern** The justification for the five-state model is insufficient. The statement that four exponential decay components imply five states is a necessary but not sufficient condition. The authors do not demonstrate that alternative models (e.g., four-state or six-state) were systematically tested and rejected based on statistical criteria. The description of the model selection procedure in the main text is vague, and the supporting information is not provided for independent assessment. Without a clear demonstration that the five-state model is uniquely favored over alternatives (e.g., via information criteria such as AIC or BIC, or posterior model probabilities), the central kinetic framework of the paper is not rigorously established.
  - **Why it matters** The entire interpretation of the results, including the assignment of macrostates and the proposed assembly pathways, depends on the validity of the five-state model. If a simpler or alternative model fits the data equally well, the conclusions regarding polarity-dependent nucleation and CTD-mediated orientation would be substantially weakened.
  - **Resolution test** Provide a systematic model comparison, including fits with different numbers of states and different connectivity schemes, with quantitative statistical criteria (e.g., AIC, BIC, or Bayesian evidence) demonstrating that the five-state model is preferred. Show residuals and uncertainty estimates for the fits. Make the model selection procedure fully transparent in the main text or a clearly accessible supplement.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M2
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Interpretation / claim support
  - **Claim pointer** The authors assign macrostates S2, S3, and S4 to distinct monomer-bound conformations, with S3 assigned to a monomer bound at the ss-dsDNA junction and S2 to a monomer at medial positions, and propose that the CTD orients the monomer in a polarity-dependent manner.
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section, Figs. 3–4, Tables S2–S6 (location not provided)
  - **Concern** The structural assignments of the macrostates are not directly supported by the FRET data. The FRET efficiency reports on the end-to-end distance of the DNA construct, which is an indirect readout of protein position and orientation. The assignment of S3 to a junction-proximal monomer and S2 to a medial monomer appears to be based on inference from the kinetic connectivity and free energy values rather than on any direct structural measurement. The claim that the CTD "orients" the monomer is particularly speculative, as the FRET measurements do not report on the CTD conformation or its position relative to the junction. The authors acknowledge this in the discussion but do not provide any experimental validation of the proposed orientation mechanism.
  - **Why it matters** The central novelty of the paper is the proposed role of the CTD in polarity-dependent orientation and nucleation. If the structural assignments are not well-supported, the mechanistic conclusions are not established. The paper would be significantly strengthened by direct evidence for the proposed orientation, such as experiments with CTD-truncated or mutated gp32 variants, or with FRET labels placed on the protein rather than only on the DNA.
  - **Resolution test** Provide direct experimental tests of the proposed model. For example, repeat key experiments with gp32 variants lacking the CTD or with point mutations in the CTD, and show that the kinetic and thermodynamic parameters change in the predicted manner. Alternatively, use protein-labeled FRET constructs to directly report on the orientation of the CTD relative to the junction. At minimum, clearly articulate the assumptions underlying the structural assignments and acknowledge the indirect nature of the evidence.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M3
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Scientific importance / biological relevance
  - **Claim pointer** The authors state that their findings "provide new insights into the roles played by the ss-dsDNA junction and the gp32 CTD in the nucleation and association steps of gp32 binding and assembly that are involved in the control of the initiation stages of the overall DNA replication process."
  - **Evidence pointer** Discussion section, Fig. 5
  - **Concern** The connection between the in vitro findings on short oligo(dT) constructs and the in vivo replication context is asserted but not convincingly demonstrated. The constructs used (14-15 nt oligo(dT) overhangs) are much shorter than the ssDNA regions typically exposed at replication forks, and the model system lacks the other replication proteins (e.g., gp43 polymerase, gp44/62 clamp loader, gp45 clamp) that would be present in vivo. The proposed model in Fig. 5, while visually appealing, is largely speculative. The authors mention future studies with gp45 but do not provide any data addressing the relevance of their findings to the actual replication complex.
  - **Why it matters** The stated significance of the work depends on its relevance to DNA replication. If the findings are specific to the artificial in vitro system and do not extrapolate to the replication context, the broader impact of the paper is substantially diminished. The authors should either provide evidence connecting their findings to the replication process or temper their claims of biological significance.
  - **Resolution test** Provide experimental evidence linking the in vitro findings to the replication context. This could include experiments with longer ssDNA constructs, inclusion of other replication proteins, or studies in a more physiologically relevant system. Alternatively, revise the discussion to more carefully delineate the speculative nature of the extrapolation to in vivo replication and identify specific, testable predictions that could be addressed in future work.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M4
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Technical soundness
  - **Claim pointer** The authors state that their experiments were carried out at salt concentrations ([NaCl] = 100 mM and [MgCl2] = 6 mM) that "result in protein-DNA binding constants that are the same as those measured in living E. coli cells."
  - **Evidence pointer** Introduction section, Materials and Methods (location not provided)
  - **Concern** The claim of equivalence between in vitro salt conditions and in vivo binding constants is not substantiated with a citation or data. The intracellular environment is highly crowded and contains many other components that affect binding equilibria. While the chosen salt concentrations may be reasonable approximations, the strong claim of equivalence requires justification. Additionally, the FRET measurements are performed on surface-immobilized constructs, which may further affect binding kinetics and thermodynamics.
  - **Why it matters** The physiological relevance of the measurements depends on the validity of this claim. If the binding conditions are not truly equivalent to in vivo conditions, the quantitative conclusions may not be directly applicable to the replication context.
  - **Resolution test** Provide a citation or data supporting the claim of equivalence between the in vitro salt conditions and in vivo binding constants. Alternatively, soften the claim and acknowledge the limitations of the in vitro system in reproducing the intracellular environment.

- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** Originality: The work is original in its application of microsecond-resolved smFRET to study gp32 nucleation dynamics and in its proposal of a CTD-mediated orientation mechanism. However, the conceptual framework builds heavily on prior work from the same group, and the novelty of the mechanistic conclusions is not fully established given the indirect nature of the evidence. Scientific importance: The question of how ssDNA binding proteins nucleate and assemble at replication forks is important, and the potential role of disordered CTDs in this process is of broad interest. However, the demonstrated importance is limited by the artificial nature of the in vitro system and the lack of direct evidence for the proposed mechanism. Interdisciplinary readership: The work bridges single-molecule biophysics, nucleic acid biochemistry, and protein biophysics, and would be of interest to researchers in these areas. The broader readership in molecular biology may find the connections to replication physiology insufficiently developed. Technical soundness: The experimental approach is sophisticated and the analysis is rigorous in principle, but the lack of transparent model selection and the indirect nature of the structural assignments are significant weaknesses. Readability for nonspecialists: The manuscript is written for a specialist audience and would be challenging for nonspecialists to follow. The abstract and introduction assume significant background knowledge in single-molecule FRET and DNA replication biochemistry.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The manuscript addresses an important question with a sophisticated experimental approach, and the proposed model is interesting and testable. However, the current presentation does not fully establish the five-state model or the structural assignments of the macrostates, and the biological significance is asserted rather than demonstrated. These issues must be addressed before the case is established.
