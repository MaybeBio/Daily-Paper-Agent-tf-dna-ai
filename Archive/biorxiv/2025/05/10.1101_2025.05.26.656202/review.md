## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text (abstract, introduction, results, discussion, materials and methods, data availability, author contributions, funding, acknowledgements)
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific content, experimental design, data interpretation, and claims as presented in the provided manuscript text. Figures, tables, and supplementary materials are referenced but not directly examined.
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors investigate whether hypernucleosome formation is a conserved feature of canonical archaeal histones, using Thermococcus kodakarensis histones HTkA and HTkB alongside Methanothermus fervidus histones HMfA and HMfB. They employ tethered particle motion (TPM) and single-molecule force spectroscopy to characterize DNA compaction and hypernucleosome stability, and examine how Mg²⁺ modulates these properties. They report that Mg²⁺ promotes hypernucleosome formation and compaction for all tested histones, but increases mechanical stability only for HTkA and HTkB, not for HMfA and HMfB. They propose a model where Mg²⁺ exerts a generic electrostatic effect on compaction, with additional histone-specific effects on stability.
- **Visible evidence base** Results sections describing TPM experiments (Figure 1, 2, 3), magnetic tweezers force spectroscopy (Figure 4, 5), Table 1 (midpoint concentrations, EED values), Table 2 (fitted mechanical parameters), Supplementary Figures 1-7, and Materials and Methods sections for protein purification, TPM, and magnetic tweezers.
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Figures and tables themselves were not provided for direct inspection. Supplementary figures referenced (Supplementary Figures 1-7) were not available. The statistical mechanics model used for fitting force-distance curves is described only in a Supplementary Method that was not provided. Raw data and fitting procedures could not be independently verified.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript addresses a timely and interesting question in archaeal chromatin biology, namely whether hypernucleosome formation is conserved among canonical archaeal histones and how divalent cations modulate these structures. The combination of TPM and single-molecule force spectroscopy is appropriate and powerful for the questions posed. The finding that Mg²⁺ promotes compaction for all histones but stabilizes only HTk hypernucleosomes is potentially significant. However, several concerns limit the strength of the conclusions as currently presented. The differential response to Mg²⁺ between HTk and HMf histones is not mechanistically explained, and the authors' proposal that electrostatic shielding is a "generic effect" while additional "differential effects" depend on histone identity is somewhat underspecified. The manuscript would benefit from a more quantitative treatment of the proposed model and from additional experiments or analyses that could distinguish between competing explanations for the observed differences. The writing is generally clear, though some figure references and data presentation details could not be verified from the text alone.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers studying archaeal chromatin structure and organization, particularly those interested in histone evolution and the diversity of nucleosome architectures. The work also has relevance for understanding how environmental factors such as ion concentrations modulate chromatin accessibility, which is of interest to the broader chromatin biology community. The single-molecule approaches used here may also appeal to biophysicists studying protein-DNA interactions.
- **Major strengths** The study addresses a significant gap in understanding archaeal histone function, specifically whether hypernucleosome formation is a general feature of canonical archaeal histones. The use of two complementary single-molecule techniques (TPM and magnetic tweezers) provides both structural and mechanical information. The inclusion of four histone variants from two different organisms allows for comparative analysis. The experiments are well-designed with appropriate controls, including the stacking mutant HTkB K27A,K62A,K66A. The finding that Mg²⁺ has differential effects on stability but not on compaction is intriguing and potentially important.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **Concern ID** R1-M1
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Mechanistic interpretation
  - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that "Mg²⁺ ions exert a generic effect on hypernucleosome compactness and stability due to electrostatic DNA shielding, with additional differential effects depending on histone identity."
  - **Evidence pointer** Results sections "Mg²⁺ ions promote hypernucleosome formation and DNA compaction by archaeal histones" and "Single-molecule force spectroscopy resolves disparate Mg²⁺-mediated effects on HTk and HMf hypernucleosome stability"; Table 2
  - **Concern** The proposed model is not sufficiently developed or tested. The authors observe that Mg²⁺ enhances compaction for all histones but only stabilizes HTk hypernucleosomes. They attribute the compaction effect to "electrostatic DNA shielding" but do not provide a quantitative test of this idea. The "differential effects depending on histone identity" are not mechanistically explained. The authors note sequence differences at positions 14 and 66 between HMfA and HMfB, but do not connect these to the observed Mg²⁺ effects. For HTkA versus HTkB, the authors state that the stacking interaction residues are identical, yet the Mg²⁺ response differs. This is a central paradox that is not resolved. The model as stated is too vague to be falsifiable or predictive.
  - **Why it matters** The central claim of the paper is the differential response to Mg²⁺ between histone variants. Without a mechanistic explanation, the finding remains descriptive. The field would benefit from a clearer hypothesis about what molecular features determine sensitivity to Mg²⁺, which could be tested with additional mutants or structural analyses.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should either (a) provide a more specific mechanistic model with testable predictions, (b) perform additional experiments with chimeric or mutant histones to identify the residues responsible for differential Mg²⁺ sensitivity, or (c) use structural modeling or molecular dynamics to propose specific interactions between Mg²⁺ and histone residues that differ between variants. At minimum, the discussion should acknowledge the limitations of the current model and outline specific future experiments.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M2
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Quantitative rigor
  - **Claim pointer** The authors report midpoint concentrations and EED values with error estimates, and fitted mechanical parameters from the statistical mechanics model.
  - **Evidence pointer** Table 1, Table 2, Results sections, Materials and Methods
  - **Concern** The statistical treatment of the data is not fully described. For TPM experiments, the authors state that "means and standard deviations of the individual measurement series for each concentration were calculated by maximum likelihood estimation assuming a normal distribution" and that outliers with Z-score >3 or <-3 were excluded. However, it is unclear how many independent experiments were performed, how many molecules were analyzed per condition, and whether the reported errors represent inter-experiment or intra-experiment variability. For the magnetic tweezers data, the fitting procedure is described only in a Supplementary Method that was not provided. The number of force-distance curves analyzed per condition, the criteria for including/excluding curves, and the uncertainty in the fitted parameters are not stated. Without this information, the robustness of the quantitative conclusions cannot be assessed.
  - **Why it matters** The conclusions about differential Mg²⁺ effects rest on quantitative comparisons of fitted parameters. If the error estimates are underestimated or the number of replicates is small, the reported differences may not be statistically significant. The field of single-molecule biophysics has established standards for reporting replicate numbers and statistical analyses that should be followed.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should provide a detailed description of the statistical analysis, including the number of independent experiments, the number of molecules analyzed, the criteria for data inclusion/exclusion, and the method for calculating and reporting errors. For the magnetic tweezers data, the full fitting procedure and model should be described in the main text or a provided Supplementary Method. Ideally, the authors would also report confidence intervals or p-values for the key comparisons (e.g., Mg²⁺ effect on HTkA vs HTkB stability).
  - **Concern ID** R1-M3
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Experimental controls
  - **Claim pointer** The authors state that "To exclude the possibility that the observed decrease in RMS is due to an increase in ionic strength, we also performed titration experiments in HTkA, wherein the KCl concentration was adjusted to compensate for the increase in Mg²⁺ ions."
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section "Mg²⁺ ions promote hypernucleosome formation and DNA compaction by archaeal histones", Figure 3B
  - **Concern** The ionic strength control is only performed for HTkA, not for HTkB, HMfA, or HMfB. Given that the authors report differential responses to Mg²⁺ between histone variants, it is important to verify that the ionic strength effect is indeed negligible for all variants. If the KCl compensation experiment was only done for HTkA, the conclusion that Mg²⁺ specifically affects the other histones may be premature. Additionally, the authors state that "increasing the K⁺ concentration in the absence of Mg²⁺ did not cause a decrease in RMS" (Figure 3C), but it is unclear what K⁺ concentrations were tested and whether they spanned the range of ionic strengths relevant to the Mg²⁺ experiments.
  - **Why it matters** The distinction between specific Mg²⁺ effects and generic ionic strength effects is central to the paper's model. If the control is only performed for one histone variant, the claim that Mg²⁺ has a "generic effect" on compaction across all histones is not fully supported.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should perform the KCl compensation control for all four histone variants, or at minimum for one HTk and one HMf histone. The range of K⁺ concentrations tested should be stated, and ideally matched to the ionic strength contribution of the Mg²⁺ concentrations used.

- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** 
  - Originality: The work is original in its systematic comparison of four archaeal histone variants and its focus on Mg²⁺ as a modulator. The finding of differential Mg²⁺ effects on stability but not compaction is novel. However, the conceptual framework builds heavily on prior work from the same group, and the incremental advance over previous studies (e.g., Henneman et al., 2021) is moderate.
  - Scientific importance: The work addresses a fundamental question about how archaeal chromatin is regulated, which is relevant to understanding the evolution of chromatin and the diversity of nucleosome architectures. The potential link to environmental sensing is interesting but speculative. The importance is moderate to high for the archaeal chromatin community, but the broader impact may be limited unless the mechanistic basis for differential Mg²⁺ sensitivity is established.
  - Interdisciplinary readership: The work bridges molecular biology, biophysics, and evolutionary biology. The single-molecule approaches may appeal to biophysicists, while the chromatin biology aspects will interest molecular biologists. However, the manuscript is written in a way that is accessible to specialists in these fields, and the broader significance may not be immediately clear to nonspecialists.
  - Technical soundness: The experimental approaches are appropriate and well-established. However, the statistical reporting is incomplete, and the mechanistic model is not quantitatively tested. The technical soundness is adequate but would be strengthened by more rigorous statistical analysis and additional controls.
  - Readability for nonspecialists: The manuscript is generally well-written and logically organized. The abstract and introduction are accessible. However, the results section assumes familiarity with single-molecule techniques and the statistical mechanics model, which may limit accessibility for nonspecialists.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The core findings are potentially interesting and the experimental approach is sound, but the mechanistic model needs to be more specific and quantitatively tested, the statistical reporting needs to be more complete, and the ionic strength controls need to be extended to all histone variants. If these concerns are addressed, the manuscript could make a solid contribution to the field.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that Mg²⁺ exerts a "generic effect" on hypernucleosome compaction across all tested histones is not fully supported because the ionic strength control was only performed for HTkA.
- The claim that "differential effects depending on histone identity" explain the observed differences in Mg²⁺ sensitivity is not mechanistically supported. The authors note sequence differences between HMfA and HMfB but do not test their functional relevance. For HTkA versus HTkB, no sequence differences in stacking residues are identified, yet differential Mg²⁺ effects are reported.
- The speculation that differences in environmental Mg²⁺ concentrations between T. kodakarensis and M. fervidus habitats may explain the observed differential responses is not supported by quantitative data on environmental Mg²⁺ levels or by experiments testing physiologically relevant Mg²⁺ concentrations.
- The statement that "hypernucleosome formation is a conserved feature of canonical archaeal histone variants" is based on only two additional histones (HTkA, HTkB) beyond the previously characterized HMfA and HMfB. This is a limited sample and the claim of conservation should be tempered.
- The authors state that the HTkB K27A,K62A,K66A mutant shows a similar trend to wildtype in response to Mg²⁺, but the data are only presented in a supplementary figure that was not available for review. This claim cannot be fully evaluated.
