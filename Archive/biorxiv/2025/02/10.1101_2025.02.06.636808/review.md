## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript addresses a potentially interesting and under-explored aspect of the Min system, namely the DNA-binding activity of the MinC–MinD complex and the role of the MinC interdomain linker. The in vitro data, as described, suggest a cooperative effect between MinC and MinD for DNA binding, and the linker mutagenesis approach is a reasonable strategy to dissect this activity. However, the manuscript has several significant weaknesses. The in vitro binding data are described qualitatively without clear quantification or controls for the effects of the His-tag removal. The electron microscopy experiments, while visually suggestive, lack a rigorous quantitative framework. The in vivo phenotype is subtle, background-dependent, and the authors themselves acknowledge that the observed chromosome spreading could be due to multiple causes. The link between the in vitro DNA-binding defect and the in vivo chromosome phenotype is correlative and not mechanistically established. The presence of off-target mutations in the engineered strains further complicates interpretation. The manuscript is clearly a preliminary report that would require substantial additional experimentation and analysis to support its conclusions.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers studying bacterial cell division, the Min system, chromosome segregation, and nucleoid organization would be interested. The work also touches on the broader question of how membrane-associated protein complexes might interact with DNA, which could be of interest to those studying bacterial chromosome dynamics and the interface between cell division and DNA metabolism.
- **Major strengths** The study addresses a novel question regarding the potential DNA-binding role of the MinC–MinD complex, moving beyond the well-characterized function of the Min system in Z-ring positioning. The use of multiple complementary in vitro assays (EMSA, liposome co-sedimentation, electron microscopy) is a strength. The construction of strains with mutations at the native locus, rather than relying solely on overexpression, is a thoughtful approach to probe physiological relevance. The authors are appropriately cautious in the discussion, acknowledging the limitations of their data and the difficulty of distinguishing between chromosome compaction and segregation defects.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **Concern ID** R1-M1
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Technical soundness of in vitro binding assays
  - **Claim pointer** The claim that the MinCD complex binds DNA strongly and that this binding is dependent on the MinC linker is central to the manuscript.
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section "The MinCD complex binds DNA in vitro", Figures 1 and S1
  - **Concern** The EMSA and liposome co-sedimentation experiments are described qualitatively. The text states that MinC and MinD alone bind "extremely weakly" and that the complex binds "strongly," but no quantification (e.g., fraction of DNA shifted, binding constants, or statistical comparison) is provided. The effect of removing the His-tag is mentioned, but the data are not shown. It is unclear if the enhanced binding is due to a specific protein-DNA interaction or to non-specific aggregation of the protein complex with DNA, especially given the high protein concentrations used (up to 4 μM). The electron microscopy data show sheet formation, but the relationship between these sheets and the specific DNA-binding event is not rigorously established.
  - **Why it matters** The central conclusion of the paper rests on the specificity and affinity of the MinCD–DNA interaction. Without quantitative binding data and appropriate controls (e.g., a non-DNA-binding protein control, competition assays, or a demonstration that the interaction is not due to aggregation), the reader cannot assess the strength or specificity of the interaction. The lack of quantification makes it impossible to compare the relative affinities of the different mutants or to understand the stoichiometry of the interaction.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should provide quantitative EMSA data (e.g., fraction of bound DNA vs. protein concentration) for MinC, MinD, and the MinCD complex, with and without the His-tag. They should include a control protein (e.g., a non-DNA-binding protein like BSA or a MinC mutant that does not bind MinD) to demonstrate specificity. A competition assay with unlabeled DNA would also strengthen the case for specific binding. For the electron microscopy, a quantitative analysis of sheet width vs. DNA length, and a control without DNA, would be necessary.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M2
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** In vivo phenotype and genetic interpretation
  - **Claim pointer** The claim that strains expressing MinC linker variants show chromosome spreading, suggesting a role for MinCD-DNA binding in chromosome organization.
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section "E. coli strains expressing MinC linker mutants from the endogenous locus show chromosomal defects", Figures 5, S6, S7, S8
  - **Concern** The in vivo phenotype is subtle and only observed in the Fishow (HUα-GFP) background, not in the MG1655 background. The authors note that the silent mutant also shows a phenotype in the Fishow background, which is concerning. They attribute this to off-target mutations from the CRISPR/Cas9 procedure, but they have not identified or characterized these mutations. The chromosome spreading phenotype is quantified by a "spread" metric that is not clearly defined, and the statistical analysis is not described in detail. The authors themselves state that the phenotype could be due to reduced compaction, segregation defects, or both, and they do not provide any experiments to distinguish between these possibilities.
  - **Why it matters** The physiological relevance of the in vitro DNA-binding activity is a key claim of the paper. If the in vivo phenotype is not robust, is background-dependent, and is potentially confounded by off-target mutations, then the link between the in vitro biochemistry and the in vivo function is not established. The lack of a clear phenotype in the MG1655 background weakens the argument that MinCD-DNA binding is important for chromosome dynamics in wild-type cells.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should identify the off-target mutations in the engineered strains and, ideally, construct strains where the off-target mutations are repaired or where the MinC linker mutations are introduced without the off-target changes. They should provide a more detailed and robust quantification of the chromosome spreading phenotype, including a clear definition of the metric and a description of the statistical test. They should also perform experiments to distinguish between compaction and segregation defects, for example by using time-lapse microscopy to track chromosome dynamics or by using genetic reporters for specific stages of the cell cycle.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M3
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Mechanistic link between linker, DNA binding, and FtsZ inhibition
  - **Claim pointer** The claim that the MinC linker is critical for both DNA binding and FtsZ polymerization inhibition, and that these two functions are linked.
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section "The linker connecting MinC N- and C-terminal domains is needed for DNA binding with MinD and affects the activity of MinC towards FtsZ", Figure 4
  - **Concern** The manuscript shows that mutations in the linker abolish DNA binding and impair FtsZ inhibition. However, it is not clear if these are two independent functions of the linker or if the effect on FtsZ inhibition is a downstream consequence of the loss of DNA binding. The authors do not provide a mechanistic model for how the linker contributes to DNA binding. The finding that the isolated MinC C domain with the linker binds DNA better than full-length MinC is interesting but not explained. The authors do not test whether the linker mutants are properly folded or whether they affect the overall structure of MinC.
  - **Why it matters** The paper proposes a dual role for the linker, but without a mechanistic understanding, it is difficult to assess the significance of this finding. If the linker's role in FtsZ inhibition is simply due to a general structural defect, then the specific claim about its role in DNA binding is weakened. The observation that the C-terminal domain with the linker binds DNA better than the full-length protein is counterintuitive and needs an explanation.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should perform structural or biophysical characterization of the linker mutants (e.g., circular dichroism, size-exclusion chromatography) to ensure they are properly folded. They should test whether the linker mutants can still bind to MinD and be recruited to membranes. They should also perform a more detailed analysis of the FtsZ inhibition mechanism, for example, by testing whether the linker mutants can still bind to FtsZ. A model for how the linker facilitates DNA binding should be proposed and tested.

- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** 
  - **Originality** The question of whether the MinC–MinD complex binds DNA and the role of the MinC linker in this process is a novel angle on a well-studied system. The idea that the Min system might contribute to chromosome dynamics is not entirely new, but the specific focus on the MinC linker is original.
  - **Scientific importance** The potential importance is moderate. If the MinCD-DNA interaction is physiologically relevant, it would add a new layer to our understanding of the Min system and its potential roles beyond cell division. However, the current evidence is not strong enough to establish this importance.
  - **Interdisciplinary readership** The topic is primarily of interest to the bacterial cell biology and microbiology community. The work is unlikely to attract a broad interdisciplinary readership in its current form.
  - **Technical soundness** The technical soundness is currently insufficient. The in vitro binding assays lack quantification and controls. The in vivo experiments are confounded by off-target mutations and a subtle, background-dependent phenotype. The electron microscopy is descriptive.
  - **Readability for nonspecialists** The manuscript is written in a reasonably clear style, but the lack of quantitative data and the complexity of the in vivo experiments make it difficult for a nonspecialist to fully assess the strength of the claims.
- **Recommendation posture** Currently not established from the provided evidence. The manuscript addresses an interesting question, but the central claims are not sufficiently supported by the data as presented. The authors need to provide quantitative binding data, address the off-target mutation issue, and provide a clearer mechanistic link between the in vitro and in vivo observations.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that the MinCD complex binds DNA "strongly" is unsupported without quantitative binding data.
- The claim that the MinC linker is "essential" for DNA binding is based on qualitative EMSA data and needs quantitative confirmation.
- The claim that the in vivo chromosome spreading phenotype is due to loss of MinCD-DNA binding is unsupported, as the phenotype is background-dependent and could be due to off-target mutations or effects on FtsZ inhibition.
- The claim that MinE and FtsZ "interfere" with DNA binding is based on qualitative assays and needs quantitative validation.
- The claim that the MinCD-DNA interaction has a "physiological role" is speculative and not directly tested.
- The electron microscopy data suggesting ordered structures is descriptive and lacks a quantitative or mechanistic framework.
