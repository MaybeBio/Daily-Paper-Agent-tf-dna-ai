They further characterize one candidate, GRP7, demonstrating in vitro binding of two uranyl ions within its RNA recognition motif (RRM) domain, identifying specific coordinating residues, and showing that uranyl binding interferes with nucleic acid binding.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript presents a potentially valuable contribution to the field of uranium toxicology in plants by identifying a set of candidate uranyl-binding proteins and providing a detailed in vitro characterization of one such protein, GRP7. The methodological approach is ambitious and combines several advanced techniques. However, the current manuscript has significant limitations. The most critical issue is the lack of direct evidence that the identified proteins, including GRP7, actually bind uranyl ions in the cellular context (in cellulo or in planta). The in vitro characterization of GRP7 is thorough but is performed with a recombinant protein, and the physiological relevance of this interaction remains speculative. The claim that uranyl binding to GRP7 contributes to uranium toxicity in plants is not supported by any in planta functional data. The manuscript would be substantially strengthened by experiments that directly test the functional consequences of uranyl binding to GRP7 in a cellular or organismal context.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in the fields of environmental toxicology, plant stress physiology, and metalloproteomics would find this work of interest. The identification of a new set of candidate uranyl-binding proteins in plants provides a starting point for further investigation. The detailed structural and biochemical characterization of GRP7's uranyl-binding sites is a novel contribution that could inform studies on the molecular mechanisms of uranium toxicity. The work may also be of interest to those studying the broader family of glycine-rich RNA-binding proteins and their potential roles in metal stress responses.
- **Major strengths**
    1.  The study addresses a significant knowledge gap by attempting to identify in cellulo targets of uranium in plants, moving beyond in vitro affinity-based approaches.
    2.  The combination of two orthogonal fractionation strategies (anion exchange and hydroxyapatite) with ICP-MS and high-resolution mass spectrometry is a robust methodological approach for metalloproteomics.
    3.  The detailed in vitro characterization of GRP7, including stoichiometry determination, NMR-based identification of binding sites, and mutagenesis, is rigorous and provides a clear molecular picture of the interaction.
    4.  The identification of specific amino acid residues (Asp5, Glu7, Asp42, Glu44) involved in uranyl coordination is a valuable finding.
    5.  The demonstration that uranyl binding interferes with nucleic acid binding to GRP7 provides a plausible mechanistic link to toxicity.
- **Major Concerns**
    - **Concern ID** R1-M1
    - **Severity** Major
    - **Blocking** Yes
    - **Axis** Evidence for in cellulo binding
    - **Claim pointer** The manuscript's central premise is the identification of "uranyl-binding proteins in Arabidopsis thaliana cells exposed to uranium." The title and abstract imply that the identified proteins bind uranyl in the cellular environment.
    - **Evidence pointer** Section 3.1, 3.2, 3.3; Figures 2, 3; Supplementary Figures S3-S6
    - **Concern** The experimental evidence presented does not demonstrate that the identified proteins are bound to uranyl in the cell. The strategy relies on fractionating protein extracts and correlating protein abundance with uranium content in chromatographic fractions. This approach can identify proteins that co-fractionate with uranium, but it does not prove a direct, specific protein-uranium interaction. The authors acknowledge that the interaction could be lost or non-specific during fractionation. The claim of "in cellulo" identification is therefore an overstatement of what the data can support. The evidence supports the identification of proteins that co-purify with uranium under specific chromatographic conditions, which is a weaker claim.
    - **Why it matters** The primary novelty of the paper rests on the identification of physiologically relevant uranyl targets. If the identified proteins are merely artifacts of the fractionation process, the significance of the findings is greatly diminished. The distinction between "proteins that can bind uranyl" and "proteins that do bind uranyl in the cell" is fundamental to the paper's contribution to understanding uranium toxicity mechanisms.
    - **Resolution test** The authors must either provide direct evidence of a uranyl-protein interaction in the cellular context (e.g., using a technique like X-ray fluorescence microscopy coupled with immunodetection, or a pull-down assay from cells with a uranyl-specific probe) or substantially revise the claims to state that they have identified "candidate uranyl-binding proteins" based on in vitro and ex vivo fractionation, without implying in cellulo binding.
    - **Concern ID** R1-M2
    - **Severity** Major
    - **Blocking** Yes
    - **Axis** Physiological relevance of GRP7-uranyl interaction
    - **Claim pointer** The abstract and discussion state that uranyl binding to GRP7 "contributes to U toxicity in plants" and that GRP7 could be a "direct molecular target of U toxicity."
    - **Evidence pointer** Section 3.4, 3.5, 3.6, 3.7, 4; Figures 5, 7, 8
    - **Concern** The manuscript demonstrates that recombinant GRP7 binds uranyl in vitro and that this binding interferes with nucleic acid binding. However, there is no experimental evidence that this interaction occurs in planta or that it has any functional consequence for the plant. The authors do not present any data on the phenotype of GRP7 knockout or overexpression lines under uranium stress, nor do they show that the identified binding sites are relevant for uranium toxicity in vivo. The conclusion that this interaction contributes to toxicity is a hypothesis that is not tested.
    - **Why it matters** The paper aims to provide "insights into the molecular mechanisms of uranium toxicity." Without functional validation, the characterization of GRP7 remains an interesting in vitro observation. The claim of a role in toxicity is a significant leap that is not supported by the presented data. This weakens the overall impact and the paper's ability to inform strategies for enhancing plant resilience.
    - **Resolution test** The authors should either provide in planta data (e.g., comparing uranium sensitivity of wild-type vs. grp7 mutant or GRP7-overexpressing plants, or showing that expression of a uranyl-binding-deficient GRP7 mutant fails to complement a phenotype) or temper the conclusions to state that the in vitro interaction suggests a potential mechanism that warrants further in vivo investigation.
    - **Concern ID** R1-M3
    - **Severity** Major
    - **Blocking** No
    - **Axis** Validation of candidate UraBPs
    - **Claim pointer** The manuscript identifies 57 candidate proteins as potential uranyl-binding proteins.
    - **Evidence pointer** Section 3.2, 3.3; Supplementary Tables S2, S3, S4
    - **Concern** The manuscript lists 57 candidate proteins but only provides detailed characterization for one (GRP7). There is no validation for the other 56 candidates. It is unclear if these candidates were selected based on specific criteria (e.g., presence of known metal-binding motifs, functional relevance) or if they are simply a list of all proteins that co-fractionated with uranium. The lack of any validation or prioritization for the vast majority of the identified candidates makes it difficult to assess the overall value of the screening effort.
    - **Why it matters** A key output of a metalloproteomics screen is a list of high-confidence candidates. Without any validation, the list of 57 proteins is of limited use to the community. The paper would be stronger if it provided some evidence, even computational or based on literature, to support the potential relevance of the other candidates, or if it clearly stated that these are unvalidated candidates requiring further study.
    - **Resolution test** The authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the 57 candidates, perhaps including a table with their known functions, predicted subcellular localizations, and any literature evidence for metal binding. They could also perform a simple validation experiment, such as an in vitro binding assay for a few of the top candidates, to demonstrate the broader applicability of their screening method.

- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria**
    - **Originality** The work is original in its application of a metalloproteomics approach to identify uranyl-binding proteins in plant cells and in the detailed structural characterization of GRP7's uranyl-binding sites. The finding that uranyl binds to the RRM domain of GRP7 and interferes with RNA binding is novel.
    - **Scientific importance** The identification of potential cellular targets of uranium in plants is important for understanding the molecular mechanisms of uranium toxicity and for developing strategies for phytoremediation. However, the lack of in vivo validation for the key claims significantly reduces the immediate impact of the findings.
    - **Interdisciplinary readership** The topic is of interest to researchers in environmental science, plant biology, toxicology, and structural biology. The combination of techniques used is likely to appeal to a broad audience.
    - **Technical soundness** The in vitro biochemical and structural work appears to be technically sound, although the lack of access to the actual data figures limits a full assessment. The metalloproteomics approach is reasonable, but the interpretation of the results as evidence of in cellulo binding is not sound.
    - **Readability for nonspecialists** The manuscript is generally well-written and the logic is easy to follow. However, the methods section is dense and may be difficult for a non-specialist to fully understand. The abstract is clear and accessible.
- **Recommendation posture** The manuscript is not currently suitable for publication in its present form. The central claims of in cellulo identification and a role in toxicity are not supported by the evidence presented. The work is promising and the in vitro characterization of GRP7 is a solid contribution, but the authors need to either provide direct in vivo evidence to support their claims or substantially revise their conclusions to be more cautious. I would be supportive of the manuscript if the authors can address the major concerns, particularly by providing in planta functional data for GRP7 and by tempering the claims about the in cellulo identification of the other candidates.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that the 57 identified proteins are "uranyl-binding proteins in Arabidopsis thaliana cells" is unsupported. The evidence only demonstrates co-fractionation of proteins with uranium in cell extracts.
- The claim that uranyl binding to GRP7 "contributes to U toxicity in plants" is unsupported. No in planta experiments were performed to test this hypothesis.
- The claim that the identified residues (Asp5, Glu7, Asp42, Glu44) are the sole or primary coordinating residues for uranyl is not fully supported, as the mutagenesis data are not shown and the possibility of additional contributing residues is acknowledged.
- The statement that "GRP7 could be a direct molecular target of U toxicity" is speculative and not directly tested.
- The assertion that the optimized oligonucleotide binding to GRP7 is directly relevant to the in vivo function of GRP7 is an extrapolation from in vitro data.
