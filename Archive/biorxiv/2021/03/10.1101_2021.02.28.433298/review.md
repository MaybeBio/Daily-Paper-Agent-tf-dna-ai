## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text (abstract, introduction, methods, results, discussion) from bioRxiv preprint
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific content and technical soundness of the reported findings; no assessment of editorial fit or publication venue
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors report that the CytR DNA-binding domain (DBD) adopts distinct structural and dynamic states depending on whether it is bound to specific, full-site, or nonspecific DNA. They find that nonspecific DNA binding results in a partially folded, highly dynamic "fuzzy" complex, that the hinge helix does not fold upon full-site binding, and that specific DNA contacts are required for full stabilization of the DBD structure. They further propose that DNA sequence-dependent DBD dynamics may contribute to differential gene regulation by CytR.
- **Visible evidence base** Abstract, Introduction, Materials and Methods (partial), Results (narrative with references to Figures 1-7 and Supplementary Figures S1-S2), Discussion, Funding
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Figures 1-7 and Supplementary Figures S1-S2 are referenced but not provided in the submitted text. Raw relaxation data, hydrogen exchange curves, and fluorescence anisotropy binding isotherms are not included. Full experimental details for some methods (e.g., NOESY acquisition parameters, data fitting procedures) are incomplete.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript addresses an important question in DNA recognition by transcription factors, specifically how a member of the LacR family distinguishes specific from nonspecific DNA and how this relates to differential gene regulation. The authors present a compelling narrative that the CytR DBD is intrinsically disordered in its free state, forms a fuzzy complex with nonspecific DNA, and only becomes fully structured upon specific binding. The comparison to LacR is valuable and highlights unique features of CytR. However, the absence of the actual figures and spectra makes it impossible to evaluate the quality of the NMR data, the validity of the assignments, or the robustness of the relaxation and hydrogen exchange analyses. The binding affinity data are presented without full error analysis or model comparison. The claim that DBD dynamics contribute to differential gene regulation is speculative and not directly tested. The manuscript is well-written and the logic is clear, but the evidence base as provided is insufficient to fully support the conclusions.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers studying transcription factor DNA recognition, particularly those interested in intrinsically disordered proteins, fuzzy complexes, and the LacR/GalR family of bacterial repressors. The findings on the coupling between DNA sequence, protein folding, and dynamics are relevant to understanding how transcription factors achieve regulatory specificity. The comparison between CytR and LacR provides insight into how homologous proteins can evolve different DNA-binding strategies.
- **Major strengths** The study addresses a fundamental question in protein-DNA recognition using a well-defined system. The combination of binding affinity measurements, NMR relaxation, and hydrogen exchange provides a multi-faceted view of the DBD dynamics. The comparison between specific, nonspecific, and full-site DNA binding is well-designed. The finding that CytR forms a fuzzy complex with nonspecific DNA is novel and contrasts with the behavior of LacR. The authors are appropriately cautious in interpreting their data and acknowledge limitations.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **Concern ID** R1-M1
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Evidence quality
  - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that the DBD bound to nonspecific DNA populates a flexible, molten state with a dearth of NOEs and complete lack of hydrogen exchange protection.
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section, Figures 3, 5, S1, S2; location not provided
  - **Concern** The central claim of a molten, fuzzy complex relies on the absence of NOEs and rapid hydrogen exchange. However, the actual spectra, NOE assignments, and exchange data are not provided. The statement that "a dearth of NOEs" is observed is qualitative and cannot be evaluated without seeing the data. Similarly, the hydrogen exchange data for the nonspecific complex are described as "too rapid to obtain quantitative rate information," but no actual time course data are shown. The comparison of NOE counts between specific and nonspecific complexes is mentioned as 36%, but the method of counting and the significance of this difference are not described.
  - **Why it matters** The conclusion that the nonspecific complex is a fuzzy, molten state is a key finding of the manuscript. If the NOE and hydrogen exchange data are not of sufficient quality or are misinterpreted, this conclusion would be unsupported. The absence of the actual data prevents the reader from assessing whether the observed effects are due to genuine protein dynamics or to experimental artifacts such as incomplete binding, sample heterogeneity, or exchange broadening.
  - **Resolution test** Provide the actual HSQC spectra for the DBD bound to specific and nonspecific DNA, showing peak assignments. Provide representative NOE strips or a plot of NOE counts per residue for both complexes. Provide hydrogen exchange decay curves for protected residues in the specific complex and show that the nonspecific complex shows no protection over the same time course. Quantify the fraction of bound protein under the NMR conditions to rule out incomplete binding as an explanation for the observed dynamics.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M2
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Data interpretation
  - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that the binding affinities of the DBD for specific and nonspecific DNA are similar, with at most a 4-fold difference, and that this is consistent with a modest intrinsic specificity.
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section, Figure 4; location not provided
  - **Concern** The fluorescence anisotropy binding data are presented as a single figure with fitted ΔG° values, but the actual isotherms, the number of replicates, the error analysis, and the model used for fitting are not described in sufficient detail. The authors acknowledge that the method is insensitive to binding stoichiometry and position, but they do not discuss whether the data could be fit by alternative models, such as cooperative binding or multiple binding modes. The claim that the apparent affinity difference between the 14 bp and 30 bp nonspecific DNAs is exactly accounted for by the number of binding sites assumes a site size of 8 bp, but this is not experimentally determined.
  - **Why it matters** The conclusion that the DBD has modest intrinsic specificity is important for the overall narrative that specificity arises from structural and dynamic differences rather than from binding affinity alone. If the binding data are not robust, this conclusion is weakened. The lack of model comparison and error analysis makes it difficult to assess the confidence in the reported ΔG° values.
  - **Resolution test** Provide the full binding isotherms for all four DNA substrates, including error bars and the number of replicates. Perform a global fit of the data with a model that accounts for the number of binding sites and compare alternative models (e.g., cooperative vs. independent binding). Report the confidence intervals for the fitted parameters. If possible, use an orthogonal method (e.g., ITC or SPR) to confirm the binding affinities.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M3
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Scope of conclusions
  - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that the lack of hinge helix folding upon full-site DNA binding and the absence of dimer formation indicate that the CBD is necessary for dimerization, and that this has implications for differential gene regulation.
  - **Evidence pointer** Discussion section; location not provided
  - **Concern** The authors conclude that the hinge helix does not fold and a dimer interface is not formed when the DBD binds to full-site DNA, based on the observation of one set of NMR signals consistent with fast exchange between the two binding sites. However, the actual evidence for this is not presented. The authors do not show any data that directly addresses the conformation of the hinge helix or the formation of a dimer interface. The conclusion that the CBD is necessary for dimerization is inferred from the absence of these features, but this is not directly tested. The connection to differential gene regulation is speculative and not supported by any functional experiments.
  - **Why it matters** The claim that the DBD does not dimerize on full-site DNA and that the CBD is required for dimer formation is a significant conclusion that goes beyond the data presented. If this is incorrect, it would affect the interpretation of how CytR regulates its target operons. The link to differential gene regulation is an important motivation for the study, but the manuscript does not provide any direct evidence for this link.
  - **Resolution test** Provide direct evidence for the absence of hinge helix folding, such as chemical shift perturbations or NOE data for the hinge region in the full-site complex. If possible, perform analytical ultracentrifugation or size exclusion chromatography to directly test for dimer formation. To support the claim about differential gene regulation, discuss how the observed DBD dynamics might affect interactions with the CBD and CRP, and ideally provide some functional data or a more detailed structural model.

- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** The absence of the actual NMR spectra, NOE data, hydrogen exchange curves, and binding isotherms is a critical failing. - **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** Originality: The finding that a LacR family DBD forms a fuzzy complex with nonspecific DNA and that specific binding induces a disorder-to-order transition is novel and adds to the growing understanding of intrinsically disordered proteins in transcription regulation. Scientific importance: The work addresses a fundamental question about how transcription factors achieve specificity and how DNA sequence can influence protein dynamics. The implications for differential gene regulation are potentially important, but not directly tested. Interdisciplinary readership: The work will be of interest to structural biologists, biochemists, and molecular biologists studying transcription regulation. The NMR methodology is specialized, but the conclusions are presented in a way that is accessible to a broader audience. Technical soundness: The technical quality cannot be fully assessed without the actual data. The methods described are appropriate, but the lack of data presentation is a major concern. Readability for nonspecialists: The manuscript is well-written and the logic is clear. The use of jargon is appropriate for the target audience, and the key concepts are explained adequately.
- **Recommendation posture** Currently not established from the provided evidence. The manuscript presents an interesting and potentially important story, but the absence of the actual data prevents a full evaluation. The authors should provide the figures and raw data, address the concerns about the binding analysis, and provide direct evidence for the claims about hinge helix folding and dimer formation. If these issues are resolved, the manuscript could be a valuable contribution to the field.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that the DBD forms a "molten" or "fuzzy" complex with nonspecific DNA is not supported by the data provided, as the NOE and hydrogen exchange data are not shown.
- The claim that the hinge helix does not fold upon full-site DNA binding is not directly supported by any data presented.
- The claim that the CBD is necessary for dimer formation is inferred and not directly tested.
- The claim that DBD dynamics contribute to differential gene regulation is speculative and not supported by functional experiments.
- The binding affinities for specific and nonspecific DNA are reported without sufficient error analysis or model comparison, making the claim of modest specificity uncertain.
